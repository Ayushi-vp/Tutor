A support agent goes live and does well: resolution rates are up, customers are happier, and the team is proud. Six weeks later the finance lead asks three questions nobody can answer. What does a resolved ticket cost? Why did last month's model bill come to $210,000 when the pilot suggested $40,000? And would it be cheaper to send the easy tickets to a smaller model? The team digs in and finds the usual story. A timestamp added to the system prompt had broken prompt caching for three weeks. Hard tickets were taking up to 30 steps, and each step re-read the whole growing conversation. The model was reasoning at maximum effort on every step, including steps that just reformatted a tool result. And the pilot had been costed on the average ticket, while the bill was driven by the long tail.

Cost and latency are not afterthoughts for agents; they decide which agent products are viable. An agent that resolves a ticket for $0.05 is a different business from one that costs $0.60, and an agent that answers in 12 seconds is a different product from one that takes 90. Both numbers are the product of design choices this track has covered: steps (ag1, ag7), context growth (ag5), tool result sizes (ag3), caching (ag5), routing and models, and budgets (ag11). This lesson puts them together quantitatively.

It builds a full cost model for an agent handling 100,000 tasks a day, from tokens per task through cached and uncached pricing at prices checked on 6 October 2026, to daily and monthly totals and where the money goes. It compares two routing policies on cost per successful task, covers step-level routing and reasoning effort, models latency per step and the effect of parallelism, and examines early exit and step caps, including the case where a cap that saves tokens costs more overall. ae2 introduced cost modelling for single LLM requests; this lesson is its agent-shaped sequel.

A taxi meter with three parts

An agent's bill works like a taxi meter. There is a flag fare, paid at the start of every ride: the system prompt and tool definitions, which every step must read. There is a per-kilometre charge that grows with distance: each step adds its output and tool result to the context, and every later step re-reads it, so a long ride costs disproportionately more than a short one. And there is the tip for the driver's talk: output tokens, including any reasoning, priced at several times the rate of input. Prompt caching is like a season ticket for roads you travel every day: once a stretch of context has been paid for, travelling it again costs a tenth as much.

The analogy points to the right questions. How long is the typical ride (steps per task), and how long is the longest (the tail)? How heavy is the luggage you add at each stop (tool results)? How much does the driver talk (output and reasoning)? And are you actually using the season ticket (cache hit share)? It breaks where agents differ from taxis: some rides fail and have to be redone, by another taxi or by a person, and that cost belongs on the meter too. That is why the unit in this lesson is cost per successful task.

One more property of the meter matters: it runs on every ride, all day. A design choice that adds one cent to a task looks trivial in a demo and costs $1,000 a day at 100,000 tasks, so the habit of multiplying every per-task number by daily volume, before debating it, keeps discussions grounded.

Where an agent's money goes

Four questions to ask of any agent's bill

Before optimising anything, answer four questions with data. How many steps does a typical task take, and how many does the expensive tail take? Steps drive the quadratic growth of input. How much does each step add? Tool results and outputs set the per-step growth that every later step re-reads. How much is being re-read at full price? The cache-read share of input tells you whether caching is doing its job. What does a failure cost? If failed runs go to a person or are retried on a bigger model, those costs belong in the same equation. Most surprises in agent bills come from not knowing one of these four numbers.

ae2 covered the pricing mechanics of single requests: price sheets, caching break-even, batch, routing and self-hosting. This lesson keeps those tools and applies them to multi-step runs, where step counts, context growth and failures change which lever matters most.

The rest of the lesson builds this picture up from first principles and then uses it to compare designs: what caching buys, what routing buys, what parallelism and early exit buy, and how to tell which lever to pull first.

1. Tokens per task

Start from ag5's growth model. A run starts with a context of C0 tokens; each step adds Δ tokens (the model's output plus the tool result); step k (from 0) reads C0 + kΔ; and n steps read

input(n) = n × C0 + Δ × n(n − 1)/2        output(n) = n × out_per_step

Use these assumptions for a support agent: C0 = 6,200 tokens (6,000 of system prompt and tool definitions, 200 of user message), Δ = 1,500, and 300 output tokens per step. Tasks come in three classes:

ClassShareStepsInput tokensOutput tokens
Easy60%44 × 6,200 + 1,500 × 6 = 33,8001,200
Medium30%88 × 6,200 + 1,500 × 28 = 91,6002,400
Hard10%1616 × 6,200 + 1,500 × 120 = 279,2004,800

The hard task has four times the steps of the easy one and more than eight times the input. That non-linearity is why averages mislead: the 10% of hard tasks carry far more than 10% of the tokens.

2. Pricing a task, uncached and cached

Prices are Anthropic's list prices checked on 6 October 2026, per million tokens. Claude Sonnet 5.5: input $2.00, five-minute cache write $2.50, cache read $0.20, output $10.00. Claude Haiku 4.5: input $1.00, write $1.25, read $0.10, output $5.00.

Uncached, a Sonnet easy task costs 33,800 × $2 + 1,200 × $10 = $0.0676 + $0.0120 = $0.080; medium $0.207; hard $0.606. Input is 85-90% of each.

Cached, two facts change the arithmetic. Within a task, each step reads the previous step's context from cache and writes only its new tokens. Across tasks, the 6,000-token prefix of system prompt and tools is identical for every task, and at 100,000 tasks a day (about 1.2 per second) it never goes cold under a five-minute lifetime, so each task reads it from cache too. Each task therefore writes only its own content: the 200-token user message plus Δ for each step after the first. For the easy task, writes are 200 + 3 × 1,500 = 4,700 tokens and reads are the other 29,100:

Once caching works, the levers that matter are output length (including reasoning), the size of what each step adds (tool results), and the number of steps.

The model leaves out lines that a real bill includes. Hosted tools: Anthropic's web search costs $10 per 1,000 searches (checked 6 October 2026), so two searches per task would add $0.02, more than a third of the cached token cost. Execution environments: Claude Managed Agents bills $0.08 per session-hour of running time on top of tokens, so a three-minute session adds $0.004 per task, $400 a day at this volume; self-hosted sandboxes have their own compute cost (ag9). Retries on transient errors (ag11), evaluation runs (ag12) and human handling of failures and escalations, which at $4 per escalation dwarfs token costs if even 5% of tasks need a person. A cost model that leadership can trust reports cost per successful task with all of these included.

4. Two routing policies, compared

Can routing cut the $5,343 a day? Compare two policies on the same traffic, with assumed success rates you would measure on your eval (ag12).

Policy A: everything on Sonnet 5.5, cached. Success: easy 97%, medium 90%, hard 75%, so 0.6 × 0.97 + 0.3 × 0.90 + 0.1 × 0.75 = 92.7%. Cost $0.0534 per task, $0.058 per successful task, $5,343 a day.

Policy B: route with a cascade. A router call on Haiku 4.5 (about 800 input tokens, mostly cached, and a one-word output: about $0.0004) predicts difficulty. It sends 95% of easy tasks and, by mistake, 8% of medium and hard tasks to Haiku 4.5, the rest to Sonnet. Haiku costs half as much per token (easy $0.0148, medium $0.0335, hard $0.078 cached) and succeeds on 94% of easy, 70% of medium and 40% of hard tasks. A verifier checks Haiku's results and catches 80% of its failures, which are re-run on Sonnet.

Policy B saves about 14% and, slightly surprisingly, succeeds a little more often: the verifier-triggered re-run gives caught failures a second attempt. Three lessons come out of it. First, the saving is modest because the cheap model can only take the easy tasks, and easy tasks are only a third of spend; route where the money is. Second, the verifier is load-bearing: if it missed most of Haiku's failures, they would reach customers, and the policy would look cheap while being worse. Third, misroutes matter: every hard task sent to Haiku costs a Haiku run plus, usually, a Sonnet re-run. Learned routers can do better than this simple scheme; RouteLLM (Ong et al., 2024) trained routers on preference data and reported cost reductions of more than half in some settings without quality loss, and ae2's FrugalGPT case showed cascades with a scorer. The method is the same: choose thresholds by expected cost per successful task, not by router accuracy.

5. Step-level routing and reasoning effort

Task-level routing sends a whole run to one model. Step-level routing chooses per step: the strong model plans, decides and writes the final answer, while a cheap model handles mechanical steps such as summarising a long tool result before it enters the main context, extracting fields, or classifying an error. This targets the medium and hard tasks that task-level routing cannot touch. The trade-offs: hand-offs between models can lose information, each model has its own cache (so switching models can cost cache writes), and the cheap model's mistakes enter the strong model's context. As always, measure.

Reasoning effort is a per-step dial on the most expensive token type. Thinking tokens are billed as output (rs7); a model that thinks 2,000 tokens per step over 20 steps adds 40,000 output tokens, $0.40 at $10 per million, more than this lesson's whole cached medium task. Set effort per step type: high for planning and hard decisions, low for routine tool calls and formatting. Because a model that thinks more may need fewer steps, judge effort settings by cost per successful task, not tokens per step.

6. Latency

One step's latency is approximately

step_latency ≈ TTFT + output_tokens / tokens_per_second + tool_time

where TTFT is time to first token. With illustrative figures (measure your own: they vary by model, region, load and prompt size), a strong model might have 0.8 seconds of TTFT on a mostly cached prompt and generate 70 tokens per second, and a small model 0.4 seconds and 150 tokens per second; with 300 output tokens and 0.6 seconds of tool time, a step takes about 5.7 seconds on the strong model and 3.0 on the small one. A 4-step easy task then takes about 23 seconds on Sonnet-class and 12 on Haiku-class models; a 16-step hard task over 90 seconds. The levers:

  • Caching cuts TTFT, because cached prefix tokens skip prefill.
  • Shorter outputs and lower effort cut generation time, the biggest term.
  • Fewer steps (better tools, workflows for known paths) cut the number of terms.
  • Parallel tool calls (ag7) collapse several round trips into one: eight sequential lookups at 5.5 seconds each become about 10 seconds.
  • Streaming progress to the user does not cut latency but makes it tolerable.
  • Faster models or fast modes for latency-critical paths, at a price: Anthropic's fast mode for Opus 5.5 is listed at $8 input and $40 output per million, double its standard price.

Latency and cost often pull in opposite directions: parallel sub-agents, hedged requests and fast modes buy time with money; batch processing (50% off on Anthropic's Batch API) buys money with time. Decide which matters for each path: an interactive support reply has a latency budget; an overnight back-office agent has a cost budget.

7. Early exit and step caps

Early exit stops a run as soon as continuing cannot help. It has two halves. Done: a verifier confirms the task is complete (the refund exists, the tests pass), so the agent does not spend further steps polishing or re-checking. Stuck: no-progress detection (ag2) notices repeated calls, repeated errors or no new information, and stops before the budget is exhausted.

Step caps are blunter, and their effect depends on what happens to the runs they cut. Take the hard tasks: 16 steps, $0.156 cached, 75% success. Cap them at 12 steps: token cost falls to $0.109. Suppose, from the success-versus-steps curve on your eval, success falls to 70% because some runs needed 13-16 steps. If a failed task goes to a person at $4:

No capCap at 12
Token cost$0.156$0.109
Escalations (× $4)0.25 × $4 = $1.000.30 × $4 = $1.20

The cap saves 4.7 cents of tokens and costs 20 cents of human time. Caps should be set from the curve of success against steps: place the cap where additional steps stop raising success, which for most agents is well beyond the median, and rely on no-progress detection, not a low cap, to kill loops.

8. From a model to a forecast

A cost model becomes a budget once it is turned into a forecast. Three adjustments make it honest. Use measured distributions per task class, not single averages, because the tail drives the bill. Project the task mix forward: if adoption grows faster among power users who bring harder tasks, the average cost rises even at constant prices. And add headroom for retries, evaluation runs and price or model changes, typically 10-20%. For the worked agent, $5,343 a day of model spend becomes a monthly forecast of roughly $160,000 plus tools, runtime, evaluation and human handling, reviewed monthly against actuals by class so that any divergence is traced to a cause (a new long-running task type, a cache regression, an effort change) rather than absorbed.

The tail sets the bill

Because input grows with the square of the step count, cost per task is highly skewed: a task at the 99th percentile of steps can cost twenty or thirty times the median. Budgets, routing and optimisation decisions made on averages miss this. Cost dashboards should show the distribution (median, p95, p99, maximum) and the most expensive runs of each day with links to their traces; the tail is where loops, retries, oversized tool results and broken caching show up first. Forecasts should be built on the distribution by task class, not on a single average, which is how the opening story's pilot underestimated its bill fivefold.

Routing errors are asymmetric

A router makes two kinds of mistake: sending an easy task to the expensive path (wasted money, bounded) and sending a hard task to the cheap path (a failure, a re-run, perhaps an escalation or a customer-visible error, sometimes unbounded). Thresholds should therefore be biased toward the expensive path where uncertainty is high, and the cheap path should always have a verifier and a fallback. The verifier's recall on cheap-path failures is the most important number in the routing design: a router with a weak verifier is a machine for converting savings into incidents.

Caches are per model, per prefix

Prompt caches belong to a model and a prefix. Switching models mid-run (step-level routing) starts a new cache for the new model; varying the system prompt by user or tenant fragments the cache into many cold prefixes; and tool lists that differ by route mean each route has its own prefix. These effects can quietly erase the savings that routing or personalisation were meant to bring. Keep the shared prefix identical across as much traffic as possible, put variable parts later, and measure cache-read share per route.

Latency budgets are per path, and tails matter more than means

Users experience the slow runs. A support agent with a 15-second median and a 90-second p95 feels slow to one customer in twenty. Model latency itself has a long tail (provider load, long reasoning), and agent runs sum many such steps, so tails compound. Practical controls: per-step timeouts with fast fallbacks, hedged requests for the slowest fraction of read-only calls (ag11), reasoning effort caps on interactive paths, and moving long work to asynchronous flows that notify the user when done.

Batch and asynchronous agents

Many agent workloads do not need an answer in seconds: overnight reconciliation, bulk document processing, weekly reports. Anthropic's Batch API halves token prices (checked 6 October 2026) in exchange for asynchronous completion, and its discounts stack with caching. Agent loops can use batch calls when each step can wait; the cost is wall-clock time per step, so batch suits wide, shallow workloads (many independent tasks of a few steps) better than deep, sequential ones. Separating interactive and background paths, each with its own models, effort settings and pricing tier, is one of the larger structural savings available.

Unit economics and pricing

The number that decides whether an agent product is viable is cost per successful task compared with what the task is worth or what a customer pays. A support resolution worth $5 of avoided staff time can afford a $0.50 agent; a $0.10 email triage cannot afford a 30-step agent. When the numbers are close, the design response is structural (workflows for common paths, routing, batch for background work), not prompt tweaking. And pricing decisions for customers (per seat, per task, per outcome) should reflect the cost distribution, especially its tail, or heavy users will be served at a loss.

Cost is a quality metric's shadow

Almost every cost lever can lower quality: shorter outputs omit reasoning, lower effort misses hard cases, cheaper models fail more, tighter caps cut off good runs. Treat cost and quality as one optimisation, with the eval's success and pass^k as constraints and cost per successful task as the objective, rather than as two teams' separate targets.

Self-hosting changes the unit, not the logic

With self-hosted models, the price per token is replaced by the price per GPU-hour, and the cost per token depends on utilisation (ae2's crossover analysis). The agent logic of this lesson still applies: steps, context growth, caching (prefix caching in the serving stack), routing between model sizes and output length. What changes is that idle capacity costs money, so agent traffic's burstiness, and the ability to fill quiet periods with batch work, become part of the cost model.

The case: RouteLLM and the economics of routing

RouteLLM (Ong, Almahairi, Wu, Chiang, Wu, Gonzalez, Kadous and Stoica, 2024) asked a question every agent team eventually faces: for each request, should it go to an expensive strong model or a cheap weak one? The authors trained routers on human preference data (comparisons of strong and weak model answers), with data augmentation, and evaluated them on standard benchmarks. Their routers reduced cost by more than a factor of two in some settings without degrading response quality, and kept working when the strong and weak models were swapped for other models at test time, which suggests the routers learned something about request difficulty rather than about one pair of models. The paper sits alongside FrugalGPT (ae2), which used cascades with a learned scorer to decide when to escalate. Both support the same practical conclusion as this lesson's worked policies: routing pays when there is a real price gap, a meaningful share of easy traffic, and a reliable way to catch the cheap model's failures; and its benefit must be measured as cost per successful task on your own traffic.

Failure modes and their signatures

Cache collapse: cost per task jumps while steps are flat; cache-read share falls (ag5). Tail blow-up: p99 cost rises after a change that lets runs go longer, or a tool that returns more. Effort creep: output tokens per step rise after a model or setting change, often from reasoning at maximum effort everywhere. Routing drift: fallback rate from the cheap path rises as traffic changes; savings shrink and errors grow. Hidden lines: hosted-tool, sandbox or escalation costs growing unseen because the dashboard shows only tokens.

What to monitor

Cost per successful task by class and route, with trend; spend by line (writes, reads, uncached input, output, thinking, tools, runtime, humans); cache-read share of input per route; steps, output and thinking tokens per task (median, p95, p99); router mix, fallback rate and failures by route; latency per task (median and p95) by path; daily spend against budget with anomaly alerts; and the top expensive runs with trace links.

A cost review in practice

Teams that keep agent costs under control usually run a short weekly review: the cost-per-success trend by class, the five most expensive runs with their traces, any change in cache-read share, and the effect of last week's releases on tokens per task. Each anomaly gets an owner and a cause. The most common findings, in rough order, are a broken cache prefix, a tool that started returning larger results, a prompt or model change that raised output or thinking per step, and a new task type with long runs. None of them is visible in a monthly invoice; all are visible in per-run traces.

Knobs

Caching (prefix layout, lifetime); model per path and per step; router thresholds and verifier strictness; reasoning effort per step type; output length guidance; tool result limits; step caps and no-progress thresholds; batch versus interactive routing; and fast modes on latency-critical paths. Every knob changes cost and quality together, so each change goes through the eval with cost per successful task as a gating metric alongside quality.

Estimate and decide: the document-review agent

A legal-tech company runs an agent that reviews contracts against a client's playbook: it reads the contract in sections, checks each clause with retrieval over the playbook, and writes a redline summary. Volume: 20,000 contracts a day. Measured averages: 22 steps per contract, a 9,000-token starting context (instructions, tools, playbook index), 3,500 tokens added per step (contract sections and retrieved clauses), 400 output tokens per step plus 1,500 thinking tokens per step at the current effort setting. It runs on a model priced at $4 input, $5 cache write, $0.20 cache read and $20 output per million tokens. Estimate the daily cost with caching (assume the shared 8,000-token prefix stays warm), find the biggest lever, and propose changes.

Answer

Tokens per contract. Input = 22 × 9,000 + 3,500 × (22 × 21 / 2) = 198,000 + 3,500 × 231 = 198,000 + 808,500 = 1,006,500 tokens. Writes = (9,000 − 8,000) + 3,500 × 21 = 1,000 + 73,500 = 74,500; reads = 932,000. Output = 22 × (400 + 1,500) = 41,800 tokens.

Cost per contract. Writes 74,500 × $5 = $0.373; reads 932,000 × $0.20 = $0.186; output 41,800 × $20 = $0.836. Total about $1.40 per contract, about $27,900 a day at 20,000 contracts. (Uncached input alone would be 1,006,500 × $4 = $4.03 per contract, so caching is clearly working.)

Biggest lever: output, and specifically thinking. Output is 60% of the cost, and thinking is 1,500 of every 1,900 output tokens per step, $0.66 per contract. Lower the effort for routine clause checks and keep high effort for flagged clauses and the final summary: if thinking drops to 300 tokens per step on 18 of the 22 steps, output falls by 18 × 1,200 = 21,600 tokens, saving $0.43 per contract, about $8,600 a day, subject to the eval showing no loss in clause-detection recall.

Second lever: per-step growth. 3,500 tokens per step accumulate for 22 steps. Give the agent section-level sub-tasks whose retrieved clauses are cleared after each section's verdict is recorded (ag5 clearing), or process sections with isolated sub-agents that return a short verdict (ag8), so the main context grows by a few hundred tokens per section instead of 3,500. That cuts writes and reads substantially.

Third: routing and batching. Many clauses are boilerplate; a cheaper model can check them with a verifier, escalating anything flagged. And contracts reviewed overnight can use batch pricing (half price) where turnaround allows.

Guardrails. Each change is evaluated on a labelled contract set for clause-level recall and precision, with cost per contract and pass^k on must-catch clauses as gates; the cost dashboard tracks thinking tokens per step so effort creep is caught.
