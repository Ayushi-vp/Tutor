A bank launches an assistant in its mobile app. In internal testing it answers in about three seconds and everyone is pleased. In production, the median user waits 1.4 seconds before anything appears and the slowest twentieth wait over 2.6 seconds; the full answer takes six to eight seconds. Session analytics show users abandoning the chat at a steep rate after about two seconds of blank screen. The model team insists the model is fast, and they are right: the model's own processing of the prompt is a few hundred milliseconds. The rest of the time is spread across a dozen small decisions nobody owns: a query-rewriting call before retrieval, an intent classifier that runs before anything else, a reranker, a queue at the provider during peak hours, a proxy that buffers the stream, and answers twice as long as anyone needs.

Latency in LLM products is different from latency in ordinary web services in three ways. A single model call takes seconds, not milliseconds, so it dominates everything else. It has two very different parts: the wait until the first token, and the generation of the rest, which grows with the length of the answer. And the experience of waiting can be changed without changing the computation, by streaming and by showing progress, which makes user-interface design part of latency engineering.

Interviewers for AI engineering roles probe this with questions like "our chat feels slow, what would you do?", "explain TTFT versus throughput", "how do you stream JSON?" or "what would you put in the SLO?". Strong answers decompose the time with numbers, know which levers move which part, and know the costs of each lever: parallel calls raise tail risk, hedging doubles some token bills, smaller models trade quality, speculation wastes work.

There is also a business reason to care beyond user patience. Latency and cost are coupled: long outputs cost money and time together, extra pre-processing calls cost both, and the fixes for one often fix the other. But some latency fixes cost money (hedging, provisioned capacity, speculative work that gets cancelled) and some cost fixes cost latency (batch processing, cascades that try a cheap model first). Knowing which is which lets you make the trade deliberately.

This lesson builds that toolkit. It defines the three latency numbers and how they combine, works a full budget for a retrieval-augmented chat request stage by stage and then optimises it with a simulation, and covers streaming over SSE as it really behaves through proxies and load balancers, partial structured output, parallel tool calls and speculative work, hedged requests with the classic Google result, routing to faster models, perceived-latency design, and how to set and enforce p50 and p95 SLOs.

Three numbers, not one

Picture ordering at a busy café. There is the time until the barista starts on your order (the queue), the time until you get the first thing in your hands, and the time until your whole order is on the counter. If your coffee comes first and the sandwich three minutes later, the wait feels fine; if everything arrives at once after four minutes, it feels long, even if the total is the same. LLM latency has the same structure. Time to first token (TTFT) is the wait until the first word appears. Tokens per second is how fast the rest arrives. End-to-end latency is when the whole answer is done. To a good approximation:

end-to-end ≈ TTFT + output tokens ÷ tokens per second

For a 350-token answer at 70 tokens per second, that second term alone is 5 seconds. For a 30-token classification, it is under half a second, and TTFT dominates. Which number matters depends on who is waiting: a person watching a streamed chat cares most about TTFT; a program waiting for a complete JSON object cares about end-to-end.

Where the time goes

Before the first token, a request crosses the network, passes the gateway (authentication, rate limiting), may run pre-processing calls (classification, rewriting, moderation), retrieves context (embedding, vector search, reranking), waits in the provider's queue, and then the model runs prefill over the whole prompt. After the first token, it decodes one token at a time, possibly pausing for tool calls, and the stream crosses the network back. Each stage can be measured, and each has its own levers.

Waiting versus watching

The last ingredient is psychological. People tolerate a wait much better when they can see progress. A response that starts appearing after 700 milliseconds and finishes after six seconds feels faster than one that appears complete after four. That is why streaming is the first thing every LLM chat product does, and why showing steps ("searching your documents…", "checking your order…") during tool calls matters. OpenAI's latency guide calls this "make your users wait less" and notes that streaming and chunking also genuinely shorten the whole user-plus-application loop, because the user finishes reading sooner.

How fast is fast enough?

Some reference points help when setting targets. People read roughly 250 words a minute, about 4 words or 5–6 tokens per second, so any stream faster than about 10 tokens per second keeps ahead of a reader, and typical hosted models stream at 50 or more. Interface research has long used about 0.1 second as the limit for feeling instantaneous, about 1 second for keeping a user's flow of thought, and about 10 seconds for keeping attention at all. In practice, chat products aim for the first token within about a second and show visible progress for anything longer. For calls that run inside other systems, the target comes from that system: a voice agent needs far less, a nightly batch job far more.

A reader who stops here should know: decompose latency into TTFT and decode; most of TTFT is outside the model; most of end-to-end time is output length; and streaming changes the wait the user feels, not the computation.

1. The anatomy of time to first token

Each stage before the first token has its own cause and its own fix.

Network and gateway. A round trip from a phone to a regional edge is tens of milliseconds; TLS setup on a cold connection adds one or two more round trips. Keep connections warm (HTTP keep-alive, connection pooling to the provider) and terminate close to users. The gateway (authentication, rate limiting, logging; hd13) should cost single-digit to low tens of milliseconds.

Pre-processing calls. Every LLM call before the main one adds its own TTFT and decode: a classifier, a query rewriter, a moderation check. These are the most common hidden cost, because each is "only" a few hundred milliseconds. The cures are to combine them into the main call, run them in parallel with other work, replace them with cheaper non-LLM methods, or make them speculative.

Retrieval. Embedding the query (tens of milliseconds), vector search (tens), reranking with a cross-encoder (often 100–300 ms for a few dozen candidates), plus any database or permission lookups. Rerank fewer candidates, use a smaller reranker, or skip reranking when the first-stage scores are decisive.

Provider queueing. At busy times a request can wait before a GPU picks it up. You see it as TTFT variance that correlates with time of day and does not correlate with prompt length. Levers are capacity-related: a priority or provisioned tier where the provider offers one, a different region, or another provider.

Prefill. Processing the prompt: roughly proportional to prompt length for typical lengths, and fast per token because it is parallel (gpu10). A 6,000-token prompt might take a couple of hundred milliseconds on a large hosted model. OpenAI's guide estimates that halving the prompt may improve total latency by only 1–5% in typical applications; it matters more with very long contexts, and prompt caching (ae9) removes most of it for a stable prefix.

2. Decode: why output length is the biggest lever

After the first token, the model produces tokens one at a time. Each step reads all the model's weights and the request's KV cache from memory (gpu10), so per-token time is roughly fixed for a given model and load: typically tens of milliseconds per token, which is 20 to 150 tokens per second depending on model size and provider. End-to-end time therefore grows linearly with output length. OpenAI's guide puts it bluntly: "cutting 50% of your output tokens may cut ~50% of your latency."

Ways to generate fewer tokens: ask for concise answers and give examples of the desired length; for structured output, use short field names and omit what is not needed (ae4); set a sensible maximum on output tokens; stop early at a stop sequence; and do not ask a model to restate its input. Reasoning models add a further term: hidden reasoning tokens are generated before the visible answer, often hundreds or thousands, which raises TTFT for the visible text. Use a lower reasoning effort or a non-reasoning model for latency-sensitive steps (rs7).

3. A latency budget, worked and simulated

Model a RAG chat request with each stage's median and 95th percentile, drawn from a skewed (lognormal) distribution, as real latencies are: network and gateway 55 ms median; an intent classifier 200 ms (p95 500 ms); a query-rewrite LLM call 350 ms (p95 900 ms); embedding 25 ms, vector search 30 ms and reranking 120 ms; provider queue plus prefill 450 ms with a long tail (p95 1,600 ms); and decoding 350 output tokens at 55–85 tokens per second. Three lessons. Restructuring (one fewer sequential call, one stage moved into parallel) cut median TTFT by 42%, more than any model change would. The p95 fell less, because the provider stage's tail remained; tails need tail-specific tools such as hedging. And end-to-end time barely moved: it is about five seconds of decoding, which only shorter answers or a faster model can cut. Note also the summed medians: 55 + 200 + 350 + 175 + 450 = 1,230 ms, less than the simulated median of 1,386 ms, because skewed distributions have means above their medians and sums of skewed variables shift; you cannot add stage percentiles to get request percentiles (ae1), which is why the simulation, or better the traces, is the source of truth.

4. Streaming over SSE, properly

Server-Sent Events (net11) are the standard way LLM APIs stream. The response has content type text/event-stream and stays open; the server writes events, each a few lines such as event: content_block_delta and data: {...}, separated by a blank line. Anthropic's stream, for example, begins with message_start, then for each content block a content_block_start, a series of content_block_delta events carrying text (or partial JSON for tool inputs), and a content_block_stop, then message_delta with final usage and message_stop, with ping events interspersed; an error such as overloaded_error can arrive in the middle of a stream.

Your application usually relays this from the provider through your backend to the browser or app, and every hop must cooperate:

  • No buffering. Reverse proxies, API gateways and content delivery networks (CDNs) often buffer responses or compress them in large chunks, which turns a smooth stream into bursts every few seconds. Disable buffering for the streaming route (in nginx, proxy_buffering off or the X-Accel-Buffering: no response header) and flush after each event.
  • Keep-alives. Load balancers close connections idle for a configured time (often 60 seconds). A long tool call can leave the stream silent; send SSE comment lines or ping events every 15–30 seconds.
  • Cancellation. When the user closes the tab or presses stop, the client disconnects; the backend must notice and cancel the upstream provider request, or you keep paying for tokens nobody reads.
  • Mid-stream errors. A stream can fail after showing half an answer. Decide in advance: show what arrived with an error and a retry button, or retry transparently and replace the text. Do not silently append a second answer.
  • Reconnection. SSE supports event ids and a Last-Event-ID header for resuming, but LLM generations cannot be resumed at the provider; resuming means your backend buffered the generation and can replay from an id, which is worth doing for long generations on flaky mobile networks.
  • Client rendering. Re-rendering a whole markdown document on every token gets slow for long answers; render incrementally and throttle to the display frame rate.

Measure on the client: TTFT as the user sees it and inter-token gaps. A server-side TTFT of 600 ms is worthless if a proxy releases the first bytes after two seconds.

5. Partial structured output

Structured outputs (ae4) seem to conflict with streaming: JSON is not valid until it is complete. In practice you stream it anyway and parse incrementally. The provider emits the JSON text (or, for tool calls, partial tool input such as Anthropic's input_json_delta events with a partial_json string) a few characters at a time. An incremental parser keeps the incomplete text and reports each field or array element as soon as it closes. The UI renders completed items (the first three search results, the summary field) while later ones generate. When the stream ends, validate the whole object against the schema and handle failures as you would without streaming.

Two design choices make this work better. Order the schema so the fields the user needs first are generated first, for example the short answer before the long explanation, a list of items before a closing summary. And expect bursts: Anthropic's documentation notes that current models emit tool input one complete key and value at a time, so there can be pauses between events while a long value is produced. Show a progress indicator for the field being generated rather than a frozen screen.

6. Parallel tool calls and speculative work

When an agent or workflow needs several independent pieces of information, fetch them at once. Many model APIs let the model emit several tool calls in one turn; the orchestrator runs them concurrently and returns all results together. Three calls of 400, 600 and 300 ms take about 600 ms instead of 1.3 seconds. The same applies in deterministic workflows: retrieval, a user-profile lookup and a classifier can all start the moment the request arrives.

The cost of parallelism is the tail. A step that waits for N calls waits for the slowest one. If each call is slow (say above 2 seconds) 1% of the time, the chance at least one of N is slow is 1 − 0.99^N: 4.9% for 5 calls, 18% for 20. This is the central observation of Dean and Barroso's "The Tail at Scale": in their example, if a request fans out to 100 servers each slow 1% of the time, 63% of requests are slow. Set per-call timeouts, return partial results when an optional call is late, and keep fan-out modest.

Speculative execution handles steps that are sequential in logic but predictable in outcome. OpenAI's guide gives the canonical example: start input moderation and the main generation simultaneously, and cancel the generation if moderation fails. Since moderation almost always passes, the user almost never waits for it. Other examples: start retrieval for the most likely intent while the classifier decides; prefetch the account summary when a user opens a billing chat; start generating with a fast model while a slower router decides. The rules: never show or act on speculative output until its guard passes, cancel promptly, and track the waste (tokens spent on cancelled work).

7. Hedged requests

A hedged request attacks tail latency directly. If a request has not answered within a delay, send a duplicate to a different backend and take whichever answers first. Dean and Barroso report a Google benchmark reading 1,000 keys from a BigTable table spread over 100 servers: hedging after 10 ms cut the 99.9th-percentile latency from 1,800 ms to 74 ms while sending only 2% more requests. Triggering the hedge only when a request has exceeded its class's 95th-percentile latency limits the extra load to about 5%.

Hedging works when slowness is a property of the backend at that moment (a busy replica, a garbage-collection pause, a noisy neighbour), not of the request. For LLM calls, that means hedging queueing delay, not long prompts or long outputs: a 100,000-token prompt is slow everywhere. Hedge on TTFT, since that is when you know the request is stuck before it has started generating: if no first token arrives within roughly the p90–p95 of TTFT, send the same request to another region, replica or provider, stream whichever starts first, and cancel the other immediately so you do not pay for two full generations. In the lab, a hedge at 1,200 ms fired on 10% of requests and trimmed TTFT p95 from about 1,970 to 1,810 ms; modest, because in that model the second request's latency comes from the same distribution. Gains are larger when one backend is having a bad moment and the other is not. Under overload, every backend is slow and hedges only add load, so cap the hedge rate and disable hedging when load signals are high.

8. Routing to faster models

Model size sets both prefill and decode speed. A small model might start in 300 ms and stream 150 tokens per second, a large one start in 800 ms and stream 50. For a 400-token answer: 0.3 + 400/150 ≈ 3.0 s versus 0.8 + 400/50 = 8.8 s. Routing (ag13, hd13) sends each request to the smallest model that handles it well: greetings, simple FAQs, extraction and classification go small; complex reasoning goes large. The router itself must be fast, a rule, a tiny classifier or embedding similarity, not another large-model call, or it eats the saving. Quality is protected by evaluating the small model on exactly the traffic it receives and by escalating when a cheap check fails (invalid JSON, low retrieval confidence), accepting a slower answer for that fraction. OpenAI's guide also suggests closing the quality gap with a more detailed prompt, more examples, or fine-tuning and distillation of the small model.

9. Perceived latency

Some techniques change how a wait feels more than its length, and they are legitimate engineering. Acknowledge immediately: show the user's message as sent and a typing indicator within 100 ms. Show real progress for multi-step work ("searching 3 documents", "checking order #1234"), which also builds trust. Use skeleton layouts that fill in as structured output streams. Put the answer first and the explanation after, so the useful part arrives early. For long jobs, switch from a synchronous wait to a background task with a notification. And avoid techniques that feel slow: spinners with no information for more than a couple of seconds, and buffered streams that arrive in bursts. Do not fake progress; users notice, and fake progress ruins trust in real progress.

10. Setting p50 and p95 SLOs

A service level objective is a target on a measured indicator over a window. For LLM features, define indicators per interaction type and measure them where the user is:

  • Streamed chat: TTFT p50 and p95 (for example 0.8 s and 2.0 s), and p95 inter-token gap (for example under 150 ms).
  • Non-streamed calls (extraction, an agent step, an API another service waits for): end-to-end p95, and for agents, time per task.
  • Availability and correctness alongside: an answer that arrives fast but is truncated or errored is not a success.

Choose targets from evidence: current performance, abandonment curves (where do users leave?), and competitors. Then define an error budget: with a p95 TTFT target of 2 s over 28 days, 5% of requests may exceed 2 s; alert when the burn rate means the budget will run out early (a fast burn over an hour, a slow burn over days), not on individual slow requests. Derive stage budgets from traces so each owner knows their share, and keep requests that are slow by design (very long documents, deep-research modes) under separate SLOs so they do not hide regressions in the common path.

11. Running a latency review

When a feature is slow, work through it in a fixed order, because the cheap, large wins are usually structural. First, measure from the client and get traces for a few hundred real requests, slicing by output length. Second, count the sequential model calls before the first token and ask of each one: can it be removed, folded into the main call, made parallel, made speculative, or replaced with a non-LLM method? Third, look at output length: is the answer longer than users need, and can the most useful part come first? Fourth, check the path for buffering and the client for rendering stalls. Fifth, look at the provider stage: is prompt caching working (ae9), does TTFT correlate with time of day (queueing), would a smaller model serve some routes? Only then consider more expensive tools such as hedging, provisioned capacity or self-hosting. For each change, predict the effect from the budget, then verify it on the trace distribution; if the prediction was wrong, the budget is wrong, and fixing it is as valuable as the change.

A useful habit is to write the budget down as a table in the feature's design document: each stage, its owner, its p50 and p95 today, and its target. It turns "the assistant is slow" into a list of numbers with names next to them, and it makes regressions visible when someone adds a stage.

Little's law and why latency rises with load

For a server, average concurrency = arrival rate × average time in system (Little's law). LLM servers batch requests, so adding requests raises throughput, but each decode step gets slower as the batch grows (more KV cache to read per step), and once memory is full new requests queue. The result is the classic hockey stick: latency is flat at low load, then rises steeply as utilisation approaches capacity. Providers manage this with rate limits and tiers; self-hosters must choose an operating point. Running engines below saturation, as OpenAI's guide notes, buys a modest speed increase at the price of utilisation. A latency SLO is in practice a cap on utilisation.

Prefill–decode interference

On a shared server, a long prefill for one request delays decode steps for every other request in the batch, which users see as stutter. Serving engines split long prefills into chunks interleaved with decode steps (chunked prefill), and some deployments separate prefill and decode onto different GPUs (disaggregated serving) so neither interferes with the other (lm13, gpu10). If you self-host and see inter-token gaps spike when long prompts arrive, this is the mechanism.

Tail latency compounds in agents

An agent that makes ten sequential model calls has an end-to-end time that is the sum of ten draws, and its tail is driven by the chance that any one of them is slow. If each call's p95 is 3 seconds against a median of 1 second, the probability that none of ten calls exceeds its p95 is 0.95¹⁰ ≈ 60%, so about 40% of tasks will contain at least one tail-length call. Cutting the number of sequential steps (planning several tool calls per turn, combining steps) is usually worth more than shaving each call. Budgets on steps and wall-clock time (ag11) bound the worst case.

Speculative decoding versus speculative execution

They share a word and an idea. Speculative decoding (lm13) is a serving-engine technique: a small draft model proposes several tokens and the large model verifies them in one pass, so output is identical but faster. Providers apply it internally; you see it only as higher tokens per second. Predicted outputs, offered by OpenAI for cases like code editing where most of the output is known in advance, let you supply the expected text so the model only spends time on the changes. Speculative execution is an application-level choice about which steps to start early. Interviewers sometimes test whether you can keep the two apart.

Measuring latency correctly

Use timestamps from one clock per measurement, record TTFT and end-to-end on both server and client, and keep the full distribution (histograms), not just averages. Report percentiles over a fixed window and slice by feature, model, region, prompt-length bucket and output-length bucket: a p95 change caused by a shift in output length is a product change, not a performance regression. Beware coordinated omission in load tests: a test client that waits for each response before sending the next under-reports latency during slowdowns, because it stops sending exactly when the system is slow; use open-loop load generators that send at a fixed rate.

Guardrails and latency

Output guardrails (sf9, lm18) that check the whole answer before showing it destroy streaming: the user waits for the full generation plus the check. Alternatives: check in chunks (sentence or paragraph) as they stream, accepting a small delay per chunk; run the check in parallel and retract on failure, which needs a UI for retraction and is only acceptable for low-severity risks; or apply strict whole-output checks only to high-risk routes. OpenAI's guide calls the chunked version "chunking": process output in pieces as it streams to your backend, then forward processed chunks.

Regional placement and data residency

Network latency is bounded by distance: light in fibre covers about 200 km per millisecond, so a transatlantic round trip is at least 60–70 ms before any processing. Serving users from a nearby region helps TTFT, but data-residency requirements (ae5, ae11) may force a particular region, and the nearest region may not offer the model you want or may be busier. Measure TTFT per region and route accordingly, within the residency constraints.

Timeouts and retries have latency budgets too

A retry after a timeout adds the whole timeout to the user's wait. If a model call has a 30-second timeout and fails at 29 seconds, the retry starts at second 29. Set timeouts from the latency distribution (somewhat above the p99 for the call's class, with separate limits for time to first token and for total generation), retry quickly on fast failures such as connection errors and rate limits with jitter, and let the overall request deadline, propagated through every hop, decide whether a retry is still worth starting. A retry that cannot finish before the user gives up only adds load.

The public case: The Tail at Scale (Dean and Barroso, Google, 2013)

Jeffrey Dean and Luiz André Barroso described why large interactive services at Google suffered from latency tails even when every component was fast on average, and what to do about it. Their central example: if a server answers in 10 ms typically but takes a second 1% of the time, a user request that fans out to 100 such servers in parallel is slow 63% of the time. Sources of variability included shared resources, background daemons, garbage collection, queueing and power management. Rather than trying to remove every source, they argued for "tail-tolerant" techniques, chief among them hedged requests: after a short delay, send a second copy of a request to another replica and use the first answer. In their BigTable benchmark, hedging after 10 ms cut the 99.9th-percentile latency for reading 1,000 values from 1,800 ms to 74 ms with just 2% more requests. They also described tied requests (both replicas know about each other and cancel the duplicate when one starts) and micro-partitioning. A decade later, the same arithmetic governs LLM applications that fan out to tools and retrievers, and hedging on time to first token is the LLM form of their technique, with the twist that duplicate generations cost real money until cancelled.

Failure modes

Buffered streams. A new CDN or gateway configuration buffers the stream; server-side TTFT is unchanged but users see the answer arrive in large chunks. Only client-side measurement catches it.

Pre-call creep. Teams add a classifier here, a rewrite there, each "only 300 ms", and TTFT doubles over a quarter. Track the number of sequential model calls per request as a metric.

Output length drift. A prompt change or model upgrade makes answers 40% longer; end-to-end latency and cost rise together. Monitor output tokens per request alongside latency.

Peak-hour queueing. TTFT p95 doubles every weekday afternoon in one region. Capacity, priority tiers, or routing to another region or provider are the fixes; prompt tweaks are not.

Orphaned generations. Users cancel but the backend keeps streaming from the provider; costs rise and capacity is wasted. Test cancellation explicitly.

Hedge storms. During a provider slowdown, hedges double traffic to an already overloaded system. Cap hedges and turn them off on load signals.

What to monitor

Client-side TTFT and end-to-end histograms per feature; server-side stage timings from traces; sequential calls per request; output tokens per request; inter-token gap p95; provider queue time where exposed; hedge rate and hedge win rate; cancellation rate and tokens generated after cancellation; and SLO burn rates. Review the slowest 1% of traces weekly: the tail is where users decide whether the product feels reliable.

Knobs, roughly in order of cost

Free or nearly free: removing or parallelising pre-calls, shorter outputs, fixing buffering, prompt-cache-friendly prompts, smaller rerank sets. Modest cost: routing easy requests to smaller models (needs evaluation), speculative moderation (a few wasted tokens), client-side rendering work. Real cost: hedging (extra requests), provisioned or priority capacity, multi-region or multi-provider redundancy, self-hosting for control over batching and placement. Teams that start at the bottom of this list usually pay for capacity they did not need.

import math, random
random.seed(1)
N = 50_000
def ln(p50, p95):                       # lognormal stage latency in ms
    return random.lognormvariate(math.log(p50), math.log(p95 / p50) / 1.645)
def pct(xs, q):
    xs = sorted(xs); return xs[int(q * (len(xs) - 1))]

def request(variant):
    net = ln(40, 90) + ln(15, 30)
    rewrite, intent = ln(350, 900), ln(200, 500)
    retrieval = ln(25, 60) + ln(30, 80) + ln(120, 300)
    first = ln(450, 1600)                       # provider queue + prefill
    decode = 350 / random.uniform(55, 85) * 1000
    if variant == "baseline":
        ttft = net + intent + rewrite + retrieval + first
    else:
        if variant == "hedged" and first > 1200:
            first = min(first, 1200 + ln(450, 1600))
        ttft = net + max(intent, retrieval) + first
    return ttft, ttft + decode

for v in ("baseline", "parallel", "hedged"):
    runs = [request(v) for _ in range(N)]
    t = [r[0] for r in runs]; e = [r[1] for r in runs]
    print(f"{v:9s} TTFT p50 {pct(t,.5):5.0f}  p95 {pct(t,.95):5.0f}   "
          f"E2E p50 {pct(e,.5):5.0f}  p95 {pct(e,.95):5.0f} ms")

What to look for. Restructuring should cut median TTFT by roughly 40% (about 1.4 s to 0.8 s); hedging should trim the TTFT p95 a little further, firing on about a tenth of requests. End-to-end barely moves, because decoding 350 tokens dominates. Now halve the output to 175 tokens and see end-to-end fall by about 2.5 s, far more than any other change. Then make the hedge fire earlier (800 ms) and estimate how many extra requests that costs. Finally, add four parallel tool calls with max(ln(300, 2000) for _ in range(4)) and watch the tail grow.

Diagnose and fix a slow assistant

Your in-app assistant's dashboard shows client-measured TTFT p50 1.9 s and p95 4.8 s, end-to-end p50 9 s. Traces show, per request: a moderation call (median 250 ms), a router LLM call that picks one of three prompts (median 600 ms), retrieval with reranking of 100 candidates (median 380 ms), and the main call (queue + prefill median 500 ms, p95 2.5 s at peak hours) producing a median 520 output tokens at 60 tokens per second. Server-side TTFT p50 is 1.6 s. Propose changes, in order, with expected effect.

Answer

First, the gap between client and server TTFT (1.9 s versus 1.6 s at the median): check for buffering in the edge or gateway and connection set-up on mobile; fix buffering and keep connections warm. Expected: most of the 300 ms.

Second, remove sequential pre-calls. Run moderation speculatively alongside everything else and cancel on failure (−250 ms). Replace the LLM router with an embedding-similarity or small classifier router (−550 ms or so), or fold routing into the main prompt. Rerank 30 candidates instead of 100, or use a smaller reranker (−200 ms). Run retrieval in parallel with routing. Expected TTFT p50 around 0.7–0.9 s.

Third, the tail: the main call's queue at peak (p95 2.5 s). Options: a priority or provisioned tier, a second region, or a hedge on TTFT at about the p90 with a cap and immediate cancellation. Prompt caching of the stable system prompt shortens prefill.

Fourth, end-to-end: 520 tokens at 60 per second is 8.7 s of decode. Ask for shorter answers with examples of the target length, put the answer first, and consider a faster model for the simpler routes. Cutting to 300 tokens saves about 3.7 s.

Finally, set SLOs (for example TTFT p50 0.8 s, p95 2 s, client-measured), add sequential-calls-per-request and output tokens per request to the dashboard, and verify each change with the trace distribution, not averages.
