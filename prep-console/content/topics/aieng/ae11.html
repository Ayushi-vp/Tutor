A fintech start-up builds its document-understanding product on the most capable model available, because it worked best in the prototype. A year later it processes two million documents a day, the model bill is the largest line in its budget after salaries, and a board member asks why they are not "just running Llama on our own GPUs, which must be cheaper". At the same time, the compliance team has learned that a large European customer requires all processing to stay in the EU, the provider has announced the retirement of the model version they tuned their prompts against, and a cheaper model released last month might be good enough for 80% of their documents. Four different questions, all versions of one: which model, from where, run by whom?

These decisions are expensive to get wrong in both directions. Teams that self-host prematurely spend months of engineering to run GPUs at 20% utilisation, paying more per token than an API would charge, for a model that is worse than what they had. Teams that never re-evaluate keep paying frontier prices for tasks a model a tenth of the price handles equally well. Teams that choose by leaderboard ship a model that tops public benchmarks and fails their own format requirements.

In senior AI-engineering interviews this shows up as "build or buy?", "how would you choose a model for this feature?", "when does self-hosting pay?" and "how would you avoid lock-in?". The interviewer wants three things: a selection process anchored in your own evaluation, a cost model you can work by hand with utilisation in it, and a list of non-price constraints you check before any of that.

This lesson covers the landscape of options and what each is good for, an evaluation-led selection process step by step, the self-hosting break-even worked by hand using GPU and API prices published on the day of writing, the hard constraints (latency, data residency, licences, security and support), lock-in and multi-provider abstraction, fine-tuning as a selection option, and when and how to re-evaluate. ae2 introduced the self-hosting crossover in a few paragraphs; here it becomes a full decision.

One more framing helps. A model decision is really three decisions with different lifetimes. Which model family and tier to use changes every few months as models improve. Whether to buy inference or run it changes every year or two as volume and prices move. And how to structure the system so the first two are cheap to change, with a gateway, an evaluation suite and portable prompts, is a decision you make once and benefit from every time the others change. Interviewers notice candidates who design for the third.

Four kinds of option

Think of choosing how to get electricity for a factory. You can buy from the grid at a price per kilowatt-hour (an API: pay per token, nothing to run). You can buy from a cheaper tariff for less critical loads (a small API model). You can lease a generator and run it yourself (self-hosting an open-weight model on rented GPUs): the fuel and lease cost the same whether the machines are busy or not, so it pays only if you keep it running near capacity. Or you can build a specialised generator tuned to your exact load (a fine-tuned model), which can be very efficient for that load and useless for anything else. The analogy captures the cost structures well. It breaks in one way: grid electricity is the same everywhere, but models are not interchangeable. Each option comes with a different quality on your task, which is why evaluation comes before price.

The decision in three filters

Good model decisions apply three filters in order. First, hard constraints: where may data go, what licences are acceptable, what latency must be met, what security review is required, who must support the system. These rule options out regardless of price. Second, quality on your task: run an evaluation and find which options are good enough. Third, cost and operations among the options that pass: per-token prices, GPU-hours and utilisation, engineering effort, and the cost of switching later.

Why "build" is usually not about the model

"Build versus buy" for LLMs rarely means training a model from scratch; almost nobody outside the largest labs should. It means choosing between buying inference per token from a provider and running inference yourself on rented or owned hardware, possibly with a model you have fine-tuned. Running inference yourself means owning a serving stack (lm13), capacity planning (gpu12), on-call, upgrades, security patches and evaluation of every new model version. Those are real engineering costs that never appear in a per-token comparison and must be added to it.

A sketch of the answer people give in interviews

Asked "would you use an API or self-host?", a weak answer picks one on principle. A strong answer goes: "It depends on three things. Are there constraints, like data residency or a licence requirement, that rule options out? Which options pass our own evaluation at the quality bar? And among those, what does each cost per successful task at our volume and traffic shape, including the GPUs we would leave idle and the engineers we would need? For most teams starting out that points to an API behind a gateway, with self-hosting justified later by constraints or by sustained high volume at a quality tier where open models are good enough." The rest of this lesson builds the parts of that answer.

A reader who stops here should remember: constraints first, then your own evaluation, then cost with utilisation in it; and the answer is often a mix of models behind a router rather than one winner.

1. The landscape of options

Frontier API models are the most capable models, available only through their makers' APIs and the major clouds. They are the fastest way to high quality, require no infrastructure, and cost the most per token. On 9 October 2026, for example, Claude Sonnet 5.5 and gpt-6.1-sol were each listed at $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens, with higher-end models costing several times more.

Small API models are the cheap, fast tiers from the same providers. On the same day Claude Haiku 5.5 (for prompts up to 100,000 tokens) and gpt-6-luna were each listed at $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output, a twentieth of the frontier tier. For classification, extraction, routing, short answers and many retrieval-augmented tasks they are often good enough, and they are faster.

Open-weight models are published weights you can download and run: families such as Llama, Qwen, Mistral, Gemma, DeepSeek and OpenAI's gpt-oss. You can call them through inference providers (per-token pricing, no infrastructure) or self-host them (per-hour pricing, full control). Their licences vary (section 7).

Fine-tunes adapt a model to your task (lm11). Some providers fine-tune and host certain API models; open-weight models can be fine-tuned and served anywhere. A fine-tuned small model can match a frontier model on a narrow task at a fraction of the price and latency, at the cost of training, data and maintenance.

In practice, mature products use several at once: a router (hd13, ag13) sends easy or high-volume requests to a small model and hard ones to a frontier model, with an open-weight or fine-tuned model where data or cost requires it.

2. Evaluation-led selection, step by step

The core of model selection is an evaluation you own (lm14). A practical process:

  1. Define the task and the bar. With the product owner, write down what success looks like per request (correct answer, valid JSON, cites a source, appropriate tone) and the minimum acceptable rate, plus latency and cost targets.
  2. Build the evaluation set. Sample a few hundred cases from real traffic, or realistic synthetic cases if there is no traffic yet, stratified by type and difficulty, including known hard and edge cases. Write references or rubrics. Two hundred cases distinguish a 10-point quality difference reliably; distinguishing 2 points needs thousands.
  3. Shortlist candidates across tiers: at least one frontier API, one small API and, if constraints or volume suggest it, one open-weight model. Public benchmarks and colleagues' experience are fine for shortlisting.
  4. Tune a prompt per candidate. A prompt tuned on one model underperforms on others; comparing all models on one model's prompt biases the result.
  5. Measure quality (automated checks plus a calibrated judge or human review for open-ended outputs), format validity, latency p50 and p95 at realistic concurrency, and cost per request.
  6. Compute cost per successful task: total cost, including retries and fallbacks, divided by successes. A model that is half the price but fails twice as often can cost more per success once retries and escalations are counted.
  7. Do error analysis. Read the failures. Two models with the same score can fail on different kinds of request, which suggests routing.
  8. Pilot. Run the leading candidate on a slice of live traffic with monitoring and a holdout, because offline evaluations miss distribution shift.
  9. Record the decision: the evaluation set version, the scores, the constraints checked, and the conditions under which you would revisit it.

A worked comparison makes step 6 concrete. Suppose for a support-classification task the frontier model succeeds on 96% of cases at $0.0045 per request, a small model on 90% at $0.0003, and failures are retried on the frontier model. The small-first cascade costs $0.0003 + 0.10 × $0.0045 = $0.00075 per request and succeeds on about 0.90 + 0.10 × 0.96 = 99.6% of requests (if failures are detectable), against $0.0045 and 96% for frontier-only. Detectability is the catch: if you cannot tell when the small model failed, you cannot escalate, and the cascade's real success rate is 90%.

How big must the evaluation be? A success rate measured on n cases has a standard error of about √(p(1 − p)/n). At p = 0.9 and n = 200 that is about 2.1 percentage points, so a 95% confidence interval is roughly ±4 points: enough to tell 80% from 90%, not 88% from 90%. Comparing two models on the same cases is more precise than comparing two independent samples, because you can look only at the cases where they disagree (a paired comparison); if they disagree on 30 of 200 cases and one model wins 22 of those 30, that is strong evidence, while 16 to 14 is noise. Grow the set where decisions are close, and keep a slice of genuinely hard cases so that improvements at the top are visible.

What to measure besides accuracy. Format validity (the share of outputs that parse and pass schema checks, ae4), refusal rate on legitimate requests, behaviour on adversarial or out-of-scope inputs, consistency across repeated runs (for sampled outputs), latency at your concurrency, and cost including retries. A model that is two points more accurate but refuses 3% of legitimate requests may be worse for the product.

3. The cost structures compared

An API bills tokens: cost per month = input tokens × input price + output tokens × output price, adjusted by caching, batch discounts and residency premiums (ae2, ae9). It is linear in volume with no fixed cost, and idle time is free.

Self-hosting bills hours: cost per month = number of GPUs × price per GPU-hour × 730 hours + fixed operations. The number of GPUs is set by peak load, not average, because you must serve the busiest hour within your latency target. It is a step function of volume with a large fixed cost, and idle time is paid for.

The bridge between them is the effective price per token of a self-hosted GPU:

$ per million output tokens = price per GPU-hour ÷ (output tokens per second per GPU × 3,600) × 10⁶ ÷ utilisation

Take an H100 at Lambda's on-demand price on 9 October 2026, $3.99 per GPU-hour, serving an 8B-class model at an assumed sustained 2,000 output tokens per second (including the prefill work for your prompts, at your latency target; measure it, since it varies several-fold with prompt length, output length and latency settings). Fully used: $3.99 ÷ 7.2 million × 10⁶ = $0.55 per million output tokens. At 50% average utilisation, $1.11; at 33%, typical for traffic with a 3× daily peak, $1.68; at 10%, $5.54. Compare with the small API tier's $0.50 per million output plus $0.10 per million input: for the same small-model quality, the API is cheaper unless the GPUs are almost always busy.

Why is the API so hard to beat at the small tier? Providers run very large fleets, so their traffic is pooled across thousands of customers in many time zones and their GPUs stay busy; they negotiate hardware prices you cannot; they run heavily optimised serving stacks with techniques such as speculative decoding and aggressive batching; and they amortise engineering across all customers. Your fleet serves one traffic curve, with its own peaks and quiet nights. Self-hosting closes the gap when your traffic is large and flat, when you can fill quiet hours with batch work, or when the comparison is against a much more expensive model that your open-weight model matches on your task.

4. The break-even worked by hand

Take a workload of 1,500 input and 300 output tokens per request, with traffic peaking at 3× the daily average, a target of running GPUs at no more than 70% of measured capacity at peak, and $12,500 a month of engineering and operations for a self-hosted stack (a fraction of an engineer, monitoring and storage; your number will differ). For an 8B model assume 2,000 output tokens per second per H100; for a 70B model assume 400.

At 2 million requests a day: API small tier = 2,000,000 × 30 × (1,500 × $0.10 + 300 × $0.50) ÷ 10⁶ = $18,000 a month. API frontier tier = 2,000,000 × 30 × (1,500 × $2 + 300 × $10) ÷ 10⁶ = $360,000. Self-hosting: average output = 2,000,000 × 300 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 6,944 tokens per second; peak ≈ 20,833; for the 8B model, 20,833 ÷ (2,000 × 0.7) ≈ 14.9, so 15 GPUs; 15 × $3.99 × 730 = $43,690 plus $12,500 = $56,190. For the 70B model, 20,833 ÷ (400 × 0.7) ≈ 74.4, so 75 GPUs, about $231,000. The lab repeats this across volumes:

5. Latency as a selection criterion

Latency differs by model size and provider and sometimes decides the choice (ae10). Measure time to first token and tokens per second at your prompt and output lengths and at realistic concurrency, at the times of day you care about, for each candidate. Smaller models are faster in both phases; self-hosting gives control over batching (you can run at low batch for low latency, at a cost in throughput, which shows up as lower tokens per second per GPU in the break-even); providers offer priority or provisioned capacity for steadier latency at a price. If you need very low latency near users, regional placement or on-device models (ae12) may be the only way.

A worked latency comparison shows how much it can matter. For a 250-token answer, a small model starting in 0.3 s and streaming 150 tokens per second finishes in about 0.3 + 1.7 = 2.0 s; a frontier model starting in 0.9 s at 60 tokens per second finishes in about 0.9 + 4.2 = 5.1 s; a self-hosted 70B model run at low batch for latency might start in 0.4 s and stream 45 tokens per second, finishing in about 6 s, and at high batch for cost it streams slower still. If the feature's p95 target is 3 seconds end to end, only the small model qualifies, whatever the price table says, unless the output can be shortened.

6. Data residency, retention and security

These are hard constraints and come first. Ask: in which country is inference performed and are prompts, outputs, logs and caches stored there; how long does the provider keep data, and are zero-data-retention terms available for your account; which sub-processors are involved; is the data used for training (for business API traffic, major providers state it is not by default, but confirm in the contract). Providers increasingly offer regional processing at a premium: on 9 October 2026 Anthropic's documentation listed a 1.1× multiplier on all token prices for US-only inference via the inference_geo parameter, and regional endpoints on its cloud platforms at a 10% premium over global ones. When no provider meets a requirement, self-hosting in your own region or environment, or a cloud provider's regional deployment, becomes the only option, and the cost comparison changes from "cheapest" to "cheapest compliant".

Security review covers the provider's certifications, how keys and access are managed, how tenant data is isolated (including caches, ae9), and incident notification terms. Enterprises often also need support commitments, uptime service levels and legal indemnity for outputs, which API providers offer in commercial terms and self-hosting does not.

7. Licences for open-weight models

"Open" covers a range. Some models ship under permissive open-source licences: OpenAI's gpt-oss models and Alibaba's Qwen3 models are released under Apache 2.0, which allows commercial use, modification and redistribution with attribution. Others use custom licences: the Llama 4 Community License allows commercial use but requires anyone whose products had more than 700 million monthly active users in the preceding month to request a separate licence from Meta, binds users to an acceptable-use policy and includes attribution requirements. Questions to answer for any candidate: commercial use, user or revenue thresholds, acceptable-use obligations you must enforce on your users, attribution and naming rules, restrictions on using outputs to train other models, and how licence terms apply to fine-tuned derivatives. Also ask about the data the model was trained on if your industry requires provenance. Most providers' API terms also restrict using outputs to build competing models, which matters if you plan to distil a frontier model into your own.

8. Lock-in and multi-provider abstraction

Switching models costs more than changing an endpoint. Prompts are tuned to a model's habits; a migration means re-tuning and re-evaluating. Embeddings are model-specific: switching embedding models means re-embedding the whole corpus, cheap in tokens (50 million chunks of 500 tokens is 25 billion tokens, about $500 at $0.02 per million) but slow and operationally risky. Fine-tunes hosted by a provider generally cannot be exported. Provider-specific features (hosted retrieval, built-in tools, stored conversation state, particular structured-output guarantees, caching semantics) become dependencies.

A multi-provider abstraction, usually an LLM gateway (hd13), reduces the mechanical part: one request shape, adapters per provider, routing, failover, quotas, unified logging and cost attribution. It cannot remove the semantic part. Keep per-model prompt variants under version control, an evaluation suite that can qualify any candidate in days, your own copies of conversation state and data, and an inventory of which features depend on provider-specific capabilities. Then failover between providers is a tested capability, not a hope: run a small slice of production traffic on the secondary continuously, because an untested fallback usually fails on the day it is needed.

9. Fine-tuning as a selection option

Fine-tuning belongs in the candidate list when the task is narrow and stable, good examples exist (or can be generated and checked), and volume makes a smaller model's lower price and latency worth the investment. Run three candidates side by side: the large model with a good prompt, the small model with a good prompt, and the small model fine-tuned. Price each per successful task including training runs, data preparation, hosting and the cost of re-training when the base model is retired or the task changes. Often the well-prompted small API model closes most of the gap, and the fine-tune is worth it only at large volume or for behaviour that is hard to prompt (a rigid output style, domain vocabulary).

10. When to re-evaluate

The landscape moves fast. Epoch AI's March 2025 analysis found that the price of reaching a fixed level of capability fell between 9× and 900× per year depending on the task (for GPT-4-level performance on PhD-level science questions, about 40× per year). Providers release new models every few months and retire old ones on published schedules. Re-evaluate on a schedule (quarterly is common) and on triggers: a new model in a relevant tier, a price change, a deprecation notice, a quality regression in monitoring (ae3), a large change in volume that moves you across a break-even, or a new constraint from a customer. If your evaluation and prompt variants are automated, a re-evaluation costs a day; if not, teams skip it and overpay.

11. A selection memo, end to end

To make the process concrete, here is the shape of a one-page decision record for a feature that extracts fields from invoices, 300,000 a day. Constraints: EU customers require EU processing; no licence restrictions beyond commercial use. Candidates: frontier API (EU region), small API (EU region), an Apache-2.0 open-weight 30B-class model via an EU inference provider. Evaluation: 400 invoices from twelve customers, field-level exact match, schema validity, p95 latency. Results (illustrative): frontier 97.5% fields correct, small 94.0%, open-weight 95.8%; all above 99% schema validity; p95 latencies 2.1 s, 0.9 s and 1.4 s. Cost per successful invoice, including a frontier fallback when validation fails: small-first cascade lowest, frontier-only highest. Decision: small API first with schema validation and frontier fallback on failure; re-evaluate when the open-weight provider adds EU capacity guarantees or at the next quarterly review. Reversibility: everything behind the gateway, prompts versioned per model. A record like this takes an afternoon once the evaluation exists, and it makes the next decision faster.

Throughput per GPU is the most uncertain input

The break-even is most sensitive to tokens per second per GPU, and that number varies several-fold with prompt length, output length, batch size, quantisation (lm12), engine and the latency target. A long-prompt workload spends much of each GPU's time on prefill; tight latency targets cap batch size and with it throughput (lm13, gpu10). The right number comes from a load test: your engine, your model and quantisation, a replay of your request length distribution, ramped until the p95 latency target is about to break; take throughput at that point and apply a safety margin. Never use a vendor's peak throughput figure measured at large batch with short prompts for a latency-sensitive product.

Utilisation strategies

Self-hosting economics improve when idle capacity is filled. Batch workloads (embedding a corpus, offline evaluation, document processing) can run in off-peak hours on the same fleet; autoscaling can shed GPUs at night, if start-up times (loading tens of gigabytes of weights) are acceptable; mixing several models or adapters on one fleet (multi-LoRA serving) pools traffic. Reserved or committed capacity lowers the hourly price but raises the cost of being wrong about volume. API providers' batch tiers, usually at about half price, are the equivalent lever on the buy side, and they move the break-even too.

Inference providers: the middle path

Hosted open-weight inference (per-token pricing for open models) combines open weights' portability and licence clarity with an API's lack of fixed cost. It is often the right first step when an open-weight model is needed for cost or control reasons: you can evaluate and launch without building a serving stack, and move to self-hosting later only if volume and utilisation justify it. Check the provider's quantisation and serving settings, because the same model served differently can score differently on your evaluation.

Cost per successful task, including escalation

With routing or cascades, the cost of a cheap model includes the cost of escalating its failures, and the quality includes whether failures are detected. A small model that fails silently is worse than one that fails loudly. Build cheap failure detectors (schema validation, confidence from retrieval scores, a small verifier) into the comparison, and evaluate the whole route, not individual models.

The hidden costs of self-hosting

Beyond GPU-hours: engineers who understand serving engines, GPU drivers, networking and capacity; on-call; security patching of images; evaluation of every new model and engine version; storage and transfer of weights; spare capacity for failures and deploys; and the opportunity cost of a team running infrastructure instead of building product. Spread over a small fleet, these can exceed the GPU bill. Large organisations amortise them across many workloads, which is one reason self-hosting is more common at scale.

Decision records and reversibility

Model decisions should be written as decision records with their assumptions: the evaluation set version, scores, prices on the date, throughput measurements, constraints. When an assumption changes (a price cut, a new model, a new customer requirement), the record shows which decision to revisit. Prefer decisions that are cheap to reverse: an API with a gateway and portable prompts is easy to leave; a fleet of reserved GPUs and a provider-hosted fine-tune are not.

Model versions and behaviour pinning

APIs usually offer both moving aliases (always the latest version of a model) and pinned versions (a dated snapshot). Aliases give improvements automatically but can change behaviour under you; pinned versions are stable until they are retired. For production features, pin versions, run the evaluation when a new version appears, and move deliberately. Self-hosting pins by default, which is one of its real advantages for regulated workloads that must re-validate every change, but it means you also inherit the job of upgrading when better models appear.

Quantisation changes the comparison

Self-hosted economics depend heavily on precision. Serving a 70B model in 8-bit or 4-bit (lm12) roughly halves or quarters the memory per GPU and raises throughput, which can cut the GPU count substantially, but quantisation can cost accuracy on some tasks. If you plan to quantise, evaluate the quantised model, not the full-precision one, and use the quantised throughput in the break-even. Inference providers sometimes serve quantised versions by default, which is another reason to evaluate through the exact endpoint you will use.

Capacity risk on the buy side

APIs have rate limits and occasional capacity shortages, especially for new models. High-volume products negotiate quotas, use provisioned or reserved throughput where offered, and keep a qualified second model or provider. The cost of that redundancy belongs in the comparison, just as spare GPUs do on the build side.

The public case: how fast prices move (Epoch AI, March 2025)

Epoch AI, an independent research group that tracks AI trends, measured how the price of reaching a fixed level of performance changed over about three years of model releases. For each of several benchmarks (including MMLU, GPQA Diamond, MATH-500, HumanEval and Chatbot Arena ratings) they found the cheapest model reaching a given score at each date and tracked its price. The decline ranged from about 9× to 900× per year depending on the milestone; reaching GPT-4's level on PhD-level science questions got about 40× cheaper per year. The practical consequence for model selection is direct: a decision that was right a year ago, at last year's prices, is very likely wrong now, and a self-hosting investment justified against last year's API prices may no longer pay. The workloads in this lesson's break-even table are a snapshot of one day; the method, rerun quarterly, is what lasts.

Failure modes

Leaderboard selection. The chosen model tops public benchmarks but fails the product's format or domain requirements; caught late because there was no task evaluation.

Premature self-hosting. A team builds a serving stack for a few hundred thousand requests a day, runs GPUs at 15% utilisation, and pays more per token than the API, plus an on-call rotation.

Stale decisions. The product keeps paying frontier prices for tasks a newer small model handles equally well, because re-evaluation is manual and nobody owns it.

Forced migrations. A deprecation notice arrives and there is no evaluation suite or per-model prompt variant; the migration takes a quarter and ships behaviour changes users notice.

Failover that fails. The secondary provider was configured but never tested with production prompts; on the day of an outage it produces malformed outputs.

Licence surprises. A product built on an open-weight model discovers late that the licence's terms conflict with a customer contract or a use case.

What to keep running

An automated evaluation per feature that runs any candidate model with its prompt variant; a cost-per-successful-task dashboard per route; a register of models in use with their licences, deprecation dates, regions and data terms; throughput and utilisation for any self-hosted fleet; and a quarterly review that re-reads the decision records against current prices and models.

Running a migration safely

Whether triggered by a deprecation, a price change or a better model, migrations follow the same steps: evaluate candidates offline with tuned prompts; compare behaviour beyond the score (length, tone, refusals, formatting) with product owners; shadow the new model on live traffic, logging both outputs without showing the new one; canary it to a small percentage with monitoring on quality, latency, cost and user feedback; ramp; and keep the old model available for quick rollback until the deprecation date. Budget for prompt re-tuning, re-embedding if the embedding model changes, and updating any downstream parsers that relied on the old model's habits. Teams with a gateway and an evaluation suite routinely do this in one to two weeks; teams without them take a quarter.

import math
HOURS = 730

def api_month(req_day, tin, tout, p_in, p_out):
    return req_day * 30 * (tin * p_in + tout * p_out) / 1e6

def selfhost_month(req_day, tout, gpu_hr, tok_s_per_gpu, peak=3.0, headroom=0.7,
                   min_gpus=2, ops_month=12_500):
    avg_tok_s = req_day * tout / 86_400
    gpus = max(min_gpus, math.ceil(avg_tok_s * peak / (tok_s_per_gpu * headroom)))
    return gpus, gpus * gpu_hr * HOURS + ops_month

TIN, TOUT = 1_500, 300
for req in (50_000, 200_000, 500_000, 2_000_000, 10_000_000):
    small = api_month(req, TIN, TOUT, 0.10, 0.50)
    front = api_month(req, TIN, TOUT, 2.00, 10.00)
    g8, c8 = selfhost_month(req, TOUT, 3.99, 2_000)
    g70, c70 = selfhost_month(req, TOUT, 3.99, 400, min_gpus=4)
    print(f"{req:>10,}/day  small ${small:>9,.0f}  frontier ${front:>10,.0f}  "
          f"8B ${c8:>9,.0f} ({g8} GPUs)  70B ${c70:>10,.0f} ({g70} GPUs)")

for util in (1.0, 0.5, 0.33, 0.1):

Advise on build versus buy

A healthcare-documentation company summarises clinical notes: 800,000 notes a day, about 2,500 input and 400 output tokens each, mostly during clinic hours (peak 4× average). It uses a frontier API at $2/$10 per million tokens with good results. A new hospital customer requires that patient data never leave the customer's country, where your provider has no regional processing. Leadership asks whether to self-host. Advise.

Answer

Constraints first. The new customer's residency requirement rules out the current API for that customer unless a cloud platform offers the model in-country; check that first, along with zero-retention and sub-processor terms, health-data regulations, and business-associate or equivalent contracts. If no API option is compliant, in-country self-hosting (or an in-country inference provider) is required for that customer regardless of price, and the question becomes whether to move everyone.

Quality. Build an evaluation set of a few hundred de-identified notes with clinician-reviewed reference summaries and a rubric (omissions, hallucinated findings, medication accuracy). Evaluate open-weight candidates with licences suitable for commercial healthcare use (for example Apache-2.0 models) at a couple of sizes, with tuned prompts, against the frontier model. Consider fine-tuning on de-identified data if a mid-size model is close.

Cost, current API. 800,000 × 30 × (2,500 × $2 + 400 × $10) ÷ 10⁶ = 800,000 × 30 × $0.009 = $216,000 a month.

Cost, self-hosted 70B-class. Average output 800,000 × 400 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 3,700 tokens per second; peak ≈ 14,800; at an assumed 400 tokens per second per H100 (long prompts make this optimistic; measure) and 70% headroom, about 53 GPUs, roughly 53 × $3.99 × 730 ≈ $154,000 plus operations, and an in-country fleet per residency region. Savings are modest relative to the risk and engineering, and depend on the quality result.

Recommendation. Keep the API for customers without residency constraints. For the new customer, use an in-country cloud or inference provider if one offers a qualifying model; otherwise self-host a validated open-weight model in that country, sized for that customer's traffic, behind the same gateway and evaluation suite. Revisit moving everyone only if the open model matches quality and a fleet can be kept busy (for example by running batch summarisation off-peak).
