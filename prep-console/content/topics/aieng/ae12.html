A note-taking app wants to add "summarise this meeting" and "rewrite in a friendlier tone". Its users are doctors, lawyers and therapists, and the first question from their customers' security teams is: where do the notes go? Sending session notes from a therapist's practice to a third-party model provider means data-processing agreements, risk assessments and, for some customers, a flat no. The app also needs to work in places with poor connectivity, such as hospital basements and flights, and the founders would rather not pay per token for a feature used thousands of times a day by every user. All three pressures point the same way: run the model on the user's device.

That is now realistic. Apple ships a roughly 3-billion-parameter model inside its operating systems and lets apps call it; Google ships Gemini Nano on Android devices; laptops run 8-billion-parameter open models at reading speed; and open-source runtimes make it a few lines of code to load a quantised model in an app. But "on device" changes almost every engineering assumption from cloud deployment. Memory is a few gigabytes shared with everything else on the phone. Speed is set by memory bandwidth, not by the chip's headline AI number. The model must be downloaded, stored, updated and evaluated on thousands of device types you do not control, under heat and battery limits, with far less visibility into how it behaves in the field.

Interviewers for AI-engineering roles increasingly ask about this: "could this feature run on device?", "how fast would a 3B model be on a phone?", "how would you split work between the device and the cloud?". Good answers do the memory and bandwidth arithmetic quickly, know which runtime fits which platform, and treat hybrid routing and model updates as first-class design problems.

This lesson covers why teams go on device and what they give up, what model sizes fit where, how on-device quantisation formats work down to bits per weight, the runtimes in use as of October 2026, why memory bandwidth caps tokens per second (worked for phones and laptops), how hybrid on-device and cloud designs route requests, and how to ship, update and evaluate models on devices. Apple's 2025 technical report on its on-device model is the public case.

A note on scope: "edge" also covers servers in factories, shops, vehicles and cell towers, which have more power and memory than phones but share the constraints of limited hardware, no data-centre operations team and intermittent connectivity. Most of this lesson applies to them too, with laptop-class numbers.

A pocket dictionary and a library

Think of a pocket dictionary and a city library. The pocket dictionary is always with you, opens instantly, works with no signal, and nobody sees what you look up. But it is small: it covers common words well and has nothing on rare ones. The library has everything, but you have to travel there, it may be closed, and the librarian sees your requests. A sensible person uses the pocket dictionary for everyday words and goes to the library when the dictionary fails. An on-device model is the pocket dictionary; a cloud model is the library; a hybrid design is the sensible person. The analogy breaks in one useful way: you can make the pocket dictionary much better for your particular needs by training it (fine-tuning and adapters) on exactly the words you look up most, which is how on-device models punch above their size on focused tasks.

The three numbers that decide feasibility

Three quick calculations tell you whether a model can run on a device and how fast. Memory: model bytes = parameters × bits per weight ÷ 8, plus the KV cache and runtime overhead, and it must fit in what the operating system lets an app use. Decode speed: each generated token reads every weight once, so tokens per second is at most memory bandwidth ÷ model bytes. Time to first token: reading the prompt costs about 2 × parameters operations per prompt token, divided by the device's achievable operations per second. A 3B model at 4.5 bits is 1.7 GB; on a phone with about 77 GB/s it cannot exceed roughly 45 tokens per second.

What on-device models are good at

A small model's knowledge and reasoning are limited, but many valuable features need neither: summarising a notification thread, rewriting a sentence, extracting a date and place from a message, classifying an email, suggesting a short reply, describing an image for accessibility, or turning speech into a calendar entry. These are focused, frequent, latency-sensitive and often private, exactly the profile where on-device wins. Open-ended questions about the world, long documents and multi-step reasoning still belong to larger models.

Who owns the model

There are two quite different ways to get a model onto a device. A platform model is built into the operating system (Apple's on-device model, Gemini Nano on supported Android phones): the OS downloads, updates and shares one copy across apps, and you call it through a system API, with no download cost to your app but no control over the model's version or availability on every device. A bundled model is one you choose, convert, quantise and ship yourself: full control over the model and its behaviour, at the cost of download size, memory and maintenance. Many apps use the platform model where it exists and fall back to a bundled model or the cloud elsewhere.

A reader who stops here should remember: on device is a trade of model quality for privacy, latency, offline use and cost; memory capacity decides what fits and memory bandwidth decides how fast it runs; and most real products combine a small local model with a cloud model.

1. Why go on device, and what you give up

Four reasons recur. Privacy: data that never leaves the device needs no data-processing agreement, cannot be breached on a server, and reassures users and regulators (ae5). Latency: no network round trip, no server queue; a local model can start answering in tens to hundreds of milliseconds, which matters for keyboard suggestions and voice. Offline: features keep working on planes, in basements, in the field. Cost: inference runs on hardware the user already owns, so a feature used thousands of times per user per day has no per-token bill.

The costs are just as concrete. Quality is capped by what fits in a few gigabytes. Battery and heat limit sustained use. Download size competes with users' storage and data plans. You support a long tail of devices, operating-system versions and accelerators. Updating a model means shipping gigabytes or designing for small adapter updates. And visibility into failures is limited, because the whole point is that data stays on the device.

2. What fits where

Memory available to a model is far less than the device's total. A phone with 8–12 GB of RAM runs the operating system, other apps and your app; the OS terminates apps that use too much, and platform models are managed by the OS precisely to share one copy. In practice, phones run models of roughly 1–4 billion parameters at 2–4 bits per weight: 0.3 to 2 GB. Laptops with 16–32 GB of unified memory comfortably run 7–14B models at 4 bits (4–8 GB), and high-end laptops with 64–128 GB can run much larger ones. Browsers can run small models through WebGPU, within tighter memory limits.

The KV cache adds to weights (lm5). For a 3B model with 28 layers and 8 KV heads of dimension 128, each token of context costs 2 × 28 × 8 × 128 × 2 bytes = 112 KiB in 16-bit; 4,096 tokens is 448 MiB, a quarter of the weights' size at 4 bits. That is why on-device models use short contexts, grouped-query attention, an 8-bit KV cache (Apple's choice) or cache sharing between layers.

3. Quantisation formats used on device, by hand

On device, quantisation (lm12) is not optional: 16-bit weights for a 3B model are 6 GB. The common formats are block formats: split each weight matrix into small blocks, store each block's weights as small integers and a per-block scale so the integers cover that block's real range.

Work llama.cpp's Q4_0 by hand. A block holds 32 weights. Each weight is a 4-bit integer from −8 to 7, so 32 × 4 = 128 bits. The block also stores one 16-bit scale d. Total: 144 bits for 32 weights, 4.5 bits per weight. To recover a weight, multiply its integer by d. Example: a block whose largest absolute weight is 0.42 gets d = 0.42 ÷ 7 = 0.06; a weight of 0.13 becomes round(0.13 ÷ 0.06) = 2, dequantised to 0.12, an error of 0.01. Smaller blocks mean scales fit the data more tightly but cost more bits for scales: with blocks of 16, the same 16-bit scale adds 1 bit per weight instead of 0.5.

k-quants refine this. Blocks are grouped into super-blocks of 256 weights; the per-block scales are themselves quantised (to 6 or 8 bits) relative to a super-block scale, saving bits; and a "mix" such as Q4_K_M keeps the most sensitive tensors (for example parts of attention and the feed-forward down-projection) at a higher type. llama.cpp's own measurements for Llama 3.1 8B list bits per weight of 4.67 for Q4_K_S, 4.89 for Q4_K_M, 5.70 for Q5_K_M, 6.56 for Q6_K and 8.50 for Q8_0, against 16.0 for F16; the Q4_K_M file is 4.58 GiB against 14.96 GiB. Lower still are 2- and 3-bit formats (IQ2, IQ3, Q2_K, Q3_K) that use importance information from sample data to decide where precision matters.

Other ecosystems use their own formats with the same ideas: MLX supports group-wise 4- and 8-bit quantisation with per-group scales and biases; ONNX Runtime and Core ML have int4 and int8 weight compression with block or channel scales; ExecuTorch and LiteRT apply similar schemes per backend. Two techniques push below 4 bits with less damage: importance-aware quantisation (protect the weights that matter most, measured on calibration data) and quantisation-aware training, which simulates the low-precision weights during training so the model adapts to them. Apple used QAT to bring its on-device model to 2 bits per weight.

4. The runtimes, as of October 2026

The landscape moves fast; check each project's current documentation. On 9 October 2026:

  • llama.cpp (ggml-org): C/C++ inference for GGUF models, with backends for Apple Metal, NVIDIA CUDA, Vulkan, SYCL and CPUs (ARM NEON, x86 SIMD). It treats Apple silicon as "a first-class citizen". GGUF files bundle quantised weights, tokenizer and metadata, and the project ships tools to convert Hugging Face checkpoints and quantise them. Widely embedded in desktop apps and local servers.
  • MLX and mlx-lm (Apple machine learning research): an array framework for Apple silicon with Python, C++, C and Swift APIs, and a package for running and fine-tuning LLMs on it. Strong on Macs, where unified memory lets large models load once for CPU and GPU.
  • ONNX Runtime GenAI (Microsoft): implements the generation loop for ONNX models, with acceleration through CPU, CUDA, DirectML, OpenVINO, Qualcomm's QNN, TensorRT-RTX and WebGPU, on Windows, Linux, macOS and Android, with iOS listed as under development. It powers Foundry Local and Windows ML.
  • Core ML and Apple's Foundation Models framework: Core ML runs converted models across the CPU, GPU and Neural Engine; the Foundation Models framework, introduced in 2025, gives apps a Swift API to the built-in ~3B Apple Intelligence model, with guided (schema-constrained) generation, tool calling and support for app-trained LoRA adapters.
  • ExecuTorch (PyTorch): exports PyTorch models with torch.export, delegates supported graph regions to CPU, GPU, NPU or DSP backends, and runs on phones, wearables, laptops, browsers and microcontrollers. Its README states it powers on-device experiences in Instagram, WhatsApp, Facebook and Messenger and on Meta's Quest and Ray-Ban devices.
  • LiteRT-LM (Google AI Edge): described as Google's production-ready layer for running LLMs with LiteRT, the successor to TensorFlow Lite, across Android, iOS, web, desktop and IoT, with GPU and NPU acceleration; its examples run Gemma 4 models. On Android, Gemini Nano is offered to apps through system services rather than shipped by the app.

Choosing among them follows the platform: Apple-only apps start from the Foundation Models framework (no download, shared with the OS) and use Core ML or MLX for custom models; Android apps look at the system model and LiteRT-LM or ExecuTorch; Windows apps at ONNX Runtime and Windows ML; cross-platform desktop apps often use llama.cpp. The deciding questions are operator coverage for your model architecture, acceleration on your target hardware, binary size, and how models are packaged and updated.

What converting a model involves. Getting a bundled model onto a device is a pipeline, and each step can change behaviour. Start from a trained checkpoint, usually from a model hub. Fine-tune or train adapters for your task on a workstation. Export to the runtime's format: llama.cpp's conversion script produces a GGUF file, ExecuTorch captures the model with torch.export and lowers it to a backend-specific program, ONNX exporters produce an ONNX graph, and Core ML tools convert to a Core ML package. Quantise, ideally with calibration data from your task so importance-aware methods protect the right weights. Then verify: compare the converted model's outputs with the original on your evaluation set (small numeric differences are normal; changed answers are not), check that every operator runs on the intended accelerator rather than falling back to the CPU, and measure memory and speed on real devices. Keep the pipeline scripted and versioned, because you will run it again for every base-model update, quantisation tweak and runtime upgrade, and a manual conversion that cannot be reproduced is a common source of field bugs.

5. Why memory bandwidth caps tokens per second

During decode, the model processes one new token at a time. For each weight matrix it multiplies one input vector by the matrix: every weight is read from memory once and used in one multiply-add, about 2 operations per weight. A phone's NPU can do trillions of operations per second (its TOPS rating, tera-operations per second), but memory delivers tens of billions of bytes per second, so the arithmetic units wait for data (gpu2). Time per token ≈ bytes read per token ÷ memory bandwidth, where bytes read is about the model size plus the KV cache for the current context.

Device bandwidth comes from the memory bus: bytes per transfer × transfers per second. A phone with a 64-bit LPDDR5X bus (8 bytes wide) at 9.6 billion transfers per second has 8 × 9.6 = 76.8 GB/s peak; flagship phones in 2025–26 sit roughly in the 60–85 GB/s range. Apple lists 153 GB/s for the base M5 chip, 307 GB/s for M5 Pro and up to 614 GB/s for M5 Max. Achievable bandwidth is lower than peak, often 60–80% of it.

Check the model against a real measurement. llama.cpp's quantisation table for Llama 3.1 8B (hardware not stated) shows text generation at 29.2 tokens per second in F16 (14.96 GiB), 50.9 in Q8_0 (7.95 GiB) and 71.9 in Q4_K_M (4.58 GiB). Multiply size by speed: about 436, 405 and 329 GiB/s of weight traffic. The first two are close to each other, consistent with a bandwidth limit; the 4-bit case falls short because dequantising the k-quant blocks adds work, and at smaller sizes other costs start to show. Halving the bytes roughly doubled the speed until those overheads appeared.

Prefill behaves differently: the prompt's tokens are processed together, so each weight read is used for many tokens and the work becomes compute-bound. Prompt cost is about 2 × parameters operations per token: 6 × 10⁹ per token for a 3B model, so a 1,000-token prompt is 6 × 10¹² operations; at an effective 10 trillion operations per second on an NPU, about 0.6 seconds to first token. This is where NPU throughput matters, and why on-device features keep prompts short.

6. Hybrid on-device and cloud routing

Most products combine a local model with a cloud model. The design questions are which requests run where, and what may leave the device.

Routing signals, cheapest first: the task type (some features are local-only, some always cloud); input length against the local context limit; device state (low battery, thermal throttling, offline); a small classifier or the local model's own confidence; and a post-check on the local output (does it parse, does it contain the entities from the input, is it within length) that escalates on failure. Tune the router on an evaluation set that scores both quality and the fraction sent to the cloud, because every escalation costs money, latency and privacy.

The privacy rule is part of the design. Decide what may be sent (the whole input, a redacted version, or only derived features), show users when a request goes to the cloud, honour a setting to disable it, and choose cloud terms with no retention where possible. Apple's design is the reference example: requests that need a larger model go to its Private Cloud Compute servers, which the company describes as built so that user data is not retained or accessible; whatever you build, users will compare it with that.

7. Shipping and updating models on devices

A model is a large asset with its own lifecycle. Practical rules: do not bundle gigabytes into the app binary; download on first use or on Wi-Fi, with resumable transfers; verify a signature and checksum before loading; store where the OS can reclaim space if needed, and handle the model being missing; keep the previous version until the new one passes an on-device smoke test; and record the model, quantisation, adapter and runtime versions with every telemetry event.

Prefer small updates. A fixed base model plus task-specific low-rank adapters lets you improve a feature by shipping megabytes instead of gigabytes; Apple's Foundation Models framework provides a toolkit for training rank-32 adapters for its on-device model, and adapters must be retrained when the base model changes. Pin runtime versions that match your model format, because formats and operators evolve. Roll out by cohort with the ability to stop, and keep a remote switch that disables a local feature or routes it to the cloud if a model version misbehaves in the field.

8. Evaluating on device

Evaluation has three layers. Offline quality: run your evaluation set through the exact model file, quantisation and runtime you ship, ideally on the target hardware, because backends differ numerically and quantisation affects tasks unevenly. Performance under real conditions: cold load time, time to first token and tokens per second on each device class, sustained over minutes to expose thermal throttling, under memory pressure, in low-power mode, and energy per request. Field quality: with privacy constraints you cannot log prompts freely, so use opt-in feedback, on-device aggregate metrics (acceptance rate of suggestions, edit distance after rewriting, fallback rate to the cloud), and differential-privacy techniques where scale allows. A rising cloud-fallback rate or falling acceptance rate after an update is the field signal that a model regressed.

9. A feasibility check, worked end to end

Suppose a product wants on-device email triage: classify each incoming email into one of eight folders and draft a one-line summary, on phones from the last three years. Work it through in order.

Task fit. Classification and short summaries of a few hundred tokens are focused tasks that small models handle well, especially with an adapter trained on examples. Good fit.

Model and memory. A 1–2B model is plausible; take 1.5B at 4.5 bits per weight: 1.5 × 10⁹ × 4.5 ÷ 8 ≈ 0.84 GB. Context is short (an email of up to 1,500 tokens plus instructions, say 2,048 total); with 24 layers, 8 KV heads of 128 and an 8-bit cache, the cache is 2 × 24 × 8 × 128 × 1 × 2,048 ≈ 100 MB. With runtime overhead, about 1.2 GB, which fits on mid-range phones.

Speed. Prefill: 1,500 tokens × 2 × 1.5 × 10⁹ = 4.5 × 10¹² operations; on a phone NPU achieving 5–10 trillion operations per second, 0.5–1 second. Decode: about 30 tokens for the label and summary; at 77 GB/s × 70% ÷ 0.94 GB ≈ 57 tokens per second, about half a second. Roughly 1–1.5 seconds per email, fine for background processing.

Energy and scheduling. A user receiving 150 emails a day needs about 3 minutes of processing; batch it while the phone is charging or idle, and process urgent senders immediately.

Quality. Build an evaluation set of a few hundred consented or synthetic emails with labels; compare the quantised model with and without an adapter against a cloud model; accept on-device if the gap is small for this task.

Fallback. Older phones that cannot run the model get rule-based folders or opt-in cloud processing; very long emails are truncated to the first part, which carries most of the signal for triage. Each step took a minute of arithmetic, and together they turn "can we do this on device?" into a defensible yes with known limits.

10. Browsers and desktops

Two more targets deserve a note. In the browser, WebGPU gives web pages access to the GPU, and runtimes such as ONNX Runtime (through its WebGPU backend) and LiteRT-LM's web support run small models client-side with no installation. Constraints are tighter: the model must download into browser storage, memory limits per tab apply, and not every browser or device exposes WebGPU, so a server fallback is required. On desktops, the situation is the easiest: plenty of memory, good bandwidth on recent laptops, and mature tools (llama.cpp, MLX, ONNX Runtime with Windows ML) that let apps ship 7–14B models. Desktop apps also commonly run a local model server that several apps share, which avoids loading the same weights twice.

Why 2-bit models are possible at all

At 2 bits a weight can take only four values per block. Post-training quantisation to that level usually breaks a model, because rounding errors across billions of weights add up. Quantisation-aware training changes the problem: the forward pass uses the quantised weights while gradients update a full-precision copy (using a straight-through estimate for the rounding step), so the network learns weights that work well after rounding. Apple's report describes this for its on-device model, with learnable scaling and an exponential moving average of weights to stabilise training, and then adds low-rank adapters trained after compression to recover quality. The lesson for engineers: aggressive on-device compression is a training-time decision, best done by the model owner, not a conversion step you bolt on at the end.

KV-cache sharing and its trade-off

Apple split its on-device model into two blocks: the first 62.5% of layers compute keys and values normally; the remaining 37.5% have no key and value projections and attend using the cache produced by the first block. Memory for the cache falls by 37.5%, and since the second block produces no keys or values, prefill can skip its computation, cutting time to first token by 37.5%. The cost is capacity: later layers can no longer form their own keys and values, which the model must compensate for in training. It is one example of on-device architecture choices (grouped-query attention, shared caches, sliding windows, small vocabularies) that trade a little quality for large savings in the scarcest resources.

Speculative decoding on device

Decode is memory-bound, so the processor has spare arithmetic during each step. Speculative decoding (lm13) uses it: a tiny draft model (or extra prediction heads on the main model, sometimes called multi-token prediction) proposes several tokens, and the main model verifies them in one pass that reads its weights once. If several tokens are accepted, you get several tokens for roughly the bandwidth cost of one. On-device runtimes increasingly support it; LiteRT-LM's examples, for instance, enable speculative decoding for Gemma 4 with multi-token prediction. The gain depends on acceptance rate, which depends on task.

Energy, heat and the tokens-per-joule metric

On a phone, energy per token matters as much as speed. Moving data from main memory (DRAM) costs far more energy than arithmetic on it, so smaller (more quantised) models save energy as well as time. NPUs are usually more energy-efficient than GPUs or CPUs for supported operators, which is why runtimes try to delegate graph regions to them, and why an unsupported operator that forces CPU fallback can cost both speed and battery. Sustained work triggers thermal throttling within minutes; design features as short bursts, defer long jobs until the device is charging, or route them to the cloud.

Distillation is how small models get good

Capable small models are rarely trained from scratch in isolation; they are distilled from larger teachers (lm19), with careful data and post-training. For your own features, the same path applies: generate high-quality task data with a large model (within its terms of use), fine-tune a small model or adapter on it, quantise with awareness of the task, and evaluate against the large model on your evaluation set. A distilled 3B model can match a much larger general model on one narrow task, which is the sweet spot for on-device features.

Security of weights and on-device guardrails

Anything on the device can be extracted by a determined user: weights, prompts, adapters. If a fine-tuned model memorised sensitive training data, it can leak; if a system prompt contains secrets, they are not secret. Guardrails implemented on device can be bypassed by a user who controls the device, so rules that must hold (payments, account actions, content that must never be produced for legal reasons) need server-side enforcement. Prompt injection remains a risk when the local model reads untrusted content and can call tools (ag10, lm18); being on device does not reduce it.

Context length is a budget on device

Cloud models advertise contexts of hundreds of thousands of tokens; on device, every token of context costs KV-cache memory and prefill time, both scarce. A 3B model with a 16-bit cache spends 112 KiB per token, so a 32,000-token context would need 3.5 GiB for the cache alone, more than the weights. On-device features therefore keep contexts short, summarise or chunk long inputs, use 8-bit caches and architectures with fewer KV heads or shared caches, and route genuinely long-context work to the cloud. When an on-device feature behaves well in testing and fails on real user data, overly long inputs being silently truncated are a common cause.

The public case: Apple's 2025 on-device foundation model

Apple's technical report on the models behind Apple Intelligence, updated in August 2025, is the most detailed public account of a production on-device LLM. The on-device model has about 3 billion parameters and is shared by system features and, through the Foundation Models framework, by third-party apps. To fit and run it efficiently, Apple compressed its weights to 2 bits per weight with quantisation-aware training, quantised the embedding table to 4 bits and the KV cache to 8 bits, and trained low-rank adapters after compression to recover lost quality. Architecturally, the last 37.5% of layers reuse the keys and values of the earlier block, cutting KV-cache memory and time to first token by 37.5%. Requests beyond the device model's ability go to a larger server model on Apple's Private Cloud Compute. The framework gives developers guided generation (output constrained to a Swift type), constrained tool calling and a toolkit for training rank-32 adapters. Every theme of this lesson appears: memory and bandwidth driving architecture, aggressive quantisation done at training time, adapters as the unit of customisation, and a hybrid design with a privacy story for the cloud half.

Failure modes in the field

Memory kills. The OS terminates the app or evicts the model under memory pressure, and the next request pays a multi-second reload. Track reload counts and model residency per device class.

Backend fallback. One unsupported operator sends part of the graph to the CPU; speed and battery collapse on some devices only. Profile backend placement for every model and runtime version.

Thermal slowdown. Long sessions get slower and hotter; users notice battery drain. Test sustained workloads, not single requests.

Quantisation regressions. A new quantised build passes general checks but hurts a language or task; caught only if the evaluation set covers it.

Fragmented fleet. Old devices cannot run the new model; features must degrade gracefully (smaller model, cloud, or disabled) with clear messaging.

Platform model changes. When the operating system updates its built-in model, your prompts, adapters and parsers meet a new model without any release on your side. Adapters trained for the old base may stop loading or behave differently. Re-run your evaluation on OS betas, version adapters against base-model versions, and keep a fallback path for the weeks when the new model and your adapters are out of step.

Download failures. Large downloads stall on mobile networks or fill storage; resumable downloads, Wi-Fi-only defaults and clear storage accounting prevent support tickets.

What to measure

Per device class and model version: load time, time to first token, tokens per second over a sustained session, energy per request, memory footprint, crash and eviction rates, cloud-fallback rate, and opt-in quality signals such as suggestion acceptance. Gate rollouts on these, cohort by cohort.

An on-device launch checklist

Before launch: evaluation passes on every shipped model, quantisation and runtime combination; performance measured on the slowest supported device class, sustained for ten minutes; memory footprint below the platform's limits with headroom; signed, resumable model downloads with storage accounting shown to users; a kill switch and cloud or rule-based fallback; telemetry that records model and runtime versions without prompt content; a privacy notice that explains what runs locally and what can be sent to the cloud; and a plan for the next base-model update, including retraining adapters. After launch: watch fallback rates, acceptance or edit rates, crash and eviction rates, and battery complaints by device class, and be ready to pause the rollout.

Goal: estimate whether models fit on devices and how fast they can generate, from parameters, bits per weight, KV cache and memory bandwidth. About 10 minutes, standard library only.

  1. Save as ae12_lab.py and run with Python 3.12:
GB = 1e9
devices = {"phone 8GB, 77 GB/s": (8, 77), "laptop M5, 153 GB/s": (24, 153),
           "laptop M5 Max, 614 GB/s": (128, 614)}
models = {"1B": (1e9, 16, 8), "3B": (3e9, 28, 8), "8B": (8e9, 32, 8)}   # params, layers, KV heads
formats = {"F16": 16.0, "Q8_0": 8.5, "Q4_K_M": 4.89, "Q4_0": 4.5, "2-bit QAT": 2.0}
CTX, HEAD_DIM, KV_BYTES = 4096, 128, 1          # 4K context, 8-bit KV cache
APP_SHARE, EFFICIENCY = 0.35, 0.7               # share of RAM an app can use; achievable bandwidth

for dev, (ram, bw) in devices.items():
    print(f"\n{dev}")
    for name, (params, layers, kv_heads) in models.items():
        kv = 2 * layers * kv_heads * HEAD_DIM * KV_BYTES * CTX / GB
        for fmt, bpw in formats.items():
            weights = params * bpw / 8 / GB
            need = weights + kv + 0.3                    # + runtime overhead
            fits = need <= ram * APP_SHARE
            tok_s = bw * EFFICIENCY / (weights + kv)
            print(f"  {name:3s} {fmt:9s} weights {weights:5.2f} GB  need {need:5.2f} GB  "
                  f"{'fits' if fits else 'NO  '}  ~{tok_s:6.1f} tok/s")

What to look for. On the phone, only 1–3B models at 4 bits or below fit within the assumed app share of memory, and the 3B at 2 bits reaches several times the speed of the same model at 8.5 bits. On the laptops everything up to 8B fits, and speed scales with bandwidth. Change CTX to 32,768 and watch the KV cache become a large share of memory for the 8B model; set KV_BYTES = 2 to see why Apple quantised its cache to 8 bits. The speeds are ceilings with an assumed 70% efficiency; compare one against a real llama.cpp or MLX run on your own machine to calibrate.

Design: private meeting notes on device

The note-taking app from the introduction wants on-device "summarise" and "rewrite" for iOS and Android phones from the last four years, and for Mac and Windows laptops. Notes average 3,000 tokens, up to 20,000. Summaries are about 200 tokens. Design the model and routing choices, estimate speed, and plan updates and evaluation.

Answer

Models. On recent Apple devices, use the system on-device model through the Foundation Models framework (no download, shared with the OS), with a LoRA adapter trained for the app's summary style if evaluation shows it helps. On Android, use the system model where available and otherwise ship a 1–3B open model (Apache-2.0 licensed) at about 4 bits via LiteRT-LM or ExecuTorch. On laptops, a 7–8B model at Q4_K_M via llama.cpp, MLX or ONNX Runtime GenAI gives better quality.

Speed. A 3B model at about 4.5 bits is 1.7 GB; at 77 GB/s and 70% efficiency, about 30 tokens per second, so a 200-token summary takes about 7 seconds of decode. Prefill of 3,000 tokens is 3,000 × 6 × 10⁹ = 1.8 × 10¹³ operations, about 2 seconds at an effective 10 TOPS. Stream the summary and show progress. On an M5 laptop an 8B Q4_K_M model reaches roughly 20 tokens per second after efficiency, 10 seconds for 200 tokens, which is acceptable with streaming.

Routing. Notes beyond the local context limit (20,000 tokens) are summarised hierarchically on device (chunk, summarise each, then summarise the summaries), which keeps data local at the cost of time; offer cloud processing only as an explicit, per-note opt-in with no-retention terms. Old devices that cannot run the model get the opt-in cloud path or no feature.

Updates and evaluation. Ship adapters, not base models, where possible; sign and verify downloads; roll out by cohort with a kill switch. Evaluate quality on a set of synthetic and consented notes per platform, model and quantisation; measure sustained speed, energy and memory on representative old and new devices; in the field, track opt-in ratings, edit rate of summaries and fallback rates.
