A product team launches an AI writing assistant. In the pilot it cost a few hundred dollars a month and nobody looked closely. Three months after general release the monthly invoice from the model provider is larger than the salaries of the team that built the feature, and it is growing faster than the user base. Finance asks three questions: what does one user cost us, which part of the product is spending the money, and what will it cost next quarter? Nobody can answer, because nobody built the model that turns product decisions into dollars.

When the team digs in, the causes are mundane. The system prompt grew from 500 to 4,000 tokens over a dozen helpful edits, and it is sent with every request. Conversations are resent in full on every turn, so a long chat costs twenty times a short one. A new "research mode" runs an agent that makes up to thirty model calls per question. Prompt caching was turned on, but a timestamp at the top of the system prompt changes every second, so nothing is ever reused. And every request, including "thanks!", goes to the most expensive model.

LLM costs behave differently from most infrastructure costs. A web request costs a fraction of a cent of compute and nobody models it individually. An LLM request costs anything from a hundredth of a cent to several dollars, depending on decisions made in prompt templates and orchestration code, often by people who never see the bill. Prices differ by twenty times or more between models from the same provider, change every few months, and come with discounts (caching, batch) that only apply if the system is designed for them. Cost is therefore an engineering property of the design, like latency, and interviewers for AI-engineering roles expect you to estimate it on a whiteboard.

This lesson builds the cost model from the ground up. It starts with tokens and the price sheet, then works one request's cost by hand using prices published on the day this lesson was written, scales it to a monthly bill, and takes it through the levers in order: prompt caching (with its break-even hit rate and why hit rate depends on traffic), routing and cascades, shrinking context and output, batch processing, agent loops and reasoning tokens, and the self-hosting crossover. The public case is FrugalGPT, the Stanford study that measured how much a cascade of cheaper models can save at matched quality.

A taxi meter, not a subscription

Paying for an LLM API is like taking a taxi with a meter that charges by distance, with two different rates. Everything you send to the model (the instructions, the documents, the conversation so far, the question) is distance on the cheaper rate: input tokens. Everything the model writes back is distance on the expensive rate: output tokens. There is no flat fee and no subscription: every request runs its own meter, and the bill at the end of the month is the sum of millions of small meters.

The analogy holds in three useful ways. The cost of a trip depends on the route you choose, not just the destination: the same answer can be produced from a 2,000-token prompt or a 20,000-token one. Different cars have different rates: a small model might charge a twentieth of a large one per kilometre. And regular routes get discounts: if many trips start with the same long stretch of road, prompt caching charges much less for that stretch after the first time.

Where the analogy breaks: a taxi passenger can see the meter. In an LLM product the person who decides the route (the engineer writing the prompt template, the product manager asking for "more context") rarely sees the meter, and the passenger (the user) never does. Unless the system records token counts per request and turns them into dollars per feature and per user, the first person to see the meter is the finance team, a month late.

One request, one month

The whole lesson rests on one formula. For a single model call,

cost = input_tokens × input_price + output_tokens × output_price

with prices quoted per million tokens (written MTok). Take a support assistant that sends 6,100 input tokens (a 3,000-token system prompt with tool definitions, 2,000 tokens of retrieved documents, 1,000 tokens of conversation history and a 100-token question) and receives a 350-token answer, using a model priced at $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens (Claude Sonnet 5.5's list price on 2 October 2026). Input costs 6,100 × 2 / 1,000,000 = $0.0122. Output costs 350 × 10 / 1,000,000 = $0.0035. One request costs $0.0157, about a cent and a half. At one million requests a month the bill is $15,700.

The levers, in the order they usually matter

There are only a handful of ways to change that number, and they map onto the formula. Send the repeated part of the input more cheaply (prompt caching). Send fewer input tokens (shrink the context: fewer, better retrieved chunks; summarised history). Use a cheaper model where it is good enough (routing and cascades). Generate fewer output tokens (concise formats, a deliberate maximum). Make fewer calls per user action (bound agent loops and retries). Pay less per token for work that can wait (batch). And, at high steady volume, rent the hardware instead of the tokens (self-hosting). Each lever has a cost in quality, latency or engineering effort, and each is worked through below with numbers.

If you stop here: cost is tokens times price times calls; read the bill by segment to see which lever matters; and every lever must be checked against quality on an evaluation set, because the cheapest answer is the one that is wrong.

1. Tokens are the unit, and tokenizers differ

Everything is billed in tokens, so the first step of any estimate is counting them. A tokenizer (lm1) splits text into pieces; for English prose a rule of thumb is about four characters, or three-quarters of a word, per token. Code, non-English languages, numbers and unusual formatting take more tokens per character. For real estimates, do not use the rule of thumb: run representative prompts through the provider's token-counting endpoint or read the token counts the API returns in every response.

Tokenizers differ between model families, and that matters for cost comparisons. Anthropic's pricing page notes that its newer models use a tokenizer that "produces approximately 30% more tokens for the same text". So a model whose per-token price is 20% lower than another's can cost more for the same prompt if its tokenizer is less compact. The only fair comparison is cost per request (or better, per successful task) measured on your own prompts, never price per token read off two price sheets.

Prices change every few months, so a cost model keeps prices in configuration, not in code.

3. From one request to a monthly bill

The per-request formula from the primer gives $0.0157 for the support assistant. Scaling it needs three more numbers: calls per user action (1 for a simple chat turn, 3–30 for agents, plus retries and any LLM-judge calls on the output), actions per user per month, and users. At one million requests a month that is $15,700; at 20,000 monthly active users that is $0.785 per user per month, which you can now compare with what a user pays.

Two habits make the estimate trustworthy. Break the input into its segments (static prefix, retrieved context, history, user message), because each segment responds to a different lever and grows for a different reason. And estimate a distribution, not a mean: history grows with conversation length, so the 10% of conversations that run to forty turns may cost more than the other 90% together. A spreadsheet with p50 and p95 rows per segment is worth more than a single average.

The segment view also tells you which lever to pull first. This assistant spends $12,200 of $15,700 on input and $6,000 on one static prefix. Cutting output length by half would save $1,750; caching the prefix could save about $5,000. Engineers who start with "make the answers shorter" because output is the dear token are optimising the wrong segment.

4. Prompt caching: the mechanics

When many requests start with the same tokens, the provider can store the processed state of that prefix (lm13) and reuse it instead of recomputing it. That is prompt caching. It is billed in two parts. The first time a prefix is seen, its tokens are a cache write, charged at 1.25× the input price for Anthropic's default five-minute lifetime (2× for a one-hour lifetime). Later requests that find it are cache reads, charged at 0.1× on most Claude models. The time to live (TTL) is refreshed each time the entry is read, so a prefix in steady use stays cached indefinitely.

Three rules decide whether caching works at all. The prefix must be byte-for-byte identical: a timestamp, a request id, a reordered tool list or user-specific text placed before the static part means every request writes a new entry and nothing is ever read. The prefix must meet the model's minimum cacheable length: 512 tokens for Claude Sonnet 5.5 but 4,096 for Claude Haiku 4.5, and a shorter prefix is simply processed without caching, with no error (the documentation suggests padding the prefix up to the minimum when it is just short). And the fixed content must come first: system prompt, then tool definitions, then long stable documents, then the variable parts. Checking that caching works is easy: the API's usage fields report cache-write and cache-read token counts on every response, and if both are zero the prefix was not cached.

5. Caching worked: the break-even hit rate

Let h be the hit rate, the fraction of requests that find the prefix in the cache. For the 3,000-token prefix on Sonnet 5.5, each request pays for the prefix either as a read ($0.20/MTok) or as a write ($2.50/MTok):

prefix cost per request = 3,000 × [h × 0.20 + (1 − h) × 2.50] / 1,000,000
                        = 3,000 × (2.50 − 2.30h) / 1,000,000

Without caching the prefix costs 3,000 × 2.00 / 1,000,000 = $0.006. Caching wins when 2.50 − 2.30h < 2.00, that is when h > 0.5 / 2.3 ≈ 21.7%. Below that, you pay the write premium more often than you collect the read discount. At h = 0 caching makes the request more expensive: $0.0172 instead of $0.0157. At h = 95% the prefix costs 3,000 × (0.19 + 0.125) / 1,000,000 = $0.000945, and the request costs 0.000945 + 0.0062 + 0.0035 = $0.010645, a 32% saving: $10,645 a month instead of $15,700.

So what decides h? Traffic per distinct prefix. With a five-minute TTL refreshed on every use, a request hits if the same prefix was used in the previous five minutes. If requests for a prefix arrive independently at an average rate of λ per minute (a Poisson model), the chance that at least one arrived in the previous five minutes is 1 − e^(−5λ). One request a minute gives 1 − e^(−5) ≈ 99.3%. One every five minutes (0.2 per minute) gives 63%. One every twenty minutes (0.05 per minute) gives 22%, right at break-even. The support assistant gets about 23 requests a minute on average across the day, so a single shared prefix is essentially always warm. But if each enterprise customer has its own 3,000-token system prompt and small customers send a request every twenty minutes, caching their prefixes loses money, and the one-hour cache (2× write, so it pays after two reads instead of one) may be the better choice for them. Hit rate is a property of your traffic shape, not a setting.

6. Routing and cascades

Many requests do not need the most capable model. A router predicts, before calling any model, which one should handle a request. Its savings follow from simple arithmetic: if a share s of requests goes to a model whose cost per request is a fraction r of the large model's, total cost falls to (1 − s) + s × r of the original.

Work it with current prices. Route 70% of the support assistant's traffic to Claude Haiku 4.5. Here a detail from section 4 bites: the 3,000-token prefix is below Haiku 4.5's 4,096-token minimum, so on Haiku nothing is cached and a request costs 6,100 × 1 / 1M + 350 × 5 / 1M = $0.00785. The 30% that stays on Sonnet 5.5 with a 95% hit rate costs $0.010645. The mix is 0.7 × 0.00785 + 0.3 × 0.010645 = $0.00869 per request, about $8,690 a month, down from $10,645 with caching alone. Padding the prefix to 4,096 tokens so Haiku can cache it would bring Haiku's cost to about $0.0055 per request: 4,096 × (0.95 × 0.10 + 0.05 × 1.25) / 1M + 3,100 × 1 / 1M + 350 × 5 / 1M ≈ $0.0055. With a pair priced twenty times apart, such as gpt-6.1-sol and gpt-6-luna, the same 70% routing would cut the bill by about two-thirds. The ratio between models is the whole story.

A cascade works differently: call the cheap model first, check its answer (does it parse, does a verifier or the model's own confidence score pass?), and escalate to the expensive model only if the check fails. Expected cost per request is cost_small + p_escalate × cost_large. With the Haiku numbers and a 25% escalation rate: 0.00785 + 0.25 × 0.010645 ≈ $0.0105, barely cheaper than caching alone, because escalated requests pay for both calls and the ratio is only 2×. Cascades shine when the price ratio is large and the check is cheap and reliable; routers shine when escalation latency is unacceptable.

Both share the risk that makes them hard: a misroute is a silent quality failure. A hard question sent to the small model gets a plausible, wrong answer, and nothing errors. So routing is evaluated end to end, on labelled data, as a curve of quality against cost across thresholds, broken down by slice.

7. Shrinking context and capping output

The cheapest token is the one you do not send. Retrieval is the usual culprit: sending 20 chunks "to be safe" instead of the best 5 after reranking multiplies the retrieved segment by four, and (lm9) more irrelevant context often makes answers worse, not better. This is the rare lever that improves quality and cost together. History is the other: keep the last few turns verbatim and summarise or drop the rest (ae6 covers the token budget). For output, set max_tokens deliberately per feature as a safety cap, and ask for the format you need ("three bullet points, under 20 words each") rather than hoping for brevity. In the worked example, trimming the average answer from 350 to 250 tokens saves 100 × $10 / 1M = $0.001 per Sonnet request and $0.0005 per Haiku request, about $650 a month on the routed mix.

8. Batch: paying less for patience

Not every model call needs an answer in seconds. Nightly summaries, classifying a backlog of tickets, generating embeddings or evaluation runs can wait. Batch endpoints accept a file of requests and return results later at a discount: Anthropic's Message Batches API charges 50% of standard prices, says most batches finish within an hour, and guarantees results (or expiry) within 24 hours; OpenAI's batch prices are likewise half. Caching discounts stack on top. The engineering cost is a different control flow: submit, poll or receive a webhook, handle partial failures and expired requests, and make the downstream pipeline idempotent so a resubmitted batch does not double-write. A common mistake is running the nightly eval suite or bulk backfills through the interactive endpoint at full price and with interactive rate limits.

9. Agent loops and reasoning tokens multiply cost

Agents (lm16) change the shape of the cost curve. Each step resends the context so far plus the latest tool result. Suppose an agent starts with 4,000 tokens and each step adds 1,500 tokens of tool output and reasoning. Step k sends 4,000 + 1,500 × (k − 1) tokens, so ten steps send in total

Σ (4,000 + 1,500 k) for k = 0..9  =  10 × 4,000 + 1,500 × 45  =  107,500 input tokens

not 40,000. Input grows roughly with the square of the number of steps, which is why a feature whose typical run takes 3 steps can have a cost tail dominated by the few runs that take 25. At $2/MTok those ten steps cost $0.215 in input alone. Caching helps a great deal here, because each step's prefix is the previous step's whole context: only the 17,500 new tokens (4,000 + 9 × 1,500) are written at $2.50/MTok and the other 90,000 are read at $0.20/MTok, for $0.044 + $0.018 = $0.062, a 71% saving. The control that matters more is a hard limit on steps and tokens per run, enforced by the orchestrator (ae1).

Reasoning models add a second multiplier. They generate hidden thinking tokens before the visible answer, and those are billed as output, the expensive type; Anthropic's API reports how many of the billed output tokens were thinking in each response. A request whose visible answer is 300 tokens may bill 3,000 output tokens. Model them per task type from measurements, and set reasoning effort or thinking budgets per feature instead of leaving them at maximum.

10. Self-hosting and the crossover

Self-hosting replaces price per token with price per GPU-hour. A single NVIDIA H100 rented on demand from Lambda is listed at $4.29 an hour. What a token costs depends on how many tokens the GPU actually serves in that hour. Suppose your benchmark of a small model on vLLM sustains 3,000 tokens per second with good batching (an assumption you must measure for your model, prompt lengths and latency target). At 100% utilisation that is 10.8 million tokens an hour, so $4.29 / 10.8 = $0.40 per million tokens. At 50% utilisation it is $0.79; at 20%, $1.99. Volume does not appear in the formula; utilisation does.

Compare with the API: Haiku 4.5's blended price for the support workload is about $1.22 per million tokens ($0.00785 over 6,450 tokens), and gpt-6-luna's is about a tenth of that, $0.12. So the self-hosted small model wins against a mid-priced API only if it stays busy, and loses to the cheapest API models almost always. Then add what the GPU price leaves out: engineers to run the serving stack, on-call, capacity headroom for peaks (which lowers average utilisation), redundancy across zones, evaluation every time you upgrade the model. Self-hosting wins clearly when data cannot leave your environment, when you need a fine-tuned or customised model, or when a narrow, steady, high-volume task keeps GPUs saturated around the clock.

11. Hidden line items

A complete cost model includes the calls nobody thinks of as product traffic. Retries and validation repairs (ae4): a 2% retry rate is 2% more calls, concentrated on the longest prompts. Guardrail and judge calls: an input safety classifier and an LLM-judge scoring 5% of responses each add calls. Embeddings for retrieval and semantic caches. Evaluation: running a 2,000-item eval suite on every pull request, twice a day, at full price, is a real budget line and a prime candidate for batch. Development and testing traffic, which in some teams rivals production. Each is small; together they are often 10–30% of the bill.

The cost–quality frontier

Choosing a model or configuration on cost alone, or on quality alone, is a mistake. The senior approach is to evaluate every candidate (model, prompt, number of retrieved chunks, reasoning effort) on the same representative eval set and plot quality against cost per task. Some candidates are dominated: another option is both cheaper and better. The rest form a frontier, and the decision is a business choice of where on the frontier to sit, ideally the cheapest point that clears the quality bar on every important slice, not just on average. The frontier moves every time a provider changes prices or releases a model, which on current schedules is every few months, so the evaluation has to be cheap to re-run (batch, cached judge results) and part of the routine.

Cost per successful task, not per request

Per-request cost hides failure. If a cheap model resolves 70% of support conversations and a dear one resolves 85%, the unresolved ones escalate to human agents at several dollars each, or retry, or churn customers. The meaningful unit is cost per successful outcome: (model spend + retries + escalation cost) / successes. Worked: at $0.004 per conversation and 70% success with $4 per human escalation, the total cost per conversation including escalations is 0.004 + 0.30 × $4 ≈ $1.20; at $0.012 and 85% success it is 0.012 + 0.15 × $4 ≈ $0.61. The model that is three times more expensive per request is half the price per outcome. This is the single most persuasive argument in cost discussions, and it requires knowing your success rate, which means evaluation (ae3, ae7).

Choosing the cache lifetime

With two lifetimes on offer, the choice follows from the gaps between requests on a prefix. The five-minute cache writes at 1.25× and pays off after one read within five minutes. The one-hour cache writes at 2× and pays off after two reads within the hour. For a prefix used every minute, both stay warm (each read refreshes the TTL) and the five-minute write is cheaper. For a prefix used every 10–30 minutes (a small tenant's custom prompt, a long document a user returns to), the five-minute cache expires between uses and keeps paying the write premium, while the one-hour cache stays warm. The Poisson formula gives the hit rates: at one request every 15 minutes, five-minute caching hits 1 − e^(−1/3) ≈ 28% and one-hour caching 1 − e^(−4) ≈ 98%. Note also that the lifetime runs from the start of the request, so a response that streams for four minutes leaves only one minute for the follow-up to hit a five-minute cache.

Unit economics and pricing design

An LLM product's gross margin depends on the distribution of usage, not the average user. If plans are flat-rate, the top 1% of users can account for a large share of tokens: they paste long documents, keep conversations going for hours, or script the product. Model the cost per user per month as a distribution from real or pilot traffic, then design plans around it: included quotas, per-seat rate limits, overage pricing, or graceful degradation (a smaller model, shorter context) after a quota. Expensive features such as agents or long-document analysis are often better sold as metered add-ons. The engineering consequence is that cost must be attributable per user and per tenant in near real time, because quotas cannot be enforced on data that arrives with next month's invoice.

Distillation and fine-tuning as cost levers

When a high-volume task is narrow and stable (classify tickets into twelve categories, extract fields from invoices), a large model with a long prompt is an expensive way to do it. Distillation uses the large model to label a few thousand examples, checks them, and fine-tunes a small model (lm11) that then runs with a short prompt. Per-request costs can fall by an order of magnitude, from both a cheaper model and a shorter prompt. The costs that do not show up in the per-request number are the ones that decide it: building and checking the training set, retraining when categories or document formats change, hosting or paying for the fine-tuned model, and keeping a fallback to the large model for inputs outside the training distribution.

Forecasting and budget controls

Spend grows from two sources that need different responses: more usage (good, if margins hold) and more cost per unit of usage (a regression). Forecast them separately: requests per day from product growth, and cost per request from the design (context size, calls per action, model mix). A design review for any new LLM feature should state its expected cost per request and per user, the same way it states expected latency. In production, enforce budgets in the gateway (ae1): per-tenant daily token quotas, per-feature spend alerts, and a kill switch that degrades an expensive feature rather than letting a bug burn a month's budget over a weekend.

Latency and cost pull in different directions

Several cost levers cost latency: batch trades seconds for hours; cascades add a second call to escalated requests; a smaller model may be faster per token but need more retries. Several latency levers cost money: fast or priority tiers (Anthropic's fast mode doubles Opus 5.5's price), hedged requests that pay for duplicate calls, keeping self-hosted GPUs at low utilisation to keep queues short. Caching is the rare lever that improves both: cached prefixes are cheaper and prefill faster. When presenting a cost plan, state its latency effect, and vice versa.

Making cost observable

Every API response reports usage: input tokens, output tokens and, with caching, tokens written to and read from the cache. Record these on the model-call span of the trace (ae1) with the model id, feature, tenant, user, route and prompt version, and compute dollars with a versioned price table. From that one record you can build every view finance and engineering need: spend per feature per day, cost per request distribution, top users and tenants, cache hit rate per prefix, calls per user action. Teams that instead reconcile from the provider's monthly invoice can see the total but never the cause.

Characteristic incidents

The broken cache. Someone adds the current date and time to the top of the system prompt "so the model knows what day it is". Every request now has a unique prefix, cache reads drop to zero, every request pays the write premium, and the bill rises 50% overnight with no change in traffic. The signal is the cache-read token share on the dashboard; the fix is to put dynamic values after the static prefix (or round the date to the day and place it late).

The runaway loop. A prompt change makes an agent less sure when it is done, and the average steps per run go from 3 to 9. Because input grows with the square of steps, cost per run rises far more than threefold. The signal is the distribution of calls per action; the fix is hard step and token limits and a canary with cost metrics for prompt changes (ae7).

The heavy tenant. One customer integrates the API into a nightly job that summarises their entire document store through the interactive endpoint. They are 1% of seats and 40% of spend, and they hit the account's rate limits during the day. The fix is per-tenant quotas, a batch path for bulk work, and pricing that reflects usage.

The silent price change. A provider releases a new model version with a different tokenizer or price, and an alias upgrade moves you onto it. Cost per request changes without a deploy. Pin model versions (ae7) and alert on cost per request, not only total spend.

The case: FrugalGPT (Chen, Zaharia and Zou, 2023)

FrugalGPT, from Stanford, is the most cited measurement of how much model selection can save. The authors reviewed the APIs then on offer and found prices differing by two orders of magnitude between models. They grouped cost-saving strategies into three families: prompt adaptation (shorter prompts, for example fewer few-shot examples, since cost rises linearly with prompt size), LLM approximation (cheaper substitutes such as caches or fine-tuned small models), and LLM cascades. Their cascade sends a query to a list of APIs in order from cheap to expensive; a learned scoring function judges whether each answer is reliable, and the cascade stops at the first answer that passes, only reaching GPT-4 for queries the cheaper models cannot handle.

The results: across their datasets the cascade saved between 50% and 98% of the cost of the best single API (GPT-4) while matching its performance, and at the same cost it could improve accuracy by up to 4%. In their HEADLINES case study (classifying financial news headlines), the learned strategy queried GPT-J and J1-L first and avoided GPT-4 when their answers were judged reliable; with a budget one-fifth of GPT-4's cost it reduced cost by 80% while improving accuracy by 1.5%. The cascade could beat GPT-4 because on some queries GPT-4 was wrong and a cheaper model was right, and the scorer learned to accept the cheaper answer. RouteLLM (Ong et al., 2024) later showed the router variant, choosing one model per query from preference data, cutting cost by more than 2× without substantially compromising quality.

The lessons for production are in the method as much as the numbers. The savings came from tasks where many queries are easy, from a large price spread between models, and from a scorer trained and evaluated on labelled data from the same task. Change any of those (a 2× price spread, a task where most queries are hard, no labels) and the savings shrink, as the routing arithmetic in the body showed. FrugalGPT is the evidence that cascades can work, and also a reminder that you have to measure them on your own task before promising a number.

What to put on the cost dashboard

Spend per day by feature and model against forecast; cost per request at p50, p95 and p99; input, cache-write, cache-read and output tokens per request; cache hit rate per major prefix; calls per user action; top 20 users and tenants by spend; share of traffic per route; batch versus interactive share; and cost per successful task where success is measured. Alert on rates of change: a cache hit rate falling from 90% to 10% or p99 cost per request doubling is an incident even when the daily total is still within budget.

import math

# USD per million tokens, Anthropic pricing page, checked 2026-10-02
PRICES = {  # input, output, 5-minute cache write, cache read
    "sonnet-5.5": (2.00, 10.00, 2.50, 0.20),
    "haiku-4.5":  (1.00,  5.00, 1.25, 0.10),
}
MIN_CACHE = {"sonnet-5.5": 512, "haiku-4.5": 4096}   # minimum cacheable prefix, tokens

def cost_per_request(model, prefix, rest, out, hit):
    """prefix: static tokens (system + tools); rest: retrieved + history + message."""
    p_in, p_out, p_write, p_read = PRICES[model]
    if prefix < MIN_CACHE[model]:          # too short to cache: billed as normal input
        hit, p_write = 0.0, p_in
    cached = prefix * (hit * p_read + (1 - hit) * p_write)
    return (cached + rest * p_in + out * p_out) / 1e6

REQ_PER_MONTH = 1_000_000
PREFIX, REST, OUT = 3_000, 3_100, 350

base = (PREFIX + REST) * 2.00 / 1e6 + OUT * 10.00 / 1e6
print(f"no caching (sonnet): ${base:.5f}/request  ${base * REQ_PER_MONTH:,.0f}/month")
for hit in (0.0, 0.2, 0.5, 0.8, 0.95):
    c = cost_per_request("sonnet-5.5", PREFIX, REST, OUT, hit)
    print(f"  cache hit {hit:4.0%}: ${c:.5f}/request  ${c * REQ_PER_MONTH:,.0f}/month")
breakeven = (2.50 - 2.00) / (2.50 - 0.20)
print(f"break-even hit rate for 5-min caching: {breakeven:.1%}")

# hit rate depends on traffic per prefix: a hit needs a request in the last 5 minutes
for per_min in (0.05, 0.2, 1, 23):
    print(f"  {per_min:5} req/min on this prefix -> hit rate {1 - math.exp(-per_min * 5):.1%}")

# routing 70% of traffic to the small model (its 4,096-token cache minimum blocks caching)
small = cost_per_request("haiku-4.5", PREFIX, REST, OUT, 0.95)
big = cost_per_request("sonnet-5.5", PREFIX, REST, OUT, 0.95)
mix = 0.7 * small + 0.3 * big
print(f"\nhaiku ${small:.5f} | sonnet cached ${big:.5f} | 70/30 mix ${mix:.5f}"
      f"  -> ${mix * REQ_PER_MONTH:,.0f}/month")

# self-hosting: cost per million tokens depends on utilisation, not on volume
gpu_hour, tok_per_s = 4.29, 3_000     # 1x H100 on-demand (Lambda); throughput is an assumption
for util in (1.0, 0.5, 0.2):
    per_m = gpu_hour / (tok_per_s * 3600 * util / 1e6)
    print(f"self-host at {util:4.0%} utilisation: ${per_m:.2f} per million tokens")

What to look for. At a 0% hit rate caching costs more than not caching ($17,200 against $15,700), and the break-even is 21.7%, which is exactly the hit rate a prefix used once every twenty minutes achieves. The Haiku line is uncached because its 4,096-token minimum is above the 3,000-token prefix: change PREFIX to 4,096 and watch Haiku's cost per request fall to about $0.0055. Then try: the one-hour cache (write price 2× input, so 4.00 for Sonnet) at 0.05 requests per minute with a 60-minute window; an agent with ten steps by replacing REST with the growing context; and the OpenAI pair from the price table to see routing save two-thirds instead of a fifth.

Work it out

500,000 requests a day. Each averages 3,500 input tokens (2,000 of them a shared system prompt) and 400 output tokens. Use Claude Sonnet 5.5 ($2 input, $10 output, $0.20 cache read, $2.50 five-minute cache write per million tokens) and Claude Haiku 4.5 ($1, $5; minimum cacheable prefix 4,096 tokens). A classifier says 70% of traffic can go to Haiku. Compute the monthly bill (30 days) for: (a) Sonnet only; (b) with routing; (c) with routing plus prefix caching on Sonnet. (d) What would you check before promising the saving?

Answer

(a) Sonnet only. Input: 3,500 × 500,000 = 1.75 billion tokens a day × $2/M = $3,500. Output: 400 × 500,000 = 200 million × $10/M = $2,000. Total $5,500 a day, $165,000 a month.

(b) Routing. Sonnet, 150,000 requests: input 525M × $2 = $1,050; output 60M × $10 = $600; $1,650 a day. Haiku, 350,000 requests: input 1.225B × $1 = $1,225; output 140M × $5 = $700; $1,925 a day. Total $3,575 a day, $107,250 a month, a 35% cut. The saving is modest because these two models are only 2× apart in price; a pair 20× apart would save far more.

(c) Plus caching on Sonnet. 150,000 Sonnet requests a day is over 100 a minute on one prefix, so the hit rate is effectively 100% (only occasional writes). The 2,000-token prefix (above Sonnet 5.5's 512-token minimum) costs $0.20 instead of $2 per million: 300M prefix tokens a day save 300 × $1.80 = $540. Haiku's prefix is 2,000 tokens, below its 4,096 minimum, so nothing is cached there unless the prefix is padded. Total about $3,035 a day, $91,050 a month, 45% below (a).

(d) Before promising it: evaluate the router end to end on labelled traffic (misroutes are silent quality failures, and the saving is worthless if resolution rates fall); verify the cache in production from the usage fields (cache-read tokens should be roughly 2,000 per Sonnet request); measure real token counts per model, since tokenizers differ; include the classifier's own cost and latency; and report cost per successful task, not just cost per request. Then consider padding Haiku's prefix to 4,096 tokens: at a near-100% hit rate it would cut Haiku's input cost for that segment by about 90%.
