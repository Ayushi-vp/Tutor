A team launches a support assistant in March. Its evaluation scores are good, users are happy and the dashboards are green. By June, complaints have doubled, but nothing on the dashboards has changed: error rate near zero, latency steady, cost per request flat. When someone finally reads a few hundred conversations, the causes are a mixture. A new product line launched in May and the assistant knows nothing about it. A partner integration now sends questions in Spanish. The model provider updated the model behind the alias the team calls, and it now wraps answers in a different format that the front end renders badly. None of these is an outage. The service kept answering the whole time, just worse.

This is the characteristic failure of machine-learning systems: they degrade silently. Traditional software fails loudly when it breaks; a crash, a 500 error and a stack trace tell you where to look. A model fails quietly. It keeps returning answers with the same confidence while the world it was built for moves away from it. Customers change, products change, fraudsters adapt, upstream data pipelines change their formats, and for LLM applications the model itself can change under you. Researchers at Stanford and Berkeley showed in 2023 that the "same" GPT-4 behind the API answered a fixed set of maths and coding questions very differently in June than in March.

The discipline that catches this is monitoring, and it is harder for ML than for ordinary services because the most important quantity, whether the answers are right, is usually not observable when the answer is given. Labels arrive late or never. So monitoring has to work with what is observable now (the inputs, the outputs, user behaviour) and with sampled, deliberate checks of quality, and it has to tell real changes apart from the noise that every statistic carries.

This lesson covers that discipline from the ground up. It starts with why and how systems degrade and the layers to monitor. It names the kinds of drift precisely, then measures drift with PSI and KL divergence worked by hand on two histograms, and shows why the popular fixed thresholds are misleading by deriving the noise floor from sample size. It covers monitoring rates such as refusals and parse failures, estimating quality without labels using proxies and sampled judging, monitoring free text with embeddings and topic mixes, and the feedback loop that turns production failures into tests and fixes. The public case is the GPT-4 behaviour-drift study.

A ship's navigator, not a smoke alarm

Monitoring ordinary software is like a smoke alarm: it stays silent until something is clearly wrong, then it screams. Monitoring an ML system is more like navigation on a ship before satellite positioning. The ship does not hit anything for days. It drifts, a little each hour, pushed by currents and wind that nobody can see directly. The navigator keeps the ship on course by watching indirect signals (speed, heading, the angle of the sun, the depth of the water) and comparing them with what the chart says they should be. When the readings disagree with the plan by more than measurement error, the navigator investigates and corrects. Waiting until the coastline appears in the wrong place (the equivalent of waiting for labels) is too late.

Three ideas in the analogy carry over. First, you watch indirect signals because the thing you care about is not directly visible: inputs and outputs are visible now, correctness is not. Second, every reading has measurement error, so the question is never "did the number change?" but "did it change by more than noise?". Third, a discrepancy is a reason to investigate, not an automatic course change: a reading can be wrong because the instrument broke (a data-pipeline bug) rather than because the ship moved.

Where the analogy breaks: the sea does not react to the ship, but a model's environment often reacts to the model. A fraud model that blocks transactions removes the evidence of whether they were fraud; a recommender shapes what users click. The system you monitor can bend the data you monitor it with.

The layers to watch

Monitoring is organised in layers, from cheap and immediate to expensive and delayed. System health: errors, latency, throughput, cost (ae1, ae2). Inputs: are requests like the ones the system was built and tested for? Outputs: are predictions, or generated answers, distributed as before (lengths, refusal rates, format failures, predicted class rates)? Quality: when labels or reviews exist, is the system right as often as before? Outcomes: is the business metric the system exists to move (resolved tickets, prevented fraud, conversion) still moving? Problems usually show up first in inputs and outputs, are confirmed in quality, and matter because of outcomes.

What one week of monitoring looks like

Picture the support assistant from the story with monitoring in place. On Monday the system layer is quiet. On Tuesday the input layer shows that the share of messages over 1,000 tokens has doubled since the launch month; a quick look at samples shows customers pasting whole invoices, which explains why cost per request crept up. On Wednesday the topic mix shows a new cluster about a product launched on Monday, and its answers have more thumbs-down than any other cluster. On Thursday the weekly sample of 500 conversations, scored by a calibrated LLM judge, confirms it: groundedness is 96% overall but 71% for the new product's questions, because the documents for it were never indexed. On Friday the documents are indexed, twenty of the failed conversations become eval cases, the change passes the eval gate and rolls out. Nobody waited for complaints to double, and nothing in the system layer ever turned red.

If you stop here: models degrade silently because their world changes; watch inputs and outputs because they are visible now; compare every statistic with its noise before reacting; investigate the data pipeline before blaming the world; and estimate quality directly by reviewing samples, because nothing else tells you whether the answers are right.

1. Why systems degrade without breaking

A model is a frozen snapshot of the relationships in the data it was built or tested on. Four kinds of change erode it. The world changes: new products, new customer segments, seasonal patterns, economic shocks, adversaries who adapt. The data pipeline changes: an upstream team renames a field, a partner sends amounts in cents instead of dollars, a join starts dropping rows, a default value of 0 replaces missing values. The model changes: for LLM applications, a provider can update the model behind an alias, and pinned versions are eventually retired. You change: a prompt edit, a new retrieval index, a different chunk size, a new tool. The first two affect every ML system; the last two are especially common in LLM applications, where behaviour depends on a dozen configuration settings (ae7).

In each case the service keeps working in the systems sense: requests succeed, latency is normal. That is why classic service monitoring (sc13) is necessary but not sufficient. You need statistics about what is flowing through the system, not just whether it flows.

2. The vocabulary of drift

Precise names make diagnosis faster, because each kind of drift has a different response. Write X for the inputs (features, or the user's message) and Y for the outcome (the label, or the correct answer).

Covariate drift (also called data drift) is a change in P(X), the distribution of inputs, while P(Y|X), the relationship from inputs to outcomes, stays the same. A lender starts marketing to students: incomes are lower on average, but a student with a given income and history defaults as often as anyone else with the same profile. The model may still be right for each applicant; it may just be seeing more of the region where it was less well trained.

Label shift (prior shift) is a change in P(Y), how common each outcome is, while each class looks the same. In a recession, defaults become more common overall. A well-calibrated model's average prediction will now be too low; recalibration (ml15) can fix it without retraining.

Concept drift is a change in P(Y|X) itself: the same inputs now mean something different. Fraudsters abandon a pattern the model learned and adopt one it rates as safe. Users start asking a support bot about a product that did not exist when its documents were indexed, so the correct answer to "how do I return it?" changed. Only concept drift necessarily breaks the model, and only new labels or new knowledge fix it.

Pipeline bugs are not drift in the world at all, but they look like it, and they are the most common cause of alarms. Check them first: they are the cheapest to confirm and the most urgent to fix.

3. Measuring a change in distribution: histograms and PSI

To say whether a distribution has changed you need a reference and a number. The baseline (reference) distribution is what you compare against: the training data, a stable launch period, or a recent rolling window. Continuous features are split into bins (often deciles of the baseline, so each holds 10% of baseline data) and categorical features use their categories. Each side becomes a vector of shares that sum to 1.

The Population Stability Index (PSI) compares the two vectors:

PSI = Σ over bins of (current − baseline) × ln(current / baseline)

Each term is positive whenever a bin's share changes in either direction, because (c − b) and ln(c/b) always have the same sign. Big relative changes in bins that hold a lot of data contribute most.

Work it on an LLM-specific feature. A support assistant buckets the length of users' messages: under 50 tokens, 50–200, 200–1,000 and over 1,000. In the launch month the shares were 40%, 30%, 20% and 10%. This week they are 25%, 30%, 25% and 20%: people have started pasting long documents.

4. KL and JS divergence, and how they relate to PSI

KL divergence (Kullback–Leibler) measures how different a distribution p is from a reference q: KL(p‖q) = Σ p ln(p/q). Intuitively it is the average extra surprise of seeing data from p when you expected q. It is not symmetric. For the worked example, KL(current‖baseline) = 0.25 × ln 0.625 + 0 + 0.25 × ln 1.25 + 0.20 × ln 2 = −0.1175 + 0.0558 + 0.1386 = 0.0769, and KL(baseline‖current) = 0.40 × ln 1.6 + 0 + 0.20 × ln 0.8 + 0.10 × ln 0.5 = 0.1880 − 0.0446 − 0.0693 = 0.0741.

Add them: 0.0769 + 0.0741 = 0.151, exactly the PSI. That is not a coincidence. Expanding Σ (c − b) ln(c/b) = Σ c ln(c/b) + Σ b ln(b/c) shows that PSI is the symmetrised KL divergence. So PSI and KL are not rival metrics; PSI is KL measured both ways. The Jensen–Shannon divergence (JS) compares each distribution with their average m = (p + q)/2 and averages the two KLs; it is symmetric, never infinite even with empty bins, and bounded by ln 2. For the example it is 0.0186. Different tools report different ones; what matters is calibrating whichever you use against its own noise, which is next.

5. The noise floor: why fixed thresholds lie

Every drift statistic computed from samples is positive even when nothing has changed, because two samples from the same distribution never match exactly. The value it takes from randomness alone is its noise floor. For PSI with k bins and samples of size n₁ and n₂, a useful approximation is

expected PSI under no drift ≈ (k − 1) × (1/n₁ + 1/n₂)

With 10 bins and 200 observations on each side that is 9 × (1/200 + 1/200) = 0.09. With 1,000 on each side it is 0.018; with 10,000, 0.0018. The lab confirms it by simulation: drawing two samples from the same 10-bin uniform distribution 2,000 times gives mean PSIs of 0.095, 0.018 and 0.0018 for those sizes, with 99th percentiles of 0.23, 0.043 and 0.0044.

Now look at the widely quoted rule of thumb: PSI below 0.1 is stable, 0.1–0.25 is a moderate shift, above 0.25 is significant. At 200 samples, no-drift data crosses 0.1 roughly a third of the time and comes close to 0.25 at the 99th percentile: a monitor using the rule on small daily slices pages people for nothing. At 10,000 samples the rule is blind to real shifts: a change that moves PSI from 0.002 to 0.05 is twenty-five times the noise and may matter a great deal, but the rule calls it stable. The fix is to set thresholds from the null distribution at your actual sample size: resample the baseline many times (or split a stable period), compute the statistic each time, and alert above, say, its 99.9th percentile. Then separately judge whether a statistically real shift matters, by looking at what moved and whether outcomes follow.

6. Monitoring rates: refusals, failures and escalations

Many of the most useful LLM signals are rates: the share of answers that are refusals, that fail to parse (ae4), that escalate to a human, that users regenerate. A rate estimated from n requests has a standard error of √(p(1 − p)/n), the typical size of its random wobble. With a normal parse-failure rate of 1% and 5,000 requests a day, the standard error is √(0.01 × 0.99 / 5,000) ≈ 0.14 percentage points. A day at 3% failures is (3 − 1) / 0.14 ≈ 14 standard errors away: unmistakable. A day at 1.3% is about 2 standard errors: indistinguishable from a bad day. The lab simulates both and only the 3% case crosses a 4-standard-error alert line.

The same arithmetic tells you how long you must wait to see a small change. Halving the standard error needs four times the data, so detecting a 0.3-point rise reliably needs about a week of traffic at this volume, not a day. Alerting rules follow: page on large, sudden jumps (format failures from 1% to 3% after a model update); track small, slow changes on weekly dashboards with confidence intervals.

7. Quality without labels: proxies and sampled review

For classic models, labels eventually arrive: a loan defaults or does not, a transaction is charged back or is not. Track the label delay explicitly and compute real metrics once labels land. For generative systems, most answers never get a label. Two families of signal fill the gap.

Proxy metrics are user behaviours that correlate with quality: regenerating an answer, editing a draft heavily before sending it (measured as edit distance between the model's text and what was sent), copying or accepting a suggestion, escalating to a human, abandoning the conversation, rephrasing the same question, and thumbs up or down. Proxies are cheap and immediate, but each is biased: thumbs are clicked by a few percent of users, skewed to extremes; a low escalation rate might mean the bot hides the escalation button. Use them as trend lines and as pointers to examples worth reading.

Sampled review estimates quality directly. Draw a random sample of traces each day or week, stratified so important slices (each intent, each language, each enterprise tier) get enough examples, and grade them against a rubric: was the answer correct, grounded in the retrieved documents, compliant with policy, in the right format? Human reviewers are the gold standard; an LLM judge (lm14) scales the same rubric to far more samples once its agreement with human labels has been measured on a calibration set and is re-checked periodically. The sample size follows from the standard error: to estimate an 85% pass rate within ±3 points at 95% confidence you need about 1.96² × 0.85 × 0.15 / 0.03² ≈ 545 graded samples per period and per slice you want to report.

8. Monitoring text: from words to distributions

PSI and its relatives work on numbers and categories, so free text has to be turned into those first. Simple features come first and catch a surprising amount: message length in tokens, language, presence of attachments or code, the share of messages that are follow-ups. Then meaning: embed each input (lm10), cluster a reference window's embeddings into topics, or classify messages into known intents with a small classifier, and track the mix of topics or intents as a categorical distribution. The lab does this for an intent mix of billing, account, technical, shipping and other: after a product launch on day 20, "other" jumps from 5% to 25% and PSI goes from about 0.002 on normal days to about 0.4, far above a threshold of 0.006 set at the 99.9th percentile of no-drift days with 3,000 messages each.

A rising "other" share or a new cluster with no counterpart in the reference window is often the most valuable alert an LLM team gets: it means users are asking about something the system was not built or tested for. The response is to read a sample, then decide whether to add documents to retrieval, add the intent to the eval set, or route it elsewhere. Outputs get the same treatment: answer length, refusal rate, language, citation rate, share of answers containing code blocks or links, all tracked against a baseline.

9. The feedback loop: from logs to evals to fixes

Monitoring only pays off if what it finds changes the system. The loop that does this is the core operating rhythm of an AI product team.

Walk it once. Traces from production (ae1) hold inputs, outputs and signals. Each week, sample two streams: a random sample (to estimate overall quality honestly) and a flagged sample (thumbs down, escalations, drift-alert clusters, judge failures) to find problems fast. Error analysis reads the failures and categorises them: "answered from memory instead of documents", "missed the new product line", "ignored the language of the question", "wrong date format". A few categories usually explain most failures, which tells you what to fix first. Representative failures become eval cases with expected behaviour, so the eval set grows from real traffic rather than from guesses. The fix might be a prompt change, new documents in the retrieval index, a guardrail, a routing change or a model change; it goes through a gated release (ae7) that runs the eval set, including the new cases, before rollout. Monitoring then confirms the fix in production, and the loop turns again.

10. Responding: retraining, recalibrating, re-indexing

What you do about a confirmed problem depends on its kind. A pipeline bug is fixed in the pipeline, and any model trained on the bad data is quarantined. Label shift can often be handled by recalibrating outputs to the new base rate. Covariate drift into poorly covered regions and concept drift need new labelled data and retraining. Classic ML teams combine a schedule (retrain monthly, which bounds staleness) with triggers (retrain early when monitoring shows sustained degradation), and every retrained model passes a validation gate: compare it with the current production model (the champion) on recent labelled data and on slices, optionally run it in shadow mode on live traffic, then promote. ae8 covers the pipeline and registry that make this routine.

For LLM applications, "retraining" is usually something else: adding documents to the index for a new product, updating the prompt for a new policy, adding few-shot examples for a new intent, pinning or upgrading the model version. The discipline is identical: confirm the problem, make a change, evaluate it against the eval set (now including the cases that revealed the problem), roll out gradually, and keep monitoring.

Estimating performance before labels arrive

Under pure covariate drift, P(Y|X) is unchanged, so a well-calibrated model's own confidence still describes its accuracy on each input. That makes performance estimable without labels: average the model's predicted probability of being right over current traffic. If a calibrated classifier's confidence on this week's inputs averages 0.82 versus 0.90 at launch, expected accuracy fell about eight points, because traffic moved into regions where the model is less sure. This is the idea behind confidence-based performance estimation (sold as CBPE in some monitoring tools), and importance weighting is a related approach: reweight labelled reference examples by how much more or less common their region has become, then recompute the metric. Both rest on two assumptions worth stating in an interview: the model is calibrated (ml15) and there is no concept drift. Under concept drift the model is confidently wrong and these estimates are blind.

Multivariate drift and domain classifiers

Univariate statistics like PSI check one feature at a time and miss changes in how features combine: each feature's distribution can be stable while their correlation flips. A domain classifier catches this. Label reference examples 0 and current examples 1, train any classifier to tell them apart, and measure its area under the ROC curve on held-out data. An AUC near 0.5 means the two sets are indistinguishable; an AUC of 0.7 means they differ, and the classifier's most important features point at what changed. Rabanser, Günnemann and Lipton compared shift-detection methods systematically and found that two-sample tests run on a reduced representation (the outputs of a pre-trained classifier) detected shift best, and that domain-discriminating classifiers were most useful for characterising a shift and judging whether it was harmful. For text, run the classifier on embeddings and read the examples it is most confident are "current".

Multiple testing and alert design

Monitoring 300 features daily, each with a 1% false-alarm threshold, produces three false alarms a day on average. Within a week nobody reads them: alert fatigue. Four habits prevent it. Tier the features: alert only on those that matter to the model (high importance) and on aggregate symptoms, and leave the rest on dashboards. Correct thresholds for the number of tests, or use stricter per-test thresholds derived from the null distribution. Require persistence: a shift must hold for two or three consecutive windows before alerting, which removes one-off flukes at the cost of a day's delay. And use seasonality-aware baselines: compare Monday with last Monday, not with Sunday, because weekly and holiday cycles are not drift.

Reference windows: fixed or rolling

Comparing against the training data answers "is the model seeing what it was trained for?". Comparing against last week answers "did something change suddenly?". They catch different problems. A fixed reference slowly accumulates legitimate drift until every alarm fires permanently and is ignored; a rolling reference adapts to gradual change and therefore never alerts on it, so a slow slide into territory the model never saw goes unnoticed. Mature setups run both: a rolling comparison for sudden changes and a fixed one, with a higher threshold, for cumulative distance from training.

Model-induced feedback loops

When a model's decisions determine which outcomes get observed, the labels it is evaluated and retrained on are biased. A fraud model blocks a transaction, so whether it was really fraud is never learned; a credit model rejects an applicant, so whether they would have repaid is unknown; a recommender only learns about items it showed. Over successive retrains the model can become confident about regions it never sees outcomes from. The standard defence is a small randomised holdout: for a few percent of cases, make the decision without the model (or with deliberate exploration), observe outcomes, and use that slice for unbiased evaluation and as training data. It has a cost (some fraud gets through, some recommendations are worse), which is the price of knowing the truth.

Judges drift too

An LLM judge that scores sampled traffic is itself a model with a version, a prompt and biases (lm14). If the judge's provider updates it, quality scores can jump with no change to the product. Pin the judge model version, version the rubric prompt, keep a fixed calibration set of human-labelled examples, and re-measure judge-human agreement whenever anything about the judge changes and periodically otherwise. When the judge must change, re-score a window of historical samples with both judges to splice the time series, or the dashboard will show a step that is purely an artefact.

Probes for third-party models

Your traffic changes and your model may change; separating the two needs a fixed yardstick. A probe suite is a fixed set of prompts with known good answers or properties (format, refusal behaviour, a few reasoning items) run on a schedule, say hourly, against the exact model configuration used in production. Because the inputs never change, any movement in probe results is a change in the model or the serving path. This is exactly the method of the GPT-4 drift study, applied continuously. It also catches provider-side incidents (degraded quality from a serving bug) that never show up as errors.

Quality SLOs

Service SLOs cover availability and latency. Mature AI teams add quality SLOs with owners: "groundedness at least 95% on the weekly random sample, measured by the calibrated judge", "escalation rate on refund-dispute intents at least 98%", "parse failures below 0.5% daily". Writing them down forces the questions monitoring needs answered: how is it measured, on what sample size, with what confidence interval, and who acts when it is breached.

Characteristic incidents

The upstream unit change. A loan model's approval rate climbs from 42% to 55% over two weeks with no deploy. The input monitor shows the income feature's distribution shifted right by roughly a factor of twelve: a partner started sending monthly instead of annual income. PSI on that feature is enormous, every other feature is stable, and the fix is in the partner integration plus a schema check that rejects implausible values, not in the model.

The silent model update. An extraction pipeline's parse-failure rate goes from 0.5% to 9% overnight. Nothing was deployed. The trace shows the model alias resolved to a new version, which now wraps its JSON in a markdown code fence. A format-failure alert catches it within an hour; the fix is pinning the version, tolerant parsing that strips fences, and structured-output features that remove the failure mode (ae4).

The new intent. After a product launch, a support assistant's "other" intent share rises from 5% to 25%, thumbs-down rises slowly, and escalations rise in that cluster. Reading samples shows questions about the new product that retrieval cannot answer, so the model improvises. The fix is adding the product documents to the index and new eval cases for the intent.

The retrain on bad data. A scheduled weekly retrain runs after an upstream bug filled a feature with zeros for three days. The new model learns that the feature is uninformative and is promoted automatically. Performance drops when the bug is fixed and the feature returns. The defence is data validation before training (ae8) and a promotion gate that compares candidates with the champion.

The case: how GPT-4's behaviour changed between March and June 2023

Lingjiao Chen, Matei Zaharia and James Zou (Stanford and UC Berkeley) evaluated the March 2023 and June 2023 versions of GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, the two most widely used LLM services at the time, on a fixed set of tasks: maths problems, sensitive questions, opinion surveys, multi-hop questions through a LangChain application, code generation, the US medical licensing exam and visual reasoning. They tracked each task's main metric plus two drift metrics that apply to any LLM service: verbosity (response length in characters) and mismatch (how often the extracted answers of the two versions differ on the same prompt).

The changes were large. On 1,000 questions asking whether a number is prime or composite, with a chain-of-thought prompt ("Think step by step and then answer [Yes] or [No]"), GPT-4's accuracy fell from 84.0% in March to 51.1% in June, while GPT-3.5's rose from 49.6% to 76.2%. GPT-4's average response length on that task fell from 638.3 characters to 3.9: the June version had stopped following the instruction to reason step by step and simply answered. With chain of thought, the March GPT-4 gained 24.4 points over a direct answer; the June version gained nothing. On 50 recent easy LeetCode problems, over 50% of GPT-4's March generations were directly executable, against only 10% in June. One cause was formatting: the June version added markdown code fences (```python) and extra comments around the code, which made the output invalid as a Python program. GPT-4 also became less willing to answer sensitive questions (21.0% to 5.0%) and opinion survey questions (97.6% to 22.1%). The authors concluded that the behaviour of the "same" LLM service can change substantially in a short time and that LLMs need continuous monitoring.

For production teams the study is a template as much as a warning. Its method (fixed probe sets, a task metric plus verbosity and mismatch, comparison across versions) is exactly what a probe suite does. The code-fence finding is the most practical lesson: a change that a human reader would barely notice destroyed a downstream pipeline that expected raw code. Format-failure rates, output-length distributions and answer-mismatch on probes would each have caught it on the first day. And the general lesson stands for every hosted model: pin versions where the provider allows it, evaluate every version change before switching, and keep probing, because your own traffic cannot tell you whether the model changed.

The production dashboard

Per feature: system health (ae1) and cost (ae2); input drift for the top features or, for text, length, language and intent or topic mix with thresholds from the null; output distributions (length, refusal, format failure, citation rate, escalation); user proxies as trends with confidence bands; the daily or weekly sampled quality score per slice with intervals; probe-suite results by model version; label-delay and, once labels land, real metrics; and the count of open error-analysis categories. Page only on high-confidence symptoms; route slow quality signals to a daily review owned by a named person.

Goal: compute PSI, KL and JS on the worked histograms, measure PSI's noise floor at three sample sizes, run a daily intent-mix monitor with a threshold set from the null distribution, and monitor a parse-failure rate with a standard-error alert. NumPy from the existing gpt_env (Python 3.12).

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def psi(base, cur, eps=1e-4):
    b, c = np.clip(base, eps, None), np.clip(cur, eps, None)
    return float(np.sum((c - b) * np.log(c / b)))

def kl(p, q, eps=1e-4):
    p, q = np.clip(p, eps, None), np.clip(q, eps, None)
    return float(np.sum(p * np.log(p / q)))

def js(p, q):
    m = (np.asarray(p) + np.asarray(q)) / 2
    return 0.5 * kl(p, m) + 0.5 * kl(q, m)

# 1. the worked example: user-message length buckets (<50, 50-200, 200-1k, >1k tokens)
base = np.array([0.40, 0.30, 0.20, 0.10])
cur = np.array([0.25, 0.30, 0.25, 0.20])
print(f"PSI {psi(base, cur):.4f} | KL(cur||base) {kl(cur, base):.4f} | "
      f"KL(base||cur) {kl(base, cur):.4f} | JS {js(base, cur):.4f}")

# 2. the noise floor: PSI between two samples of the SAME distribution
def sample_hist(p, n):
    return np.bincount(rng.choice(len(p), n, p=p), minlength=len(p)) / n
p10 = np.full(10, 0.1)
for n in (200, 1_000, 10_000):
    null = [psi(sample_hist(p10, n), sample_hist(p10, n)) for _ in range(2_000)]
    print(f"no drift, 10 bins, n={n:6,d}: mean PSI {np.mean(null):.4f}, 99th pct {np.quantile(null, .99):.4f}")

# 3. daily monitor on an intent mix; a new product launch shifts traffic from day 20
intents = np.array([0.35, 0.25, 0.20, 0.15, 0.05])          # billing, account, tech, shipping, other
shifted = np.array([0.30, 0.20, 0.15, 0.10, 0.25])
null = [psi(intents, sample_hist(intents, 3_000)) for _ in range(2_000)]
threshold = np.quantile(null, 0.999)
print(f"\nalert threshold (99.9th pct of no-drift PSI at n=3,000): {threshold:.4f}")
for day in range(15, 26):
    p = intents if day < 20 else shifted
    v = psi(intents, sample_hist(p, 3_000))
    print(f"day {day}: PSI {v:.4f} {'ALERT' if v > threshold else ''}")

# 4. a proportion monitor: share of outputs that fail to parse (1% normal, 3% after an update)
base_rate, n = 0.01, 5_000
se = np.sqrt(base_rate * (1 - base_rate) / n)
for rate in (0.01, 0.013, 0.03):
    obs = rng.binomial(n, rate) / n
    z = (obs - base_rate) / se
    print(f"true failure rate {rate:.1%}: observed {obs:.2%}, z = {z:5.1f} {'ALERT' if z > 4 else ''}")

Work it out

A feature was uniform across four buckets in training (25% each). Live traffic this week is 10% / 20% / 30% / 40%. (a) Compute the PSI. (b) Is it significant if the live sample is 400 requests? If it is 40,000? (c) What do you investigate, in what order?

Answer

(a) PSI = Σ (live − train) × ln(live / train):

  • (0.10 − 0.25) × ln 0.40 = −0.15 × −0.916 = 0.137
  • (0.20 − 0.25) × ln 0.80 = −0.05 × −0.223 = 0.011
  • (0.30 − 0.25) × ln 1.20 = 0.05 × 0.182 = 0.009
  • (0.40 − 0.25) × ln 1.60 = 0.15 × 0.470 = 0.071

PSI = 0.228. The folklore bands would call it "moderate, just under significant".

(b) Use the noise floor instead. The training sample is large, so only the live sample's noise matters: no-drift PSI ≈ (k − 1)/n = 3/400 = 0.0075 at 400 requests, and 3/40,000 = 0.000075 at 40,000. An observed 0.228 is about 30 times the noise floor at 400 and 3,000 times at 40,000, so the shift is real in both cases. The sample size changes how confident you are, not the conclusion here; it would matter for a PSI of 0.02.

(c) In order: a pipeline bug first (an upstream schema change, new default value, unit change, or a join dropping rows), because it is common, cheap to check and urgent; then which traffic moved (a new customer segment, channel or partner, a campaign, seasonality: compare with the same week last year); then whether outputs and outcomes moved (prediction rates, any labels that have arrived, proxies). If it is covariate drift and the model handles the new region well, document and update the reference; if performance dropped, plan new labels and retraining through the usual validation gate. Do not retrain automatically on the alarm.
