A company launches an LLM feature that summarises customer-support conversations for its agents. It works well. Six months later three things happen in the same week. A customer exercises their right to have their data deleted; the support team deletes the ticket, and an engineer discovers the customer's messages also live in the tracing system (every prompt and response stored in full for debugging), in a semantic cache, in a spreadsheet of "interesting failures" used as an eval set, and in the logs of the model provider, whose retention period nobody checked. A security researcher shows that a carefully worded ticket can make the summariser include text from other customers' tickets that the retrieval step happened to fetch. And the regulator asks for the data protection impact assessment the company never wrote.

None of this required a malicious insider or a sophisticated attack. It came from the normal way LLM applications are built: copy everything everywhere because it might be useful for debugging, evaluation or improvement later. LLM products process the most personal kinds of text there are (emails, contracts, medical notes, chat logs, call transcripts) and they move it through more systems than a traditional application: a third-party model provider, vector indexes, caches, review tools, evaluation pipelines and sometimes training runs.

Regulators have noticed. In December 2024 Italy's data protection authority fined OpenAI €15 million over ChatGPT, citing among other things a data breach in March 2023 that it said OpenAI had not notified to the authority, and the processing of users' personal data for training without an adequate legal basis. The breach itself came from an ordinary engineering bug in a caching library.

Privacy is also a product-quality issue that users notice. People share more with an assistant they trust, and they stop using one the moment it shows them someone else's data or repeats something they said in a way that feels wrong. The controls in this lesson are what make it safe to put an LLM in front of real customer data at all.

This lesson treats privacy as what it mostly is for an AI engineer: a set of architectural requirements. It starts with what counts as personal data and the GDPR principles that turn into design constraints, maps every copy an LLM application makes, then covers detecting and pseudonymising personal data (with a sample record redacted by hand and a scrubber you can run), provider terms, permission-aware retrieval, logging and retention, and the erasure fan-out. It closes with the wider responsible-deployment questions an interviewer will expect you to raise unprompted: per-slice fairness, transparency, human oversight and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework.

Every copy is a liability

Think of personal data as cash in a building. Every room it passes through needs a lock, a log of who entered, and a rule for when the cash leaves. A sensible bank moves cash through as few rooms as possible, counts it at each step, and does not leave a pile in the break room because it might be handy later. An LLM application that stores full prompts in traces, copies conversations into spreadsheets for evaluation and caches answers that quote customer records has left cash in a dozen rooms, most of them unlocked and unlogged.

The analogy carries three working rules. Fewer rooms: do not send, store or copy personal data unless the purpose needs it (data minimisation). Locks and logs: each place that holds it has access controls and records access. A schedule: each copy has a retention period after which it is deleted, and you can find and remove one person's data from every room on request. Where the analogy breaks is that cash is easy to recognise. Personal data is not: a name is obvious, but "the only cardiologist in a village of 300 people" identifies someone too, and a model's output can contain personal data that was never in the input because the model saw it in training.

The working pattern

The pattern most well-run LLM products converge on has four parts. Minimise at the edge: detect personal data when it enters and replace it with placeholders before it reaches the model, the logs or anything else that does not strictly need the real value. Scope everything to the user: retrieval, caches and tools only ever see data the requesting user is allowed to see, enforced in the data layer, not by asking the model nicely. Keep a deletion map: for every store, know how to find and delete one person's data, and how long anything lives there by default. Contract and document: know what your model provider does with what you send, and write down why you process what you process.

A worked flow, start to finish

Follow one message through a system built this way. A customer types "I'm Priya Shah, my card ending 4417 was charged twice". The gateway attaches her identity and tenant, and the detector replaces her name (known from her account) and the card fragment with tokens before anything is logged. The orchestrator retrieves only documents her tenant may see, assembles a prompt containing tokens, and sends it to a provider under a data processing agreement with training disabled and EU processing. The model answers in terms of tokens; the output filter checks that no card-number-shaped string appears that is not a token; the application re-inserts her name in the reply she sees. The trace stores the tokenised version for 30 days. If she later asks for erasure, her vault entries are deleted first, which immediately makes every tokenised copy unattributable, and the deletion job then removes the copies themselves on its schedule.

If you stop here: personal data is broader than names and card numbers; every copy is a liability; redact or pseudonymise before the model and before the logs; enforce permissions where data is stored; know how to delete one person from everywhere; and read the provider's terms before the first request, not after the first incident.

1. What counts as personal data

The most influential definition is the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), which applies to processing personal data of people in the EU, wherever the company is. Article 4(1) defines personal data as "any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person", and an identifiable person is one who can be identified "directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier or to one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural or social identity of that natural person". The person is the data subject.

Three consequences matter for engineers. First, it is broader than the US term PII (personally identifiable information), which in practice usually means a list of direct identifiers. Under GDPR an IP address, a device id, a customer number or a free-text description ("the head of finance at our Leeds office") can all be personal data. Second, combinations count: fields that are harmless alone (quasi-identifiers such as postcode, birth date and job title) can single someone out together. Third, some data is special category (Article 9): data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, trade union membership, genetic data, biometric data used for identification, health data, and data about sex life or sexual orientation. Processing it is prohibited unless one of the article's exceptions applies. Support chats routinely contain health details ("I missed the payment because I was in hospital"), so an LLM product can process special-category data without anyone having designed for it.

2. The principles that become engineering requirements

GDPR's Article 5 principles read like legal prose, but most of them are design constraints. Lawfulness and purpose limitation: you need a legal basis for each processing purpose (contract, consent, legitimate interests and so on), and data collected for one purpose cannot simply be reused for another; "we might train on it later" is a new purpose. Data minimisation: data must be "adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary" for the purpose. If summarisation works with placeholders, sending real names to the provider is not necessary. Storage limitation: data is kept in identifiable form "for no longer than is necessary", which means every store needs a retention period. Integrity and confidentiality: appropriate security. Accountability: you must be able to demonstrate compliance, which means documentation and logs.

Two further articles make it explicitly an engineering matter. Article 25 requires data protection by design and by default: appropriate technical measures (pseudonymisation is named) built into the system, and defaults that process only what is necessary. Article 35 requires a data protection impact assessment (DPIA) before processing likely to result in high risk; new technologies processing personal data at scale often qualify. The roles matter too: your company is usually the controller (it decides why and how data is processed) and the model provider a processor acting on your instructions, governed by a data processing agreement. Penalties are serious: the most severe infringements can draw fines up to €20 million or 4% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher.

3. Map the flows before you protect them

You cannot protect copies you do not know exist. The first deliverable of a privacy review is a data-flow map like the primer's diagram, listing for each store what personal data it holds, why, who can access it, how long it is kept and how one person's data is deleted. For a typical RAG assistant the list is long: the client and gateway logs, the orchestrator's conversation store, the assembled prompt sent to the provider and whatever the provider retains, the response, the trace store (ae1), exact and semantic caches, the vector index and its source documents, human review and labelling queues (ae3), eval sets, analytics tables and any fine-tuning datasets. The map usually reveals that the cheapest large reduction in risk is not a clever detector but deleting or redacting stores nobody needed.

4. Detecting personal data

Detection methods differ in what they are good at, and production systems layer them.

Patterns with validation find formatted identifiers: email addresses, phone numbers in known national formats, postcodes, national insurance or social security numbers, IBANs, and payment card numbers. Validation removes false positives: card numbers carry a Luhn check digit, so a 16-digit order number that fails the check is not redacted. Patterns are fast, cheap and explainable, and blind to anything without a format.

Known values use what you already know. The account holder's own name, email, address and account number are in your database; searching the message for them (case-insensitively, with simple variants) catches the most common personal data of all at near-perfect precision.

Named-entity recognition (NER) models label spans as person, location, organisation and so on. They find names and addresses that no pattern or list covers, at the cost of misses on unusual names, over-redaction of product or place names, and lower accuracy on informal text and non-English languages. Open-source frameworks such as Microsoft Presidio combine NER with pattern recognisers.

LLM-based detection asks a model to mark personal data in context. It handles indirect identifiers and messy text best and costs the most; it is also itself a processing step that sees the data, so it should run inside your trust boundary (a self-hosted model) or under the same provider terms.

Whatever the combination, measure it. Label a few hundred messages from your own traffic with every personal-data span, run the detector, and compute recall (share of real personal data found) and precision (share of detections that are real) per entity type (ml7). Recall is the safety metric: every miss leaks. Precision is the utility metric: over-redaction removes the information the model needs ("my order from [LOCATION] arrived damaged" may be fine; "[PERSON] says the dosage is [NUMBER]" may make a medical summary useless).

5. A sample record, redacted by hand

Take one support message:

Hi, I'm Priya Shah, card 4111 1111 1111 1111 was charged twice for order
1234567890123456. Email priya.shah@example.com or call +44 7700 900123.
Address: 12 Hill Rd, London NW3 2AB.

Walk through the layers. The account holder's name is in the customer record, so the known-values pass replaces "Priya Shah". The pattern for card numbers matches two digit strings. "4111 1111 1111 1111" passes the Luhn check (it is a standard test card number), so it is a card. "1234567890123456" fails the check, so it is left alone: it is an order number the model needs. The email pattern matches the address; the UK mobile pattern matches the phone number; the postcode pattern matches "NW3 2AB". The result, using the lab's scrubber:

Hi, I'm [PERSON_18853a], card [CARD_36ad8a] was charged twice for order
1234567890123456. Email [EMAIL_38e530] or call [PHONE_85e183].
Address: 12 Hill Rd, London [POSTCODE_010360].

Notice what survived: "12 Hill Rd, London". Street addresses have no reliable format, so patterns miss them; an NER model or an address parser is needed. Combined with the postcode, the street address narrows to a handful of homes, so the residual message is still personal data. That is the honest result of a pattern-only scrubber: it removes the high-risk formatted identifiers cheaply and leaves the free-text ones for other layers. On the lab's small labelled set the scrubber finds every formatted identifier but misses a name it has no list for ("Dr. Ana Lima") and an email deliberately written as "sam at example dot com": recall 67%, precision 100%.

6. Redaction, pseudonymisation and anonymisation

There are three things you can do with a detected identifier. Redaction removes it ("[REDACTED]"); simple, irreversible and often too destructive, because the model then cannot tell two people apart. Pseudonymisation replaces it with a token that is consistent and can be mapped back using information kept separately; GDPR Article 4(5) defines it as processing such that the data "can no longer be attributed to a specific data subject without the use of additional information, provided that such additional information is kept separately". Anonymisation makes re-identification impossible by any means reasonably likely to be used, and is the only one that takes data out of GDPR's scope. Recital 26 is explicit that pseudonymised data "should be considered to be information on an identifiable natural person". For rich free text, true anonymisation is very hard; assume what you have is pseudonymised and still personal data.

The lab generates tokens with an HMAC, a keyed hash: the token is the first characters of HMAC-SHA256(secret key, "EMAIL:priya.shah@example.com"). This gives three useful properties. The same value always gets the same token, across messages and sessions, so the model can tell that two messages concern the same person and analytics can still count distinct customers. Nobody without the key can compute the token for a guessed email, which a plain unkeyed hash would allow. And no central counter is needed. The reverse map from token to value lives in a protected store (a "vault") with its own access control and retention, and the key is rotated and stored in a secrets manager.

7. Placeholders through the model and back

Pseudonymisation is most useful when the model never needs the real value. A summariser can write "[PERSON_18853a] reports a duplicate charge on card [CARD_36ad8a]" perfectly well. The application then re-inserts real values when rendering the summary for a support agent who is allowed to see them, and stores only the tokenised version in logs and traces. Three practical details make it work. Tell the model in the system prompt that bracketed tokens are placeholders to be copied exactly, never expanded or altered. Choose a token format the tokenizer handles well and that the model is unlikely to mangle (short, consistent, bracketed). And validate the output: every token in the output should exist in the request's vault, and any card-number-shaped string in the output that is not a token is a red flag worth blocking.

8. The model provider

Sending a prompt to a hosted model is a transfer of whatever is in it to a processor. Before the first production request, an engineer should be able to answer: Is there a data processing agreement? Does the provider use API data to train models by default, and how is that controlled? How long are prompts and outputs retained, and is a zero-data-retention arrangement available for this use? Where is data processed, and are regional endpoints available (both major providers charge a 10% uplift for data-residency endpoints on recent models, ae2)? Which sub-processors are involved? What security certifications apply? The answers go into the record of processing and, where needed, the DPIA. Minimisation still applies even with the best terms: pseudonymise anything the task does not need.

9. Permission-aware retrieval and tenant isolation

Retrieval is the most common path by which an LLM application shows a user data they should not see. If the vector index holds every customer's documents and the search is not filtered, a question can retrieve another customer's contract, and the model will quote it. Telling the model "only use documents belonging to the user" does not help: once text is in the context, it can be repeated, and a prompt injection (lm18) can ask for it explicitly. The fix is in the data layer. Each chunk carries metadata copied from its source document: tenant id and access-control list. Every query is filtered by the requesting user's identity, attached by the gateway, before ranking, ideally using the vector store's native filtering or separate namespaces per tenant. Caches are keyed by tenant and permission scope. And permission changes in the source system (someone leaves a project) are synchronised into the index metadata quickly, or for sensitive collections checked against the source at query time.

10. Logs, traces and retention

Observability (ae1) is where personal data most often outlives everything else, because traces are designed to capture prompts and outputs verbatim and debugging culture says "keep everything". Three controls change that. Redact at the edge, so traces store tokenised text by default, with access to raw values only through the vault and only for specific roles. Set retention per store deliberately (for example 30 days for full traces, a year for aggregated metrics that contain no personal data), and enforce it with automatic deletion rather than policy documents. Restrict and log access to traces like any other production data. When samples are copied into review queues or eval sets (ae3), they carry the same rules, and eval sets built from production should be pseudonymised before they are shared with a wider team or a labelling vendor.

11. The erasure fan-out

GDPR Article 17 gives data subjects the right to obtain erasure of their personal data "without undue delay" where one of its grounds applies (for example, the data is no longer necessary, or consent is withdrawn). In an LLM application one request fans out to many stores, which is why the deletion map is built before launch.

Two details deserve emphasis. Graph-based vector indexes such as HNSW (lm10) do not delete cheaply: removing a node can damage the graph's connectivity, so most systems mark items with a tombstone that searches skip, then rebuild the index periodically; the rebuild schedule is part of your erasure promise. And a model that was fine-tuned on someone's data may have memorised parts of it; there is no reliable, cheap way to make it forget one person. The practical answer is to not fine-tune on personal data that may be subject to erasure (pseudonymise training data, or train on synthetic or consented data) or to accept a retraining obligation.

12. Breaches and the 72-hour clock

Article 33 requires the controller to notify the supervisory authority of a personal data breach "without undue delay and, where feasible, not later than 72 hours after having become aware of it", unless it is unlikely to result in a risk to people. For an engineering team that turns into concrete requirements: the ability to detect that data went to the wrong person (cross-user checks, anomaly alerts), traces good enough to determine who was affected and what they saw within hours, and a runbook that brings in the privacy and legal teams on day one. The March 2023 ChatGPT incident in the case below is a textbook example of a breach caused by an ordinary caching bug.

Memorisation and training on user data

Language models can memorise and reproduce rare strings from their training data, especially strings repeated several times: email signatures, phone numbers, code with credentials. Fine-tuning a model on support transcripts therefore risks it emitting one customer's details to another. The defences are layered. Deduplicate training data (repetition drives memorisation), scrub or pseudonymise identifiers before training, exclude special-category data unless there is a specific need and basis, and test the trained model with extraction probes: prompts designed to elicit training strings, such as the beginning of a known record. Differential privacy in training (adding calibrated noise to gradient updates so no single example has much influence) gives formal guarantees but costs accuracy and is rarely used for LLM fine-tuning in practice. The governance question comes first: training on user data is a new purpose that needs its own legal basis and, often, an opt-out, which is one of the issues the Italian regulator raised.

Inference-time leakage through injection

Everything in the model's context can be extracted by someone who can influence the input (lm18). A retrieved web page can instruct the model to repeat the previous user's message; a document can ask it to render a link whose URL contains the conversation, so the user's browser sends the data to an attacker's server when it loads a preview. Privacy controls therefore extend to output handling: do not render model-generated links and images from untrusted domains automatically, filter outputs for identifiers that should not appear (any card-number-shaped string that is not a placeholder), keep secrets and other users' data out of the context entirely, and require confirmation for tools that send data out (email, webhooks). The principle is the one from ae1: the model is an untrusted component, and confidentiality has to hold even when it misbehaves.

Re-identification from "anonymous" text

Free text resists anonymisation. Removing names and numbers from "the only female surgeon at the Truro hospital who was off sick in March" leaves a sentence that identifies one person. Analysts and models can combine quasi-identifiers across records, and LLMs are good at it: given a set of pseudonymised posts, a capable model can often infer location, age range and occupation from writing style and content. This is why regulators treat pseudonymised text as personal data, why sharing "anonymised" transcripts with vendors still needs a contract and controls, and why aggregate statistics are the safe thing to publish rather than redacted examples.

Fairness in classifiers and in generation

An aggregate metric hides groups the system fails. A loan classifier with 94% accuracy can be 96% accurate for 90% of applicants and 76% for the other 10% (0.90 × 0.96 + 0.10 × 0.76 = 0.94). Report metrics per slice, by protected attribute where it is lawful to hold one and by proxies such as language, region or device otherwise, and set alerts on the worst slice, not the average. Choosing what "fair" means is a product and legal decision: Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan proved that, when base rates differ between groups, calibration within groups and equal false-positive and false-negative rates cannot all hold except in degenerate cases. For generative systems, fairness is tested by counterfactual probes: the same request with only a name, dialect or demographic detail changed, compared for quality, tone, refusal rate and content. A support bot that is terser with non-native English or refuses more often for some names is unfair in a way no aggregate metric shows.

Transparency and human oversight

People should know when they are dealing with an AI and when content is AI-generated. In the EU, Article 50 of the AI Act requires providers to design AI systems that interact directly with people so that the people concerned are informed they are interacting with an AI system, and sets rules for marking synthetic content; the Act's timetable applies it from 2 August 2026. Meaningful human oversight for consequential decisions is more than a person clicking approve: the reviewer needs the evidence the system used, the ability to override, enough time, and a record of what they decided. Measuring the override rate is a useful health check: a reviewer who never overrides may be rubber-stamping, and one who always does suggests the system is not helping.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework

NIST's AI RMF 1.0 (January 2023) is a voluntary US framework that enterprise risk, procurement and security teams increasingly use as a checklist. It describes characteristics of trustworthy AI (valid and reliable as the base, then safe, secure and resilient, accountable and transparent, explainable and interpretable, privacy-enhanced, and fair with harmful bias managed) and organises risk work into four functions. Govern: policies, roles, accountability and culture. Map: understand the context, intended use, users, data and potential impacts. Measure: test and track the risks with metrics. Manage: prioritise, mitigate, monitor and respond. Its Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1, 2024) lists risks specific to generative systems, including data privacy and information security, with suggested actions. In an interview, mapping your design onto these four verbs shows you can talk to the risk function in its own language.

Documentation as an engineering artefact

Model cards (Mitchell et al., 2018) record a model's intended use, out-of-scope uses, training data provenance, evaluation results by slice and known limitations; datasheets do the same for datasets; DPIAs and records of processing do it for personal data. Treat them like code: versioned, reviewed when the system changes, and generated partly from the same configuration and eval results the release pipeline already produces (ae7), so they stay true instead of becoming stale prose.

Failure modes in production

Full-text traces kept forever. The tracing tool stores every prompt and response verbatim with no retention policy, and dozens of engineers can search it. It is the largest store of customer data in the company and nobody designed it. Fix: redact at the edge, retention by policy, role-based access, audit logging.

The unfiltered index. A shared vector index serves all tenants, and the filter is applied in application code that one new endpoint forgot. A customer's question retrieves another customer's document. Fix: enforce tenant scoping in the data layer (namespaces or mandatory filters) and run automated cross-tenant probes in CI.

The eval spreadsheet. An engineer copies 500 real conversations into a shared spreadsheet to build an eval set. It is emailed to a labelling vendor. Fix: pseudonymise before eval sets leave the production boundary, keep eval data in governed storage, and include it in the deletion map.

The cache that crosses users. A caching bug returns one user's data to another, the failure mode in the case below. Fix: include user or tenant in every cache key, and verify on read that returned data belongs to the requester (the "redundant checks" OpenAI added after its incident).

The case: ChatGPT, a cache bug and a €15 million decision

On Monday 20 March 2023 OpenAI took ChatGPT offline. Its published account explains that a bug in redis-py, the open-source Python client for the Redis cache it used to avoid hitting its database on every request, allowed some users to see titles from another active user's chat history, and possibly the first message of another user's new conversation. A change OpenAI made at 1 a.m. Pacific time caused a spike in cancelled Redis requests; with the asyncio client for Redis Cluster, a cancelled request could leave a connection in a state where the next request received a response meant for someone else. OpenAI found that the same bug may have made payment-related information of 1.2% of ChatGPT Plus subscribers active during a nine-hour window visible to other users: first and last name, email address, payment address, card type, the last four digits of the card number and the expiry date (full card numbers were never exposed). Among its fixes, OpenAI "added redundant checks to ensure the data returned by our Redis cache matches the requesting user".

Italy's data protection authority, the Garante, had opened an investigation into ChatGPT in March 2023 and briefly blocked the service in Italy. On 20 December 2024 it announced its decision: a fine of €15 million (calculated, it said, taking into account the company's cooperative attitude) and an order, using new powers for the first time, to run a six-month institutional communication campaign on radio, television, newspapers and the internet explaining how ChatGPT collects data from users and non-users for training and how people can object, rectify and delete. According to the Garante, OpenAI had not notified the authority of the March 2023 breach; had processed users' personal data to train ChatGPT without first identifying an appropriate legal basis; had violated the principle of transparency and the related information obligations toward users; and had not provided age-verification mechanisms, risking exposure of children under 13 to inappropriate responses. Because OpenAI had meanwhile established its European headquarters in Ireland, the Garante forwarded the file to the Irish Data Protection Commission as lead authority for ongoing issues. (These are the regulator's findings in an administrative decision, not a court judgment.)

Read as an engineering review, the case touches nearly every section of this lesson. The breach came from a cache serving data across users, the classic cross-user leak, and the fix was an ownership check on read. The notification finding is about the 72-hour process: detecting, scoping and reporting a breach is an operational capability that must exist before the incident. The training finding is about purpose limitation and legal basis: using conversations to improve models is a separate purpose that needs its own justification and an opt-out. And the transparency findings are about the product telling people, clearly, what happens to their data.

What to monitor

Detector recall and precision on a labelled weekly sample, per entity type; the share of requests with detected personal data by feature (a sudden rise means a new kind of input); output filter hits (identifier-shaped strings in outputs that are not placeholders); cross-tenant probe results; access to raw-value vaults and traces by role; retention job success per store; open erasure requests and their age; and provider configuration drift (training opt-out, retention settings, regions) checked automatically.

Goal: build a small pseudonymiser: pattern detection with a Luhn check for cards, known-value detection for the account holder's name, deterministic HMAC tokens with a reverse map, and a recall/precision measurement on a labelled set that shows what patterns miss. Standard library only; Python 3.12.

import hashlib, hmac, re

SECRET = b"rotate-me-and-keep-in-a-vault"     # key for deterministic pseudonyms

def luhn_ok(digits):
    total, alt = 0, False
    for d in reversed(digits):
        n = int(d) * (2 if alt else 1)
        total += n - 9 if n > 9 else n
        alt = not alt
    return total % 10 == 0

PATTERNS = [
    ("EMAIL", re.compile(r"[\w.+-]+@[\w-]+\.[\w.-]+")),
    ("CARD", re.compile(r"\b\d(?:[ -]?\d){12,18}\b")),
    ("PHONE", re.compile(r"(?:\+44\s?7\d{3}|\b07\d{3})\s?\d{3}\s?\d{3}\b")),
    ("POSTCODE", re.compile(r"\b[A-Z]{1,2}\d[A-Z\d]?\s?\d[A-Z]{2}\b")),
]

def pseudonym(kind, value):
    tag = hmac.new(SECRET, f"{kind}:{value}".encode(), hashlib.sha256).hexdigest()[:6]
    return f"[{kind}_{tag}]"

def redact(text, known_names=()):
    """Replace PII with stable placeholders; return redacted text and the reverse map."""
    vault = {}
    def sub(kind, value):
        token = pseudonym(kind, value)
        vault[token] = value
        return token
    for name in known_names:                                  # account holder's own name
        text = re.sub(re.escape(name), lambda m: sub("PERSON", m.group()), text, flags=re.I)
    for kind, pat in PATTERNS:
        def repl(m, kind=kind):
            v = m.group()
            if kind == "CARD" and not luhn_ok(re.sub(r"\D", "", v)):
                return v                                       # order numbers are not cards
            return sub(kind, v)
        text = pat.sub(repl, text)
    return text, vault

record = ("Hi, I'm Priya Shah, card 4111 1111 1111 1111 was charged twice for order "
          "1234567890123456. Email priya.shah@example.com or call +44 7700 900123. "
          "Address: 12 Hill Rd, London NW3 2AB.")
clean, vault = redact(record, known_names=["Priya Shah"])
print(clean)
print("reverse map:", vault)
print("same input, same token:", redact("priya.shah@example.com")[0])

# recall/precision on a small labelled set: (text, set of PII strings truly present)
LABELLED = [
    ("Refund to 5500-0000-0000-0004 please", {"5500-0000-0000-0004"}),
    ("my email is sam at example dot com", {"sam at example dot com"}),
    ("call me on 07700 900456 after 6", {"07700 900456"}),
    ("I'm Dr. Ana Lima from SW1A 1AA", {"Ana Lima", "SW1A 1AA"}),
    ("tracking 9400111202555842332669", set()),
    ("ping jo@corp.io re invoice 4417", {"jo@corp.io"}),
]
tp = fp = fn = 0
for text, truth in LABELLED:
    _, found = redact(text)
    found = set(found.values())
    tp += len(found & truth); fp += len(found - truth); fn += len(truth - found)
    for miss in truth - found:
        print("missed:", miss)
print(f"recall {tp / (tp + fn):.0%}, precision {tp / (tp + fp):.0%} (tp={tp}, fp={fp}, fn={fn})")

Work it out

A loan-support assistant reports 94% answer accuracy. Customers in group A are 90% of traffic and get 96%; group B is 10% and gets 76%. (a) Confirm the aggregate. (b) What happens to the headline as group B grows to 30% of traffic? (c) A customer in group B invokes the right to erasure. List what must be deleted in this RAG-backed assistant, and which items are hard.

Answer

(a) 0.90 × 0.96 + 0.10 × 0.76 = 0.864 + 0.076 = 0.940. The aggregate is dominated by the majority group, so a 20-point gap is invisible in the headline.

(b) At 30%: 0.70 × 0.96 + 0.30 × 0.76 = 0.672 + 0.228 = 0.900. The metric falls as the underserved group grows, so a successful expansion into that segment looks like a model regression, and the team hunts for a cause that was there all along. Report per slice, alert on the worst slice, and decide which fairness definition the product commits to.

(c) Erasure is a fan-out. Their source documents and parsed copies; every chunk and embedding derived from them (hard: HNSW-style indexes need tombstones and a rebuild on a known schedule); exact and semantic cache entries that quote their data; request traces and logs (where personal data most often survives; easier if they were tokenised at the edge and the vault entry is deleted); review queues and eval sets built from their conversations; provider-side copies, governed by the contract's retention terms or zero data retention; analytics tables keyed by their id; and any fine-tuning data or fine-tuned model (hard: a trained model cannot forget one person; the real answer is not to fine-tune on data that may need erasing, or to accept a retraining obligation). Record the request and its completion, and do it without undue delay.
