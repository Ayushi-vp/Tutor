A cashier giving change for 63 cents in US coins does not think hard: take as many quarters as fit (two, 50 cents), then dimes (one, 60), then pennies (three). Six coins, and it is the fewest possible. At each step the cashier takes the biggest coin that fits and never reconsiders. That is a greedy algorithm: make the choice that looks best right now, commit to it, and move on.

Now imagine a country whose coins are 1, 3 and 4 cents, and you need 6 cents. The same strategy takes a 4, then a 1, then another 1 — three coins. But two 3-cent coins would do. The greedy choice (the 4) looked best locally and ruined the global answer. Nothing in the problem statement warned you; the coins just happened to be arranged so that greed fails.

That is the whole story of greedy algorithms in interviews. When they work, they are the simplest and fastest solutions available — often a sort and a single scan. Interval scheduling, jump games, gas stations, partitioning strings, pairing people into boats, assigning tasks, Huffman compression and the shortest-path and spanning-tree algorithms of later lessons are all greedy. But a greedy idea is easy to invent and easy to get wrong, and the examples in a problem statement rarely expose the failure. Interviewers know this, which is why "why is that correct?" is the standard follow-up to any greedy solution.

This lesson teaches both halves: the classic greedy problems with traces, and the two proof techniques — the exchange argument and the stays-ahead argument — that let you justify a greedy in a sentence or two. It also shows how to derive the right sort key instead of guessing, how "greedy with regret" uses a heap to undo bad choices, and how to test a greedy against brute force when you cannot find a proof.

The mental model: hiking in fog

Imagine hiking up a mountain in thick fog. You can only see a few metres around you, so you take the steepest step upwards each time. On a smooth hill with a single summit, this gets you to the top. On a rugged mountain with several peaks, it may strand you on a small hilltop while the real summit is elsewhere — and since you never walk downhill, you cannot recover. A greedy algorithm is that hiker: it looks only at the immediate choice, takes the best one, and never backtracks. Whether that works depends entirely on the shape of the problem.

The property that makes the "smooth hill" case work has a name: the greedy-choice property. Some locally best choice is always part of some optimal solution. If that holds at every step, and the remaining problem after the choice is a smaller version of the same problem (optimal substructure), the greedy is correct. If it fails even once, the greedy can be wrong.

Two ways to prove a greedy

You do not need formal proofs in interviews, but you do need a convincing two-sentence argument. Almost every greedy proof is one of two kinds.

Exchange argument. Take any optimal solution that differs from the greedy one. Find the first place they differ, and swap the optimal solution's choice there for the greedy choice. Show the result is still feasible and no worse. Repeating the swap turns the optimal solution into the greedy one without losing anything, so the greedy is optimal. (This is how activity selection was proved in al11.)

Stays-ahead argument. Define a measure of progress — "the furthest index reachable", "the number of intervals selected so far", "the time at which the k-th task finishes". Show by induction that after every step the greedy's measure is at least as good as any other algorithm's after the same number of steps. Then at the end it is at least as good, which means optimal.

Where the hiking analogy breaks: real hikers can see ahead a little, and many greedy algorithms quietly use some lookahead or preprocessing — sorting all items first, or computing every letter's last occurrence — before making their irreversible choices. The defining feature of greedy is not ignorance of the future; it is never undoing a choice (except in the "regret" variant, which explicitly allows one kind of undo).

A practical consequence: when you propose a greedy in an interview, propose its proof shape at the same time. "I'll sort by deadline and take the earliest; I think an exchange argument works because swapping a later-deadline job for the earliest one can only make room." Even a sketch of the argument tells the interviewer you know greedy needs justification, and it often reveals a flaw before you have written any code.

The shape of a greedy solution

Most greedy solutions have three parts: an order in which to consider items (often a sort), a rule for accepting or rejecting each item, and a running state that the rule checks. Activity selection is the archetype: order by end time, accept an interval if it starts after the last accepted end, state = last accepted end. When you meet a new optimisation problem, try to fill in those three blanks — and then try to break your rule with a small counterexample before coding.

Reachability: Jump Game I and II

Jump Game: from index i you may jump up to nums[i] steps; can you reach the last index? Track reach, the furthest index you can get to using the indices seen so far. Every index up to reach is reachable (you can always jump shorter), so the only question is whether reach ever falls behind the scan.

def can_jump(nums):
    reach = 0
    for i, step in enumerate(nums):
        if i > reach:
            return False          # stuck: i cannot be reached
        reach = max(reach, i + step)
    return True

On [3, 2, 1, 0, 4]: reach becomes 3, 3, 3, 3, then i = 4 > 3 — false. The stays-ahead measure is reach itself: no strategy can reach further than the maximum over all reachable indices of i + nums[i].

Jump Game II: the minimum number of jumps (the end is guaranteed reachable). A DP over positions is O(n²). The greedy view is breadth-first search by levels: the indices reachable with 0 jumps are [0, 0]; with 1 jump, [1, furthest from level 0]; with 2 jumps, everything up to the furthest point reachable from level 1; and so on. Each level is a contiguous range, so you only need its end.

def jump(nums):
    jumps = level_end = furthest = 0
    for i in range(len(nums) - 1):        # no jump needed from the last index
        furthest = max(furthest, i + nums[i])
        if i == level_end:                 # finished scanning this level
            jumps += 1
            level_end = furthest
    return jumps

Trace [2, 3, 1, 1, 4]: i = 0 → furthest 2, level end reached → jumps 1, level_end 2; i = 1 → furthest 4; i = 2 → furthest 4, level end → jumps 2, level_end 4. Answer 2 (0 → 1 → 4).

A running-total argument: Gas Station

Stations on a circle; station i gives gas[i] and the trip to the next costs cost[i]. Find the starting station that lets you go all the way round, or −1. The brute force tries every start: O(n²). Two observations make it O(n).

First, if the total gas is less than the total cost, no start works; if it is at least the total cost, a start exists (a classic result; the problem also promises that a valid start, when one exists, is unique). Second, if you start at s and your tank first goes negative when leaving station i, then no station between s and i can be a valid start either: each of them was reached from s with a non-negative tank, so starting there with an empty tank is no better, and you would still run dry by i. So skip straight to i + 1.

def can_complete_circuit(gas, cost):
    if sum(gas) < sum(cost):
        return -1
    start = tank = 0
    for i in range(len(gas)):
        tank += gas[i] - cost[i]
        if tank < 0:              # nobody from start..i works
            start, tank = i + 1, 0
    return start

Extending a part as far as necessary: Partition Labels

"Split a string into as many parts as possible so that each letter appears in at most one part." Precompute the last index of every letter. Scan, keeping the current part's end as the furthest last occurrence of any letter seen in the part; when the scan reaches that end, the part can be closed — every letter in it has no occurrences later.

def partition_labels(s):
    last = {c: i for i, c in enumerate(s)}
    sizes, start, end = [], 0, 0
    for i, c in enumerate(s):
        end = max(end, last[c])
        if i == end:
            sizes.append(end - start + 1)
            start = i + 1
    return sizes

Closing each part at the earliest legal moment maximises the number of parts: any valid partition must extend a part at least to the last occurrence of every letter it contains, so the greedy's parts are as short as possible (stays ahead on "number of parts closed by position i").

Deriving the sort key: Two City Scheduling

2n people must be split evenly between cities A and B; person i costs a[i] to send to A and b[i] to B. Minimise the total. Start with everyone in B and ask what it costs to move a person to A: a[i] − b[i]. You must move exactly n people, so move the n with the smallest (most negative) differences. In code: sort by a − b, send the first half to A and the rest to B.

The general technique is worth learning: compare two adjacent items and ask when swapping them helps. If person x goes to A and y to B, swapping changes the cost by (a[y] + b[x]) − (a[x] + b[y]) = (a[y] − b[y]) − (a[x] − b[x]). The swap helps exactly when y's difference is smaller than x's, so the optimal assignment sends smaller differences to A. That calculation produced the sort key; it was not a guess.

The same derivation gives "Largest Number" (concatenate numbers to form the largest result): for two numbers a and b, a should come first when the string a + b is larger than b + a. Sorting with that comparator gives the optimal order. In Python: sorted(map(str, nums), key=functools.cmp_to_key(lambda x, y: -1 if x + y > y + x else 1)).

Always serve the smallest first: Hand of Straights

"Can the cards be divided into groups of W consecutive values?" The smallest remaining card cannot be in the middle or end of a group — nothing smaller is left — so it must start a group. Greedily start a group there, remove v, v + 1, …, v + W − 1, and repeat.

from collections import Counter
def is_n_straight_hand(hand, W):
    if len(hand) % W: return False
    count = Counter(hand)
    for v in sorted(count):
        need = count[v]
        if need:
            for x in range(v, v + W):
                if count[x] < need: return False
                count[x] -= need
    return True

Processing all copies of the smallest value at once (subtracting need from each of the next W values) keeps it O(n log n) for the sort plus O(distinct · W).

Two directions, two passes: Candy

Children stand in a line with ratings; each needs at least one candy, and a child with a higher rating than a neighbour must get more than that neighbour. Minimise the total. The constraint has two directions (left neighbour and right neighbour), and a single scan can only respect one. So do two passes: left to right, give each child one more than the left neighbour if rated higher, else 1; right to left, raise each child to one more than the right neighbour if rated higher. Taking the maximum at each child satisfies both directions with the least candy.

def candy(r):
    n = len(r); c = [1] * n
    for i in range(1, n):
        if r[i] > r[i - 1]: c[i] = c[i - 1] + 1
    for i in range(n - 2, -1, -1):
        if r[i] > r[i + 1]: c[i] = max(c[i], c[i + 1] + 1)
    return sum(c)

Ratings [1, 0, 2]: the first pass gives [1, 1, 2]; the second raises the first child to 2 → [2, 1, 2], total 5.

Pairing extremes: boats, cookies and arrays

A family of pairing problems is solved by sorting and matching extremes. Boats to save people (al5): the heaviest person pairs with the lightest if they fit, otherwise goes alone — if the lightest cannot share with the heaviest, nobody can. Assign cookies: give the smallest adequate cookie to the least demanding child. Array partition (maximise the sum of the min of each pair): sort and pair neighbours, so each pair's smaller element is as large as possible. Each has a one-line exchange argument: if an optimal pairing matched the extremes differently, swapping partners would not make it worse.

Fractional versus 0/1 knapsack

If items can be split (gold dust, bandwidth), the greedy is optimal: sort by value per unit weight and take as much as possible of the best, then the next. If items are indivisible (a laptop, a camera), the same greedy fails: capacity 10 with items (weight 6, value 6), (5, 5), (5, 5) — by value density all are equal, and greedy may take the 6 first and fit nothing else (value 6), while the two 5s give 10. The 0/1 knapsack needs dynamic programming (al22). This pair is the textbook demonstration that a tiny change in the problem flips greedy from optimal to wrong.

Greedy with regret

Sometimes the right greedy is "take everything tentatively, and when a constraint breaks, undo the worst choice so far". Course Schedule III: courses have a duration and a deadline; take as many as possible. Sort by deadline, add each course, and if the total time exceeds the current deadline, drop the longest course taken so far (a max-heap of durations finds it). Dropping the longest course keeps the count the same as dropping any other but frees the most time for the future. The same pattern solves "minimum number of refuelling stops" (pass stations, remember their fuel in a max-heap, and refuel from the biggest passed station only when you run out) and "IPO" (al10).

When the greedy is wrong: test it

If you cannot find a proof in a minute or two, test. Write a brute force (try all subsets or orders for n ≤ 8) and compare on a few thousand random small inputs. A counterexample appears in seconds when the greedy is wrong, and it is usually small enough to understand why — which often suggests the right approach (dynamic programming, or a different sort key). This is not cheating in an interview; describing it ("I'd validate this greedy against brute force on small cases") is a sign of engineering maturity.

Why greedy works on some structures: matroids

There is a precise theory of when "sort by weight and take whatever keeps the set valid" is optimal. A matroid is a collection of "independent" sets with two properties: subsets of independent sets are independent, and if one independent set is larger than another, some element of the larger can be added to the smaller while keeping it independent (the exchange property). On any matroid, the greedy algorithm finds a maximum-weight independent set. The forests of a graph form a matroid, which is why Kruskal's minimum spanning tree algorithm (sort edges, add if it does not create a cycle) is correct (al20). Sets of linearly independent vectors form a matroid too. Interviews will not ask you to prove matroid properties, but knowing the concept explains why some greedy algorithms are guaranteed and why problems that lack the exchange property (knapsack, coin change in general) are not.

The adjacent-swap method for scheduling

Many single-machine scheduling problems are solved by finding an order, and the order is found by comparing adjacent jobs. To minimise total completion time, put shorter jobs first (shortest processing time): swapping a longer job ahead of a shorter one delays the short one by more than it speeds up the long one. To minimise the maximum lateness, order by deadline (earliest deadline first). To minimise weighted completion time, order by processing time divided by weight (Smith's rule). Each rule comes from the same calculation: write the cost of "a then b" and "b then a", subtract, and read off the comparator. The comparator must be transitive for a sort to be valid; ratios and differences are, but some ad hoc comparators are not, which is a subtle source of wrong answers.

Greedy as an approximation

For NP-hard problems, greedy algorithms often give guaranteed approximations. For set cover — choose the fewest sets whose union covers everything — repeatedly picking the set that covers the most uncovered elements gives a solution within a factor of about ln n of optimal, and no polynomial algorithm can do much better unless P = NP. For scheduling jobs on m identical machines to minimise the finishing time, assigning each job to the least-loaded machine (with jobs sorted longest first) is within 4/3 of optimal. In system design follow-ups, "the exact problem is hard, but greedy gives a provable approximation" is a valuable thing to be able to say.

Huffman coding: a greedy with a heap

To compress text, give frequent symbols short bit codes and rare ones long codes, with no code being a prefix of another so decoding is unambiguous. Huffman's algorithm: put every symbol in a min-heap by frequency; repeatedly pop the two least frequent, merge them into a node whose frequency is their sum, and push it back; when one node remains, it is the root of the code tree. The exchange argument: in some optimal tree the two least frequent symbols are siblings at the deepest level, so merging them first is safe. It is optimal among prefix codes and runs in O(n log n). It remains in daily use inside DEFLATE (gzip, zlib, PNG) alongside LZ77.

Interval-style greedy versus heap-style greedy

Greedy algorithms come in two implementation styles, and recognising which one a problem needs saves time. Sort-and-scan greedies decide everything from a single fixed order: activity selection, partition labels, two-city scheduling, candy. Heap-driven greedies must repeatedly find the best option among a set that changes as the scan proceeds: IPO (affordable projects grow over time), course schedule III (drop the longest taken course), meeting rooms (earliest free room), Huffman (two smallest current frequencies). If the "best" option depends on choices already made, you need a heap or a balanced tree; if it depends only on the input, a sort is enough.

When a greedy is "almost" right

Some problems are greedy only after a transformation. Jump Game II becomes greedy once you see the BFS levels. Gas Station becomes greedy once you prove the skip argument. Task Scheduler has a closed form once you think in frames of size n + 1. Conversely, some problems look greedy and are not: "minimum number of coins" (DP), "longest increasing subsequence" (DP or patience sorting with binary search), "maximum profit with at most k transactions" (DP). A good habit is to state the property your greedy relies on and ask whether the problem guarantees it; if it does not, reach for dynamic programming before coding.

In the interview room

Greedy problems are where candidates most often give a confident wrong answer. The interviewer's follow-up "why is that optimal?" is not hostile; it is the main thing being tested. Have the argument ready in one of the two shapes: "suppose an optimal solution chose something else first; swapping in my choice keeps it feasible and no worse" (exchange), or "after every step, my reach / count / finishing time is at least as good as any other strategy's" (stays ahead). If you cannot form either, say so and propose validating against brute force, or switch to dynamic programming.

The common implementation bugs are small: off-by-one in Jump Game II (looping to the last index adds an extra jump), forgetting the total-gas check in Gas Station, using the wrong comparator direction in sort keys, and forgetting edge cases like a single element or all-equal ratings. And a classic recognition mistake: applying the coin-change greedy to arbitrary coin systems, or the fractional-knapsack greedy to 0/1 items.

Interviewers also like to change one constraint to see whether the greedy survives: weighted intervals instead of unweighted, indivisible items instead of divisible ones, "at most k transactions" instead of unlimited. When the greedy-choice property breaks, say so and pivot to DP; that pivot is a strong signal.

Finally, greedy solutions are often the shortest code in the round, which can make candidates rush. Slow down for the proof and for edge cases; a five-line correct greedy with a clear argument scores better than a five-line greedy that was simply lucky on the examples.

A practical technique when stuck between greedy and DP in the room: work out the answer by hand for three or four tiny inputs using both the greedy rule and exhaustive reasoning. If they always agree and you can articulate why, commit to the greedy; if you find a disagreement, you have both a counterexample and a strong hint about which extra state the DP needs.

On online assessments, greedy problems often hide behind large constraints (n up to 10⁵ with values up to 10⁹), which rule out DP over values and point to "sort, then one pass". If a DP you are considering has a dimension of size 10⁹, look for the greedy.

In real systems

Greedy algorithms run a surprising amount of infrastructure. Huffman coding sits inside gzip, zlib and PNG via DEFLATE (RFC 1951), and canonical Huffman codes appear in JPEG and many other formats. Network routing relies on Dijkstra's greedy shortest paths (OSPF and IS-IS link-state protocols), and network design uses minimum spanning trees. Load balancers often route each request greedily to the least-loaded or least-connections backend, and "power of two choices" (pick two random backends, send to the less loaded) is a famous greedy with provably good balance. Operating-system schedulers have used shortest-job-first and earliest-deadline-first rules; cluster schedulers place tasks greedily by resource fit. Ad auctions and recommendation re-ranking often use greedy selection with diversity constraints because exact optimisation is too slow at request time. The engineering lesson matches the interview one: greedy is fast and simple, and its correctness (or approximation quality) must be argued, not assumed.

def coins_greedy(coins, amount):
    n = 0
    for c in sorted(coins, reverse=True):

def coins_dp(coins, amount):
    INF = float("inf"); dp = [0] + [INF] * amount
    for a in range(1, amount + 1):
        dp[a] = min((dp[a - c] + 1 for c in coins if c <= a), default=INF)
    return dp[amount] if dp[amount] < INF else None

def jump_greedy(nums):

    for i in range(len(nums) - 1):
        far = max(far, i + nums[i])
        if i == end: jumps += 1; end = far
    return jumps

def jump_bfs(nums):                      # brute force: shortest path
    n, dist = len(nums), {0: 0}; frontier = [0]
    while frontier:
        nxt = []
        for i in frontier:
            for j in range(i + 1, min(n, i + nums[i] + 1)):
                if j not in dist: dist[j] = dist[i] + 1; nxt.append(j)
        frontier = nxt
    return dist.get(n - 1)

for _ in range(3000):
    nums = [random.randint(1, 3) for _ in range(random.randint(1, 10))]
    assert jump_greedy(nums) == jump_bfs(nums), nums
print("Jump Game II greedy matches BFS")

Prove it or break it

For each greedy, say whether it is correct; give a proof sketch or a counterexample. (a) To minimise the number of platforms at a train station, sort trains by arrival and assign each to the platform that became free earliest. (b) To maximise the number of tasks finished before their deadlines (each task takes one unit of time), always do the task with the earliest deadline next. (c) To maximise total value in a knapsack of capacity W with indivisible items, take items in decreasing order of value until nothing fits.

Answer

(a) Correct. It is Meeting Rooms II: a new platform is opened only when every existing platform is occupied at this train's arrival, so at that moment the number of overlapping trains equals the number of platforms — no solution can use fewer (stays-ahead on platforms versus maximum overlap so far). (b) Correct, provided expired tasks are skipped. Process tasks in deadline order and schedule a task in the next free time slot only if that slot is still before its deadline; otherwise skip it. Exchange argument: if an optimal schedule does some task with a later deadline in a slot where the greedy put the earliest-deadline task, swapping them keeps both on time (the earlier-deadline task moves earlier or stays, the later one moves to a slot no later than the earlier task's deadline, which is before its own). A naive "earliest deadline next" that does not skip already-expired tasks wastes slots on them and can lose. (c) Wrong. Capacity 10, items (weight 10, value 10), (5, 6), (5, 6): greedy by value takes the 10-weight item for value 10, while the two 5-weight items give 12. Greedy by value density also fails in general (see the fractional versus 0/1 example). 0/1 knapsack needs dynamic programming over capacity (al22).
