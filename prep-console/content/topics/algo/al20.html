A delivery app knows travel times along every road segment in a city. Between the restaurant and your door there are thousands of possible routes; the app needs the fastest one, in milliseconds, for millions of orders a day. Breadth-first search (al16) will not do: it finds the route with the fewest road segments, which might be a slow crawl through the city centre rather than a longer but much faster ring road. Once edges carry weights — minutes, kilometres, costs, latencies — "shortest" means smallest total weight, and you need different algorithms.

Interviewers love weighted shortest paths because the core algorithm, Dijkstra's, is short enough to write in a round but subtle enough to reveal understanding: why it needs a priority queue, why it fails with negative weights, how to handle outdated heap entries in Python, and how to adapt it when the "length" of a path is not a sum at all — the steepest climb on a hike, the probability that a message gets through, the highest water level you must wait for. Around Dijkstra sit three relatives you should know: Bellman-Ford for negative weights and "at most k edges" constraints, Floyd-Warshall for all pairs on small graphs, and the topological-order trick for DAGs.

A neighbouring question is different but often confused with shortest paths: not the best route between two points, but the cheapest set of links connecting everything — laying cable between offices, or connecting points on a plane. That is a minimum spanning tree, built greedily by Prim's or Kruskal's algorithm.

This lesson builds the idea of relaxation, then Dijkstra with a full trace and proof sketch, its variants, Bellman-Ford and the k-stops problem, Floyd-Warshall, and spanning trees — ending with a decision table for picking the right algorithm from the weights and the question.

The mental model: water spreading through pipes of different lengths

Imagine pouring water into one junction of a network of pipes, where each pipe takes a known time to fill. The water reaches nearby junctions first and spreads outward, but not in neat rings as in BFS: a junction at the end of one long pipe may be reached later than a junction three short pipes away. If you watched and wrote down the moment the water first arrived at each junction, you would have every junction's shortest travel time from the source. Dijkstra's algorithm simulates exactly this: it always processes next the junction the water would reach soonest.

The bookkeeping uses one idea, relaxation. Each vertex has a tentative distance — the best route found so far, initially infinity (and 0 for the source). When you process a vertex u, you look at each pipe u → v of length w and ask: is going through u faster than the best route to v I know? If dist[u] + w < dist[v], update dist[v]. Every shortest-path algorithm is a strategy for which edges to relax, and in what order.

Dijkstra's rule is: always process the unprocessed vertex with the smallest tentative distance. A min-heap finds it quickly. Once a vertex is popped, its distance is final — provided no edge has a negative weight. That proviso is the most important fact in this lesson: with all weights non-negative, any route that has not been explored yet must pass through some vertex at least as far away as the one just popped, so it cannot be shorter.

Where the water analogy breaks: water cannot flow backwards in time, but a negative-weight edge is exactly that — arriving at a vertex earlier by taking a detour. Real costs can be negative (a cashback, a refund, an energy gain going downhill), and then Dijkstra's "first arrival is final" logic is wrong. Bellman-Ford, which keeps relaxing every edge until nothing changes, handles that case, at a higher cost.

It is worth fixing the cost model before going further. Each vertex is popped once in a useful way and each edge relaxed at most once per pop of its source, so the work is dominated by heap operations: about E pushes and pops, each O(log E). For a road network with half a million edges, that is roughly ten million elementary heap steps — well under a second in a compiled language and a few seconds in Python. Bellman-Ford on the same graph would be V × E, about 10¹¹: hopeless. The choice of algorithm is not a detail.

A second break: water spreads from one source to everywhere. If the question is "shortest distance between every pair", repeating Dijkstra from each vertex works, but for small dense graphs a different algorithm, Floyd-Warshall, is simpler and just as fast.

Dijkstra's algorithm with heapq

import heapq
def dijkstra(graph, src):              # graph[u] = [(v, w), ...], w >= 0
    dist = {src: 0}
    heap = [(0, src)]
    while heap:
        d, u = heapq.heappop(heap)
        if d > dist[u]:
            continue                   # stale entry: a shorter path was found later
        for v, w in graph[u]:
            nd = d + w
            if nd < dist.get(v, float("inf")):
                dist[v] = nd           # relax
                heapq.heappush(heap, (nd, v))
    return dist

Python's heapq has no "decrease priority" operation, so when a vertex's distance improves, the code simply pushes a new entry. The old entry stays in the heap; when it is eventually popped, its distance is larger than the recorded best, and the d > dist[u] check skips it. This is lazy deletion. The heap can hold up to E entries, so each operation is O(log E), and since E ≤ V², log E ≤ 2 log V: the total is O(E log V).

Trace the figure's graph from S. The heap starts with (0, S). Pop S: relax A to 4 and B to 1; heap [(1, B), (4, A)]. Pop B (1): relax A to 1 + 2 = 3 (better than 4; push (3, A)) and C to 1 + 5 = 6; heap [(3, A), (4, A), (6, C)]. Pop A (3): relax C to 3 + 1 = 4 (push). Pop (4, A): stale (4 > 3), skip. Pop C (4): relax D to 7. Pop (6, C): stale. Pop D (7). Final distances: S 0, B 1, A 3, C 4, D 7.

To recover the actual route, record parent[v] = u whenever you relax, and walk back from the destination. If you only need the distance to one target, you may stop as soon as it is popped — that is when it becomes final.

Network Delay Time is Dijkstra from the source, then the maximum of the distances (or −1 if some node is missing): the last node to receive the signal determines when everyone has it.

Why it works, in one paragraph

Claim: when u is popped (and not stale), dist[u] is the true shortest distance. Suppose not: some path P to u is shorter. P starts at the source, which is finalised, and ends at u, which is not yet; let y be the first vertex on P that is not finalised, and x the vertex just before it, which is. When x was finalised, the edge x → y was relaxed, so dist[y] ≤ dist[x] + w(x, y), which is the length of P up to y. Because all weights are non-negative, that prefix is no longer than P itself, which is shorter than dist[u]. So dist[y] < dist[u], and y would have been popped before u. Contradiction. The only place non-negativity is used is "a prefix of a path is no longer than the path" — exactly what a negative edge violates.

Variants: when the path cost is not a sum

Dijkstra works for any path cost that (1) is built edge by edge and (2) never gets better when the path is extended. That covers several interview favourites.

  • Path With Minimum Effort (grid, effort = the largest height difference between consecutive cells): cost of extending is max(cost, |h[u] − h[v]|). The maximum never decreases, so it qualifies.
  • Swim in Rising Water (earliest time t such that a path exists using only cells with elevation ≤ t): cost is max(cost, elevation[v]) — a minimax path.
  • Path with Maximum Probability: cost is the product of edge probabilities, which never increases because each factor is at most 1. Use a max-heap (push negated values), or transform with −log so products become sums of non-negative numbers.

Minimax problems also have two other standard solutions — binary search on the threshold with a BFS reachability check (al7), and adding edges in increasing weight order with union-find until source and target connect (al19) — which are worth mentioning as alternatives.

Bellman-Ford: negative weights and hop limits

Bellman-Ford makes no assumptions about weights. It relaxes every edge, and repeats V − 1 times. After round i, every vertex whose shortest path uses at most i edges has its correct distance — because a shortest path in a graph without negative cycles is simple (at most V − 1 edges), V − 1 rounds suffice. If a V-th round still improves something, there is a negative cycle reachable from the source, and shortest paths are undefined (you could loop forever, getting cheaper).

def bellman_ford(n, edges, src):           # edges: (u, v, w), any sign
    INF = float("inf")
    dist = [INF] * n
    dist[src] = 0
    for _ in range(n - 1):
        changed = False
        for u, v, w in edges:
            if dist[u] + w < dist[v]:
                dist[v] = dist[u] + w; changed = True
        if not changed: break              # already converged
    for u, v, w in edges:
        if dist[u] + w < dist[v]:
            return None                    # negative cycle reachable
    return dist

O(V·E): much slower than Dijkstra, but general. The same round structure solves Cheapest Flights Within K Stops, where the limit is on the number of edges (k stops = at most k + 1 flights). Run exactly k + 1 rounds, and in each round relax using a copy of the previous round's distances:

def find_cheapest_price(n, flights, src, dst, k):
    INF = float("inf")
    dist = [INF] * n; dist[src] = 0
    for _ in range(k + 1):
        prev = dist[:]                     # only use paths from the last round
        for u, v, w in flights:
            if prev[u] + w < dist[v]:
                dist[v] = prev[u] + w
    return -1 if dist[dst] == INF else dist[dst]

Without the copy, relaxing edges in a lucky order could chain several flights within one round and exceed the stop limit. With it, round i extends paths by exactly one more flight. This problem is a classic trap for plain Dijkstra: the cheapest route to an intermediate city might use too many stops, while a slightly more expensive route with fewer stops is the one that leads to a valid answer. If you prefer Dijkstra, the state must be (city, stops used).

Floyd-Warshall: all pairs on small graphs

When you need distances between every pair and V is small (a few hundred), Floyd-Warshall is the simplest option. Start with dist[i][j] = edge weight (∞ if none, 0 on the diagonal). Then for each vertex k, allow it as an intermediate stop: for every pair (i, j), if going i → k → j is shorter, take it.

def floyd_warshall(n, edges):
    INF = float("inf")
    d = [[0 if i == j else INF for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]
    for u, v, w in edges:
        d[u][v] = min(d[u][v], w)
    for k in range(n):                     # k must be the OUTER loop
        for i in range(n):
            for j in range(n):
                if d[i][k] + d[k][j] < d[i][j]:
                    d[i][j] = d[i][k] + d[k][j]
    return d

After processing k, d[i][j] is the shortest path using only vertices 0..k as intermediates; after all k, it is the true shortest distance. The order of loops matters: k outermost. O(V³) time, O(V²) memory; it handles negative edges, and a negative value on the diagonal reveals a negative cycle. "Find the City With the Smallest Number of Neighbours at a Threshold Distance" (n ≤ 100) is a direct application: 10⁶ steps.

Minimum spanning trees: Prim and Kruskal

A minimum spanning tree picks V − 1 edges that connect every vertex with the smallest total weight. It answers "the cheapest way to connect everything", not "the shortest route between two vertices". Two greedy algorithms build it, both justified by the cut property: for any split of the vertices into two groups, the lightest edge crossing the split belongs to some MST.

Kruskal (al19): sort edges by weight; add each edge that joins two different components (union-find); stop at V − 1 edges. O(E log E).

Prim: grow one tree from any start vertex; repeatedly add the cheapest edge that connects the tree to a vertex outside it. With a heap it looks almost exactly like Dijkstra — the difference is the priority: the edge weight alone, not the distance from the source.

def prim(n, graph):                        # graph[u] = [(v, w), ...], undirected
    in_tree, total = [False] * n, 0
    heap = [(0, 0)]                        # (edge weight, vertex)
    while heap:
        w, u = heapq.heappop(heap)
        if in_tree[u]: continue
        in_tree[u] = True; total += w
        for v, wv in graph[u]:
            if not in_tree[v]:
                heapq.heappush(heap, (wv, v))
    return total if all(in_tree) else None   # None: graph not connected

For dense graphs such as "Min Cost to Connect All Points" (every pair of points is an edge, so E ≈ V²/2), an array-based Prim — keep best[v], the cheapest edge from the tree to each outside vertex, and scan for the minimum each step — runs in O(V²) without storing any edges, beating both heap Prim and Kruskal's sort.

Multi-source and reverse searches

Two small tricks extend Dijkstra cheaply. Multi-source: to find each vertex's distance to the nearest of several sources (the closest hospital, the nearest charging station), push every source with distance 0 at the start; one run gives the answer for all vertices, exactly like multi-source BFS on grids. Reverse graph: to find every vertex's distance to a single target in a directed graph (how long from each house to the fire station), reverse every edge and run Dijkstra from the target. Combining both directions answers "shortest path from s to t that passes through some vertex in a set": run forward from s and backward from t, then minimise d_s(v) + d_t(v) over the set.

A*: Dijkstra with a compass

When you need one target and have a good estimate of the remaining distance, A* orders the heap by g + h, where g is the distance so far and h a heuristic estimate to the target. If h never overestimates (admissible — for example, straight-line distance on a road map), the first time the target is popped its distance is optimal; if h is also consistent (h(u) ≤ w(u, v) + h(v)), each vertex is popped at most once, exactly like Dijkstra. With h = 0, A* is Dijkstra. A good heuristic focuses the search towards the target and can cut explored vertices by orders of magnitude, which is why games and robots use it. In interviews, "A* is Dijkstra plus an admissible heuristic" and an example heuristic is the expected depth.

Negative edges: Johnson's algorithm and the trouble with SPFA

For all pairs on a sparse graph with negative edges, Floyd-Warshall's O(V³) may be too slow and V runs of Bellman-Ford are worse. Johnson's algorithm runs Bellman-Ford once from a new virtual vertex connected to all others with weight 0, obtaining potentials h(v), then reweights every edge as w'(u, v) = w(u, v) + h(u) − h(v), which is non-negative and preserves shortest paths. Then it runs Dijkstra from every vertex: O(V·E log V). A popular Bellman-Ford optimisation, SPFA (only re-relax edges out of vertices whose distance changed, using a queue), is fast on random graphs but has the same O(V·E) worst case and can be forced into it by crafted inputs; competitive programmers learned this the hard way. Mentioning that you would not rely on SPFA for adversarial input is a sign of depth.

State-expanded graphs

Many hard shortest-path problems become ordinary Dijkstra on a bigger graph whose vertices carry extra information: (city, stops used) for flights with a stop limit, (cell, fuel left) for refuelling, (node, discount coupons left) for "you may halve one edge's cost", (node, time mod k) for periodic schedules. The cost is that the graph has V × (states) vertices. The modelling skill — "what must I know about my situation to decide the future?" — is the same one used for BFS with state on grids (al17) and for dynamic programming states (al21).

Road networks at scale

Plain Dijkstra on a continental road network (tens of millions of vertices) takes seconds per query — too slow for a navigation app. Production routing engines preprocess the graph. Contraction hierarchies order vertices by importance and add "shortcut" edges that skip unimportant ones; a bidirectional search that only climbs the hierarchy then answers queries in about a millisecond. Other techniques include arc flags, hub labelling and customisable route planning, which separates the graph's structure (preprocessed once) from its weights (updated with live traffic). OSRM and GraphHopper, two open-source routing engines, implement contraction hierarchies. The interview-level takeaway: Dijkstra is the core, bidirectional search halves the work, and preprocessing makes repeated queries fast.

Bidirectional Dijkstra

Running Dijkstra simultaneously from the source (forward) and the target (on the reversed graph, backward), alternating steps, explores roughly two discs of radius d/2 instead of one of radius d — about half the work on road-like graphs. The stopping rule is subtle: stop when the sum of the two heaps' minimum keys is at least the best s–t path found so far (tracked by checking, on every relaxation, whether the vertex has been reached from the other side), not merely when the searches first touch. Getting this wrong returns a slightly suboptimal path, which is a classic implementation bug.

Why the greedy MST algorithms are right

Both Prim and Kruskal rely on the cut property: take any partition of the vertices into two non-empty sets S and V − S, and let e be the lightest edge crossing it. Suppose an MST T does not contain e. Adding e to T creates a cycle, which must cross the cut at some other edge f with weight at least w(e). Replacing f with e gives a spanning tree no heavier than T, so some MST contains e. Prim applies this to the cut between the current tree and the rest; Kruskal to the cut between the two components an edge joins. The companion cycle property — the heaviest edge on any cycle is in no MST (if weights are distinct) — justifies discarding edges, and underlies the critical/pseudo-critical edge problem.

In the interview room

Dijkstra is a standard "write it from scratch" question, so practise the twelve lines until they are automatic. The common bugs: forgetting the stale-entry check (correct output, but much slower, and wrong if you also do work per pop); marking a vertex visited when it is pushed (that is BFS logic and produces wrong distances in weighted graphs); using a max-heap by accident; pushing (vertex, distance) instead of (distance, vertex), so the heap orders by vertex id; and not handling unreachable vertices (missing keys versus infinity). For Bellman-Ford with a hop limit, the classic bug is relaxing in place instead of from a copy. For Floyd-Warshall, putting the k loop inside.

Interviewers almost always ask "why does Dijkstra fail with negative weights?" — have the one-sentence answer ready (a popped vertex is assumed final, and a later negative edge can undercut it) plus a tiny counterexample: S→A weight 2, S→B weight 5, B→A weight −4; Dijkstra finalises A at 2, but the true distance is 1. Also expect "how would you speed this up for a map app?" (bidirectional search, A* with straight-line heuristic, preprocessing such as contraction hierarchies) and "what if you need the k shortest paths?" (Yen's algorithm, or Dijkstra allowing each vertex to be popped k times).

When the problem is a disguised shortest path — minimum effort, maximum probability, rising water, stops limits — say explicitly what the vertices are, what the edge cost is, and why the cost never improves when a path is extended. That justification is what separates a memorised template from understanding.

On online assessments, shortest-path problems are often stated as costs of tickets, toll roads, or network latencies with additional rules ("at most k transfers", "one discount coupon", "some roads are one-way"). Translating each rule into either a modified edge cost or an extra state dimension is the main work; the algorithm itself is usually plain Dijkstra on the transformed graph.

In real systems

Shortest-path algorithms run the internet and the roads. Link-state routing protocols such as OSPF and IS-IS flood the network topology to every router, and each router runs Dijkstra to build its forwarding table — RFC 2328 specifies the procedure. Distance-vector protocols such as RIP are distributed Bellman-Ford: routers repeatedly share their distance estimates with neighbours, which is why they converge slowly and suffer the "count to infinity" problem when links fail. Navigation services run Dijkstra-derived searches over road graphs with preprocessing (contraction hierarchies in OSRM and GraphHopper) and live traffic weights. Game engines path-find with A*. Logistics companies compute delivery routes on top of shortest-path distance matrices. Minimum spanning trees appear in network design (laying fibre or power lines), in clustering (single-linkage), and as approximations for harder problems such as the travelling salesman problem, where an MST gives a 2-approximation for metric distances.

Model and choose

A ride-hailing company models a city as 2 × 10⁵ intersections and 5 × 10⁵ road segments with travel times. (a) For one rider, find the fastest route from pickup to drop-off. (b) A promotion gives a free ride on at most one segment (its time counts as zero); find the fastest route with the coupon. (c) Each night, compute the travel time between all 300 depots. (d) Design the cheapest set of fibre links to connect the 300 depots given candidate link costs.

Answer

(a) Dijkstra from the pickup with early exit when the drop-off is popped: O(E log V) ≈ 5 × 10⁵ × 18 ≈ 10⁷ heap operations. For production, bidirectional Dijkstra or A* with a straight-line-distance heuristic (divided by the maximum speed so it never overestimates), and preprocessing like contraction hierarchies for millisecond queries. (b) State-expanded Dijkstra over (intersection, coupon_used ∈ {0, 1}): from state (u, 0) an edge can be taken normally to (v, 0) or for free to (v, 1); from (u, 1) only normally to (v, 1). The answer is min(dist[(t, 0)], dist[(t, 1)]). The graph doubles: still O(E log V). (c) 300 runs of Dijkstra, one per depot, O(300 · E log V) ≈ 3 × 10⁹ — heavy but feasible overnight in a compiled language; Floyd-Warshall would be O(V³) on the full 2 × 10⁵-vertex graph (far too slow), so it only applies if you first reduce to a 300 × 300 matrix, which you cannot do without the Dijkstra runs. (d) Minimum spanning tree over the depots with candidate link costs: Kruskal with union-find if candidates are a sparse list, or array-based Prim in O(300²) if every pair is a candidate. Note that (c) and (d) are different questions — travel times are shortest paths, the fibre network is an MST.
