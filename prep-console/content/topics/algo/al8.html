A code editor checks brackets as you type. You write foo(bar[1], {x: (2 and it underlines the line, because the parenthesis you most recently opened has not been closed. To decide whether a closing bracket is legal, the editor only cares about one thing: which bracket was opened most recently and is still open. Once that bracket is closed, the one opened before it becomes the most recent. That "most recent unfinished thing first" order is exactly what a stack provides: you add (push) and remove (pop) only at the top.

That order shows up in a surprising number of interview problems. Matching brackets, evaluating arithmetic, decoding nested strings like 3[a2[c]], undoing operations, simplifying file paths, and every recursive algorithm (the call stack is a stack) all resolve the most recent open item first.

A second, cleverer use is the monotonic stack. "For each day, how many days until a warmer one?" has a brute force that, for every day, scans forward — O(n²) on a year of hourly readings. But think about which days are still waiting for an answer as you scan: they form a decreasing sequence of temperatures, and when a warm day arrives it answers the most recent waiting days first. That is a stack again, and keeping it ordered gives an O(n) solution to a whole family: next greater element, previous smaller element, stock spans, the largest rectangle in a histogram, trapping rain water, and sums over all subarrays.

This lesson covers both halves: the classic stack problems (matching, evaluation, nesting, min stack) and the monotonic stack, including the two hard classics interviewers love — Largest Rectangle in Histogram and Sum of Subarray Minimums — with full traces and the reasoning that makes each step safe.

The mental model: a stack of plates, and people looking along a line

A stack is a pile of plates: you put a plate on top, and you take the top plate off. You never pull one from the middle. That is LIFO — last in, first out. In Python, a list is a stack: append pushes, pop removes the top, and stack[-1] peeks. All three are O(1).

For the monotonic stack, a different picture helps. Imagine people standing in a line, all facing right, each wanting to know who is the first taller person ahead of them. Walk along the line from left to right, keeping a group of "people still waiting for an answer". When a new person arrives, everyone waiting who is shorter than the newcomer has their answer — it is the newcomer — and leaves the group. Who leaves first? The most recent waiters, because they are standing right next to the newcomer. And the group that remains is always ordered from tallest (waited longest) to shortest (most recent): whenever someone joins, everyone shorter has just left, so the newcomer is the shortest member. The group is a stack, sorted by height.

Two facts make this fast. Each day joins the waiting group once and leaves it at most once, so the total work is O(n). And once a day leaves, it is never needed again, because its question has been answered. The stack holds only the candidates that could still matter, kept in sorted (monotonic) order.

Where the analogy breaks: real people looking along a line see over shorter people, so "who is the first taller person ahead" is answered by eyesight in parallel. The algorithm has to discover the answer by scanning, and the order in which answers are discovered — most recent first — is what forces the stack. If the question were "the first taller person ahead that arrived earliest", the structure would be different. Always check that "most recent unresolved first" matches the question before reaching for a stack.

It is worth seeing what the stack saves. The brute force for "next warmer day" scans forward from every day, so a year of hourly data (8,760 readings) on a long cooling trend costs tens of millions of comparisons, and a million readings would cost on the order of 10¹¹. The stack version does at most two operations per reading — one push and one pop — so a million readings take a couple of million steps. The answers are identical; the stack simply refuses to compare a day against readings that can no longer be its answer.

There is also a simple test for whether a problem is a stack problem at all: if you processed the items by hand, would you ever need to "go back" to the item you saw most recently and finish it? Brackets, nested decoding, waiting days and histogram bars all pass that test. If instead you need the oldest item first (tasks in arrival order), you want a queue, and if you need the smallest or largest item regardless of when it arrived, you want a heap (al10).

Matching: valid parentheses

def is_valid(s):
    pairs = {")": "(", "]": "[", "}": "{"}
    stack = []
    for ch in s:
        if ch in "([{":
            stack.append(ch)
        else:
            if not stack or stack[-1] != pairs[ch]:
                return False          # closer with no matching opener
            stack.pop()
    return not stack                  # leftover openers are unmatched

Three failure conditions, and the code checks each: a closer arriving with an empty stack ("())"), a closer that does not match the most recent opener ("([)]"), and openers left over at the end ("(("). A counter is enough when there is one bracket type, but "([)]" shows why several types need the stack: counts are balanced, order is not.

Variations follow the same shape. "Minimum additions to make valid" counts unmatched closers during the scan plus leftover openers at the end. "Longest valid parentheses" pushes indices instead of characters, with a sentinel index −1 at the bottom marking the position before the current valid run; the length of a valid run is i − stack[-1].

Evaluation: postfix and calculators

Postfix notation (Reverse Polish notation) writes the operator after its operands: 2 1 + 3 * means (2 + 1) × 3. It needs no brackets and evaluates with one stack: push numbers; on an operator, pop two, apply, push the result.

def eval_rpn(tokens):
    st = []
    for t in tokens:
        if t in "+-*/":
            b, a = st.pop(), st.pop()          # right operand pops first
            if t == "+": st.append(a + b)
            elif t == "-": st.append(a - b)
            elif t == "*": st.append(a * b)
            else: st.append(int(a / b))        # truncate toward zero
        else:
            st.append(int(t))
    return st[0]

Two details catch people: the right operand is popped first, so a − b must use them in the reverse pop order; and many problems specify truncation toward zero, which in Python is int(a / b), not a // b (floor division rounds −7 // 2 to −4).

For ordinary infix expressions with + and − and parentheses (Basic Calculator), you do not need full parsing. Keep a running result, the current number being read, and the current sign. On "+" or "−", fold sign * number into the result and set the new sign. On "(", push the outer (result, sign) and start fresh; on ")", finish the inner result, pop the outer pair, and combine: result = outer_result + outer_sign * inner_result. The stack depth equals the bracket nesting depth. Adding * and / with precedence uses the classic pattern of pushing signed terms and multiplying or dividing the top term immediately when * or / appears, then summing the stack at the end.

Nesting: decoding strings

"Decode 3[a2[c]]" to accaccacc. Every "[" opens a new context that must be finished before the outer one continues — the stack pattern. Push the outer context (the string built so far and the repeat count) on "[", and restore it on "]".

def decode_string(s):
    stack, cur, num = [], "", 0
    for ch in s:
        if ch.isdigit():
            num = num * 10 + int(ch)            # multi-digit counts
        elif ch == "[":
            stack.append((cur, num)); cur, num = "", 0
        elif ch == "]":
            prev, k = stack.pop()
            cur = prev + cur * k
        else:
            cur += ch
    return cur

Trace 3[a2[c]]: read 3; "[" pushes ("", 3); "a" → cur = "a"; read 2; "[" pushes ("a", 2), cur = ""; "c" → cur = "c"; "]" pops ("a", 2) → cur = "a" + "cc" = "acc"; "]" pops ("", 3) → cur = "accaccacc". The same push-context, pop-and-combine shape solves "simplify a Unix path" (push directory names, pop on ".."), "parse nested lists", and "count atoms in a chemical formula".

Min stack

"Design a stack with push, pop, top and getMin all in O(1)." The minimum changes only on push and pop, and the stack's history is exactly what pop restores. So store, with each element, the minimum of the stack at the moment it was pushed:

class MinStack:
    def __init__(self): self.st = []                 # (value, min_so_far)
    def push(self, x):
        m = x if not self.st else min(x, self.st[-1][1])
        self.st.append((x, m))
    def pop(self): self.st.pop()
    def top(self): return self.st[-1][0]
    def getMin(self): return self.st[-1][1]

Popping automatically "restores" the previous minimum, because the element below carries the minimum as it was before. This idea — store a summary of everything below with each stack entry — also gives O(1) max stacks and is the basis of the two-stack queue with O(1) minimum.

The monotonic stack template: next greater element

For each index, find the next index to the right with a strictly greater value. Keep a stack of indices whose values are decreasing from bottom to top. These are the indices still waiting for an answer.

def next_greater(a):
    ans = [-1] * len(a)
    st = []                                  # indices, values decreasing
    for i, x in enumerate(a):
        while st and a[st[-1]] < x:          # x answers every smaller waiter
            ans[st.pop()] = i
        st.append(i)
    return ans                               # -1: no greater element

Why the stack stays decreasing: we only push i after popping everything smaller than a[i], so a[i] is at most everything below it. Why popped elements are safe to discard: their answer has just been found, and the first greater element to the right is unique. Why it is O(n): each index is pushed once and popped at most once.

Spans and fleets: absorbing what you pop

Online Stock Span asks, for each new price, how many consecutive days up to and including today had a price at most today's. Keep a stack of (price, span). For a new price, pop every entry with price ≤ today's and add its span to today's span; then push (price, span). A popped day can never stop a future span, because today is at least as high and more recent — so it is safe to absorb it. Amortised O(1) per day.

Car Fleet: cars at different positions heading to a target, each with a speed; a faster car that catches a slower one ahead must slow down and they form a fleet. Sort by position, closest to the target first, and compute each car's arrival time if unobstructed. Walking from the front, a car whose arrival time is no greater than the fleet in front catches up and merges; otherwise it starts a new fleet. A stack of fleet times (always increasing from front to back) makes this explicit; its size is the answer.

Largest Rectangle in Histogram

Given bar heights, find the largest rectangle that fits under the histogram. For any bar used as the rectangle's height, the rectangle extends left and right until it meets a lower bar. So the answer is max over i of heights[i] × (next smaller index − previous smaller index − 1). A monotonic stack finds both boundaries in one pass: keep indices with increasing heights; when a lower bar arrives at index i, it is the next smaller bar for every taller bar on the stack, and after popping one, the new stack top is that bar's previous smaller bar.

def largest_rectangle(h):
    st, best = [], 0
    for i, x in enumerate(h + [0]):            # sentinel flushes the stack
        while st and h[st[-1]] > x:
            height = h[st.pop()]
            left = st[-1] if st else -1         # previous smaller index
            best = max(best, height * (i - left - 1))
        st.append(i)
    return best

The sentinel 0 at the end forces every remaining bar to be popped and measured, so no special code is needed after the loop. "Maximal Rectangle" in a binary matrix reduces to this: for each row, build heights[c] = number of consecutive 1s ending at this row in column c, and run the histogram algorithm. O(R·C).

Contribution counting: Sum of Subarray Minimums

"Return the sum of min(sub) over every contiguous subarray." There are O(n²) subarrays. Turn the sum around: instead of asking "what is each subarray's minimum?", ask "for each element, in how many subarrays is it the minimum?". Element i is the minimum of every subarray that starts after its previous smaller element and ends before its next smaller element. If left[i] is the number of choices for the start and right[i] the number of choices for the end, then a[i] contributes a[i] × left[i] × right[i].

Duplicates need care. In [2, 2], the subarray [2, 2] has two equal minimums; if both claim it, it is counted twice. The fix is to break ties on one side only: use "previous strictly smaller" for the left boundary and "next smaller or equal" for the right. Then a subarray with several equal minimums is credited only to the leftmost of them. Both boundaries come from monotonic stacks in O(n), so the whole sum is O(n). The same technique gives the sum of subarray maximums, and their difference gives "sum of subarray ranges".

Greedy with a monotonic stack: Remove K Digits

"Remove k digits from the number '1432219' to make it as small as possible." The leftmost digits matter most. Scanning left to right, if the current digit is smaller than the previous kept digit, removing the previous one makes the number smaller (a larger digit in a more significant position is replaced). Keep an increasing stack: while k > 0 and the top is greater than the current digit, pop and decrement k; then push. Leftover k removes from the end (the tail is increasing, so its last digits are the largest). For 1432219, k = 3: keep 1; 4; 3 pops 4; 2 pops 3; 2; 1 pops 2; 9 — result 1219. Strip leading zeros, and return "0" if nothing remains.

Recognising the pattern

Use a plain stack when the problem has nesting or "most recent unmatched": brackets, expressions, decoding, paths, undo, or converting recursion to iteration. Use a monotonic stack when the question is about the nearest greater or smaller element in some direction, spans or "how far until", rectangles bounded by the shortest bar, or sums over all subarrays of a min or max. Decide the comparison for equal values before coding.

Why popped elements are never needed again

Every monotonic-stack solution rests on one of two arguments for why a popped element is never needed again. The first is answered: in next greater element, when a new element x pops an index j, x is the first element to the right of j that is greater, so j's question is settled and it leaves for good. The second is dominated: for problems where elements are popped not because they are answered but because something better has arrived — the sliding-window maximum deque, stock span, remove-k-digits — the argument is: the newer element is at least as good for every future query (larger and later), so the older one can never be the answer again. Stating which of the two arguments applies ("answered" or "dominated") is the cleanest way to justify the algorithm in an interview.

Strict versus non-strict comparisons

With distinct values, < and ≤ give identical results. With duplicates they give different, well-defined answers: popping on a[top] < x finds the next strictly greater element, while popping on a[top] <= x finds the next greater-or-equal. For the histogram, either works, because equal bars yield the same maximal rectangle (one of them computes it correctly, the other a smaller one, and the maximum is unaffected). For contribution counting, the choice is load-bearing: exactly one side must be strict so that each subarray is assigned to exactly one minimum. When an interviewer asks "what about duplicates?", this is the answer they want.

Trapping rain water, the stack way

Al5 solved Trapping Rain Water with two pointers by computing water column by column. The monotonic stack computes it layer by layer: keep a decreasing stack of bar indices; when a taller bar arrives at i, pop the top j (the bottom of a basin); the new top k is the left wall; the water above j fills a rectangle of width i − k − 1 and height min(h[k], h[i]) − h[j]. Each pop adds one horizontal slab. Both methods are O(n); the stack version generalises to questions about the basins themselves (how many, their widths), which the two-pointer version cannot answer.

Cartesian trees and range minimum queries

The previous-smaller and next-smaller relationships define a tree: each element's parent is the larger of its previous-smaller and next-smaller neighbours. This is the Cartesian tree of the array, and the monotonic stack builds it in O(n). Its root is the minimum, and the minimum of any range a[l..r] is the lowest common ancestor of l and r in the tree. That connection is how theoretical range-minimum-query structures achieve O(n) preprocessing and O(1) queries, and it explains why so many range-min problems (histogram, subarray minimums) have stack solutions: they are traversing the Cartesian tree implicitly.

The shunting-yard algorithm

Full infix expressions with precedence (2 + 3 × 4) and brackets are parsed with Dijkstra's shunting-yard algorithm (1961): an output queue and an operator stack. Numbers go to the output. An operator pops operators of higher (or equal, for left-associative) precedence from the stack to the output before being pushed. "(" is pushed; ")" pops to the output until the matching "(". At the end, the remaining operators are popped. The output is postfix, which the RPN evaluator above computes. Knowing this exists, and that "Basic Calculator II/III" are simplified versions of it, is enough for interviews; writing it fully takes about 25 lines.

Two stacks make a queue

A queue (first in, first out) can be built from two stacks: push onto an "in" stack; to pop, if the "out" stack is empty, move everything from "in" to "out" (reversing the order), then pop from "out". Each element is moved at most once, so every operation is amortised O(1). Combined with the min-stack trick, this gives a queue with O(1) minimum — another route to the sliding-window minimum. It is a common warm-up question and a neat demonstration of amortised reasoning.

Stacks and recursion

Every recursive function runs on the call stack, which pushes a frame (arguments, local variables, return address) per call and pops it on return. Converting recursion to iteration means managing that stack yourself: push the arguments of pending calls and loop while the stack is non-empty. It matters in Python because the default recursion limit is 1,000 frames: a recursive depth-first search on a 10⁵-node path crashes, an iterative one does not. When post-order work is needed (do something after children finish), push a marker or a (node, state) pair so the node is revisited after its children — the same trick as the "enter/exit" events in iterative tree traversals (al14).

In the interview room

Stack problems are judged on edge cases and invariants. The common failures: popping from an empty stack (a closer before any opener, an operator with too few operands); forgetting the leftover stack at the end (unmatched openers, bars never popped — which the sentinel fixes); getting operand order wrong for − and /; using floor division where truncation is required; and, for monotonic stacks, storing values when the problem needs distances (store indices — you can always read the value from the array). Test with an empty input, a single element, all equal values, strictly increasing and strictly decreasing inputs; increasing and decreasing are the two extremes of stack behaviour (nothing popped until the end versus everything popped immediately).

Interviewers almost always ask about complexity because of the nested loop: answer with the push-once-pop-once argument in one sentence. For the histogram, expect "walk me through why the width is i − stack[-1] − 1", and for Sum of Subarray Minimums, "how do you handle duplicates?". Having those explanations ready is what makes a hard problem look comfortable.

A good habit is to say which stack you are building before coding: "a stack of indices with strictly decreasing values, popped when a warmer day arrives; each pop answers the popped day". That single sentence fixes the content (indices), the order (decreasing), the comparison (strict), and the meaning of a pop (answered) — the four decisions that otherwise get made inconsistently in the middle of the code.

Online assessments often wrap monotonic stacks in a story — "for each building, how many buildings to its right can it see?", "for each order, the next order with a higher price", "the largest billboard that fits under a skyline". The tell is the phrase "the next / previous / nearest ... larger / smaller", or a request to sum something over every contiguous range. Translate the story into "nearest greater or smaller in a direction" and pick the variant.

In real systems

Stacks are built into how computers run programs. The call stack holds one frame per active function call, which is why infinite recursion ends in a stack overflow (in Python, a RecursionError when the interpreter's limit is reached). CPython and the Java virtual machine are stack machines: bytecode instructions push and pop operands on an evaluation stack — dis.dis on any Python function shows instructions like LOAD_FAST (push) and BINARY_OP (pop two, push one), the same pattern as the RPN evaluator. Compilers and interpreters parse expressions and nested blocks with stacks (shunting-yard, or recursive descent, which uses the call stack). Editors and design tools implement undo and redo as two stacks: an action pushes onto undo; undoing pops it and pushes onto redo; any new action clears redo. Browsers keep back and forward history the same way. And monotonic-stack logic appears in financial software computing spans and running highs, and in skyline and layout algorithms that need "the nearest taller element".

def summin_brute(a):
    return sum(min(a[i:j+1]) for i in range(len(a)) for j in range(i, len(a)))

def summin_stack(a, strict_left=True):
    n = len(a); left = [0]*n; right = [0]*n; st = []
    for i in range(n):                      # previous strictly smaller
        while st and (a[st[-1]] >= a[i] if strict_left else a[st[-1]] > a[i]):

        right[i] = (st[-1] if st else n) - i; st.append(i)
    return sum(a[i] * left[i] * right[i] for i in range(n))

Explain a hard one cleanly

An interviewer gives you Largest Rectangle in Histogram, accepts your O(n) stack solution, and asks: "Prove that every candidate rectangle that could be the answer is measured at some point, and tell me what changes if bars can have height 0."

Answer

The optimal rectangle has some height H, which must equal the height of its shortest bar, say bar j (otherwise it could be made taller). Its left edge must be just after the previous bar lower than h[j], and its right edge just before the next bar lower than h[j] — otherwise it could be extended. So the optimum is "bar j's maximal rectangle" for some j, and it suffices to measure the maximal rectangle of every bar. The algorithm does exactly that: bar j is popped when the first lower bar to its right arrives at index i (its right boundary), and after popping, the stack top is the nearest bar to its left that is lower or equal (the stack holds increasing heights, so everything between that top and j was popped earlier by j or by bars taller than j, which means it is at least h[j] tall). The width i − top − 1 is therefore j's maximal width. With equal heights, one of the equal bars may measure a slightly short width, but the other measures the full one, so the maximum is still correct. The sentinel 0 makes sure every bar is popped. Height-0 bars need no change: they pop everything taller, contribute area 0 themselves, and act as natural separators.
