In January 2021 a security researcher at Pen Test Partners noticed that Peloton's API would answer questions about any rider. Ask the class-statistics endpoint about a workout and it returned the riders' user IDs, ages, genders, cities, weights and workout statistics — including riders who had set their profiles to private. The requests did not even need a login. He reported it on 20 January. On 2 February Peloton shipped a quiet fix: the endpoint now required authentication. The researcher's write-up has the punchline: now only the roughly three million authenticated Peloton members could see the data. Any member could still read any other member's private details.

That second state is the one worth staring at, because it is the most common shape of real access-control failure. Somebody was asked "is this endpoint protected?" and answered honestly "yes, it requires login". They were answering a different question from the one that mattered. Login answers who are you. Nobody had written the code that answers are you allowed to see this particular rider. The fix that eventually landed, in May after a journalist got involved, was that missing decision.

Every senior system-design interview reaches this point. You have drawn the load balancer, the services, the databases and the queues, and the interviewer asks: "And how do users, services and tenants authenticate and get authorised?" A weak answer says "we use JWTs" or "OAuth" and stops. A strong answer names the principals in the system, says how each one proves who it is, where each decision is made, what is logged, and what an attacker would try first. This track builds that answer from the ground up, and this first topic gives you the vocabulary and the map that the other eleven hang on.

We'll separate the three jobs of access control — authentication, authorisation and accounting — and see why they fail independently. We'll meet the kinds of identity a modern system has to handle: people, services, machines and now AI agents. We'll build a small threat model for an auth system, look at assurance levels, and then place each check in an architecture: what the gateway can decide, what only the service can decide, and what the database can enforce as a last line. Throughout, we'll use this app — the Prep Console you are reading — as a real, local example, since it signs people in with Google and GitHub and keeps every learner's progress separate.

The office building

Think of an office building. At the front desk a receptionist checks your ID card against your face and gives you a visitor badge. That is authentication: establishing who you are by checking evidence. From then on nobody looks at your ID card again; they look at the badge. The badge is a session — a cheaper token that stands in for the original proof for the rest of the day.

Next, you try to open a door on the fourth floor. The badge reader doesn't care who you are in the abstract; it asks whether this badge may open this door at this time. That is authorisation. The building can have a perfect front desk and still be wide open if every badge opens every door. That's Peloton: the front desk was added, the doors were never fitted with readers.

Finally, every swipe is written down: badge 4471 opened door 4B at 14:02, badge 4471 was refused at the server room at 14:05. That is accounting, the third A. It doesn't stop anything by itself, but it is how security notices that badge 4471 tried forty doors in a minute, how a manager answers "who was in the server room on Tuesday?", and how, after a theft, you work out exactly what was taken. The three together are often called AAA, a name that comes from network-access servers such as RADIUS, which logged dial-up users in, decided what they could reach, and counted their minutes for billing.

The analogy breaks in two useful places. First, in software the "badge" is usually just a string of bytes — a cookie or a token — and anyone who copies it is you, so stealing sessions is a major attack in its own right (au3 and au4). Second, most of the visitors to a software system are not people. They are other programs: a billing service calling the orders service, a nightly job reading a bucket, a phone app, an AI agent booking a meeting for its user. Each of these needs an identity and a way to prove it, and none of them can type a password or look at a phone.

Principals and credentials

The word for "anyone or anything the system can name and grant permissions to" is principal. A principal can be a user, a service, a machine or an agent. When a request or token is about a principal, that principal is called the subject (you will see this as the sub claim in tokens later in the track).

A principal proves its identity with a credential: a password, a code from a phone app, a signature from a passkey, a client certificate plus its private key, an API key. The device or secret that produces the proof is the authenticator. Credentials come in factors: something you know (a password), something you have (a phone, a security key), something you are (a fingerprint that unlocks a key on your device). Using two different factors is multi-factor authentication (MFA), covered fully in au7.

The key pattern, true for people and for software, is check the strong credential once, then issue a weaker, shorter-lived stand-in. You type a password and get a session cookie. A service presents its platform identity and gets a token valid for an hour. The stand-in exists because checking the strong credential on every request would be slow (a good password hash deliberately takes tens of milliseconds, au2) or impossible (you can't ask a person to tap a security key forty times to load one page). The price is that the stand-in becomes the thing attackers steal.

The big picture in four moves

Every auth design you'll meet in this track is a variation on four moves. Establish identity: a principal proves who it is with a credential (passwords au2, MFA and passkeys au7, single sign-on au6, certificates and workload identity au10). Carry identity: the system issues a session or token so later requests don't repeat the proof (sessions au3, tokens au4, OAuth au5). Decide: for each request, a policy says whether this subject may do this action on this resource (authorisation models au8, tenants au9). Record: every decision that matters is logged with enough context to reconstruct it. Secrets management (au11) is what keeps the keys behind all four from leaking, and au12 applies the whole picture to AI systems, where an agent acts for a user and retrieval must respect each user's permissions.

If you stop reading here, keep this: login is one decision made once; authorisation is a decision made on every request, about a specific object; and both are worth very little if you can't later say who did what.

1. Three questions that fail independently

Start with the cleanest definitions, because interviewers probe the edges.

Authentication (authn) is the process of verifying a claimed identity. The input is a claim ("I'm alice@example.com", "I'm the billing service") plus evidence (a password, a signature). The output is an identity the rest of the system trusts for the duration of a session or token — typically an internal user ID like 12, plus facts about how the proof was made (which method, whether MFA was used, when).

Authorisation (authz) is the decision whether an identified principal may perform an action on a resource. Its input is a triple — subject, action, resource — plus context such as time, tenant, device or how recently the user re-entered their password. Its output is allow or deny, ideally with a reason.

Accounting is recording what happened. In the original network-access sense it meant counting usage for billing; in application security it means an audit trail: an append-only record of security-relevant events that can answer who, what, when, from where, and with what result.

They fail independently, and the combinations are instructive:

AuthnAuthzAccountingWhat it looks like
brokenfinefineCredential stuffing takes over accounts; each takeover is logged and each acts within that account's rights.
finebrokenfinePeloton after February 2021: every member identified correctly, every member can read every rider.
finefinebrokenAn insider with legitimate access exfiltrates records; nothing shows which records, so the breach notice must assume "all".

That third row is underrated. When a regulator or customer asks "was my data accessed?", the honest answer without logs is "we can't tell", and the legal answer is then usually "assume yes". Good accounting turns a breach of everything into a breach of 412 named records.

A small, sharp point that interviewers like: HTTP status codes reflect the split. 401 Unauthorized despite its name means unauthenticated — no credential, or an invalid one; the client should log in or refresh a token. 403 Forbidden means the caller is known and the answer is no. For objects the caller should not even learn exist, many APIs return 404 Not Found instead of 403, so that probing IDs reveals nothing (swe13 covers this choice).

2. The principals a modern system has

"User" is too narrow a word for the things that call your system. It helps to list them explicitly whenever you design auth, because each class has different ways to prove identity and different failure modes.

People. Customers, employees, support staff, administrators. They authenticate with something they know (passwords, au2), have (phones, security keys, au7) or are (biometrics that unlock a key on a device). People are slow, forgetful and phishable, so their credentials leak through reuse and deception, and recovering access when they lose a factor is the weakest door in most systems. People usually get a session that lasts hours to weeks.

Services. Programs calling programs: the checkout service calling payments, a web backend calling a search cluster. They can hold keys, perform cryptography and rotate credentials automatically, which means they can — and should — use far stronger credentials than people: private keys behind certificates, short-lived tokens issued by the platform they run on (au10). Their failure mode is long-lived shared secrets copied into config files, images and CI logs (au11).

Machines and devices. Servers, laptops, CI runners, IoT devices. A device identity proves "this request comes from a company-managed laptop with disk encryption on" or "this is thermostat serial 8812". Zero-trust access (net14) often combines a person's identity with their device's identity. Devices are physically stealable, so their keys should live in hardware where possible (a TPM or secure enclave).

Agents. AI agents that act on behalf of a person: read their mail, file a ticket, call an API. An agent is software, so it can hold strong credentials, but its purpose is to exercise someone else's authority. That raises questions the other classes don't: should it hold the user's full rights or a subset? How long should its grant last? When the agent deletes a file, whose action is it? The answer this track builds towards (au12) is: the agent is its own principal acting under a delegation from the user, with a narrower scope than the user, and both identities appear in every log line.

There is also the anonymous principal — someone with no credential at all — which you should model explicitly. Many systems allow anonymous reads of public content; Peloton's original bug was that a private-data endpoint treated anonymous callers like everybody else.

Two more terms tie these together. A tenant is a customer organisation in a multi-tenant system — Acme Corp's whole workspace — whose data must be isolated from every other tenant's (au9). An identity provider (IdP) is a service whose job is authentication: it verifies credentials and issues signed statements about who someone is, which applications trust (Google, Okta, Microsoft Entra ID, or your own login service). This app uses Google and GitHub as identity providers.

3. Credentials, and the "check once, carry cheaply" pattern

Consider what happens when you sign in to the Prep Console. You click "Sign in with Google". The backend redirects your browser to Google, which handles the password and any second factor — the app never sees them. Google redirects you back with a one-time code; the backend exchanges that code for tokens, including an ID token stating your email and whether Google has verified it (au5 and au6 trace this protocol in detail). The backend then checks email_verified, looks up whether that email is an administrator (configured through the ADMIN_EMAILS environment variable) or on the invite list, upserts a row in its users table, and stores only the user's numeric id in the session: session["uid"] = 12.

That session is a Flask signed cookie called prep_session, marked HttpOnly (JavaScript can't read it), SameSite=Lax (not sent on cross-site POSTs) and Secure when the app is served over HTTPS, with a 30-day lifetime. "Signed" means the server appends an HMAC — a keyed hash computed with the server's SECRET_KEY — so a user can read their cookie but cannot change the id inside it without the signature failing. (Flask uses the itsdangerous library with HMAC-SHA1 for this; au4 recomputes an HMAC signature by hand.)

On every later request, a before_request hook calls load_user(), which verifies the cookie's signature, reads the uid, and re-reads that user's row from SQLite. If the row is gone or marked disabled, the session is cleared and the request continues as anonymous. That re-read is a deliberate design choice worth noticing: the cookie alone would stay valid for 30 days, but because the server checks the database each time, an administrator can disable a user and the very next request fails.

So the strong credential (your Google login, possibly with MFA) is checked once, at sign-in, by Google. The stand-in (a signed cookie with a user id) is checked on every request, cheaply. The trade-off is the classic one: the cookie is a bearer credential — whoever holds it is you — and it lasts a long time. The app limits the damage with HttpOnly (an injected script can't read it), Secure (it doesn't travel over plain HTTP), and the per-request user lookup (disabling an account takes effect immediately). It does not keep a server-side list of sessions, so "log out everywhere" for one stolen cookie is not possible short of rotating SECRET_KEY, which logs everyone out. au3 discusses exactly this trade-off between signed client-side sessions and server-side sessions.

4. Authorisation has three levels

OWASP's API Security Top 10 (2023 edition) splits authorisation failures into three entries, and the split is the most useful mental checklist in this topic.

Function level (API5, broken function-level authorisation, BFLA): may this caller use this operation at all? A regular user calling POST /admin/users/12/disable should be refused regardless of which user is targeted. Function-level checks depend only on who the caller is and which route they hit, so they can live in middleware, decorators, or even at the gateway. In this app, @admin_required on the admin endpoints is a function-level check: it returns 401 without a session and 403 if the session's email isn't in ADMIN_EMAILS.

Object level (API1, broken object-level authorisation, BOLA — the API name for IDOR): may this caller touch this particular record? GET /riders/77 is a function every member may call; whether it returns rider 77's private data depends on rider 77. This check needs the object, or at least its owner and tenant, so it can only run where the object is loaded. It is the top entry in the OWASP API list because it is easy to forget and invisible in tests that only use your own data.

Property level (API3, broken object property-level authorisation): may this caller see or change this field of the record? A rider's public profile may show their username and total workouts but not their weight; a user may edit their display name but not their is_admin flag. Failures here come as excessive data exposure (the API returns the full object and trusts the front end to hide fields) and mass assignment (the API copies every JSON field into the database row, including ones the user should not control).

Here is the object-level bug and fix in this app's own style. Suppose the Prep Console had an endpoint to read a note by its database row id:

@app.get("/api/notes/<int:note_id>")
@login_required
def get_note(note_id):
    row = c.execute("SELECT text FROM notes WHERE rowid = ?", (note_id,)).fetchone()
    return {"text": row["text"]}          # any signed-in user reads any note

@login_required makes it look protected — that's the Peloton trap. The real app avoids it by construction: notes are keyed by (user_id, id) and every query includes WHERE user_id = ? with the id from the session, never from the request. The fixed version:

    row = c.execute("SELECT text FROM notes WHERE user_id = ? AND id = ?",
                    (g.user["id"], item_id)).fetchone()
    if row is None:
        abort(404)                        # not yours, or doesn't exist: same answer

The pattern generalises: take the subject from the authenticated session, take only the object's name from the request, and let the query itself express "owned by this subject". au9 extends this to tenants and to database row-level security.

5. Assurance: how sure are we?

Not every login proves the same thing. A password typed on a phishing page proves only that someone knows the password; a passkey signature proves possession of a private key bound to your real domain. NIST's digital identity guidelines give names to these strengths. SP 800-63B-4, published in August 2025, defines three authenticator assurance levels:

  • AAL1: one factor is enough. A password alone qualifies (and must then be at least 15 characters under the current revision).
  • AAL2: two distinct factors, for example password plus a code app or a passkey; the verifier must offer at least one phishing-resistant option.
  • AAL3: a phishing-resistant cryptographic authenticator whose private key cannot be exported — in practice a hardware security key or a device-bound key; synced passkeys are explicitly not allowed at AAL3.

Why this matters for design: you can attach a required level to each action instead of to the whole application. Browsing your progress needs a session from any login. Disabling another user's account might require that the admin's session was established with MFA in the last 15 minutes; if not, the app asks them to prove it again. That pattern is called step-up authentication, and it requires that the session record how and when the user authenticated, not just who they are. OpenID Connect carries exactly that in two ID-token claims, acr (the level achieved) and amr (the methods used), which au6 returns to. NIST separately defines identity assurance (IAL: how well the person's real-world identity was checked at sign-up) and federation assurance (FAL: how strongly assertions are protected between IdP and application); you'll hear those in regulated industries.

6. A threat model for an auth system

A threat model is a structured answer to "what could go wrong, and what are we doing about it?" (swe13 introduced STRIDE as a checklist). For auth specifically, four lists get you most of the way.

Assets — what an attacker wants: accounts (to act as someone), sessions and tokens (the stand-ins, easier to steal than the credentials), stored password hashes (crackable offline, au2), signing keys (whoever holds the key that signs tokens can mint any identity — the most valuable secret in the system, au4 and au11), and the data behind accounts.

Attackers — who is plausibly trying: an outsider with billions of leaked username/password pairs; a legitimate customer poking at IDs to reach other customers' data (the IDOR attacker is usually authenticated); a malicious or careless insider with real access; a compromised service or dependency inside your network; and, for AI products, untrusted content that steers an agent (ag10).

Entry points — where credentials and identities enter: login, signup, password reset, "magic link" email login, MFA enrolment and removal, OAuth callbacks, token refresh, API key creation, admin impersonation tools, and every API endpoint that accepts a session. Reset and recovery deserve special attention: an attacker who can't guess your password will happily convince support to change your email address.

Trust boundaries — the lines a request crosses where trust changes: browser to edge, edge to service, service to service, service to database, your system to a third-party IdP. At each boundary, ask what the receiving side can verify for itself and what it is merely being told.

From those lists the standard controls fall out, each covered later: slow salted hashes, breach checks and rate limits on login (au2); HttpOnly, Secure, SameSite cookies and rotation at login (au3); short-lived signed tokens validated strictly (au4); PKCE and exact redirect URIs (au5); phishing-resistant MFA and hardened recovery (au7); centralised, object-level authorisation (au8); tenant filters in the data layer (au9); workload identity instead of shared keys (au10); vaults and rotation (au11). Volume is worth a number: credential stuffing — automated logins using pairs leaked elsewhere — is consistently the largest source of login attacks for consumer services, because a small fraction of people reuse passwords and attackers can try millions of pairs cheaply.

7. Where auth lives in an architecture

Now place the checks. A typical request path is browser or client → edge (CDN, load balancer, API gateway) → service → maybe more services → database. Each layer can see different things, so each can make different decisions.

Two roles from access-control literature (NIST SP 800-207 uses them for zero trust) make this precise. A policy enforcement point (PEP) is where a request is intercepted and either blocked or forwarded. A policy decision point (PDP) is where the allow/deny decision is computed. They can be the same code (an if in a handler) or separate (a handler that calls a policy engine such as OPA, au8).

At the edge. An API gateway (sc2) is an excellent PEP for coarse questions: is there a credential at all; is its signature valid and has it expired; is this kind of client (mobile app, partner integration, public API key) allowed on this route; is this caller over its rate limit? Rejecting junk at the edge protects every service behind it and centralises login logic. What the gateway cannot do is decide "may user 12 read invoice 1043", because it doesn't know who owns 1043 — and teaching it would mean copying business data and rules into the gateway, which drifts.

In the service. The service that loads the invoice is the only place with all the facts for object-level and property-level decisions. The failure mode is inconsistency: twenty handlers, each with a slightly different if user.is_admin or invoice.owner_id == user.id. The fix is one authorisation function or policy engine that every handler calls, with deny as the default when no rule matches.

In the database. The data layer can enforce a last line that holds even when a handler forgets: a connection role that can only see one tenant's rows, PostgreSQL row-level security policies keyed on a session variable, or a repository layer that refuses to build a query without a tenant filter. au9 works these through. The database cannot enforce business rules like "editors may publish only during business hours" well, and putting complex logic there makes it hard to test and observe.

In the client. Never a security control. Hiding the admin button in the UI is user experience; the server must still check. Peloton's private-profile setting was honoured by its app's screens and ignored by its API.

8. Carrying identity across hops

Once the gateway has authenticated a request, the services behind it need to know who the caller is. There are three patterns, and the choice matters more than people expect.

Forward the original token. The gateway passes the user's access token through; each service verifies it itself (signature, expiry, audience). This is simple and lets every service make its own decision, but the same token is then accepted by every service it reaches, so a token leaked from a low-value service works against a high-value one, unless tokens are minted with an audience naming a specific service (au4).

Mint per-hop credentials. The gateway, or each service, exchanges the incoming token for a new one scoped to the next hop: narrow audience, short life, only the scopes that hop needs. This is OAuth token exchange, which au12 uses for agents. It contains leaks at the cost of a call to the token service per hop (usually cached).

Trusted internal context. The gateway strips the external token and adds headers like X-User-Id: 12 and X-Tenant-Id: 7. This is fast and very common — and safe only if nothing except the gateway can reach the services, and the gateway always overwrites those headers. If an attacker can reach a service directly (a misconfigured load balancer, an SSRF bug, a compromised neighbour pod), they set X-User-Id to anyone. A sturdier version signs the internal context, or relies on service identity (mTLS, au10) so the receiver knows the header came from the gateway.

The Prep Console has a small, real instance of the third pattern's risk. It can run behind a reverse proxy (Tailscale Funnel or Caddy), and then trusts the proxy's X-Forwarded-For and X-Forwarded-Proto headers through Werkzeug's ProxyFix, configured for exactly one hop. Its optional developer login — sign in as any email without OAuth — is allowed only when DEV_LOGIN is set and the request comes from a loopback address and carries no X-Forwarded-For or Forwarded header, precisely so that a request relayed by a proxy from the internet can never pass as "local".

9. The confused deputy

One classic failure pattern deserves its own name because it recurs in every architecture, including AI agents. A confused deputy is a component with more authority than its caller that is tricked into using that authority on the caller's behalf. The term comes from a 1988 paper by Norm Hardy about a compiler that could write to a billing file the user could not.

A modern example: a reporting service connects to the database with a role that can read every tenant's rows, and accepts ?tenant_id= from the web front end. The front end always sends the user's own tenant — until a user edits the URL. The reporting service, using its own broad authority, happily returns another tenant's report. The service was not compromised; it was confused about whose authority it was exercising.

The cure is always the same in spirit: make the deputy act with the caller's authority, not its own. Derive the tenant from the verified identity, not from a parameter; pass the user's identity downstream and check it at the point of use; give the deputy down-scoped credentials per request. In AI systems the deputy is the agent, the trick is a prompt injection, and the cure is the same — delegated, narrow credentials (au12) and gateways that check every tool call (ag10).

10. Accounting that's actually useful

An audit log is a structured, append-only record of security-relevant events. Useful entries share a shape:

{"ts": "2026-10-07T14:02:11Z", "event": "authz.decision",
 "subject": "user:12", "actor": "agent:mail-assistant", "tenant": "t:7",
 "auth": {"method": "oidc:google", "mfa": true, "session": "sha256:9f2c…"},
 "action": "rider.read", "resource": "rider:77",
 "decision": "deny", "reason": "profile_private",
 "request_id": "req-5d1e", "ip": "203.0.113.9"}

Note the separate subject (on whose behalf) and actor (who actually made the call) — they differ when an admin impersonates a user or an agent acts for one. Note the session is recorded as a hash, not the token itself: a log that contains bearer tokens is a credential dump waiting to leak. Log denials as well as allows; an IDOR probe looks like hundreds of 404s or 403s across sequential IDs from one subject, and you only see it if denials are recorded. Ship logs somewhere the application's own credentials cannot delete or rewrite, because the first thing a capable intruder does is clean up.

The Prep Console is honest about its scale here: it records each user's last login time and sends warnings to the application log when an OAuth callback fails, but it has no dedicated audit trail. For a two-person study tool that's a reasonable trade; for a product holding customer data it would be the first thing a security review flags.

Authentication is a claim with a shelf life

Junior designs treat "logged in" as a boolean. Senior designs treat it as a set of facts with timestamps: who, by which method, with which factors, at what time, from which device. Those facts age. A session established with a password eight days ago is weaker evidence than a passkey tap thirty seconds ago, even though both say "user 12". Keeping auth_time, amr and acr on the session lets authorisation rules ask for freshness ("re-authenticated within 10 minutes") and strength ("MFA used") on sensitive actions without forcing them on everything. It also gives incident response a lever: when a credential type is found to be compromised — say a batch of SMS codes was intercepted — you can revoke exactly the sessions that relied on it.

Authorisation is a data problem more than a code problem

The code of an authorisation check is short. The hard part is having the facts it needs, fresh and in the right place: who owns this document, which groups this user is in, which tenant this project belongs to, whether the share link has expired. In a monolith with one database those are a join away. In microservices they're scattered: the documents service knows owners, the identity service knows groups, the billing service knows which features a tenant pays for. Every architecture choice in au8 — embedding roles in tokens, calling a central policy service, replicating relationship data into a Zanzibar-style store — is a different answer to "how do we get the facts to the decision point quickly and consistently?" Embedding facts in tokens is fastest but stale until the token expires; querying a central service is fresh but adds a network call to every request; a replicated relationship store sits in between and must deal with consistency, which is exactly what Google's Zanzibar paper spends most of its pages on.

Identity propagation and the "ambient authority" problem

Ambient authority is authority a component has simply by existing — its database role, its cloud IAM role, its position on the network — as opposed to authority passed to it with each request. Ambient authority is what makes confused deputies possible: the reporting service could read every tenant because its database role could, whatever the request said. Systems that minimise ambient authority pass explicit, narrow credentials along with each request (capabilities, scoped tokens) and give services only the standing permissions they need when acting on their own behalf. The cost is plumbing: every hop must carry and check the delegated credential. The pay-off is that a bug in one service exposes only what the current caller could reach. Workload identity (au10) shrinks ambient authority on the infrastructure side; token exchange (au12) shrinks it on the user side.

Failing closed, and the default branch

Every authorisation function has an implicit "else". In a deny-by-default design the else is deny: a new route with no rule is unreachable, a new role with no grants can do nothing, an exception in the policy engine returns 403 or 503. In an allow-by-default design, every omission is an exposure. Real systems drift towards allow-by-default because it is convenient during development ("we'll add checks later"), and the cure is structural: a framework hook that refuses to serve any route not explicitly annotated with its policy, plus a test that enumerates routes and fails on unannotated ones. The same logic applies to the policy engine's availability: if the PDP times out, the PEP must deny (or serve a cached decision with a short bound), never allow — which turns the PDP into a critical dependency whose latency and uptime you now have to engineer.

Who is the user in a B2B system?

In consumer products a person is the account. In business software a person has an identity (alice@acme.com, possibly managed by Acme's own IdP) and memberships in one or more tenants, each with roles. The same person can be an admin in Acme's workspace and a guest in a partner's. That means the authenticated subject alone is not enough to authorise: every request must also carry or select the tenant context, and the check is "may Alice, as a member of tenant 7, do this?". Getting this wrong produces a characteristic bug: a user who belongs to two tenants switches workspace in the UI, but the API keeps using the tenant from the token they were issued earlier, or vice versa — and data from one tenant appears in the other. au9 is built around this.

Authentication as a dependency

Centralising authentication in an IdP is right for security and for users, and it creates an availability problem: when the IdP is down, nobody can log in, and if services validate every request by calling the IdP, nobody can do anything. Two design habits contain this. First, validate tokens locally: signed tokens (au4) can be checked with a cached public key, so an IdP outage blocks new logins but not existing sessions. Second, size session and token lifetimes knowing the IdP will sometimes be unavailable — a five-minute token with no ability to refresh means a five-minute outage of everything. The tension between short lifetimes (fast revocation) and long ones (resilience) is one you will see in every topic from here on.

Agents change the shape of the principal

Classic access control has two kinds of actors: people, who are accountable and slow, and services, which are fast and act on their own fixed authority. Agents are fast software exercising a person's authority on tasks the person describes in natural language — and they can be steered by text they read. That combination breaks two assumptions: that the actor's intent equals the principal's intent, and that the actor's authority is fixed in advance. The responses build on everything above: model the agent as a separate principal; give it delegated, down-scoped, short-lived credentials; record both the user and the agent in every decision; and require step-up confirmation from the human for consequential actions. The question "whose authority is this?" — the confused-deputy question — becomes the central one.

What auth failures look like from the inside

Broken authorisation rarely raises errors; it returns 200 OK with someone else's data. That is why it lingers for months (Peloton's endpoints were found by a curious outsider, not by monitoring). The signals you can build: per-subject counts of distinct object IDs accessed per hour (an IDOR scrape touches thousands of IDs; a normal user touches dozens); rates of 403/404 per subject (probing produces many); accesses where the object's tenant differs from the caller's tenant (should be zero, so alert on one); and sudden access to fields or endpoints a client type never used before.

Broken authentication is noisier. Credential stuffing shows up as a spike in failed logins spread across many accounts and many IP addresses, a low success rate (often well under 1%) and logins from data-centre or residential-proxy ranges; account takeover follows as successful logins from new devices immediately changing email, password or payout details. Teams watch failed-login rate, success rate per source network, MFA challenge rate and the rate of sensitive changes shortly after login.

Knobs people tune

Session and token lifetimes trade convenience and resilience against revocation speed; most consumer products keep browser sessions for days to weeks with idle timeouts, while admin consoles use hours and re-authentication. Rate limits on login and reset are tuned per account and per source, with progressive delays rather than hard lockouts that attackers can use to lock victims out (au2). Step-up thresholds decide which actions demand fresh MFA. Authorisation cache TTLs (how long a "yes" is remembered) trade latency against how quickly a revoked permission takes effect; seconds to a minute is typical.

Common production mistakes

Debug and admin endpoints exposed without the same middleware as the main API. Internal services reachable from the internet through a forgotten load-balancer rule while trusting an X-User-Id header. Authorisation implemented in the front end only. Tests that never use a second user. Logs that contain session tokens or password-reset links. Service accounts with administrator rights "because it was easier". A recovery path (support agent changes the account email) that bypasses MFA entirely. Each of these has produced real breaches.

The Peloton case, read as an engineer

Pen Test Partners' write-up describes three exposures: a class-statistics endpoint, a user-search endpoint and several GraphQL endpoints, all returning data without checking the caller's right to it. The disclosure was made privately on 20 January 2021 and acknowledged the same day; on 2 February a silent partial fix made the class-statistics endpoint require authentication; after 90 days with no further response a journalist contacted Peloton, and once the company's security lead engaged in early May the issues were resolved within about a week. Three engineering lessons come out of it. The privacy setting existed in the product but not in the API's authorisation logic, so the UI and the API disagreed about who could see what. GraphQL endpoints, which let clients choose fields, are especially prone to property-level exposure unless authorisation runs per field. And the partial fix demonstrates the mental model this topic is about: authentication without authorisation changes the size of the attacker pool, not the existence of the hole.

Ownership

In organisations that handle auth well, one team owns the identity service, the token formats and the authorisation library or policy engine, and every other team uses them rather than writing their own. Security reviews check new endpoints for their policy annotation; CI runs cross-tenant tests; on-call runbooks cover "signing key compromised", "IdP down" and "mass account takeover". When these pieces are missing, the same bugs are rediscovered team by team.

Goal: see authentication without authorisation fail, then fix it, using only Python 3.12's standard library. About 15 minutes.

  1. Create pipeline.py with a dictionary of three users ({token: user_id}, tokens made with secrets.token_urlsafe(16)), and a dictionary of riders {77: {"owner": 2, "private": True, "weight": 81}, ...}.
  2. Write authenticate(token) that returns a user id or raises PermissionError("401"), and handle_v1(token, rider_id) that authenticates and returns the rider's record. Call it with user 1's token for rider 77 (owned by user 2). It returns the weight — the Peloton bug after its first fix.
  3. Write authorize(user_id, action, rider): allow if the user owns the rider or the profile is not private; otherwise deny. Make handle_v2 call it and raise LookupError("404") on deny. Repeat step 2: now refused.
  4. Add property-level filtering: for non-owners of public profiles return only {"username", "workouts"}.
  5. Add accounting: append a JSON line per decision to audit.jsonl with subject, action, resource, decision and reason, and a SHA-256 hash of the token instead of the token.
  6. Simulate an IDOR scrape: user 1 requests rider IDs 1-500. Count denials per subject from the log.

What to look for: in step 2 the request succeeds although authentication worked perfectly — proof that the two checks are separate. After step 3 the same request is refused, and the response is identical for "exists but not yours" and "doesn't exist". In step 6, hundreds of denials from one subject in a few seconds is exactly the signal a production detector would alert on, and the log never contains a usable token.

Design review: "every endpoint requires login"

You join a team running a B2B project-management API (tenants are customer companies; users belong to one or more tenants). The lead says auth is done: an API gateway validates a JWT on every request and forwards X-User-Id and X-Tenant-Id headers to twelve microservices, which trust them. Admin endpoints check if user.role == "admin". There are no audit logs beyond access logs. List the risks in priority order and propose concrete changes.

Answer

1. Object-level authorisation is unverified. The gateway proves who the caller is, not that they may touch project 1043. Inventory every route that takes an ID and check each service compares the object's tenant (and, where relevant, owner or membership) with the caller's. Add an automated test per route that uses a second tenant's IDs and expects 404. This is the most likely live bug.

2. Trusted headers. Services trust X-User-Id/X-Tenant-Id. Confirm the gateway always overwrites (never passes through) these headers, and that no path reaches a service except via the gateway — check load balancers, internal ingress, SSRF-capable features. Better: have the gateway forward a signed internal token, or adopt mTLS service identity so services know the caller is the gateway.

3. Tenant selection for multi-tenant users. Where does X-Tenant-Id come from? If the client chooses it, the gateway must check the user is a member of that tenant; otherwise a user of two tenants — or a malicious one — can name any tenant.

4. Admin role scope. role == "admin" — admin of which tenant? A tenant admin must not reach platform-admin functions or other tenants. Split platform-staff roles from tenant roles and require step-up MFA for platform admin actions.

5. Consistency. Twelve services each writing checks will diverge. Introduce a shared authorisation library or policy engine with deny-by-default and a route annotation that CI enforces.

6. Accounting. Add structured audit events for logins, permission changes, admin actions, data exports and authorisation denials, with subject, tenant, actor, resource and decision, shipped to storage the services can't modify; alert on cross-tenant accesses and denial bursts.

7. Defence in depth at the data layer. Add tenant filters enforced in a shared repository layer or database row-level security, so a forgotten check fails closed.
