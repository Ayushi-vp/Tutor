Between May and September 2023, someone logged in to about 14,601 23andMe accounts. They didn't break the company's encryption or find a bug in its login code. They had lists of email addresses and passwords stolen from other websites, and they tried them on 23andMe, betting that some people reuse passwords. Some did. According to the UK Information Commissioner's penalty notice, the attacker made about 273,465 failed and 14,601 successful logins over those months, rotating through thousands of IP addresses so no single address looked busy. Through a feature that let customers see genetic relatives, those 14,601 accounts exposed data about millions of other customers. In 2025 the regulator fined the company £2.31 million and listed what was missing: mandatory MFA, a password deny list, detection of an obviously shifted ratio of failed to successful logins.

That's the first half of the password problem: passwords get reused, so somebody else's breach becomes your incident. The second half is what happens when your database leaks. If you stored passwords badly — plaintext, or a fast hash like MD5 or SHA-256 — the attacker recovers most of them in hours on a gaming graphics card, and now your users' passwords feed the next site's credential-stuffing attack. Store them well and most passwords survive a leak, and the strong ones survive indefinitely.

Passwords are the oldest and weakest authenticator we have, and they're not going away soon: most products still have them, as a primary factor or as a fallback when the phone is lost. A senior engineer is expected to get them exactly right: how to store them (salts, slow and memory-hard hash functions, cost parameters you can justify in guesses per second), how to stop online guessing without letting attackers lock out real users, what the current NIST rules actually say, and how to build a password-reset flow that isn't a back door. This topic works through each, with real numbers from a real GPU benchmark, the 23andMe case, and a 2024 Okta bug that came from one forgotten property of bcrypt.

The locked diary

Imagine you're a school storing every pupil's diary-lock combination so you can check them at the door. Storing the combinations themselves is obviously bad: anyone who steals the list opens every diary. So instead you store a fingerprint of each combination — something you can compute from the combination, and compare, but can't run backwards. At the door, a pupil tells you their combination, you compute its fingerprint and compare it with the stored one. That's password hashing, and the fingerprint function is a cryptographic hash.

Now the thief steals the fingerprint list. They can't run the fingerprint backwards — but they don't need to. They can guess: compute the fingerprint of 0000, 0001, 0002, and so on, and look for matches. How fast they can do that is the whole game. If computing one fingerprint takes a nanosecond, every four-digit combination falls instantly. If it takes a full second, the thief needs hours for even four digits. Password hashing is the art of making each guess as expensive as possible for the attacker while keeping one check cheap enough for the honest login.

Two more tricks complete the picture. If two pupils chose the same combination, they'd have the same fingerprint, and the thief would crack both with one guess — so before fingerprinting, you add a random salt, unique to each pupil, written next to their fingerprint. Now each pupil's fingerprint must be attacked separately. And if you keep one extra secret ingredient — a pepper — in the head teacher's safe rather than on the list, a thief who steals only the list can't even start guessing.

The analogy breaks in one important place: real attackers don't guess in order. They guess the passwords people actually choose — "123456", "password", "Liverpool2019!" — from lists of billions of real leaked passwords and rules that mutate them. So the strength of a stored password depends on two things you control (how slow the hash is, whether there's a salt) and one you only partly control (how predictable the user's password is). That's why storage is only half the job; the other half is refusing predictable passwords and adding a second factor.

Two kinds of attack

Keep a clean split in mind, because the defences are different.

Offline attacks happen after the attacker has a copy of your password hashes. Nothing you do at the login page matters any more; they guess on their own hardware, as fast as it goes. Your only defences are the hash function's slowness, the salt, a pepper, and users having picked unpredictable passwords.

Online attacks go through your login endpoint: credential stuffing (leaked pairs from elsewhere), password spraying (a few common passwords against many accounts) and targeted guessing. Here the hash's speed barely matters — the attacker is limited by how many attempts you allow. The defences are rate limits, bot detection, breached-password checks, anomaly detection and MFA.

A reader who stops here has the right picture: assume the hash table leaks; make each guess slow and per-user; refuse the passwords attackers guess first; and stop online guessing with limits and a second factor rather than lockouts.

1. What a hash does, and why speed is the problem

A cryptographic hash function such as SHA-256 takes any input and produces a fixed-size output (256 bits), with two properties that matter here: you can't find an input from its output except by guessing, and changing the input in any way changes the output unpredictably. Storing SHA-256(password) instead of the password means a database thief doesn't directly see passwords.

The trouble is that SHA-256 was designed to be fast — it hashes gigabytes per second, which is what you want for checking file downloads. Password cracking exploits exactly that. A public benchmark of the hashcat password cracker (version 6.2.6) on one NVIDIA RTX 4090, a consumer gaming card, measured about 21,976 million SHA-256 hashes per second — roughly 22 billion. Let's turn that into passwords.

Take every 8-character password made of lowercase letters and digits: 36 choices per character, so 368 = 2,821,109,907,456 ≈ 2.8 × 1012 candidates. At 2.2 × 1010 per second, trying them all takes 2.8 × 1012 ÷ 2.2 × 1010 ≈ 128 seconds. Two minutes, on one card, for the entire space. And real attackers don't search the whole space; they start with wordlists of billions of passwords leaked from earlier breaches, so most human-chosen passwords fall far sooner.

Without a salt it's worse still. Unsalted, every user with the password "letmein" has the same hash, so each guess is checked against every user at once by looking it up in a hash table: one computation cracks all of them. Attackers can even precompute: a rainbow table is a compact precomputed structure that maps hashes back to passwords for an unsalted function, built once and reused against every leak.

2. Salts: make every user a separate problem

A salt is a random value — 16 bytes is typical — generated fresh for each password and combined with it before hashing: store salt and H(salt ‖ password). (The ‖ symbol means concatenation, sticking the bytes together.) Two consequences:

  • Identical passwords now have different hashes, so the attacker can't spot shared passwords or crack them together.
  • Each guess must be computed separately per user — H(salt_alice ‖ guess) is useless for Bob. With a million users, an attack that tries one guess against everyone now costs a million hash computations instead of one, and precomputed tables are worthless because nobody can precompute for every possible salt.

The salt is stored in plaintext next to the hash, because the verifier needs it at login. That surprises people, but secrecy isn't its job — uniqueness is. Use a cryptographically secure random generator (Python's secrets or os.urandom), never a user ID or username, which repeat across systems and are predictable.

Salting fixes the "all users at once" problem. It does nothing for the speed of each guess: a targeted attack on one salted SHA-256 hash still runs at 22 billion guesses per second. For that we need slow functions.

3. Slow hashes and cost parameters

A password hash is a key derivation function (KDF) — a function that turns a secret into key material — deliberately tuned to be expensive, with a cost parameter that sets how expensive. The honest server pays the cost once per login; the attacker pays it once per guess. If the server spends 50 milliseconds, that's invisible to a user logging in and devastating to an attacker making billions of guesses.

There are four functions you'll meet, in historical order.

PBKDF2 (Password-Based Key Derivation Function 2, 2000) repeats an HMAC — a keyed hash — many times; the cost is the iteration count. OWASP's current recommendation is 600,000 iterations of HMAC-SHA256 (or 220,000 of HMAC-SHA512). PBKDF2 uses almost no memory, so a GPU runs it in thousands of parallel lanes. Its main virtue is regulatory: it's approved under FIPS 140, the US government standard for cryptographic modules, so some environments must use it.

bcrypt (1999) is built on the Blowfish cipher's expensive key setup. Its cost factor is a power of two: cost 10 means 210 = 1,024 rounds of the expensive step, cost 11 is twice as slow, cost 12 four times. It needs about 4 KB of fast memory per computation, which happens to fit badly on GPUs, making it far more resistant than PBKDF2 per unit of server time. OWASP says cost 10 at minimum. It has one sharp edge: it only uses the first 72 bytes of input, silently ignoring the rest — remember that for the Okta story below.

scrypt (2009) introduced memory hardness: every evaluation must fill and then randomly read a large block of memory, so computing many guesses in parallel needs many blocks of memory. Its parameters are N (CPU/memory cost, a power of two), r (block size) and p (parallelism); memory is about 128 × N × r bytes. OWASP's first scrypt option is N = 217, r = 8, p = 1, which is 128 × 131,072 × 8 = 134,217,728 bytes = 128 MiB per guess. Python has it built in as hashlib.scrypt.

Argon2 won the Password Hashing Competition in 2015 and is standardised as RFC 9106. Its hybrid variant, Argon2id, is OWASP's first choice. Its parameters are memory m (in KiB), passes over that memory t, and parallelism p. OWASP lists five equivalent minimum configurations trading memory for passes, from m = 47,104 KiB (46 MiB), t = 1 to m = 7,168 KiB (7 MiB), t = 5, all with p = 1; the commonly quoted one is m = 19,456 KiB (19 MiB), t = 2, p = 1. The "id" means its first pass resists side-channel attacks that watch memory access patterns, while later passes make GPU attacks expensive.

4. Worked example: cost parameters as guesses per second

The same RTX 4090 benchmark gives us real numbers for slow hashes. The benchmark runs bcrypt at cost 5 (32 iterations), PBKDF2 at 999 iterations and scrypt at N = 16,384; we scale each to the production settings above. Since the work is proportional to the cost, scaling is division:

The benchmark doesn't include Argon2, so reason from the hardware. Argon2id with m = 19 MiB and t = 2 must write and read the full 19 MiB block of memory at least twice per guess; counting the reads of earlier blocks, that's on the order of 100 MB of memory traffic per guess. The RTX 4090's memory bandwidth is about 1 TB/s, so it can't do more than roughly 10,000 guesses per second even if computation were free — and its 24 GB of memory can hold only about 1,200 of those 19 MiB states at once. That's a ceiling from arithmetic, not a measurement, but it shows how memory hardness caps the attacker: the limit comes from memory, which is expensive to multiply, rather than from cores, which are cheap.

Now redo the 8-character lowercase-and-digit search against bcrypt cost 10: 2.82 × 1012 ÷ 5,750 ≈ 4.9 × 108 seconds ≈ 15.5 years on one GPU, for one user's hash, versus two minutes for SHA-256. That's the 3.8-million-fold difference that slow hashing buys.

But don't over-read it. A realistic attack uses a dictionary: the classic RockYou leak list has about 14.3 million passwords, and a standard rule set ("best64", 77 mutation rules such as appending digits and capitalising) turns it into 14,344,391 × 77 ≈ 1.1 × 109 guesses. Against one bcrypt cost-10 hash that's 1.1 × 109 ÷ 5,750 ≈ 192,000 seconds — about 2.2 days. And against a whole leaked database, attackers try the top 1,000 passwords against every user: for 10 million users that's 1010 hashes, about 20 days on one card or a day on twenty rented ones, and it cracks every account whose password is that common. So the honest conclusion is: slow hashes make strong passwords effectively uncrackable and make mass cracking slow and costly; they do not save weak passwords of a determined target. That's why the next sections — blocklists and MFA — exist.

5. Choosing parameters, and upgrading them

The server pays the same cost per login, so cost is a budget decision. Measure on your actual login servers and choose the highest setting that fits your latency target and peak capacity. OWASP's rule of thumb is that one hash should take well under a second; tens to a couple of hundred milliseconds is common. Then do the capacity sum: if one verification takes 100 ms of a core, one core handles 10 logins per second, so a 16-core login fleet handles 160 per second. A credential-stuffing burst of 1,000 attempts per second would saturate it — which is a reason to rate-limit before the hash runs (section 7), not a reason to weaken the hash.

Memory-hard functions add a memory budget: Argon2id at 19 MiB with 50 concurrent logins needs about 1 GB. On small containers that's real, which is one reason OWASP offers lower-memory, higher-pass configurations.

Because the stored string records its own algorithm and parameters ($2b$12$… for bcrypt cost 12, $argon2id$v=19$m=19456,t=2,p=1$… for Argon2id), you can raise costs over time without a migration: on each successful login, after verifying, check whether the stored parameters are below the current target, and if so re-hash the plaintext you have just verified and save the new string. This rehash on login pattern also moves users between algorithms. Users who never log in keep the old hash, so pair it with a deadline after which stale accounts must reset.

A legacy database of weak hashes deserves a faster fix: wrap them. Replace every stored md5(p) with argon2id(md5(p)) in one offline job, then on each login convert to argon2id(p). The database is protected the same day. The catch is password shucking: if the same users' unsalted MD5 hashes leaked elsewhere, an attacker can test those known MD5 values through your outer Argon2 instead of guessing passwords, which is why OWASP warns against layering a fast inner hash permanently.

6. Peppers

A pepper is a secret key shared by all password hashes and stored somewhere other than the database — a secrets vault, a cloud key-management service (KMS) or a hardware security module (HSM); au11 covers all three. The usual construction is to compute HMAC-SHA256(pepper, password) and feed that into the slow hash. A thief with only a database dump now can't test a single guess, because they don't know the HMAC key. The point is that databases leak in different ways from application secrets — SQL injection, a misplaced backup, an over-shared analytics replica — so separating them buys real protection.

Peppers cost something. If you lose the pepper, every password becomes unverifiable, so it needs backup and access control as serious as any signing key. Rotating it can't be done by re-hashing (you don't have the passwords), so you version it: store a pepper ID with each hash, use the new pepper for new hashes and on rehash-at-login, and keep old peppers until no hashes reference them. And with bcrypt, feeding in a raw 32-byte HMAC output is risky because bcrypt treats a zero byte as the end of its input; OWASP's suggested form base64-encodes it first: bcrypt(base64(hmac-sha384(password, pepper)), salt, cost).

7. Online attacks: credential stuffing, spraying and rate limits

Now the login endpoint itself. Credential stuffing replays username/password pairs leaked elsewhere. Password spraying tries a few very common passwords ("Winter2025!") against many accounts. Both are designed to look innocuous per account and per IP address. The 23andMe numbers show the shape: about 288,000 attempts over four and a half months, of which 5.1% succeeded (14,601 ÷ 288,066), from 7,813 IP addresses for the successful logins in May alone. Per IP that's a handful of attempts; per account, one or two. A rule like "lock the account after 5 failures" never fires.

Defences come in layers, and you need several:

  • Breached-password checks. Refuse passwords known from breaches at signup and change, and optionally check at login and force a reset if a user's current password appears. Have I Been Pwned's Pwned Passwords service makes this private via k-anonymity: you send only the first 5 hex characters of the password's SHA-1 hash (one of 165 = 1,048,576 buckets) and get back every hash suffix in that bucket with its breach count; the match happens on your side, so the full password hash never leaves your server. You can also download the corpus and query locally.
  • Rate limits at several grains. Per source IP, per network (subnet or autonomous system, which catches proxy farms), per account, and global failed-login rate. Use progressive delays (each failure doubles the wait for that account or source) rather than permanent lockout.
  • Bot detection and challenges. Device and browser fingerprinting, proof-of-work or CAPTCHA challenges for suspicious traffic, and known-device cookies so returning users skip friction.
  • Anomaly detection. The ICO notes 23andMe's normal login mix was about 80% successful and 20% failed, and during the attack it shifted to about 70/30; logins also arrived with an empty HTTP referrer, unlike real users coming from the login page. Either signal, alerted on, could have caught the attack months earlier.
  • New-device notifications and MFA. Telling users about logins from a new device turns them into detectors; requiring a second factor makes a correct password insufficient (au7). The ICO's notice lists mandatory MFA as one of the measures 23andMe lacked.

Hard lockout deserves its own warning. Locking an account for good after N failures lets anyone who knows a username lock its owner out — a denial-of-service against your users, which attackers can run at scale against an enterprise's whole directory. NIST SP 800-63B-4 caps consecutive failures at 100 before the authenticator is disabled; in practice, use throttling and step-up challenges well before that.

One more online leak: username enumeration. If "unknown user" returns faster or differently from "wrong password", attackers learn which emails have accounts. Return the same message, and for unknown users run a dummy hash so timing matches; do the same for signup ("check your email") and reset.

8. What NIST says now

NIST SP 800-63B-4, published in August 2025, is the reference most policies cite, and it overturned several old habits:

  • Passwords used as the only factor SHALL be at least 15 characters; with another factor, at least 8.
  • Verifiers SHOULD permit at least 64 characters and should accept spaces and Unicode.
  • Verifiers SHALL NOT impose composition rules (one digit, one symbol…) and SHALL NOT require periodic changes — but SHALL force a change on evidence of compromise.
  • Verifiers SHALL check new passwords against a blocklist of breached, dictionary and context-specific values (the service's name, the username).
  • Consecutive failed attempts SHALL be limited to no more than 100.
  • Password hints and knowledge-based questions ("first pet?") are out.

The reasoning is about human behaviour. Composition rules produce Password1!; forced rotation produces Password2!; both make passwords harder to remember, which drives reuse — the root of credential stuffing. Length plus a blocklist plus a password manager is what actually works. Also: allow paste into password fields, so password managers work.

9. Password reset: the back door everyone builds

The reset flow is a second way to log in that bypasses the password, so it must be at least as strong. A safe design:

The details that matter, each closing a real attack:

  • Random, long tokens from a CSPRNG (cryptographically secure pseudo-random number generator): 32 bytes from secrets.token_urlsafe(32) is 256 bits. Never a timestamp, a counter, or random.
  • Stored hashed, because a token is password-equivalent; a fast SHA-256 is fine here since the token has 256 bits of entropy and can't be guessed.
  • Single use and short expiry (15-60 minutes), and invalidate all outstanding tokens when one is used or the password changes.
  • Uniform response to the request, so it can't be used to enumerate accounts, and rate limits per account and source, so it can't be used to spam inboxes.
  • Links built from configuration, never from the incoming Host header. Otherwise an attacker requests a reset for the victim with Host: evil.example, the victim receives a genuine email with a link to the attacker's domain, and clicking it delivers the token to the attacker ("host header poisoning").
  • Keep the token out of referrers and logs: set Referrer-Policy: no-referrer on the reset page and don't load third-party scripts there.
  • Revoke sessions and notify: completing a reset should end all existing sessions (the reason someone resets may be that an attacker is logged in) and email the user that the password changed.
  • Respect MFA: if the account has a second factor, the reset should still require it, or a stolen inbox becomes a full takeover. au7 returns to recovery as the weakest link.

The Prep Console sidesteps all of this by not having passwords at all: users sign in with Google or GitHub, so password storage, breach checks, lockout and reset are those providers' problems. That's a legitimate design choice — "don't store passwords" is the most robust password storage — whose costs are dependence on the providers and the account-linking questions au6 discusses.

Why memory hardness changes the economics

An attacker's cost is roughly the hardware they must buy or rent multiplied by the time they need it. For compute-bound functions like PBKDF2, hardware cost per guess per second falls every year, and custom chips (ASICs, built for one function) make it fall dramatically — this is what happened to Bitcoin's SHA-256 mining. Memory-hard functions tie each in-flight guess to a block of memory, and memory doesn't get cheaper per byte nearly as fast as logic, nor can an ASIC avoid it. The useful mental model is area-time cost: chip area (dominated by memory) times time per guess. Argon2's designers chose its memory-access pattern so that trading memory for recomputation (storing less and recomputing missing blocks) costs the attacker far more time than it saves area. That's also why Argon2 has both data-independent (Argon2i) and data-dependent (Argon2d) modes: data-dependent access resists such trade-offs better but leaks timing information through cache behaviour; Argon2id does the first half-pass data-independently and the rest data-dependently to get most of both.

Server-side cost is an attack surface

A 100 ms, 19 MiB login hash is an amplifier: an attacker sending cheap HTTP requests makes you spend expensive CPU and memory. At 1,000 junk logins per second you need 100 cores and 19 GB of RAM just to say no. The defences are ordering and isolation: run cheap checks first (rate limits, bot signals, does this account exist — while keeping timing uniform), cap concurrent hash computations with a semaphore so a burst queues rather than exhausting memory, and run the login path on its own pool so a hash storm doesn't starve the rest of the application. Some systems offload hashing to dedicated workers. Keep the cap's queue bounded and fail fast with 429 rather than letting latency explode for everyone.

Server relief and client-side hashing

Some designs push part of the work to the client: the browser computes a slow hash, and the server stores a fast hash of the result. This is called server relief. It reduces server cost, but the client-side output becomes the real password — anyone who steals it from a log or a proxy can log in without knowing the original — so the server must still hash what it receives, and the client's work only helps if it's per-user salted (otherwise it's a fast unsalted hash in disguise). Most teams find the complexity not worth it; passkeys (au7) are the cleaner route to "the server never sees a password".

Unicode, normalisation and length limits

Allowing Unicode passwords, as NIST recommends, raises a subtle issue: the same visible string can have different byte encodings ("é" as one code point or as "e" plus a combining accent). If a user sets their password on a Mac and types it on Windows, the bytes may differ and login fails. NIST suggests normalising with NFKC or NFKD before hashing. Length limits are another trap: NIST wants at least 64 characters allowed, but unbounded length is a denial-of-service vector for PBKDF2 (long passwords are hashed into the HMAC key once, so it's cheap) and a silent truncation for bcrypt (72 bytes). Cap input at a few hundred bytes, reject rather than truncate, and if you use bcrypt make the limit explicit.

The Okta bcrypt bug as a design lesson

Okta's advisory explains that its AD/LDAP delegated-authentication feature, which checks passwords against a customer's own directory through an on-premises agent, cached successful logins under a key computed by bcrypt over userId + username + password. Bcrypt reads at most 72 bytes; with a username of 52 or more characters, userId plus username filled those bytes and the password was never read. When the agent was unreachable (high load, network trouble) and a cache entry existed for that user, the cache answered — and any password matched. The vulnerability existed from 23 July 2024 and was fixed on 30 October 2024 by switching to PBKDF2. Three lessons generalise. Password hashes are for verifying passwords, not for building cache keys or MACs; use an HMAC for that. Every primitive has input semantics (bcrypt's 72 bytes and zero-byte termination) you must check, ideally in code that rejects out-of-range input. And fallback paths — here, "answer from cache when the directory is down" — are where authentication bugs hide, because they're exercised rarely and tested less.

Measuring password strength honestly

"Entropy" calculations based on character classes (8 characters × log2(94) ≈ 52 bits) describe random passwords, which humans don't choose. Real strength is how early a password appears in an attacker's guessing order. Estimators like Dropbox's zxcvbn approximate this by detecting dictionary words, names, dates, keyboard patterns and l33t substitutions and estimating guess counts. For policy, a breach-corpus blocklist plus a minimum length captures most of the value; a strength meter helps users choose better when it's honest about guess counts instead of rewarding P@ssw0rd!.

Detection is a data problem

23andMe's attack lasted from late April to September 2023 and was found when data appeared for sale, not from login telemetry. The ICO's notice says the attacker used thousands of IP addresses, so IP-based rate limiting didn't trigger, but the aggregate success/failure ratio moved and the HTTP referrer pattern changed. The lesson is that per-entity thresholds miss distributed attacks; you need population-level baselines — failure ratio, logins per account per day, new-device logins, logins followed quickly by data export — with alerts on deviation. Those are cheap to compute from the auth logs au1 argued for.

Failure modes you will actually see

Legacy hashes lingering. A system that migrated to bcrypt years ago still has 8% of rows in the old format because those users never logged in. A leak exposes exactly those. Track the share of each scheme in the users table as a metric, and set a deadline after which old-format accounts must reset.

Login latency regressions. Raising the cost factor from 10 to 12 quadruples hash time; if the login service was sized for the old cost, p99 login latency spikes at the morning peak. Roll out cost increases gradually (the rehash-on-login pattern does this naturally) and watch CPU on the login pool.

Memory exhaustion. Argon2id or scrypt with unbounded concurrency in a small container gets OOM-killed under a credential-stuffing burst. Cap concurrent hashes and size containers to the cap.

Stuffing waves. Symptoms: failed logins up 10-100×, spread across many accounts and IPs; success ratio down; many logins with no prior device cookie; traffic from hosting providers or residential-proxy networks. Response playbook: enable challenges for unknown devices, tighten global and per-network limits, find accounts that succeeded during the wave and force resets or step-up, and notify users.

Support-desk takeovers. An attacker who can't get past the login page calls support and asks to change the account's email. Treat support tools as an authentication path: require the same verification as self-service recovery, log every change, and notify the old address.

Metrics to keep

Login attempts and success ratio, overall and per network/ASN; failed logins per account per hour; percentage of logins with MFA; time per hash (p50/p99) and hashes in flight; reset requests and completions; accounts force-reset due to breached passwords; share of users per hash scheme; new-device logins followed within minutes by email, password or payout change.

The 23andMe timeline, as a case study

From the ICO's penalty notice: the forensic investigation dates the attack's start to 29 April 2023. From 1 to 16 May, the attacker made about 183,380 failed and 9,974 successful logins while scraping relatives and ancestry data. On 6 July 2023 over a million logins, mostly to a single account, briefly made the platform inoperable — an attempt to transfer profiles that 23andMe investigated without identifying the wider attack. In August, data was offered for sale and customers wrote in about it. Only after a post on the unofficial 23andMe subreddit on 1 October did the company open its investigation; it reset sessions on 9 October and mandated password resets on 10 October. Overall, between 1 May and 18 September the attacker made approximately 14,601 successful and 273,465 unsuccessful logins. The regulator found the password policy required only eight characters (raised to twelve afterwards), there was no deny list, MFA wasn't mandatory, customers weren't alerted to new-device logins, and the login-ratio shift went unnoticed. Each finding maps to a section of this topic.

Build or buy

Most teams shouldn't write password storage code. Use the framework's hasher (Django's default is PBKDF2 with Argon2 available; Spring Security's DelegatingPasswordEncoder records the algorithm id per hash) or a managed identity provider that handles hashing, breach detection and bot defence. Your job is choosing parameters, wiring the rate limits and detection to your traffic, and making sure the recovery path is as strong as the front door.

Goal: feel the difference between fast and slow hashes, then build salted, peppered, upgradeable storage with Python 3.12's standard library only. About 15 minutes.

  1. Fast hash speed. Time 1,000,000 hashlib.sha256(b"guess%d" % i).digest() calls with time.perf_counter(); print guesses per second.
  2. scrypt speed. Time 5 calls of hashlib.scrypt(b"pw", salt=os.urandom(16), n=2**17, r=8, p=1, maxmem=2**28, dklen=32). (The default memory limit is too small for N = 217, which needs 128 MiB — hence maxmem.) Compute the ratio to step 1.
  3. Store and verify. Write hash_pw(pw) returning a string scrypt$17$8$1$<salt b64>$<hash b64> and verify(pw, stored) that parses the parameters and compares with hmac.compare_digest.
  4. Pepper. Feed hmac.new(PEPPER, pw, "sha256").digest() into scrypt instead of pw, with PEPPER read from an environment variable. Show that a stolen stored string plus the right password fails without the pepper.
  5. Rehash on login. Store a hash made with n=2**14; make login() verify it and, if its N is below 217, re-hash and replace it. Print the stored string before and after.
  6. Optional breach check. SHA-1 "password123", send only the first 5 hex characters to https://api.pwnedpasswords.com/range/<prefix> with urllib.request, and search the response for the remaining 35.

What to look for: step 1 runs at millions per second on one CPU core, step 2 at a few per second — a gap of roughly a million, before the attacker's GPU advantage. Two hashes of the same password differ (salt). Step 4 shows a database-only leak is useless without the pepper. Step 6 returns hundreds of suffixes, one of which matches, with a large count — the server never saw which password you checked.

Estimate and design: a fitness app's login after a breach elsewhere

You run a consumer app with 8 million accounts. Passwords are stored as bcrypt cost 8 (set in 2016). Logins peak at 400 per second; the login pool has 24 cores. A large retailer just disclosed a breach of 200 million email/password pairs, and you expect stuffing within days. (a) How fast could an attacker crack your hashes if your database leaked, at cost 8 versus cost 12, using the RTX 4090 figure of 184,000 bcrypt hashes per second at cost 5? (b) Can you move to cost 12 without new hardware? (c) What do you deploy this week against stuffing?

Answer

(a) Cost 8 is 23 = 8 times cost 5: 184,000 ÷ 8 = 23,000 guesses/s per GPU. Cost 12 is 27 = 128 times: ≈ 1,440/s. The "top 1,000 passwords against everyone" attack is 8 × 109 hashes: about 4 days on one GPU at cost 8, about 64 days at cost 12 — a factor of 16, which also multiplies the attacker's rental bill by 16.

(b) Measure on the login servers first. Suppose cost 8 takes 15 ms per hash on one core; cost 12 is 24 = 16 times the work, so 240 ms. Today's peak is 400 × 0.015 = 6 core-seconds per second — 6 of 24 cores. At cost 12 it would be 400 × 0.24 = 96 cores, four times the pool, with no headroom for an attack. Options: go to cost 11 (half that), add cores, or move to Argon2id at a measured setting. Roll out via rehash-on-login so the CPU increase follows logins gradually, and put rate limits before the hash so attack traffic can't consume the budget.

(c) This week: check new and changed passwords against Pwned Passwords via k-anonymity, and check at login, forcing a reset for accounts whose current password is in the corpus; add per-network and global failed-login limits with progressive delays (not hard lockout); challenge logins without a known-device cookie when the failure ratio is elevated; alert on success/failure ratio and on new-device logins followed by email or password change; email users on new-device logins; and offer MFA, with a prompt for accounts flagged as reused. Prepare a runbook to force-reset accounts that logged in successfully from suspect networks during a wave.
