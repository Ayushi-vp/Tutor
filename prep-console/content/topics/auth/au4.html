In June 2023 analysts at the US State Department noticed something odd in a log of mailbox access — a custom alert they had named "Big Yellow Taxi" fired on applications reading mailboxes in unusual ways. They told Microsoft. Over the next ten days Microsoft found that an espionage group it calls Storm-0558 had been reading the email of 22 organisations and 503 related personal accounts — about 60,000 emails from the State Department alone, including senior officials. The attackers hadn't stolen anyone's password or session. They had a Microsoft signing key, and with it they minted their own authentication tokens. Microsoft's mail servers checked the signatures, found them valid — they really were signed by a Microsoft key — and let them in.

The US Cyber Safety Review Board's report reads like a checklist of this topic. The key had been created in 2016 for consumer accounts and was supposed to be retired in March 2021, but Microsoft had paused its manual key rotation and had no alert for keys that had grown old. The key should only have been able to sign tokens for consumer mail, but a validation flaw meant Exchange Online also accepted consumer-key-signed tokens for enterprise accounts. And the forged tokens were only caught because one customer had paid for, and actually analysed, a premium audit log.

Tokens — and in particular JSON Web Tokens, JWTs — are how most modern APIs carry identity. They are compact, self-contained, and verifiable by any service with the right key, without calling home. Those same properties make their failure modes severe: a token that verifies is believed; a key that signs is all-powerful; a token that's been issued can't easily be taken back. This topic takes a JWT apart to the bytes and recomputes its signature by hand; compares symmetric and asymmetric signing and how keys are distributed; builds the complete validation checklist and the classic bugs that each check prevents; and then deals with the life cycle — expiry, refresh tokens, rotation and reuse detection, revocation — and the old question of JWTs versus opaque tokens.

The sealed letter of introduction

Before telephones, a traveller carried a letter of introduction: "The bearer, Ada Lovelace, is my trusted colleague; please extend her every courtesy. — Charles Babbage", sealed with Babbage's wax seal. The recipient didn't need to contact Babbage; they recognised his seal and trusted the letter. That's a self-contained token. The letter states facts (claims), the seal proves who wrote it and that nobody altered it (signature), and anyone who knows the seal can check it.

The analogy shows the strengths and the risks at once. The recipient can verify without a round trip — fast, and it works even if Babbage is unreachable. But anyone holding the letter can present it, so it's a bearer token. The letter can't be recalled once written; if Babbage falls out with Ada, every copy remains valid until its date runs out. The letter should say whom it's addressed to — a letter for the Royal Society shouldn't open doors at the Bank of England — and should carry a date. And everything rests on the seal: whoever has Babbage's signet ring can write any letter they like. That's Storm-0558.

The analogy breaks in one technical place: a wax seal can't be checked against a published reference, but digital signatures can. With asymmetric signatures, the issuer keeps a private key and publishes the matching public key; anyone can check a signature, but only the private-key holder can produce one. With symmetric signatures (an HMAC), the same secret both creates and checks — so everyone who can check can also forge.

What a JWT is

A JWT (JSON Web Token, RFC 7519, usually pronounced "jot") is a string of three parts separated by dots:

eyJhbGciOiJIUzI1NiIsInR5cCI6IkpXVCJ9 . eyJpc3MiOiJodHRwczov…In0 . WmW-o7c3kZvCqlEGuuUhaBLD2ezS_tz2cyUkh2sRG5E
└──────────── header ─────────────┘   └──── payload ────┘   └────────────── signature ──────────────┘

The header says how it's signed ({"alg":"HS256","typ":"JWT"}); the payload holds claims — statements like "subject is user 12, issued by auth.example.com, for the prep-api, expires at 08:15"; the signature covers the first two parts. Each part is base64url-encoded: a way of writing bytes using only letters, digits, - and _, safe to put in URLs and headers. That's an encoding, not encryption: anyone can decode the header and payload. Technically this signed form is a JWS (JSON Web Signature); an encrypted form, JWE, exists but is far less common.

Where tokens come from

Tokens are issued by an authorisation server or identity provider after it has authenticated someone — a user through a login (au5, au6) or a service through its own credential (au10). The holder then presents the token to resource servers, the APIs that hold the data, usually in an HTTP header: Authorization: Bearer eyJ…. The word "Bearer" is honest: whoever bears it gets in. Two kinds of token appear constantly. An access token is short-lived and goes to APIs on every call. A refresh token is longer-lived and goes only back to the authorisation server, to get a new access token when the old one expires. Keeping that split in mind explains most of the life-cycle design later in this lesson.

If you stop here: a JWT is a sealed, readable letter of introduction. Check the seal with a key you chose for that issuer, check it's addressed to you and still in date, keep it short-lived because you can't recall it, and guard the signing key above everything else.

1. Base64url, by hand

Base64 turns every 3 bytes (24 bits) into 4 characters of 6 bits each, using a 64-character alphabet: A-Z are 0-25, a-z are 26-51, 0-9 are 52-61, and two more symbols. Standard base64 uses + and / and pads with =; base64url uses - and _ and drops the padding, so tokens survive URLs and headers.

Every JWT header starts eyJ, and here's why. The JSON begins with the bytes {"a: 0x7B, 0x22, 0x61, or in bits 01111011 00100010 01100001. Regroup into sixes: 011110 110010 001001 100001 = 30, 50, 9, 33. In the alphabet, 30 is e, 50 is y, 9 is J, 33 is h: eyJh. Any JSON object starting with {" encodes to eyJ, which is why you can spot JWTs in logs at a glance — and why secret-scanning tools look for that prefix.

2. A JWT decoded and its signature recomputed

Here is a real token for this lesson, signed with HS256 and the 32-byte key k3y-from-vault-please-32-bytes!! (a teaching key; a real one would be random bytes from a vault). The header and payload, as compact JSON:

{"alg":"HS256","typ":"JWT"}
{"iss":"https://auth.example.com","sub":"12","aud":"prep-api",
 "iat":1791360000,"exp":1791360900,"scope":"progress:write"}

The times are Unix seconds: 1791360000 is 2026-10-07 08:00:00 UTC, and exp is 900 seconds later, 08:15. Encoded:

header  = eyJhbGciOiJIUzI1NiIsInR5cCI6IkpXVCJ9
payload = eyJpc3MiOiJodHRwczovL2F1dGguZXhhbXBsZS5jb20iLCJzdWIiOiIxMiIsImF1ZCI6InByZXAtYXBp
          IiwiaWF0IjoxNzkxMzYwMDAwLCJleHAiOjE3OTEzNjA5MDAsInNjb3BlIjoicHJvZ3Jlc3M6d3JpdGUifQ

(The payload is one line; it's wrapped here.) The signing input is header + "." + payload as ASCII: 199 bytes. HS256 means HMAC with SHA-256 (net9 introduced HMAC). HMAC is defined as:

HMAC(K, m) = SHA256( (K' ⊕ opad) ‖ SHA256( (K' ⊕ ipad) ‖ m ) )

where K' is the key padded with zero bytes to SHA-256's 64-byte block size, ipad is the byte 0x36 repeated 64 times, opad is 0x5c repeated, ⊕ is XOR and ‖ is concatenation. Step by step:

  1. Pad the key: 32 bytes of k3y-from-vault-please-32-bytes!! followed by 32 zero bytes.
  2. Inner key: XOR each byte with 0x36. The first byte, k = 0x6B, becomes 0x6B ⊕ 0x36 = 0x5D; the next three give 0x05, 0x4F, 0x1B. Padding bytes become 0x36.
  3. Inner hash: SHA-256 of (inner key ‖ the 199-byte signing input) = dedf718d83e6b9b9…502a18c9.
  4. Outer key: XOR the padded key with 0x5c: first byte 0x6B ⊕ 0x5C = 0x37; padding bytes become 0x5c.
  5. Outer hash: SHA-256 of (outer key ‖ the 32-byte inner hash) = 5a65bea3b737919bc2aa5106bae52168 12c3d9ecd2fedcf6732524876b111b91.
  6. Base64url the 32 bytes without padding: WmW-o7c3kZvCqlEGuuUhaBLD2ezS_tz2cyUkh2sRG5E — 43 characters.

The full token is the three parts joined with dots. To verify, a server repeats steps 1-6 over the first two segments exactly as received and compares the result with the third segment using a constant-time comparison (hmac.compare_digest in Python), which doesn't leak through timing how many leading characters matched. The two-layer structure of HMAC is there to stop length-extension attacks that a naive SHA256(key ‖ message) would allow.

Notice what verifying required: the same key that signed. Any service that can verify this token could also have minted it. That's the defining property of HS256, and the reason the next section exists.

3. Symmetric vs asymmetric signing, and how keys are distributed

JWT signature algorithms come in two families (the names come from RFC 7518, JSON Web Algorithms):

algWhat it isSignature sizeWho can mint
HS256HMAC-SHA256, shared secret32 bytesevery verifier
RS256RSA PKCS#1 v1.5 + SHA-256256 bytes (2048-bit key)private-key holder only
PS256RSA-PSS + SHA-256256 bytesprivate-key holder only
ES256ECDSA on P-256 + SHA-25664 bytesprivate-key holder only
Ed25519 (formerly EdDSA)Edwards-curve signatures64 bytesprivate-key holder only

Symmetric (HS256): fast, tiny, simple — and anyone who can verify can forge. Fine when the issuer and the verifier are the same service, such as a backend issuing tokens to itself. Wrong when tokens are verified by many services, because each becomes a place the secret can leak from and a party that could mint tokens for any user.

Asymmetric (RS256, PS256, ES256, Ed25519): the issuer signs with a private key, verifiers check with the public key. You can hand the public key to anyone, including other companies. RSA has large signatures and slow signing but very fast verification and universal library support; ECDSA and Ed25519 have small signatures and fast signing. Ed25519 is deterministic — it doesn't need a fresh random number per signature — which removes a whole class of ECDSA bugs where a bad random number leaks the private key. In 2025 RFC 9864 registered the algorithm name Ed25519 and deprecated the older, ambiguous EdDSA (which didn't say which curve); expect to see both in the wild for a while.

Public keys are distributed as a JWKS (JSON Web Key Set, RFC 7517): a JSON document listing keys, each with a key type, the key material and a kid (key ID). Issuers publish it at a URL — for OpenID Connect providers it's named in their discovery document, as au6 shows; Google's is linked from https://accounts.google.com/.well-known/openid-configuration, which is exactly the URL this app's backend configures for Google sign-in. Verifiers fetch the set, cache it, and pick the key whose kid matches the token's header. Publishing several keys at once is what makes rotation painless.

4. The validation checklist

Validation is not "check the signature". It's a sequence, and each step exists because skipping it has been exploited. For an access token arriving at your API:

  1. Parse exactly three segments; reject anything else (five segments means JWE, which you probably don't accept).
  2. Algorithm: compare the header's alg against an allow-list configured on the server for this issuer. Never use the header's value to choose the verification method. Reject none unconditionally.
  3. Issuer and key: determine the expected issuer (from configuration, or from the unverified iss but only to select among a fixed set of configured issuers), load that issuer's keys, and select by kid only within that set.
  4. Signature: verify over the received encoded segments, constant-time.
  5. Claims: iss equals the expected issuer exactly; aud contains this service's identifier; exp is in the future and nbf (not before) in the past, with a small clock-skew allowance (30-120 seconds; Authlib, which this app uses, defaults to 120 when parsing ID tokens); iat not absurdly in the future.
  6. Type: check typ, so a token of one kind isn't accepted as another — RFC 9068 gives JWT access tokens typ: at+jwt, distinct from OpenID Connect ID tokens.
  7. Scope and constraints: required scopes present; tenant claim matches the resource's tenant; the signing key is allowed to sign tokens for this population (the Storm-0558 check); jti not on a denylist, if you keep one.
  8. Then authorise: a valid token tells you who and what was granted; whether this caller may act on this object is still au8's question.

5. The classic JWT bugs

Tim McLean's 2015 write-up of vulnerabilities across JWT libraries named the pattern: the token was allowed to choose how it would be verified. Every classic bug is a variant.

alg=none. The JWT specification defines an "unsecured" JWT with "alg":"none" and an empty signature, for cases where integrity is guaranteed some other way. A library whose verify(token, key) reads alg from the header and dispatches on it will happily "verify" a forged token with no signature. Change our payload's sub to "1" and scope to "admin", set the header to {"alg":"none","typ":"JWT"}, end with a trailing dot, and a naive verifier accepts it.

RS256/HS256 key confusion. A service expects RS256 tokens and is configured with the issuer's RSA public key. A library function verify(token, key) uses whatever algorithm the header names. The attacker sets alg: HS256 and computes an HMAC over their forged token using the public key's bytes as the HMAC secret. They know those bytes — the key is public. The library HMACs with "the key" it was given, gets the same result, and accepts. The fix is the same as for none: the server pins the algorithm per key, and libraries now type keys so an RSA public key object can't be used as an HMAC secret.

Header-supplied keys. JWS headers can carry jwk (an embedded public key), jku (a URL to fetch keys from) or x5u (a certificate URL). A verifier that trusts them lets the attacker sign with their own key and tell you where to find it. Ignore these headers unless you explicitly pin the allowed URLs.

kid injection. The kid header is attacker-controlled text. Verifiers that use it as a file path (/keys/../../dev/null, giving an empty key), in a SQL query, or in a shell command turn it into an injection vector. Treat kid as an opaque lookup key into a fixed map.

Missing audience or issuer checks. A token your IdP issued for a low-value internal tool is accepted by the payments API because both trust the same IdP and payments doesn't check aud. Or a service trusting two issuers takes a token signed by issuer A that claims iss B. This is the general shape of Storm-0558's validation flaw: a key trusted for one population was accepted for another.

Signature bugs in crypto libraries. In April 2022 (CVE-2022-21449, "psychic signatures"), Java 15 through 18's ECDSA verification accepted a signature whose two components were both zero as valid for any message and any key, so ES256 JWTs could be forged with a blank signature on affected JVMs. You can't validate your way around that; you patch quickly, which is an argument for knowing exactly which crypto library verifies your tokens.

6. Expiry, refresh tokens and rotation

Because a JWT is checked locally, it can't be recalled. So access tokens are short-lived — 5 to 60 minutes is typical — and the user's longer session is carried by a refresh token: a longer-lived credential that the client presents only to the authorisation server's token endpoint to get a new access token. The access token goes to many APIs and is exposed often; the refresh token goes to one place and can be kept in server-side storage, where it can be revoked.

Refresh token rotation (recommended by RFC 9700, the OAuth security best current practice, for clients that can't otherwise protect refresh tokens) makes theft detectable. Each refresh returns a new refresh token and invalidates the old one. Trace it:

Implementation: store each refresh token hashed with a family ID, user, client, scopes, issued time and a used flag. On refresh, if the token is unused and unexpired, mark it used and issue the next one in the family; if it's already used, revoke every token in the family. Give the family an absolute lifetime (say 30 days) and an idle lifetime (say 7 days), and revoke families on logout, password change and MFA change. Real clients sometimes refresh twice concurrently (two tabs, a retry), so many servers allow the immediately previous token a grace period of a few seconds.

Two further tools reduce the value of stolen tokens. Sender-constrained tokens bind a token to a key the client holds: with DPoP (RFC 9449) the client signs a small proof with a private key on each request and the token records that key's thumbprint; with mTLS-bound tokens (RFC 8705) the token records the client certificate. A thief with the token but not the key gets nothing. And audience restriction means a token stolen from one service is useless at another.

7. JWT vs opaque tokens

An opaque token is just a random string — say 32 random bytes — with no readable content. The resource server learns what it means by asking the authorisation server through introspection (RFC 7662): POST /introspect token=… returns {"active": true, "sub": "12", "scope": "…", "exp": …}. Or it looks it up in a shared store, exactly like a server-side session (au3).

Neither is universally better. A common production shape uses both: clients outside your network get opaque tokens (nothing leaks, revocation is instant, you can change the internal format freely); the gateway introspects once and mints a short-lived internal JWT for the services behind it (sometimes called the "phantom token" pattern). Another uses JWTs everywhere with lifetimes of a few minutes plus an emergency jti denylist.

8. How this app relates

The Prep Console doesn't issue JWTs. Its own session is the signed Flask cookie from au3 — structurally close to an HS256 JWT (payload, timestamp, HMAC), issued and verified by the same service, which is exactly the case where symmetric signing is appropriate. It consumes one JWT: when you sign in with Google, the token response includes an OpenID Connect ID token, and Authlib validates it — signature against Google's published JWKS, issuer, audience equal to the app's client ID, expiry with 120 seconds of leeway, and a nonce it generated for that login — before the app reads email and email_verified. au6 walks through that validation. The Google and GitHub access tokens themselves are used once, server-side, to fetch the profile and verified emails, and are never stored or sent to the browser.

Key rotation done properly

Rotation with a JWKS is a three-phase dance, and the timing comes from cache lifetimes. Phase 1, publish: add the new public key to the JWKS while still signing with the old one, and wait longer than the longest verifier cache TTL, so every verifier has seen the new key before any token uses it. Phase 2, switch: start signing with the new key. Phase 3, retire: keep the old public key published until the last token it signed has expired (the longest token lifetime, plus skew), then remove it. Verifiers should refetch the JWKS when they see an unknown kid, rate-limited so attackers can't force a refetch storm with random kids. The CSRB's central finding about Storm-0558 is what happens when rotation stops: a 2016 key was still trusted in 2023 because manual rotation had been paused after it caused an outage, and nothing alerted on key age. Automate rotation, monitor it, and alert when any active key exceeds its intended age.

Key scope and the blast radius of a key

A signing key's blast radius is everything that trusts it. One key per issuer that signs every token for every customer means one leak compromises everyone. Narrower scopes — separate keys per product, per environment, per region, or per large tenant — limit damage, at the cost of more keys to manage and publish. Scope only helps if verifiers enforce it: a key published for consumer tokens must be rejected when it signs an enterprise token. That is precisely the validation flaw the CSRB describes — Exchange Online accepted a consumer key for enterprise mailboxes — and why the board recommended per-customer key scoping and stateful token validation as practices other cloud providers already followed.

Stateful tokens: detecting forgeries made with real keys

A forged token signed with a stolen key is cryptographically indistinguishable from a real one. Two techniques make forgery detectable anyway. Record a unique ID (jti) for every token at issuance and have high-value verifiers (or an asynchronous auditor) check that the ID exists; a forged token's ID won't. Or embed data only the issuer knows how to produce — an internal sequence, a MAC with a second key held elsewhere — that a forger wouldn't reproduce. The CSRB report notes Google moved its identity system towards stateful tokens with IDs recorded at issuance. The cost is a lookup or a log join, which is why it's often done for high-value tokens or asynchronously for detection rather than inline.

Cross-JWT confusion

Organisations end up with many kinds of JWTs signed by the same keys: ID tokens, access tokens, logout tokens, internal service tokens, email-verification links. If verifiers only check signature and expiry, one kind can be substituted for another — an ID token accepted as an access token, or a token for an "unsubscribe" link accepted by an API. RFC 8725's defences are explicit typing (typ values like at+jwt), distinct audiences, distinct issuers or keys per kind, and verifiers that check all three. Interviewers like "why shouldn't an API accept an OpenID Connect ID token as an access token?": its audience is the client application, not the API, and it says who logged in, not what the bearer may do.

Claims design: what to put in a token

Every claim is readable, travels with every request and is frozen until expiry. That argues for small tokens: subject, tenant, scopes, issuance metadata, and perhaps a few coarse roles. Fine-grained permissions ("can edit documents 4, 9, 172…") don't belong: they bloat tokens beyond header limits (many proxies cap headers at 8-16 KB), go stale the moment a share changes, and leak your data model. Put stable facts in the token and look up volatile ones at decision time (au8). Personal data such as email or name in access tokens ends up in every log that records headers, which is a privacy problem (ae5) as much as a security one.

Clock skew and time

exp, nbf and iat compare against the verifier's clock, so skew matters. A verifier with a clock 5 minutes fast rejects fresh tokens; one 5 minutes slow accepts expired ones. Libraries add leeway (often 0-120 seconds). Keep servers on NTP, keep leeway small, and alert on clock drift. Very short token lifetimes (under a minute) become fragile under skew, which is one reason lifetimes rarely go below a few minutes.

Why bearer tokens are being phased out at the top end

Bearer tokens make every copy as good as the original, which turns any leak — a log line, a HAR file, malware in a browser — into account access. The industry direction for high-value tokens is proof of possession: DPoP for public clients and browsers, mTLS-bound tokens for servers, and device-bound sessions in browsers (au3). The cost is complexity: clients must manage keys, servers must check proofs and track replay (DPoP proofs carry a unique jti and a timestamp that servers must check within a window). For interview answers, the senior move is to name bearer-ness as the root weakness and pick the binding mechanism that fits the client type.

What goes wrong in production

Rotation outages. A new key starts signing before some verifiers have refreshed their JWKS cache; those services reject every new token with "unknown kid" until their cache expires. Symptom: a sudden spike in 401s from a subset of services right after rotation. Fix: publish before you switch, by more than the cache TTL, and refetch on unknown kid.

Clock drift. A host whose NTP broke starts rejecting tokens as "not yet valid" or "expired". Symptom: 401s from one host only. Alert on drift.

Header bloat. Tokens grow as teams add claims (roles, groups, feature flags) until requests exceed proxy header limits and fail with 431 or 400 at a load balancer, often only for users in many groups. Keep tokens small and look up the rest.

Tokens in logs. Access logs, error trackers and debugging middleware that record the Authorization header leak live tokens to everyone with log access. Scan logs for eyJ prefixes; redact by default.

Refresh storms. Thousands of clients refreshing at the same moment (all tokens issued at 9:00 with 60-minute lifetimes) load the token service; add jitter to client refresh timing.

Metrics

Validation failures by reason (signature, expired, audience, issuer, unknown kid) per service; refresh-token reuse detections (each is a likely theft or a client bug); token issuance rate per client; age of each active signing key; JWKS fetch rate and failures; median and p99 token size.

Storm-0558, read as an engineer

The CSRB's review (March 2024) is the most detailed public account of a token-forgery intrusion. Facts from it: the actor compromised Exchange Online mailboxes of 22 organisations and 503 related personal accounts in May and June 2023, downloading about 60,000 emails from the State Department alone; tokens were signed with a Microsoft consumer (MSA) key created in 2016 that was meant to be retired in March 2021; consumer key rotation had been manual and was stopped entirely in 2021 after an outage linked to it, with no alert on key age; a flaw in Exchange Online's token validation let consumer-key-signed tokens access enterprise accounts. Microsoft has not determined how the key was obtained — a September 2023 blog post's crash-dump theory was later corrected — and the board found Microsoft lacked the logs to tell. After detection, Microsoft revoked the key, fixed the validation flaw, rotated other signing keys from more hardened infrastructure, and watched the actor fail to mint new tokens. Every recommendation in the deep section above — automated rotation, key age alerts, HSM storage, key scoping enforced by verifiers, stateful validation, sender-constrained tokens, and logging good enough to detect misuse — appears in the board's list of practices.

Library choice

Use a maintained library with explicit algorithm allow-lists and typed keys (for example PyJWT or joserfc in Python, jose in JavaScript, nimbus-jose-jwt in Java), configure issuer and audience checks explicitly, and subscribe to its security advisories. Write a small test suite of known-bad tokens — alg none, HS256 signed with your RSA public key, wrong audience, expired, future nbf, unknown kid — and run it against every service's validation path.

Goal: sign, verify, break and fix JWTs with Python 3.12's standard library (hmac, hashlib, base64, json, time). About 15 minutes.

  1. Encode. Write b64u(b) = base64.urlsafe_b64encode(b).rstrip(b"=").decode() and its inverse (re-add "=" * (-len(s) % 4)). Encode the header and payload from the body section with json.dumps(..., separators=(",", ":")).
  2. Sign. HMAC-SHA256 the ASCII header.payload with key b"k3y-from-vault-please-32-bytes!!". Your signature must be WmW-o7c3kZvCqlEGuuUhaBLD2ezS_tz2cyUkh2sRG5E.
  3. HMAC by hand. Recompute it without hmac: pad the key to 64 bytes, XOR with 0x36 and 0x5c, two SHA-256 calls. Compare.
  4. Naive verifier. Write verify_naive(token, key) that reads alg from the header and accepts none with an empty signature. Forge a token with sub: "1", scope: "admin", alg: none; watch it pass.
  5. Strict verifier. Write verify(token, key, issuer, audience) implementing the checklist: allow-list {"HS256"}, constant-time compare, iss, aud, exp/nbf with 60 s leeway. Run it on the forged token, on the real token with audience="billing", and on the real token with the time faked past exp.
  6. Refresh rotation. Keep a dict of hashed refresh tokens {hash: {"family": f, "used": False}}. Implement refresh(rt) that rotates, and revokes the family on reuse. Replay an old token and print the family's state.

What to look for: step 2 reproducing the exact 43-character signature proves you've implemented the spec; step 3 matching proves you understand HMAC. Step 4 shows a perfectly unsigned admin token accepted, step 5 rejects all three bad tokens with distinct reasons, and step 6 kills the whole family on the first replay. (RS256 and Ed25519 need big-integer or curve arithmetic that the standard library doesn't provide as signing APIs, so this lab stays symmetric.)

Design: tokens for a public API and 40 internal services

You're designing auth for a B2B platform: a web app, a mobile app, a public REST API used by customers' servers, and 40 internal microservices. Today, a monolith issues HS256 JWTs valid for 24 hours with the user's roles and email inside, stored in localStorage by the web app; every microservice has the HS256 secret. Propose a new design and a migration path, with numbers.

Answer

Problems now. Every one of 40 services holds a secret that can mint any user's token — 40 places to leak a master key. 24-hour bearer tokens can't be revoked, so a fired employee or a stolen token works for up to a day. Roles in the token go stale. Email in every token lands in logs. localStorage exposes tokens to any XSS.

Signing. Move to an authorisation server signing with ES256 or Ed25519 in a KMS/HSM; publish a JWKS; services verify with public keys only. Rotate monthly with the publish → switch → retire phases; alert on key age over 45 days.

Lifetimes. Access tokens 10 minutes; refresh tokens rotating with reuse detection, 7-day idle and 30-day absolute family lifetime. Revocation latency becomes ≤ 10 minutes; for emergencies add a jti/user-epoch denylist checked from an in-memory cache refreshed every few seconds.

Claims. iss, sub, tenant, aud, scope, exp, iat, jti, typ=at+jwt. No email, no fine-grained roles; services look up permissions (au8) with short caching.

Audience. Mint per-audience tokens (or exchange at the gateway) so a token for the notifications service isn't accepted by billing.

Clients. Web app: backend-for-frontend holding tokens server-side, browser gets an HttpOnly SameSite session cookie. Mobile: authorisation code + PKCE (au5), refresh tokens in the OS keystore, DPoP if supported. Customer servers: client-credentials grant with private-key JWT client authentication, tokens audience-bound to the public API.

Migration. Phase 1: services accept both old HS256 and new ES256 tokens (separate issuer configs, separate allow-lists). Phase 2: clients move to the new flows; old tokens' 24-hour lifetime means one day after the last client switches, no old tokens remain. Phase 3: remove the HS256 secret from all 40 services and rotate it anyway, since it was widely held. Test every service against the bad-token suite (alg none, HS256-with-public-key, wrong aud, expired) before and after.
