On 20 July 2022, more than a hundred Cloudflare employees and family members received text messages telling them their work schedules had changed, with a link to a convincing copy of Cloudflare's Okta login page on a freshly registered domain. At least 76 employees received them; three typed in their usernames and passwords. The phishing kit was built to beat two-factor authentication: as soon as a victim entered credentials, they were relayed to the attacker through Telegram, and when the real site asked for a one-time code, the fake page asked the victim for it and relayed that too, inside its 30-second window. Against most companies' MFA, those three people would have handed over working sessions.

Nothing happened. Every Cloudflare employee logged in with a FIDO2 hardware security key, and a security key doesn't produce a code that can be relayed. It signs a challenge together with the address of the site that's asking — and the site asking was the phishing domain. The signature was useless at the real login page. Around the same time the same attackers went after Twilio, and researchers later linked the campaign to well over a hundred targeted organisations; where employees typed codes, it got in.

Two months after that, Uber was breached when an attacker who had a contractor's password triggered push approval prompts over and over until the contractor accepted one. In 2023, Retool lost control of an employee account after an SMS phish and a deepfaked phone call — and discovered the employee's authenticator codes had been synced to the same Google account the attacker then controlled.

All three companies "had MFA". What separated them is what kind. This topic explains the second factors in use today from the inside — computing a TOTP code by hand from a secret and a timestamp, then seeing why codes and push prompts fall to real-time phishing, why SMS is classified as restricted, and how WebAuthn and passkeys bind a login to the website's origin so that phishing simply fails. Then it deals with the part attackers increasingly target instead: enrolment, lost devices and account recovery, which are only as strong as their weakest path.

Two kinds of proof

Imagine two ways to prove to a bank teller that you're you over the phone. In the first, you and the bank agreed on a secret codebook years ago; the teller asks for today's code and you read it out. In the second, you have a sealing ring; the teller reads you a random phrase plus the name of the bank, you seal a letter containing both and post it, and the teller checks the seal against the impression on file.

Now a con artist calls you pretending to be the bank. With the codebook, you read today's code to the con artist, who immediately reads it to the real bank: you've been relayed. With the ring, the con artist reads you a phrase — but your letter says the name of whoever actually asked, and the ring is engraved to only seal letters naming your real bank. The con artist gets a letter that the real bank will refuse. That's the difference between codes (TOTP, SMS, email codes) and origin-bound signatures (WebAuthn). Both are "something you have"; only the second is phishing-resistant.

Where the analogy breaks: in WebAuthn you don't check the name yourself — the browser writes the real origin into what's signed, so it works even when the fake site looks perfect. People can't reliably spot phishing; the design doesn't ask them to.

Why a second factor at all

A password is one secret, typed into many places, remembered by a human and reused across sites — every weakness in au2 follows from that. A second factor adds evidence of a different kind, so an attacker who has the password still lacks something. The categories are something you know, something you have and something you are; MFA means two different categories, so a password plus a security question is still one factor. In practice "something you have" is a phone or a security key holding a secret or a private key, and "something you are" is a fingerprint or face check that unlocks that key locally. The question for the rest of this topic is how each kind of "have" can be stolen, copied or talked out of its owner.

1. One-time passwords from a shared secret: HOTP

The code-generating apps (Google Authenticator, Microsoft Authenticator, 1Password and many others) implement two RFCs. HOTP (RFC 4226, 2005) defines a code from a shared secret K and a counter C:

HOTP(K, C) = Truncate(HMAC-SHA1(K, C)) mod 10^digits

C is an 8-byte big-endian integer. HMAC-SHA1 (the keyed hash from au4, with SHA-1 inside) gives 20 bytes. Dynamic truncation turns them into a short number: take the low 4 bits of the last byte as an offset (0-15), read the 4 bytes starting at that offset as a big-endian integer, clear the top bit (so the result is a non-negative 31-bit number regardless of signed arithmetic), and reduce modulo 106 for six digits. With a counter, both sides must stay in sync — each button press advances it — which is awkward, so most systems use time instead.

2. TOTP, computed by hand

TOTP (RFC 6238, 2011) replaces the counter with the number of 30-second steps since the Unix epoch: T = floor(unix_time / 30). Both sides compute the same T as long as their clocks roughly agree.

Let's compute one. Enrolment gave the user a QR code containing otpauth://totp/Example:ada?secret=JBSWY3DPEHPK3PXP&issuer=Example. The secret is in base32 (letters A-Z and digits 2-7, chosen so it can be typed without ambiguity). JBSWY3DPEHPK3PXP decodes to the 10 bytes 48 65 6c 6c 6f 21 de ad be ef — "Hello!" followed by 0xDEADBEEF, a well-known demo secret. (Real secrets are 20 random bytes; RFC 4226 requires at least 16 and recommends 20.)

The time is 2026-10-07 08:00:00 UTC, Unix time 1791360000.

  1. Time step. T = floor(1791360000 / 30) = 59,712,000 = 0x038F2200.
  2. Counter bytes. 8 bytes big-endian: 00 00 00 00 03 8f 22 00.
  3. HMAC. HMAC-SHA1(secret, counter) = b9 b2 3b fc 66 b4 1e 21 a0 a3 f0 54 b4 a6 ea 0f b9 9f a3 15.
  4. Offset. The last byte is 0x15; its low 4 bits are 0x5, so offset = 5.
  5. Four bytes from offset 5. Bytes 5-8 are b4 1e 21 a0 = 0xB41E21A0.
  6. Clear the top bit. 0xB41E21A0 & 0x7FFFFFFF = 0x341E21A0 = 874,389,920.
  7. Six digits. 874,389,920 mod 1,000,000 = 389920.

The authenticator app shows 389920 from 08:00:00 to 08:00:29. The codes for the steps either side are 560726 (07:59:30) and 529698 (08:00:30). You can check an implementation against RFC 6238's own test vector: with the ASCII secret 12345678901234567890, time 59 seconds and 8 digits, SHA-1 gives 94287082.

3. Verifying TOTP on the server

The server stores the shared secret for each user (encrypted — it's equivalent to the authenticator) and, on login, computes codes for T−1, T and T+1 to tolerate clock drift and typing time. Three details matter:

  • Replay. A code is valid for its whole step. Record the last accepted T per user and reject codes for that step or earlier, or a code shoulder-surfed or captured from a log can be reused within seconds.
  • Rate limiting. With three acceptable codes at any moment, each guess succeeds with probability 3 in 106. With NIST's cap of 100 consecutive failures, an attacker's total chance is about 100 × 3×10−6 = 0.03%. Without limits, 10 guesses a second for a day is 864,000 guesses, and 1 − (1 − 3×10−6)864,000 ≈ 1 − e−2.59 ≈ 92%. Six digits are safe only because attempts are limited per account.
  • Secret handling. The secret appears once, in the enrolment QR code; confirm enrolment by asking for one valid code, then never display it again. Because the server holds it, a database breach that includes TOTP secrets lets an attacker generate every user's codes — unlike passkeys, where the server holds only public keys.

4. Push approvals and their failure modes

Push MFA sends a notification to an enrolled app: "Approve sign-in?" The user taps Approve, and the app signs the approval with a key it holds. It's convenient and the cryptography is sound. The weakness is the human step. In MFA fatigue (or "push bombing"), an attacker who has the password triggers approval after approval — late at night, dozens of times — until the user taps Approve to make it stop, or believes a follow-up message from "IT" saying to accept it. Uber's September 2022 breach began this way: the attacker repeatedly tried to log in to a contractor's account, each attempt sent an approval request, and eventually the contractor accepted one.

Push is also relayable: a phishing page can trigger the real site's push at the moment the victim expects one, and the victim approves the attacker's session believing it's their own.

Mitigations: number matching — the login page displays a two-digit number that the user must type into the app — ties the approval to the screen in front of the user (it doesn't stop a relay where the phishing page shows the number, but it stops blind approvals and fatigue); show the requesting location, device and application in the prompt; rate-limit pushes and alert security on bursts; give users a "deny and report" button that locks the login. Treat push as a convenience factor, not a phishing defence.

5. SMS, voice and email codes

SMS codes are TOTP's weaker cousin: the server generates a random code and texts it. They add the phone network to the attack surface. SIM swapping — persuading or bribing a mobile carrier's staff to move a number to a SIM the attacker holds — gives the attacker every subsequent text. Number porting to another carrier does the same. Weaknesses in the SS7 signalling network between carriers have been used to intercept messages, and phone malware can read them. NIST SP 800-63B-4 designates authentication through the public switched telephone network as restricted: allowed, but verifiers must offer an alternative and assess the risk. Email codes inherit the security of the user's mailbox, which is often protected by nothing more than a reused password.

The honest summary: SMS stops credential stuffing (the attacker has the password but not the phone) and fails against targeted attacks. It's acceptable as a consumer baseline where nothing better is adopted and wrong for administrators.

6. WebAuthn: public-key login bound to the origin

WebAuthn (W3C Web Authentication; Level 3 became a W3C Recommendation on 25 August 2026) is a browser API that lets a website create and use public-key credentials. Together with CTAP — the protocol between the browser and external authenticators such as USB security keys — it makes up the FIDO Alliance's FIDO2. The parts:

  • The relying party (RP) is the website, identified by an RP ID, normally its registrable domain such as example.com.
  • The authenticator holds private keys: a roaming authenticator (a security key over USB, NFC or Bluetooth) or a platform authenticator built into the device (Windows Hello, Apple's Touch ID/Face ID with iCloud Keychain, Android's screen lock with Google Password Manager), or a password manager acting as one.
  • The credential is a key pair created for one RP ID; the RP stores the public key and a credential ID.

Registration (navigator.credentials.create()): the server sends a random challenge, its RP ID and name, a user handle (an opaque user ID, not an email) and acceptable algorithms (COSE identifiers: −7 for ES256, −8 for EdDSA, −257 for RS256). The authenticator creates a new key pair scoped to that RP ID, checks the user is present (a touch) and optionally verifies them (PIN or biometric), and returns the public key and credential ID, optionally with an attestation — a signed statement about the authenticator model, useful in enterprises that only allow certain hardware. The server checks the challenge and origin, then stores credential ID, public key, signature counter and flags.

Authentication (navigator.credentials.get()): the server sends a fresh challenge (and optionally the allowed credential IDs). The browser builds clientDataJSON:

{"type":"webauthn.get",
 "challenge":"q8E3…(base64url of the server's random bytes)",
 "origin":"https://example.com","crossOrigin":false}

The authenticator finds a credential for the RP ID, checks presence (and verification if requested), and signs:

authenticatorData = SHA-256("example.com")  (32 bytes, the RP ID hash)
                  ‖ flags (1 byte: UP, UV, BE, BS, …)
                  ‖ signCount (4 bytes)
signature = Sign(private key, authenticatorData ‖ SHA-256(clientDataJSON))

The server verifies: the challenge is one it issued and hasn't expired or been used; type is webauthn.get; origin is exactly its own origin; the RP ID hash equals SHA-256 of its RP ID; the flags meet policy — UP (user present) always, UV (user verified) if it wants two factors in one; the signature verifies with the stored public key; and the signature counter has increased if the authenticator uses one (a counter going backwards suggests a cloned authenticator; synced passkeys often report 0).

Why phishing fails. Two independent bindings. The authenticator only uses a credential on the RP ID it was created for, and the browser only lets a page request RP IDs matching its own domain — so on examp1e-login.com the real site's credential is simply unavailable. And even if an attacker proxies the real site's challenge, the browser writes the phishing page's origin into clientDataJSON, which is inside the signature, and the real server rejects it. The user doesn't need to notice anything. That's the formal meaning of phishing-resistant: the authenticator output can't be relayed to the real site from anywhere else.

Note what the server stores: a public key. A database breach gives the attacker nothing to log in with and nothing to crack.

7. Passkeys

A passkey is the consumer name for a FIDO/WebAuthn discoverable credential — one stored on the authenticator together with the user handle, so the site can ask "any credential for example.com?" without the user typing a username first. With user verification, a passkey is a whole login in one step: possession of the device's key plus the local PIN or biometric is two factors. Two kinds matter for policy:

  • Synced passkeys are copied across a user's devices by their platform or password manager, protected by end-to-end encryption tied to the user's cloud account. Lose the phone, and the passkey is on the laptop and the new phone. WebAuthn's flags report this: BE (backup eligible) and BS (backed up).
  • Device-bound passkeys live only in one device's secure hardware — a hardware security key, or a platform key marked non-syncable. Lose the device, and the credential is gone.

NIST SP 800-63B-4 accepts syncable authenticators at AAL2 but says they shall not be used at AAL3, which requires a non-exportable private key. The usual policy follows: synced passkeys for customers and most employees (they fix recovery, the biggest practical problem with hardware keys), two hardware keys each for administrators and anyone with production access.

Can this app use passkeys? Indirectly, yes: the Prep Console has no passwords and delegates authentication to Google and GitHub, both of which support passkeys and security keys. A user who protects their Google account with a passkey gets phishing-resistant sign-in to the Prep Console without the app doing anything. That's one of federation's real benefits (au6): the strongest authenticator the IdP supports becomes available to every relying application.

8. Enrolment and recovery: the weakest link

Every MFA system has three doors besides the login: enrolment (adding a factor), removal (deleting one) and recovery (getting in when factors are lost). Attackers go for whichever is weakest.

  • Enrolment must require an existing strong factor (or happen during a verified onboarding). If a session from a phished password can add a new authenticator, MFA protects nothing — Retool's attacker added their own device to the employee's Okta account.
  • Recovery codes: show 8-10 single-use random codes at enrolment, store them hashed like passwords (au2), and let users regenerate them.
  • Multiple authenticators: encourage or require two (a phone passkey and a hardware key, or synced passkeys on two devices), so losing one isn't a recovery event.
  • Slow paths for true loss: identity verification, a waiting period of a day or more with notifications to every registered channel so the real owner can cancel, and limits on what a freshly recovered account can do immediately (no payout or email changes).
  • Help desks: scripted verification that doesn't rely on knowledge an attacker can research or a voice that can be faked; for privileged accounts, in-person or video verification plus manager approval. Retool's attacker used a deepfaked voice of a member of IT staff.
  • Synced secrets: if TOTP seeds sync to the same cloud account that a password unlocks, the second factor collapses into the first. Retool's write-up describes exactly that with Google Authenticator's cloud sync.

What exactly makes WebAuthn phishing-resistant — and what doesn't

Phishing resistance comes from the browser, not the cryptography alone. The browser enforces that a page at origin O can only request credentials for RP IDs that are O's domain or a registrable suffix of it, and it writes O into clientDataJSON. An attacker who controls the browser (malware), or a native app that implements its own WebAuthn client without those checks, can break the binding — which is why platforms require apps to prove association with a domain (Apple's associated domains, Android's Digital Asset Links) before they can use that domain's passkeys. Similarly, an attacker who controls a subdomain of the RP ID can request the parent's credentials, because RP IDs are domain-scoped; keep untrusted content off subdomains of your RP ID (the same advice as for SameSite cookies in au3). And phishing resistance protects the login; it does nothing about a session stolen afterwards by infostealer malware, which is why device-bound sessions (au3) are the natural companion.

Attestation: when to care

Attestation lets an authenticator prove its model (via an AAGUID and a certificate chain from the manufacturer, checked against the FIDO Metadata Service). Consumer sites generally request no attestation: it adds friction, leaks device information and excludes users. Enterprises and regulated environments use it to require specific certified hardware — for example to meet AAL3, where the RP must know the key is non-exportable. Synced passkey providers usually return no attestation, which is itself a signal that the key isn't hardware-bound.

Signature counters and cloned authenticators

WebAuthn authenticators may include a counter that increments with each signature. If the server sees a counter lower than or equal to the stored one, the credential may have been cloned (two copies signing independently). For hardware keys that's a useful tripwire. Synced passkeys legitimately exist on several devices, so they typically report a counter of 0, and servers must not treat that as cloning. Policy should key off the BE/BS flags: for device-bound credentials, enforce counter monotonicity; for synced ones, ignore it.

Conditional UI and passkey adoption

Adoption depends on UX. WebAuthn's "conditional mediation" lets the browser offer passkeys in the username field's autofill dropdown, so users who have a passkey just pick it, and users who don't see an ordinary form. Combined with prompts to create a passkey after a password login, this lets sites migrate gradually without a hard switch. The security payoff arrives only when the password path is removed or demoted for users who have passkeys; otherwise attackers simply phish the password path. Measure the fraction of logins that used a phishing-resistant method, not the fraction of users who registered one.

Risk-based and adaptive authentication

Not every login needs the strongest factor. Adaptive systems score each attempt — known device cookie, IP reputation, impossible travel, time of day, recent failures — and decide whether to skip, require or step up MFA. Done well, it reduces friction for routine logins and concentrates challenges on suspicious ones. Done badly, it becomes an oracle (attackers probe until they find conditions that skip MFA) and a source of inconsistency. Risk signals should only ever add requirements to a baseline policy, never remove the requirement for privileged access.

The economics of attacks on MFA

Every step up the hierarchy changes the attacker's cost structure. Against passwords alone, attacks are automated and scale to millions of accounts (credential stuffing). SMS and TOTP force attackers to phish in real time or SIM-swap — still automatable with phishing kits, but per-victim. Push forces a live interaction with the victim. Origin-bound credentials force attackers to compromise the device itself or a recovery path, which is expensive and noisy. Real attacker behaviour follows: kits for relaying codes are sold as a service, help-desk social engineering has grown as phishing-resistant MFA spreads, and session-cookie theft by infostealers bypasses MFA entirely. A senior answer names where the attacker will go next once you deploy each control.

Passwordless without passkeys: magic links

"Passwordless" also describes email magic links and one-time codes by email. They remove passwords (and so credential stuffing) but move all security to the user's email account and are phishable: an attacker can trigger a code and ask the victim to read it out. Treat them as convenience login equivalent to an email-based factor, keep links short-lived and single-use, bind them to the browser that requested them where possible (so a link opened on another device asks for confirmation), and don't treat them as MFA on their own.

What goes wrong in deployments

Fallback paths that undo the upgrade. A company deploys security keys but leaves "send me a code instead" enabled; phishing kits simply choose the code path. Inventory every way to satisfy MFA per user and remove the weak ones for high-risk groups.

Enrolment hijack. New-device enrolment allowed with only a password, or by help-desk ticket; attackers phish the password, enrol their own authenticator, and now hold a legitimate factor. Require an existing strong factor or verified identity for enrolment, and alert the user on every new authenticator.

Clock drift breaking TOTP for users whose phones have the wrong time; widen the window slightly or show a clear error, never widen it to minutes.

Lost-device floods after a hardware-key rollout without second keys — the help desk drowns and is tempted to shortcut verification. Ship two keys, or pair a key with a synced passkey.

Push bombing: a user reports dozens of prompts at 2 a.m. Treat it as a confirmed password compromise: force a password change, revoke sessions, and check for any approved prompt.

Metrics

Share of logins by method (password-only, SMS, TOTP, push, WebAuthn); share of privileged logins using phishing-resistant methods (target 100%); MFA challenge failure rate and push denial rate; push requests per user per hour (fatigue detection); new-authenticator enrolments and removals per day; recovery requests and their approval rate; time from report to lock for "this wasn't me".

The three cases, read as engineers

Cloudflare, July 2022: SMS phishing to at least 76 employees, three entered credentials, a kit relayed credentials and codes via Telegram in real time and attempted to install AnyDesk remote-access software; every employee used FIDO2 keys with origin binding, so no account was compromised. Cloudflare then blocked the phishing domain and searched for affected devices. Uber, September 2022: a contractor's credentials were stolen (Uber believes via malware on a personal device); repeated login attempts each sent a push approval, and the contractor eventually accepted one. Retool, August 2023: SMS phishing led an employee to a fake portal, a deepfaked voice call impersonating IT obtained an MFA code, the attacker added their own device to the employee's Okta account, and Google Authenticator's cloud sync meant compromising the employee's Google account exposed their codes; 27 cloud customers were affected. Together they draw the hierarchy this topic teaches: codes are relayed, pushes are approved, synced secrets inherit their account's weakness, and only origin-bound keys held.

Talking to users

Users need to know three things, and most products tell them none: what a legitimate prompt looks like (and that you will never phone them for a code), what to do when they get a prompt they didn't trigger (deny and report, with one tap), and what happens when they lose a device (the recovery path and how long it takes). Clear messaging reduces both fatigue approvals and help-desk pressure to shortcut verification.

Rollout guidance

Start with the accounts whose compromise is catastrophic (identity-provider admins, cloud root, CI/CD, finance), give them two hardware keys and remove all other methods for them. Then move all employees to passkeys with a sunset date for codes. For customers, offer passkeys with conditional UI and prompts after login, keep TOTP for those who want it, and treat SMS as a last resort. Throughout, harden enrolment and recovery first — otherwise each improvement just redirects attackers to the help desk.

Goal: implement HOTP/TOTP from the RFCs and a server-side verifier with replay protection and rate limits, using Python 3.12's standard library (hmac, hashlib, struct, base64, time). About 15 minutes.

  1. HOTP. Write hotp(key, counter, digits=6): struct.pack(">Q", counter), hmac.new(key, msg, "sha1").digest(), offset h[-1] & 0x0F, struct.unpack(">I", h[o:o+4])[0] & 0x7FFFFFFF, modulo 10**digits.
  2. RFC check. TOTP with key b"12345678901234567890", time 59, 8 digits must give 94287082.
  3. This lesson's code. base64.b32decode("JBSWY3DPEHPK3PXP"), time 1791360000 → 389920; times 1791359970 and 1791360030 → 560726 and 529698. Print the intermediate HMAC and offset and compare with the body.
  4. Your phone. Generate a 20-byte secret with secrets.token_bytes(20), base32 it, build the otpauth://totp/Lab:you?secret=…&issuer=Lab URI and type the secret into an authenticator app (or render a QR code if you have a tool). Compare its codes with yours for a minute.
  5. Verifier. verify(user, code, now) accepts T−1..T+1, rejects any T ≤ the user's last accepted step (replay), and locks after 5 consecutive failures with exponential backoff.
  6. Guessing. Simulate an attacker sending random codes at your verifier without the lockout for 100,000 attempts and count successes; then with it.

What to look for: steps 2-3 reproduce the RFC vector and the lesson's numbers exactly. Step 4 matches a real authenticator app, proving there's no magic. Step 5 rejects the same code twice in one window. Step 6 finds roughly 0.3 successes per 100,000 guesses (3 × 10−6 each) without lockout — near-certain success over a day at scale — and essentially none with it.

Decide: MFA strategy for a fintech with consumers, support agents and engineers

A payments app has 2 million consumers (SMS MFA at login today), 300 support agents who can change customers' phone numbers and emails, and 80 engineers with production access (TOTP). A phishing campaign is targeting support agents, and SIM-swap fraud against consumers is rising. Design the next 6 months, naming what you'd accept for each group and why.

Answer

Engineers (highest blast radius). Two FIDO2 hardware keys each, enforced at the IdP for SSO and production access, all other methods removed for them; device-bound, so AAL3-capable. Enrolment of a new key requires an existing key or in-person verification. Done in the first month.

Support agents (targeted now, and they control recovery). Hardware keys or device-bound platform passkeys, no codes. Their tools are a recovery path for 2 million accounts, so: changing a customer's phone or email requires a second agent's approval or the customer's confirmation via an existing passkey; every change notifies the old channel; changes trigger a cooling-off period before payouts. Log and review agent actions.

Consumers. Offer synced passkeys with conditional UI and prompt after each SMS login; target passkeys as default for new devices. For users still on SMS: block payouts for 24-72 hours after a phone-number change or new-device login, detect SIM swaps (carrier APIs where available, or sudden number-change signals), and require step-up via an existing passkey for adding payees. Keep TOTP as an option. Recovery: recovery codes at enrolment, waiting periods with notifications, and no agent override without strong verification.

Measure. Phishing-resistant share of logins per group (engineers and agents 100% by month 2), consumer passkey adoption, SIM-swap-related fraud losses, agent-initiated account changes per day and their reversal rate.
