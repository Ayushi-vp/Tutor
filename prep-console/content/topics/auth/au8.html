Imagine you're building a document editor. On day one, authorisation is one line: if doc.owner_id == user.id. Then customers ask to share a document with a colleague, so you add a shared_with list. Then they want to share with a whole team, so you add groups. Then folders, and documents should inherit a folder's sharing. Then "anyone in my company with the link can view". Then enterprise admins want "nobody may share outside the company", and legal wants "documents labelled confidential can't be downloaded by contractors". Then a support engineer needs to see a customer's document for an hour to debug it, with the customer's permission. Each rule is reasonable. Two years later, the authorisation logic is spread across forty handlers, no two of which agree about inheritance, and the security team can't answer the simplest audit question: "who can see this document, and why?"

Google faced this across Drive, Docs, Photos, Calendar, YouTube and Cloud, and in 2019 published how it solved it: Zanzibar, a single authorisation system holding trillions of access-control entries and answering millions of checks per second with 95th-percentile latency under 10 milliseconds and better than 99.999% availability over three years. Its central idea — represent permissions as relationships in a graph and derive access by following them — has since spawned a family of open-source systems.

Not every product needs Zanzibar. Many need roles and an ownership check; some need attribute rules; most large ones end up combining models. au1 established that authorisation is the decision "may this subject do this action on this resource?", made on every request at the object level. This topic is about how to make that decision well at scale: the four classic models (ACLs, RBAC, ABAC, ReBAC) and what each is good and bad at; the same question — "can Alice edit doc 42?" — answered by RBAC and by ReBAC step by step; how Zanzibar works, including the consistency problem it names the "new enemy"; policy engines (OPA and Cedar) that move rules out of application code; and where enforcement should live in a distributed system. This app's own authorisation, two roles and ownership scoping, serves as the small baseline.

Four ways to run a building's doors

Picture four ways to decide who may enter which room in an office building.

A list on every door: room 401 has a sheet saying "Alice, Bob, Carol". That's an access control list (ACL). Very direct: to know who can enter 401, read its sheet. Painful when Alice changes teams and you must find every sheet with her name.

Badges by job: everyone gets a badge type — engineer, finance, visitor — and each door accepts certain types. That's role-based access control (RBAC). Easy to manage and audit ("what can engineers open?"), but it can't express "Alice may enter Bob's office while Bob is on holiday" without inventing a new badge type, and soon there are hundreds of badge types.

A guard who checks a rulebook: "finance staff may enter finance rooms during business hours if their security training is current". The guard looks at facts about you, the room and the time. That's attribute-based access control (ABAC). Flexible, but the guard is only as good as the facts (is training status up to date?) and the rulebook can become unreadable.

Following relationships: "you may enter a room if you're a member of a team that owns the floor it's on". To decide, the guard follows links: room → floor → owning team → members. That's relationship-based access control (ReBAC). It models how organisations actually share things — through ownership, membership and containment — and one change (Alice leaves the team) correctly updates access to everything reached through that link.

The analogy's limit: in software, decisions happen millions of times a second, across many services, on data that changes constantly. That turns the choice of model into a systems problem — where the data lives, how fresh it is, how fast a check runs — as much as a modelling one.

What every model must get right

Whichever model you pick, four properties decide whether it works in production. Correctness: the rules say what the business means, including the awkward cases (a user in two teams, a document moved between folders, a contractor who becomes an employee). Freshness: when someone loses access, how many seconds until every service agrees? Speed: a check runs on nearly every request, so it has a latency budget of a few milliseconds at most. Explainability: for any decision, someone can say why — which role, attribute or relationship produced it — because auditors, customers and your own on-call engineers will ask. Models trade these off differently: ACLs are explainable and fresh but awkward to manage; roles are fast and explainable but coarse; attributes are flexible but hard to explain; relationship graphs are expressive and explainable but need careful engineering for speed and freshness.

If you stop here: roles for coarse job functions, relationships for sharing and hierarchy, attributes for context like time, location and labels — and in every case, default deny, one place where the rules live, and enforcement where the object is loaded.

1. The vocabulary of a decision

Every authorisation decision has the same inputs: a subject (who is asking — a user, a service, an agent, as au1 described), an action (edit, view, delete, export), a resource (document 42, invoice 1043, the admin panel) and context (time, IP address, whether MFA was used recently, the tenant). Its output is allow or deny. Architecture adds three roles, standardised in ABAC literature such as NIST SP 800-162: the policy enforcement point (PEP), which intercepts the request and enforces the result; the policy decision point (PDP), which computes it; and the policy information point (PIP), which supplies facts the PDP needs — group memberships, document owners, labels.

Two rules apply to every model. Default deny: if no rule grants access, the answer is no, and errors or missing data lead to no. Least privilege: grant the minimum for the minimum time. The models differ in how they express grants.

2. Access control lists

An ACL attaches to each resource a list of entries (principal, permissions). File systems are the classic example: a POSIX file has owner, group and other permissions, and extended ACLs add named users and groups; cloud storage buckets and objects have ACLs too. Checking is a lookup: read the resource's ACL, see if the subject (or a group they're in) has the action.

ACLs are excellent for per-object sharing ("share this one file with Bob"), and the question "who can access this resource?" is answered by reading one list. They're weak at the reverse question — "what can Bob access?" means scanning every ACL — and at change: when Bob leaves the company, every ACL mentioning him must be found. Groups in ACL entries help, but inheritance (a folder's ACL applying to its contents) must be implemented by copying or by walking parents, and copying goes stale. Cloud providers' experience is telling: AWS now recommends disabling S3 object ACLs and using policies instead, because per-object ACLs made it too easy to make individual objects public unnoticed.

3. Role-based access control

RBAC inserts roles between users and permissions: permissions are granted to roles (editor → document.edit, document.view), users are assigned roles. NIST's RBAC model adds role hierarchies (admin inherits editor) and constraints such as separation of duties (the person who creates a supplier can't also approve payments to it).

RBAC's strengths are management and audit. "What can editors do?" and "who are the admins?" are single queries. Onboarding is assigning a role that matches a job. Most products have some RBAC, even if it's two roles. This app has exactly that: signed-in users and administrators, where "administrator" is decided by the email being in the ADMIN_EMAILS environment setting, re-evaluated on every request by admin_required.

Its weakness is that roles don't know about objects. "Editor" means editor of everything the role covers. In multi-tenant products that's fixed by scoping roles to a tenant (Alice is an editor in Acme), which is still coarse. When products need per-object exceptions — this one project, this one document, this customer's records — teams create roles for them, and you get role explosion: thousands of roles that nobody can audit. Role explosion is the clearest signal that some decisions belong to attributes or relationships.

4. Attribute-based access control

ABAC computes decisions from attributes using policies. Attributes come from the subject (department, clearance, employment type), the resource (owner, classification label, region), the action, and the environment (time, network, device posture, how recently MFA was used). A policy might read: "Allow view if subject.department == resource.department and resource.label != 'restricted', or if the subject is the resource's owner; deny all download by contractors from unmanaged devices."

ABAC handles context that roles can't — time windows, data residency, device trust, step-up freshness — and avoids role explosion by expressing rules over properties. Its costs: decisions depend on attributes being correct and fresh (a stale department attribute grants the wrong access); policies can become hard to read, so reasoning about "who can see this?" requires evaluating policies against every user; and a missing attribute must evaluate to deny, not to an accidental allow. Good ABAC systems treat policies as code: versioned, reviewed, unit-tested and analysed.

5. Relationship-based access control

ReBAC represents facts as relationships — user is member of group, group is editor of folder, folder is parent of document — and derives permissions by following them. It fits collaborative products, where access spreads through sharing, groups and containment, and it makes changes cheap: removing Alice from one group changes her access to everything reached through that group, without touching any document.

Google's Zanzibar is the reference design. Its data model is the relation tuple:

⟨object⟩#⟨relation⟩@⟨user⟩            doc:42#owner@user:carol
⟨object⟩#⟨relation⟩@⟨object#relation⟩   folder:7#editor@group:design#member

The second form uses a userset — "everyone who is a member of group:design" — as the subject, which is how groups and nesting work. Each namespace (document, folder, group) has a configuration with userset rewrites that define relations in terms of others:

document:
  owner   = this
  editor  = this ∪ owner ∪ (parent → editor)     # direct editors, owners, and the parent folder's editors
  viewer  = this ∪ editor ∪ (parent → viewer)
folder:
  editor  = this ∪ (parent → editor)
group:
  member  = this

this means directly stored tuples; owner inside editor means "owners are also editors" (a computed userset); parent → editor means "follow this object's parent tuple and take that object's editors" (Zanzibar calls it tuple-to-userset). Services ask four kinds of question: Check (does user U have relation R on object O?), Read (list tuples), Expand (show the full tree of who has R on O) and Write; a Watch API streams changes for building indexes.

6. Worked example: "can Alice edit doc 42?"

The setup: tenant Acme. Doc 42 lives in folder 7, a design team folder. Alice is a designer. Bob is a salesperson who's an editor in Acme generally. Carol owns doc 42.

With RBAC. The data:

role_permissions: editor → {document.view, document.edit}; viewer → {document.view}
user_roles:       (alice, acme) → editor;  (bob, acme) → editor;  (dave, acme) → viewer
documents:        42 → tenant acme

Check: Alice's roles in doc 42's tenant (Acme) = {editor}; editor's permissions include document.edit; allow. One lookup of Alice's roles, one of the role's permissions, one of the document's tenant. Now the same check for Bob: also allow — and for every other document in Acme. RBAC can't say "design documents are editable by designers only" without roles like design-editor, and can't say "Carol shared this one document with Dave as editor" without a per-document exception, which is exactly how role explosion begins.

With ReBAC. The tuples:

doc:42#owner@user:carol
doc:42#parent@folder:7
folder:7#editor@group:design#member
group:design#member@user:alice
group:sales#member@user:bob

Check doc:42#editor@user:alice, following the rewrite editor = this ∪ owner ∪ (parent → editor):

  1. this: is there a tuple doc:42#editor@user:alice? Read 1 — no.
  2. owner: is Alice the owner? The owner tuple names Carol (same read) — no.
  3. parent → editor: read doc 42's parent tuples (read 2) — folder:7. Now check folder:7#editor@user:alice: its this tuples (read 3) include the userset group:design#member. Check group:design#member@user:alice (read 4) — yes.
  4. Result: allow, with the path doc:42 → parent folder:7 → editor group:design → member alice.

The same check for Bob fails at step 3 (Bob is in sales, not design). If Carol shares doc 42 with Dave as editor, that's one new tuple doc:42#editor@user:dave, and step 1 succeeds for Dave on doc 42 only. If Alice leaves the design team, one tuple is deleted and every document reached through folder 7 or any other design-team grant is no longer editable by her. And "why can Alice edit this?" has a precise answer: the path.

7. Zanzibar's consistency problem: the new enemy

Authorisation data changes, and checks read replicas and caches. Zanzibar's paper names the danger the new enemy problem and gives two examples. First: Alice removes Bob from a folder's ACL, then adds a new confidential document to it; if a check for Bob reads a stale snapshot taken before the removal while the document is already visible, Bob sees content added after he was removed. Second: Alice removes Bob, then asks Charlie to add new content; the ordering between different users' actions must also be respected.

Zanzibar solves it with Google's Spanner database, whose TrueTime clocks give a global ordering of commits, and with zookies: opaque tokens encoding a timestamp. When a client changes content (say, saves a new version of doc 42), it asks Zanzibar for a zookie for that object and stores it with the content version. Later checks of that content pass the zookie, and Zanzibar evaluates them at a snapshot at least as fresh as the zookie — so any ACL change committed before the content change is visible. Checks without a fresh-content requirement can use older snapshots, which are served from caches and are much faster. That split — fresh enough for correctness, otherwise as stale as is fast — is how Zanzibar reaches sub-10 ms p95 at its scale.

Two other engineering pieces from the paper matter in interviews. Deeply nested groups make checks slow (group of groups of groups), so Zanzibar maintains Leopard, an index that denormalises nested group membership into flat sets for fast intersection. And popular objects create hot spots, handled by request deduplication and caching tuned per namespace.

8. Policy engines: OPA and Cedar

Whatever the model, writing rules as scattered if statements is how inconsistency starts. Policy engines move rules into a dedicated language that's versioned, tested and evaluated by one engine.

Open Policy Agent (OPA), a graduated CNCF project, evaluates policies written in Rego, a Datalog-inspired language, against JSON input (the request) and data (loaded facts). The doc-edit rule as Rego (OPA 1.0 syntax):

package docs.authz

default allow := false

allow if {
    input.action == "edit"
    some role in data.user_roles[input.tenant][input.user]
    "document.edit" in data.role_permissions[role]
    data.documents[input.resource].tenant == input.tenant
}

allow if {
    input.action in {"view", "edit"}
    data.documents[input.resource].owner == input.user
}

OPA runs as a library, a sidecar next to each service, or a central service; it's popular for infrastructure policy too (Kubernetes admission control, Terraform plan checks), because anything that can be expressed as JSON can be checked.

Cedar, open-sourced by AWS in 2023 and used by Amazon Verified Permissions, is a language designed specifically for application authorisation. Policies are permit or forbid over typed principals, actions and resources, with conditions:

permit (
    principal,
    action == Action::"edit",
    resource is Document
) when { principal in resource.folder.editors };

forbid (
    principal,
    action == Action::"download",
    resource is Document
) when { resource.label == "confidential" && principal.employment == "contractor" };

Cedar's semantics are deliberately simple: with no matching permit, the result is deny; any matching forbid overrides every permit. Entities can have parents (principal in group), so it can express some relationship logic, and a schema lets tools validate policies and analyse them — for example, prove that no policy lets a guest delete. Cedar's documentation describes it as designed for verification, and AWS has published work on formally verifying the engine.

The engines separate policy from data. The PDP still needs facts — Alice's roles, doc 42's folder, folder 7's editors — from a PIP. Getting fresh facts to the engine is the hard part, which is why ReBAC systems store relationships themselves and why OPA deployments often have data-synchronisation pipelines.

9. Where to enforce

Enforcement and decision are separate choices. Enforcement (the PEP) must live where the object and action are known precisely: inside the service, around every code path that touches data — HTTP handlers, but also batch jobs, exports, admin tools, message consumers. The gateway (sc2) can enforce coarse rules (is there a token, is this client allowed on this route, required scopes) but not object-level ones.

Decision placement has three common shapes:

Large systems mix them: tenant and scope checks at the gateway, relationship checks against a central ReBAC service, attribute rules (time, device, labels) in a local engine, and data filters in queries. Whatever the mix, deny by default when the PDP is unavailable, and make "this endpoint's authorisation" something a reviewer can find in one place.

Authorisation for lists is a query problem

Per-object checks answer "may Alice see doc 42?". Products constantly ask a different question: "show Alice the documents she can see, sorted by date, page 3". Checking each candidate after fetching works for tiny sets and fails at scale: you fetch 1,000 documents to show 20, pagination shifts unpredictably, and counts leak. Three patterns exist. Filter in the database when the rule is simple (tenant plus owner plus a shares table) — this is how the Prep Console's WHERE user_id = ? works. Ask the authorisation system for the accessible set ("lookup resources") and intersect it with the query — feasible when the set is small or the system maintains a reverse index. Or index coarse permission data with the content (tenant ID, ACL group IDs on each search document), filter in the search engine, and post-check only the top results exactly. The last is the standard design for enterprise search and RAG (hd2, au12), with the subtle requirement that the index's permission data is updated as fast as permissions change.

Caching decisions without breaking revocation

Authorisation checks are hot: every request makes one or more. Caching is essential and dangerous. Cache keys must include everything the decision depended on (subject, action, resource, relevant context), and TTLs bound how long a revoked permission keeps working. Zanzibar's design is instructive: it caches aggressively at snapshot timestamps, and zookies decide when a cached snapshot is too old for a given request. Without consistency tokens, the practical approach is short TTLs (seconds), explicit invalidation on permission writes for the affected subjects or objects, and a bypass for sensitive actions. Measure the revocation latency you actually achieve.

Combining models

Real policies are hybrids. Google Drive's sharing is ReBAC, but a Workspace admin's "no external sharing" is an attribute policy layered on top, and some actions are reserved to roles (domain admins). A clean way to combine them is layering with explicit precedence: forbid-style guardrails (tenant boundaries, legal holds, data residency) evaluated first and able to override; then relationship and role grants; then default deny. Cedar's "forbid overrides permit" encodes exactly that precedence. The design mistake to avoid is two systems that can each independently grant access, so that turning a permission off in one leaves it on in the other.

Explaining and auditing decisions

"Who can access this, and why?" is a question regulators, customers and incident responders ask. ACLs answer it directly; RBAC answers via role membership; ReBAC answers via Expand (the tree of usersets) and per-check paths; ABAC is hardest, because you must evaluate policies against every subject. Build explanation in: return the matching policy or the relationship path with each decision (at least in logs), and offer an "access review" feature that lists principals and the reason for each. Periodic access reviews — managers confirming their reports' access — are a compliance requirement in many industries and only work if the system can enumerate access.

Policy as code, tested like code

Policies deserve the same engineering discipline as application code: version control, code review, unit tests (OPA has a built-in test runner; Cedar has validation against a schema), and regression tests built from real decisions. A powerful technique when migrating is shadow evaluation: run the new engine alongside existing checks, log disagreements, and investigate each — disagreements are frequently real bugs in the old code. Another is differential testing of policy changes: evaluate the old and new policy sets against a sample of recent requests and review every changed decision before deploying.

Negative permissions and exclusions

Pure grant-based models struggle with "everyone in engineering except interns" or "all of the folder except this one file". ReBAC systems add exclusion and intersection operators to their rewrites (Zanzibar's configs support union, intersection and exclusion of usersets), and Cedar has forbid. Use them sparingly: exclusions make reasoning harder, because adding a user to a group can now remove access elsewhere, and they interact badly with caches that assume permissions only grow when tuples are added.

Authorisation for machines and agents

Services and agents are principals too (au1, au10). Service authorisation is often expressed as "service A may call method M of service B" — function-level rules, natural to enforce in a service mesh with workload identity. Agents acting for users need two decisions at once: may this user do this, and may this agent do this on the user's behalf? ReBAC handles that elegantly — the agent's permissions can be modelled as a relation derived from the user's, intersected with what the delegation allows — and au12 builds on it.

Failure modes

Forgotten enforcement points. The web handler checks, the CSV export job doesn't; the REST API checks, the GraphQL resolver for a nested field doesn't. Most IDOR findings are here. Mitigate with framework-level enforcement (a decorator or middleware every handler must declare), and tests that call every route with a second tenant's IDs.

Stale permission data. A user removed from a group keeps access because a cache, a token or a replicated policy bundle hasn't updated. Symptoms surface as customer complaints after offboarding. Measure revocation latency end to end.

PDP outages. A central authorisation service on every request's path is critical infrastructure; when it's slow, everything is slow. Budget its latency (single-digit milliseconds p99), cache, run it multi-region, and decide per action whether to fail closed or serve a bounded-age cached decision.

Policy drift. Two services enforce "editors can delete" differently because each copied the rule. Centralise rules in one policy repository even if evaluation is distributed.

Over-broad admin roles. Support and operations roles accumulate permissions until they can read every customer's data. Use just-in-time elevation with approval and expiry, and log every use.

Metrics and signals

Decisions per second and PDP latency (p50/p99) per service; deny rate per endpoint and per subject (spikes suggest probing or a broken deployment); cross-tenant access attempts (should be zero); cache hit rate and revocation latency; number of roles and of users per role (role explosion shows up as a long tail of single-user roles); count of standing privileged grants vs just-in-time grants.

The Zanzibar paper as a production case

The paper's production numbers are worth knowing precisely: trillions of ACLs, millions of authorisation requests per second, 95th-percentile latency under 10 milliseconds, and availability above 99.999% over three years, serving Calendar, Cloud, Drive, Maps, Photos, YouTube and other Google services. Its engineering lessons generalise: separate consistency needs (fresh for content-protecting checks, cached otherwise); flatten nested groups into an index; deduplicate concurrent identical checks; and give every client the same semantics so sharing behaves the same across products. Open-source systems inspired by it — OpenFGA, SpiceDB and others — implement the tuple model and modelling language; whether to adopt one is a question of whether your product's permissions are really a graph (sharing, groups, hierarchy) or mostly roles plus ownership.

Testing authorisation

Authorisation bugs are silent, so tests carry more weight than usual. Keep a matrix test per resource type: for each role or relationship (owner, editor, viewer, other user in the tenant, user in another tenant, anonymous), assert the expected result for each action. Generate it from the policy where possible, so a policy change shows up as a reviewed diff in the expected matrix. Add property-style tests for invariants such as "no request where the resource's tenant differs from the caller's tenant is ever allowed", run against random IDs.

Right-sizing

The Prep Console is the other end of the spectrum: each user's data is private, there's no sharing, and two roles suffice. Its authorisation is a decorator per route plus owner-scoped queries, and that's the right amount. The senior skill is recognising which shape you're in and when you're about to cross into the next — usually the day someone asks for sharing.

Goal: build a tiny RBAC checker and a Zanzibar-style ReBAC checker in Python 3.12 (standard library only) and compare their answers. About 15 minutes.

  1. RBAC. Dicts: role_perms = {"editor": {"doc.view", "doc.edit"}, "viewer": {"doc.view"}}, user_roles = {("alice", "acme"): {"editor"}, ("bob", "acme"): {"editor"}, ("dave", "acme"): {"viewer"}}, docs = {42: "acme"}. Write rbac_check(user, perm, doc). Check Alice and Bob for doc.edit on 42.
  2. Tuples. A set of 3-tuples (object, relation, subject) where subject is "user:alice" or a userset "group:design#member". Load the five tuples from the body.
  3. Rewrites. A dict per namespace, e.g. {"document": {"editor": ["this", ("computed", "owner"), ("parent", "editor")]}}.
  4. Check. Write recursive check(obj, rel, user, seen) handling this (direct tuples, expanding usersets by calling check on them), computed relations and parent traversal; use seen to stop cycles. Return the path as a list.
  5. Compare. Alice and Bob on doc 42; then add doc:42#editor@user:dave and check Dave; then remove Alice from design and re-check.
  6. New enemy. Give each tuple a write version. Implement check_at(version) that ignores tuples written after it and honours deletes up to it. Remove Bob from a folder at version 10, add doc 99 at version 11, and check Bob on doc 99 at version 9 (stale) and at version 11 (zookie).

What to look for: RBAC allows Bob, ReBAC denies him; Dave's access appears for one document only; removing one membership tuple changes Alice's answer everywhere. The printed path explains each allow. In step 6 the stale snapshot wrongly allows Bob — the new enemy problem — and the zookie-fresh check denies him.

Design: authorisation for a multi-tenant project tracker

A B2B project tracker has tenants (companies), workspaces within tenants, projects within workspaces and tasks within projects. Requirements: tenant admins manage everything in their tenant; workspace members see all projects in the workspace; projects can be private (members only); a task can be shared with an external guest; contractors may never export data; support staff can access a tenant only during an approved ticket window. Choose models, sketch the schema and policies, and say where enforcement and decision happen.

Answer

Models. ReBAC for the containment and sharing structure; RBAC-like relations for tenant admin; ABAC/forbid policies for contractors and support windows.

ReBAC schema (Zanzibar-style).

tenant:    admin = this;  member = this ∪ admin
workspace: tenant (parent); member = this ∪ (tenant → admin)
project:   workspace (parent); private (flag tuple)
           member = this ∪ (workspace → member, unless private) ∪ (workspace → tenant → admin)
task:      project (parent); guest = this
           viewer = guest ∪ (project → member);  editor = (project → member)

"Unless private" is cleanest as two relations: projects without the private flag get workspace → member; private ones don't. Guests are a direct tuple on one task.

Policies (Cedar). forbid(principal, action == Action::"export", resource) when { principal.employment == "contractor" }; and permit for support only when { context.ticket_window_active && context.ticket_tenant == resource.tenant }, with tenant boundary as a forbid: any request where the resource's tenant differs from the principal's tenant is denied unless it's the support permit.

Enforcement. Gateway: token, tenant claim, coarse scopes. Each service: PEP on every handler and job, calling a central ReBAC check service (cached, with zookies on content writes) and a local Cedar engine for the guardrails; lists filtered by workspace and project IDs from a reverse index plus tenant filter in SQL. Log every decision with the path or policy ID; build an access-review page per project.

Tests. Cross-tenant IDs return 404 on every route; contractor export is denied even for tenant admins who are contractors; support access fails outside the window; a guest sees exactly one task.
