Picture a candidate we will call Priya (an illustrative example, not a real person). She is applying for an AI Engineer role. She solves both coding problems cleanly, designs a retrieval-augmented support assistant in the system design round with sensible caching and evaluation, and leaves the building confident. A week later the recruiter calls: no offer for the senior role, but they would consider her one level down. The feedback, as far as the recruiter can share it, is that the behavioural round “didn’t show enough ownership or scope”.

Priya is baffled. The behavioural round felt like the friendliest conversation of the day. She talked about the big LLM launch her team shipped last year, the long nights, how proud everyone was, how much she cares about quality. The interviewer nodded, smiled, and typed a lot.

What Priya did not know is what the interviewer was typing and what happened to it afterwards. The interviewer was not recording an impression. They were writing down every concrete thing Priya said she did, and when they later sat down with a scoring guide that asked, in effect, “what evidence did the candidate give that they own outcomes beyond their assigned task?”, they found very little. Most of her sentences were about what “we” did, about how she felt, or about the project’s size. Those sentences were sincere, and they were nearly impossible to score.

This happens to strong engineers all the time, and it is fixable, because behavioural rounds are much less mysterious than they feel. At most large technology companies they are structured: the questions are chosen in advance to test named capabilities, the answers are scored on a written scale, and the decision is made from written evidence by people who were not in the room. Once you understand that machinery, you know what to put into an answer, how long each part should take, and why some stories that feel impressive score badly while modest ones score well.

This lesson is the foundation for the rest of the track. It covers why companies ask behavioural questions at all and what the research says about them; what a signal is and how the interviewer separates it from noise; what a rubric looks like and how answers are scored on it; how the same evidence gets levelled differently for mid-level, senior and staff roles; how the final decision is made, including the idea of a bar raiser; and how to budget your time in a 45-minute round. It finishes with one question answered twice, once weakly and once strongly, with what the interviewer writes down after each sentence.

The most useful mental model is this: a behavioural interviewer is an auditor collecting evidence, not a friend having a conversation. An auditor arrives with a checklist of things to verify. They ask you questions designed to surface evidence for each item. They write down what you say, close to word for word. Afterwards, away from you, they compare that evidence against a written standard and give a grade for each item. Your warmth and confidence matter about as much to the final grade as an accountant’s friendliness matters to an audit: it makes the process pleasant, but it is not what gets written in the report.

The analogy breaks in one useful place. An auditor can open your files; a behavioural interviewer cannot. The only evidence that exists is what you say out loud in the room. If you did something brilliant and do not describe it, from the scoring point of view it did not happen. That is why preparation for these rounds is mostly about learning to put your real experience into words in a form the auditor can use.

The process has five steps, and every later topic in this track refers back to them.

  1. Competencies are chosen in advance. Before you arrive, the company has decided which capabilities matter for this role and level: for an AI engineer these are typically ownership, judgement under ambiguity, collaboration (especially when people disagree), learning from failure, communication with non-experts, and impact. Each interviewer in the loop is assigned one or two of them.
  2. Questions are designed to draw out evidence. “Tell me about a time you disagreed with a researcher about how to evaluate a model” is not small talk: it is aimed squarely at how you handle conflict and how you reason about evidence.
  3. The interviewer takes notes. Amazon’s candidate guidance says it directly: if you see your interviewers typing, they are taking detailed notes to share with the rest of the team. Some companies now record transcripts with the candidate’s consent.
  4. Notes are turned into scores. After the interview, the interviewer reads their notes against a rubric, a scale with written descriptions of what a poor, borderline, solid and outstanding answer looks like, and scores each competency. They write feedback justifying the score with quotes from your answers.
  5. Scores become a decision. All the interviewers’ written feedback is combined in a debrief or by a hiring committee. Someone whose job is to protect the standard, at Amazon a Bar Raiser from another team, makes sure the decision is not driven by one person’s enthusiasm or one team’s urgency. The result is a hire or no-hire, and a level.

Two consequences follow, and they are worth holding on to before we go into detail. First, the people who make the decision often never met you. A hiring committee or a bar raiser reading feedback sees sentences like “Candidate said she rebuilt the eval set from 300 escalated tickets and found recall had dropped from 0.81 to 0.64” or “Candidate described the launch at length but could not say what she personally decided”. Your answer has to survive being summarised by someone else. Second, specific, first-person, quantified actions are worth far more than anything else you can say, because they are the only things that can be quoted as evidence. The rest of this lesson explains why each of those statements is true and what it means for how you answer.

1. Why companies ask about the past

The idea behind behavioural interviewing is simple: the best available predictor of how someone will act in a situation is how they acted in similar situations before. Amazon’s SDE II preparation page puts it in one line: a significant portion of the conversation will focus on how you have demonstrated the Leadership Principles in past jobs “because past behavior is an indicator of future success”. That is why the questions start with “Tell me about a time…” rather than “How would you…?”. Anyone can describe an ideal way to handle a disagreement; fewer people can describe a real disagreement, what they actually did, and what happened.

This is not just folk wisdom. Industrial-organisational psychologists have studied hiring methods for decades by comparing the scores candidates received with how well those people later performed in the job. The strength of that relationship is reported as a correlation coefficient, called the method’s predictive validity: 0 means the scores tell you nothing about later performance, 1 means they predict it perfectly. The classic summary, by Schmidt and Hunter in 1998, put structured interviews at about 0.51 and unstructured interviews at about 0.38. A large re-analysis by Sackett, Zhang, Berry and Lievens in 2022 argued that the older numbers had been over-corrected for a statistical effect called range restriction, and revised most estimates downward. In their table structured interviews came out on top, at about 0.42, while unstructured interviews fell to about 0.19.

Two things in those numbers matter for you. The first is the gap between structured and unstructured: an interview where everyone gets the same questions and the same scoring guide is roughly twice as predictive as a free-flowing chat. That is why large companies have invested so heavily in structure, and it is why you should expect your answers to be scored, not just enjoyed. The second is that 0.42 is far from 1. Even a well-run interview is a noisy measurement. A correlation of 0.42 means the interview explains roughly a sixth of the variation in later performance (0.42 squared is about 0.18). Companies know this, so they combine several interviewers, and so should you: one bad round is rarely fatal by itself, and one good round rarely saves a weak loop.

2. Behavioural versus hypothetical questions

Google’s re:Work guide on structured interviewing describes two kinds of question. Behavioural questions ask candidates to describe prior achievements and match those to what the job requires (“Tell me about a time…”). Hypothetical questions present a job-related situation you may never have been in (“What would you do if…?”). Behavioural questions reveal patterns of behaviour; hypothetical questions show how you respond to something new.

Behavioural rounds are mostly the first kind, but you will meet both, sometimes in the same question. “Tell me about a time a model you shipped regressed in production” is behavioural. “Your offline evals look great, but the product manager says users hate the new summaries. What do you do?” is hypothetical. The scoring differs slightly. For a behavioural question, the evidence is what you did and why. For a hypothetical, it is the quality of your reasoning: whether you gather facts before acting, whether you name trade-offs, whether you know who should decide. A strong habit for hypotheticals is to answer the hypothetical directly and then, if you can, anchor it in a real example: “I would first check whether the offline eval set reflects real traffic. Something close to this happened to me last year…” That hands the interviewer both kinds of evidence at once.

The trap runs the other way too. When asked a behavioural question, many candidates drift into hypothetical language: “In that situation I would always make sure to align with stakeholders.” Interviewers are trained to notice the shift from “I did” to “I would” or “I always”, because it usually means the candidate does not have a real example, or is describing their ideal self rather than their actual behaviour. In the notes, that sentence scores nothing.

3. Competencies: what the interviewer is trying to find out

Before the loop, the company decides which capabilities matter for the role. Different companies call them different things: competencies, attributes, signal areas, values, or principles. Amazon publishes sixteen Leadership Principles and maps its behavioural questions to them. Google’s re:Work guide describes three categories it finds helpful: role-related knowledge, problem solving and leadership, the last described for individual contributors as “emergent leadership”, meaning taking initiative, working collaboratively across teams and driving your own development. Many other companies use their own published values. Underneath the different names, a behavioural loop for an AI engineer usually looks for some version of these:

Judgement under ambiguityYou make sound decisions with incomplete data and know which decisions can be reversed.Tell me about a time you had to choose an approach before the evals were ready.

In this lesson a signal is any piece of your answer that the interviewer can use as evidence for one of these competencies. The word comes from the way engineers think about measurements: there is the thing you want to measure (signal) and everything else that gets in the way (noise). Interviewers often say things like “I got strong signal on ownership but no signal on conflict”, meaning the candidate gave lots of usable evidence on one competency and none on the other.

Each interviewer in a loop is usually assigned one or two competencies, and the loop as a whole is designed so that every important competency is covered, often twice. That is why different interviewers ask apparently similar questions: they are measuring different things. It is also why you need several different stories. If you tell the same story to three interviewers, the debrief sees three copies of one piece of evidence, which is much weaker than three independent ones. Building a set of stories that covers the competencies is the subject of beh2.

4. What the interviewer writes down: signal versus noise

While you talk, the interviewer types or writes, aiming to capture what you say as closely as possible. They are not yet judging; they are collecting. After the interview, often within the same day, they go back through the notes and pull out evidence for each competency. This second pass is where your answer is really scored, and it is ruthless about what counts.

Here is how typical sentences fare when the interviewer goes back through the notes.

What you sayWhat it gives the interviewer
“I noticed the eval set had no multi-turn conversations, so I sampled 200 from production logs and labelled them with two teammates.”Signal: a specific action, with the reason, at a defined scale. Usable for ownership and judgement.
“I argued for waiting a week, because a 3-point drop in answer accuracy would have doubled escalations.”Signal: a decision, the trade-off, and a number tying it to business impact.
“In hindsight I should have shared the regression analysis with the PM before the review, not during it.”Signal: honest reflection, specific enough to be believable.
“We launched it to 2 million users and everyone was thrilled.”Noise: a team outcome with no indication of your part.
“I’m extremely passionate about model quality.”Noise: a self-description. Anyone can say it; it cannot be verified.
“The company had been around since 2009 and had three product lines…”Noise: background beyond what is needed to understand the stakes.
“I would always make sure everyone is aligned.”Noise: a hypothetical in a behavioural answer.

A useful way to think about it is that every sentence you say either adds a quotable line to the notes or uses up time. A round has a fixed length, so noise is not neutral: a two-minute background section is two minutes not spent on the actions that carry the score. This is also why the pronoun matters so much. Amazon’s official interview guidance asks candidates to focus on their individual contributions even if they worked on a team, and adds, “It’s helpful to use ‘I’ instead of ‘we.’” The interviewer is not trying to discourage teamwork. They simply cannot write “candidate did X” when you said “we did X”, so a “we” sentence gives them nothing to score. The fix is not to claim the team’s work as your own, which follow-up questions will quickly expose, but to say what you did inside the team: “I wrote the evaluation harness; Sam owned the fine-tuning; I ran the comparison and argued for shipping the smaller model.”

Notice that “signal” does not mean “impressive”. A story about a small data-cleaning job can be full of signal, and a story about a famous product launch can have almost none. What makes a sentence signal is that it describes an observable thing you did or decided, preferably with the reason and a measurable consequence.

5. The rubric: how evidence becomes a score

Once the interviewer has the evidence, they score it against a rubric. Google’s re:Work guide describes the practice: scoring with standardised rubrics “so that all reviewers have a shared understanding of what outstanding, solid, borderline, and poor response looks like”, and documenting with illustrative examples what each level would cover for the attributes a question tests. Psychologists call this a behaviourally anchored rating scale (BARS): each point on the scale has an anchor, a description of the behaviour you would hear in an answer at that level, rather than a vague word like “good”.

Why anchors and not just a 1 to 4 number? Without anchors, a “3” from a generous interviewer and a “3” from a strict one mean different things, and scores drift toward whatever impression the candidate made in the first minute. The re:Work guide points to exactly this: in first encounters we make snap judgements and then hunt for evidence that confirms them, which psychologists call confirmation bias. Anchors force the interviewer to ask “did I hear the behaviour described in the ‘solid’ box?”, which is a question about the notes, not about their feelings.

The rubric in the figure is an illustration, but it reflects a pattern you will see everywhere. Poor answers contain a warning sign or no personal contribution at all. Borderline answers are the most common outcome for unprepared but capable candidates: there is a real story, but the actions are generic and the result is vague, so the interviewer cannot write a confident “yes”. Solid answers have specific decisions, reasons and a measured result. Outstanding answers add a lasting change beyond the immediate task. This is good news, because the step from borderline to solid is mostly about how you tell the story, not about what happened. Most capable engineers have solid stories; they just tell them as borderline ones.

Most loops then combine the competency scores into an overall recommendation for that interview, usually something like strong no-hire, no-hire, hire, strong hire, with written justification. A good interviewer’s justification quotes you: “Strong evidence for ownership: candidate noticed the eval set lacked multi-turn conversations, sampled 200 from logs, labelled them, and showed accuracy was 9 points lower than reported.” If your answer does not contain sentences like that, the interviewer has nothing to quote, and an interviewer with nothing to quote rarely writes “strong hire”.

6. Levelling: the same story, a different bar

A behavioural round does not only decide hire or no-hire; at many companies it is one of the main inputs into levelling, deciding whether you join as, say, a mid-level, senior or staff engineer. Coding rounds show whether you can solve problems; behavioural stories show the size and shape of the problems you have actually been trusted with. Interviewers look at three dimensions.

  • Scope: how big was the thing you owned? A task (a feature), a project (a system), several teams’ work, or an organisation’s direction?
  • Ambiguity: how well defined was the problem when it reached you? Were you handed a spec, or did you have to work out what the problem even was?
  • Influence: whose behaviour changed because of you? Your own, your team’s, other teams’, the company’s?
Level (typical)What the stories need to showExample for an AI engineer
Early careerOwned well-defined tasks; learned fast; asked for help well.Built the evaluation script for a classifier and found a labelling bug.
Mid-levelOwned a project end to end with moderate ambiguity; made sound trade-offs inside it.Shipped a RAG feature from prototype to production, including monitoring.
SeniorDefined the problem; decisions affected neighbouring teams; raised the team’s practice.Noticed eval sets did not reflect production, built a shared eval pipeline, got two teams to gate releases on it.
Staff and aboveSet direction across teams or an org; influence without authority; long time horizons.Set the organisation’s approach to model evaluation and release, and changed how research and product teams hand off work.

The implication is uncomfortable but important: a solid answer for one level can be a borderline answer for another. If you are applying for a senior role and your stories are all about tasks someone else defined, the interviewer may write “solid execution, but scope is mid-level”, and the loop will down-level you, which is what happened to Priya. The fix is usually not to find bigger stories but to tell the existing ones with their real scope: who else depended on your decision, what was unclear when you started, and what changed in how other people worked after you were done.

7. From scores to a decision: debriefs, committees and the bar raiser

After the loop, each interviewer submits written feedback, usually before seeing anyone else’s. That ordering matters. If interviewers talk first, the most confident voice in the room pulls everyone else toward its view, and you lose the main advantage of having several interviewers: independent measurements whose errors partly cancel out. Writing first keeps the measurements independent.

Then the evidence is combined. Some companies hold a debrief, a meeting of the interviewers and the hiring manager. Others send the written packet to a hiring committee of people who did not interview you. Many add someone whose specific job is to protect the hiring standard. Amazon’s candidate guide says you will likely meet two to seven people, which may include managers, teammates, stakeholders, “and a Bar Raiser (someone from another team focused on hiring quality)”.

The reasoning behind the bar raiser idea is worth understanding even if your target company does not use the term. A hiring manager with an empty seat and a deadline has an incentive to say yes. Their team, which is overloaded, shares it. A person from another team, with no stake in filling this particular role and with training in the company’s standard, can ask the question nobody else in the room is motivated to ask: is this candidate better than the people we already have at this level? Other companies get a similar effect in other ways, for example a committee that reviews only written feedback. For you, the practical meaning is that your evidence has to stand up in front of a sceptical reader who never met you. Amazon’s guide adds a direct instruction: treat each interviewer the same, because it is not necessary to tailor your answers to their role.

Finally, there are red flags: things that can block a hire on their own, whatever the scores. The common ones in behavioural rounds are blaming others for failures, disparaging a former employer or colleagues, details that change under follow-up (a sign the story is exaggerated or invented), and sharing information a former employer would consider confidential. The last is especially easy to trip over for AI engineers proud of their work: you can describe what you did and the size of the improvement without naming unreleased models, private datasets or customers.

8. The shape of a 45-minute round

Knowing the scoring tells you how to spend your time. A typical 45-minute behavioural round, or the behavioural half of a mixed round, has three phases. The first few minutes are introductions and perhaps “tell me about yourself”, which beh3 covers. The middle 30 to 35 minutes are the questions: usually three or four, each with a first answer from you and then several minutes of follow-ups. The last five minutes or so are for your questions to the interviewer, which beh8 covers. Amazon’s guide notes that they try to leave a few minutes at the end for your questions, and its SDE II page says each interviewer will typically ask two or three behavioural questions.

Within one question, the first answer should be about two to three minutes. Much shorter and there is not enough evidence; much longer and the interviewer cannot ask the follow-ups they need, which is where a large part of the scoring actually happens. Inside that answer, use the STAR structure: Situation, Task, Action, Result (beh2 covers it in depth). Budget your time unevenly, because the parts do not carry equal evidence:

These numbers are a working guide rather than a rule any company publishes, but they follow directly from the scoring. Situation and task exist only so that the actions make sense: a listener needs to know the stakes and your role, and nothing more. Actions carry most of the evidence, so they get most of the time. The result proves the actions mattered, and one sentence of reflection (what you learned or would change) is evidence of its own. Amazon’s guide suggests a habit that works everywhere: pause after your answer and ask whether the interviewer would like more context. The follow-ups that come next (“Why did you choose that?”, “What did the researcher say?”, “What would you do differently?”) are how the interviewer checks that the story is real and finds the evidence for an outstanding score.

9. Worked example: one question, a weak and a strong answer

The question, as an interviewer testing ownership and impact for a senior AI engineer role might ask it: “Tell me about an AI feature you shipped that you’re proud of.” Both answers below are illustrative examples written for this lesson; the candidate, company and numbers are invented to show the scoring, not taken from a real person.

The strong answer is only about 30 seconds longer than the weak one, and it is about the same project. The difference is that nearly every sentence gives the interviewer something to write after “Candidate…”. It shows ownership (picked up a post-launch problem and drove it), judgement (data before opinions, time-boxed disagreement), technical depth (hybrid search, re-ranking, caching), a measured result and adoption by someone else, which is the kind of reach that moves an answer from solid toward outstanding. Notice also what it does not contain: no company history, no adjectives about passion, no claims for the team’s work, and no confidential detail such as the company’s name or internal model names.

The interviewer will still follow up, and that is where the remaining scoring happens: “How did you persuade research?” tests influence; “What happened to the other third of the failures?” tests thoroughness; “What would you have done if the test had favoured fine-tuning?” tests whether the candidate commits to evidence. A candidate who lived the story can answer all three easily. One who embroidered it usually cannot, which is why honesty is not only right but strategically safer.

Why several interviewers beat one, and why they must not talk first

A single interview is a noisy measurement: the interviewer’s mood, how well the question fits your experience and plain luck all move the score. If each interviewer’s error is partly independent of the others’, averaging several scores cancels some of the error, the same reason you average several training runs before believing a benchmark. This only works if the errors really are independent. When interviewers discuss you before writing feedback, the first strong opinion anchors everyone else, the errors become correlated, and five interviews carry little more information than one. That is the mechanism behind the near-universal rule that feedback is written before the debrief. For a candidate, it means each interviewer is a fresh audience: a weak first round does not automatically contaminate the rest, so reset and give the next interviewer full evidence.

The halo effect and why big names do not carry stories

The halo effect is the tendency for one strong impression, such as a famous former employer, an elite university or a confident manner, to spill over into scores on unrelated competencies. Rubrics exist partly to suppress it: an interviewer who has to justify “solid on ownership” with quotes cannot rely on “worked at a top lab”. Candidates from well-known companies sometimes lean on the brand (“at a big lab, we trained frontier models”) and are surprised to score borderline. The brand tells the interviewer that you were in an interesting place; only your actions tell them what you did there. The reverse is also true: candidates from small or unknown companies are not penalised if their stories are specific, because the rubric asks about behaviour, not logos.

Why “culture fit” became “values” and what that changes

Unstructured interviews often ended with an informal judgement of “culture fit”, which in practice tended to mean “similar to us”: similar background, similar humour, similar hobbies. That is both a fairness problem and a validity problem, because similarity to the interviewer does not predict job performance. Structured processes replace it with published values or principles, described in terms of behaviour, and score them like any other competency. Amazon’s sixteen Leadership Principles are the best-known example: each has a short description, and behavioural questions are mapped to them. For you, this means “fit” is not a mysterious vibe. It is a list you can read before the interview and prepare evidence for, which beh9 does company by company.

Follow-ups are designed, not improvised

The re:Work guide describes structured questions as an initial prompt plus predetermined follow-ups, designed to bring out detail the first answer did not cover, and its example behavioural question comes with three follow-ups (“What was your primary goal and why? How did your teammates respond? Moving forward, what’s your plan?”). Interviewers trained this way often use a pattern sometimes called peeling the onion: each follow-up goes one level deeper into the same moment (“What did you say to the researcher? What did they say back? What did you do then?”). The purpose is twofold. Detail at depth is what distinguishes a solid answer from an outstanding one, and it is very hard to fake: an invented or borrowed story runs out of detail at the second or third level. This is the strongest practical argument for only telling stories you lived, and for knowing your own stories well enough to go three levels deep on any part of them.

How levelling interacts with the rest of the loop

The behavioural evidence and the technical evidence are combined, and they can disagree. A candidate might write senior-level system design and tell mid-level stories, or the reverse. Different companies resolve this differently, but a common pattern is that the level offered is constrained by the weaker of the two: technical depth shows what you can do, and behavioural scope shows what you have been trusted to do. That asymmetry is why levelling outcomes surprise strong technical candidates. It also means the behavioural round is where a senior candidate should be most deliberate: choose stories whose scope matches the level, and make the scope explicit (“this decision affected the two teams that consumed our embeddings”) rather than assuming the interviewer will infer it.

What research does and does not tell you

The validity research is strong evidence that structure helps companies choose well on average. It says much less about any single interview. Sackett and colleagues note that structured interviews have one of the largest spreads of validity across studies, because “structured interview” covers many different designs that measure different mixtures of things. A badly written structured interview can be worse than a thoughtful unstructured one. The practical reading for candidates is modest but useful: expect most large employers to use some structure, prepare for scored evidence rather than chemistry, and do not over-interpret a single rejection as a verdict on your ability, because even good interviews are noisy instruments.

When AI tools take the notes

Amazon’s current candidate guidance notes that some interviews use transcription technology, with the candidate’s consent, so the interviewer can concentrate on probing instead of typing. This makes the scoring model more literal, not less: the evidence that reaches the decision is now close to exactly what you said. Precise language (“I chose”, “I measured”, “it fell from 40% to 17%”) survives transcription and summarisation; vague language (“we kind of looked into things”) becomes vague evidence. Treat it as one more reason to say the important sentence plainly at least once.

How behavioural rounds go wrong in practice

Interviewers who run many loops see the same failure patterns again and again, and recognising them in your own practice answers is the fastest way to improve.

The “we” fog. The candidate describes the team’s work in the first person plural throughout. The interviewer’s notes fill up with question marks, the follow-ups become “and what did you do?”, and the candidate starts to sound defensive. Cause: modesty, or a habit from internal culture where “we” is polite. Fix: practise telling each story with an explicit split of who did what.

The long runway. A minute or more of company background, team structure and history before anything happens. By the time the action starts, the interviewer is already planning to interrupt. Cause: the candidate assumes the listener needs the full picture. Fix: one sentence of situation, one of task, then straight to what you did; add context only when asked.

The missing ending. The story stops at “and then we launched it”. With no measured result there is no proof the actions mattered, and with no reflection there is no evidence of learning. Fix: for every story, know the number that changed (or the honest reason you cannot measure it) and one specific thing you would do differently.

The hypothetical slide. Asked “tell me about a time”, the candidate answers “I’m the kind of person who always…”. Interviewers score that as no evidence and often note that the candidate lacked an example. Fix: if you truly have no example, say so briefly, give the closest real example you have, and say how you would handle the case asked about.

The over-rehearsed script. A memorised answer delivered word for word, which then falls apart when the interviewer asks about a detail in the middle. Amazon’s own guidance says they do not expect perfect answers or rehearsed scripts. Fix: memorise the skeleton (the decisions, numbers and lesson) and tell the story in fresh words each time.

The red flag in passing. A throwaway line such as “the research team didn’t really know what they were doing” or “my manager was useless” can outweigh several good answers, because it is evidence about how you treat colleagues when things go badly. Fix: describe other people’s positions fairly and specifically, the way you would want them to describe yours.

How to see your own performance

You cannot see the interviewer’s notes, but you can reproduce them. Record yourself answering a question, transcribe it (most phones can do this), and go through it sentence by sentence as the interviewer would, marking each one as signal or noise and noting which competency it supports. Two numbers are worth tracking: the share of answer time spent on actions (aim for well over half), and the number of distinct, specific decisions you described (aim for at least three per story). Doing this once is more instructive than reading any amount of advice, because almost everyone discovers they spent far longer on background than they thought.

After a real loop, ask the recruiter for feedback. Many companies share little or nothing for legal reasons, but a phrase like “didn’t see enough scope” or “stories lacked depth” maps directly onto the rubric dimensions in this lesson and tells you what to fix.

What the company’s own material tells you

Companies with structured processes usually publish a lot about them, because candidates who understand the format give better evidence and the company gets better measurements. Amazon publishes its Leadership Principles, its interview-loop guide (including STAR and the “I” instead of “we” advice), and role-specific preparation pages; its SDE II page states that each interviewer will typically ask two or three behavioural questions tied to the principles. Google’s re:Work guide explains how its structured interviews and rubrics are built and what categories it assesses. Reading the official material for your target company is the cheapest high-value preparation there is, and it has the advantage of being accurate, unlike interview folklore on forums. When the official material and folklore disagree, trust the official material and say so in your preparation notes.

An AI-engineer-specific note

AI engineering stories carry a particular risk and a particular opportunity. The risk is jargon and confidentiality: a story full of internal model names, unreleased benchmark numbers or customer names is both hard for the interviewer to follow and a red flag about discretion. Describe models by what they are (“a 7-billion-parameter open model we fine-tuned”) and results as relative changes if absolute ones are sensitive. The opportunity is that AI work produces unusually clear evidence: evaluation numbers, regression rates, latency and cost figures, rejection rates. Interviewers value candidates who measured what they shipped, because so much AI work is shipped on vibes. A story that shows you built the measurement before trusting the model is strong evidence of judgement in almost any rubric.

Goal: score one of your own answers the way an interviewer would, and see your signal-to-noise ratio. Time: about 30 minutes. You need a phone or laptop that can record audio, and a document or sheet of paper.

  1. Answer cold. Without preparing, set a timer and answer out loud: “Tell me about an AI or ML feature you shipped that you’re proud of.” Record it. Stop when you naturally finish.
  2. Transcribe. Use your phone’s transcription or type it out. Split it into sentences, one per row of a three-column table: what I said, what the interviewer would write, signal or noise.
  3. Mark each sentence. Signal = a specific action or decision you took, the reason for it, a number, or a specific lesson. Noise = background beyond one sentence, team outcomes with no personal part, adjectives about yourself, hypotheticals.
  4. Measure. Note the total length, the seconds spent before your first action, the share of time on actions, the number of distinct decisions, and whether there is a measured result.
  5. Score. Using the illustrative ownership rubric in this lesson, give yourself 1–4 and write the one-line justification an interviewer would write, quoting yourself.
  6. Rewrite and re-record. Cut the background to one sentence, convert every “we” into who did what, add the number that changed and one specific lesson. Record again and repeat steps 2–5.

What to look for: most people find their first recording spends 30–60 seconds before any action and contains one or two scoreable decisions. A good second version starts the action within about 30 seconds, has three or more decisions with reasons, ends with a number, and is about the same length. If your score moved from 2 to 3 without the story changing, you have seen the main idea of this lesson for yourself: the evidence was always there, and the telling decides whether it reaches the decision.

Diagnose the score

You are coaching a friend for a senior AI engineer loop. Their answer to “Tell me about a time you improved a model in production” is: “Our recommendation model was underperforming, so the team decided to retrain it with fresher data. It was a big effort across data engineering and ML. We set up a new pipeline and the results were much better, and leadership was really happy. I learned how important data freshness is.” Predict the interviewer’s notes and score, and say exactly what you would change.

Answer

The interviewer’s notes would read roughly: “Rec model underperforming. Team retrained, fresher data. Big cross-team effort. New pipeline. ‘Much better’, leadership happy. Learned data freshness.” Not one note begins “Candidate did…”. There is no measured problem (underperforming by how much, on what metric?), no personal decision, no trade-off, no number for the result, and the lesson is generic. That is borderline at best for any level and clearly below the bar for senior, where the interviewer also needs to see scope and influence.

The changes, in order of impact: (1) Replace every “we” with who did what, and make the friend’s own decisions explicit, e.g. “I found that click-through had dropped 12% over six weeks and traced it to a seven-day lag in the feature pipeline.” (2) Add the reasoning and a trade-off: why fresher data rather than a new model, and what it cost (compute, pipeline complexity). (3) Give the measured result and the time frame. (4) Show senior scope: who else depended on the pipeline, whether the fix became a standard others used, and what the friend did to bring data engineering along. (5) Replace the generic lesson with a specific one, such as adding a freshness alert so the next lag is caught in days, not weeks. Cut the answer’s opening to one sentence so the action starts within 30 seconds.
