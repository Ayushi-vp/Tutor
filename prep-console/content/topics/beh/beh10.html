The hardest part is over. After weeks of preparation and a full day of interviews, the recruiter calls with good news: the team wants to hire you. Then comes a moment many engineers handle worse than any interview question. The recruiter describes an offer: a level, a base salary, some equity on a schedule, perhaps a sign-on bonus. One candidate (call him Arjun, an illustrative example) is so relieved that he says yes on the call. Another (call her Lena, also illustrative) has read online that you should always push hard, and replies that she has a much better offer elsewhere, which is not quite true; when the recruiter asks for details, the conversation turns awkward, and the trust that had built up through the process starts to drain away.

Both mistakes are common, and both are avoidable. Arjun did not realise that the verbal offer is normally the start of a conversation, that the level he had been given might be worth discussing, or that the components of an offer can often move. Lena did not realise that negotiation works best as honest, well-reasoned problem-solving with someone who usually wants to close the hire, not as a bluffing contest. Neither had prepared for the offer stage the way they prepared for the interviews, even though this conversation can affect years of pay and the scope of the job.

AI engineering offers can be especially complicated. They often combine base pay, bonuses and equity in different forms (restricted stock at public companies, options at startups), and levels vary from company to company. Pay information is also more available than it once was: several US states now require employers to publish pay ranges, and some restrict questions about your salary history.

This lesson walks through the offer process from the first recruiter call to a signed offer. It covers the salary-expectations question you may be asked on day one, what level means and how to discuss it, the parts of an offer and how to compare them, your best alternative (the idea negotiation researchers call your BATNA), why first offers act as anchors and how to respond, how to make a counter that is clear and honest, deadlines, competing offers and counteroffers, and what to check before you sign. Following the brief for this track, it quotes no salary figures: pay changes quickly and varies by company, level and location, so use the current official ranges and your own research on the day. The worked example is an offer call handled badly and well, annotated with what the recruiter is likely to note.

A helpful way to picture an offer negotiation is joint problem-solving with a budget. The recruiter (and the hiring manager behind them) wants you to accept; they have spent weeks and many interviewers’ hours getting here. They also have constraints: a pay band for the level, internal fairness with existing employees, approval rules for exceptions. You want an offer that reflects your value and beats your alternatives. The negotiation is the search for terms that satisfy both. Framed that way, the conversation is collaborative rather than adversarial: you are giving the recruiter the information and the reasons they need to make the best case for you inside their constraints.

The analogy has a limit worth keeping in mind. The recruiter is on the company’s side, and some of their constraints are flexible while others are not. Part of negotiating well is finding out which is which: is the base capped by the band, but the sign-on flexible? Is the level fixed, or could more evidence change it? You learn this by asking.

Two consequences follow. First, the strongest negotiation move happens before any negotiation: interviewing at the right level with evidence that supports it. Everything in beh1 to beh9 about scope and level feeds directly into your offer. Second, the offer call is not the moment to decide. Thank the recruiter, show you are pleased, ask for the details in writing, and arrange a follow-up. That gives you time to compare, to think about your alternatives and to prepare a clear counter.

It also helps to separate two kinds of question you will face at the offer stage, because they need different skills. Some are information questions: what level is this, how does the equity vest, what happens to the sign-on if I leave early. These need no negotiation at all, only asking, and many candidates leave money and clarity on the table simply by not asking them. Others are decision questions: is this enough, should I ask for more, which offer should I take. Those are where your preparation (your alternatives, your target, your priorities beyond money) does the work. Most anxiety about negotiating comes from mixing the two. Ask all the information questions first, calmly, and the decision questions become much easier.

Three ideas from negotiation research will carry you through the rest. Your BATNA, your best alternative to a negotiated agreement, sets your real position. Anchoring means first numbers pull outcomes toward them, so you need your own well-researched target before you hear theirs. And honesty is not just ethical but strategic: negotiations between a candidate and a future employer are the start of a long relationship, and trust is worth more than any single concession.

1. The salary-expectations question

The first money question often comes early, in the recruiter screen: “What are your salary expectations?” It is asked for legitimate reasons, since the recruiter needs to know whether you are within the range for the role, but answering with a specific number before you understand the role and level can anchor you low. There are several reasonable responses.

  • Defer politely: “I’d like to understand the scope and level better before I put a number on it. Could you share the range budgeted for this role?”
  • Refer to the posted range, where one exists: “The posting lists a range for this role; assuming the level fits, that range works for me as a starting point.”
  • Give a researched range if pressed, tied to the level you are targeting and explicitly dependent on the full package: “For a senior role, I’d expect total compensation in the range of X to Y, depending on the mix of base and equity.” Base X and Y on current research, not on your current pay.

The law is on your side in several places. California’s Labor Commissioner explains that employers generally may not seek an applicant’s salary history, orally or in writing, though they may ask about salary expectations; that applicants have the right to the pay scale for the position on reasonable request; and that employers with 15 or more employees must include the pay scale in job postings. New York’s Department of Labor explains that its pay-transparency law requires businesses with four or more employees to list compensation ranges in postings for designated jobs, and prohibits retaliation against applicants who ask for this information. Rules differ by location and change over time, so check the official pages for where the job is based. Wherever you are, you are not obliged to volunteer your current salary, and you usually should not, because it can anchor the offer to your past rather than the role.

2. Level: the biggest lever

At most larger technology companies, every role has a level, and each level has a pay band: a range the company budgets for that level and location. Bands for adjacent levels may overlap, but the top of one level is usually well above the middle of the one below. That makes level the biggest single lever in an offer: negotiation can usually move you within a band; changing level moves the band.

Level is decided mainly from interview evidence (beh1): the scope, ambiguity and influence in your stories, and your performance in technical rounds. That is why behavioural preparation pays off in money as well as in getting the offer. If you believe you were levelled too low, it is reasonable to ask the recruiter how the level was determined and what the next level expects. If you have evidence the interviews did not capture, such as projects you led across teams or a scope that did not come up, share it concisely in writing and ask whether the level could be reconsidered. Sometimes the answer is an additional interview; often it is no. Either way, asking politely does not cost you the offer, and the answer tells you how promotion works from where you would start.

An offer is a bundle. Its typical components are:

To compare offers fairly, lay them out year by year over the period you expect to stay, typically three or four years, using each offer’s actual vesting schedule. Note which parts are certain (base), which are expected but variable (bonus, public-company stock whose price moves), and which are highly uncertain (startup equity). Then add the things money does not capture: the level and its path to promotion, the work and team, the manager, location and flexibility. A modestly lower offer on a team where you will grow faster is often the better choice over a few years.

Startup equity deserves particular care. Options give you the right to buy shares at a fixed strike price; they are only worth something if the company’s shares become worth more and you can eventually sell them, which may take years or never happen. Ask how many shares you would get as a percentage of all shares outstanding, the strike price, the vesting schedule and cliff, how long you would have to exercise after leaving, and the company’s funding situation. Value it conservatively.

4. Your alternatives: BATNA and the walk-away point

Negotiation researchers use the term BATNA, best alternative to a negotiated agreement, for what you will do if this negotiation fails. Harvard’s Program on Negotiation describes it as among the most important pieces of information a negotiator can have; the concept comes from Roger Fisher and William Ury’s book Getting to Yes. For a job offer, your BATNA might be another offer, staying in your current job (with its pay, prospects and satisfaction), or continuing to search. It determines your reservation point, the worst terms you would accept; below that, you would rather take your alternative.

Two practical points follow. First, know your BATNA before the offer call, and write down your walk-away point and your target. It protects you from accepting in the relief of the moment. Second, improve your BATNA honestly: finishing another interview process, or being clear with yourself about the real value of staying, does more for your position than any phrasing trick. Never invent an alternative. Recruiters talk to many candidates, may ask for details or documentation, and a discovered bluff can end the conversation or sour a relationship you will have for years.

5. First offers and anchoring

Psychologists have shown that the first number mentioned in a negotiation tends to pull the outcome toward it, an effect called anchoring. In an influential study, Galinsky and Mussweiler (2001) found that first offers acted as anchors on final settlements, and that the anchor’s pull was weakened when negotiators focused on information that contradicted it, such as the other side’s alternatives or their own target. In job negotiations the employer usually makes the first offer, so the practical lessons are about responding to an anchor rather than setting one.

  • Decide your target before you hear their number, based on research and your BATNA. A target written down in advance is much harder to be anchored away from.
  • Do not negotiate on the spot. On the offer call, thank them, ask clarifying questions and for the details in writing, and arrange to follow up. Reacting immediately is when anchors work best.
  • Counter with your own specific number and reasons, not with a percentage adjustment of theirs. “Based on the scope of the role and another offer I have, I’d like to be at X” sets a new anchor; “could you do a bit better?” stays in theirs.
  • Make it justifiable. An ambitious counter with reasons (the band for the level, a competing offer, the scope, relocation costs) is credible. A number with no reasons invites a quick no.

Anchoring also explains why the early salary-expectations question matters: a low number you give in the first call can anchor the eventual offer. That is a second reason to defer it or to give a researched range tied to the level.

6. Making the counter

A good counter has a recognisable structure.

  1. Enthusiasm first. “I’m really excited about the team and the work, and I’d like to make this happen.” It tells the recruiter the deal is close and that they are negotiating with someone who wants to say yes, which makes them more willing to push internally.
  2. One consolidated request. Put everything you want in a single ask: level, base, equity, sign-on, start date. Asking for one thing, getting it, and then asking for another erodes goodwill.
  3. Specific reasons. The scope you are taking on, a competing offer, equity you would leave unvested at your current job, relocation costs.
  4. Flexibility on form. “I’m flexible on how we get there: base, equity or a sign-on would all work.” Recruiters often have more room in some components than others.
  5. A clear path to yes. When appropriate: “If we can get to X, I’m ready to sign.” Only say this if it is true.

Many people prefer to make the counter in writing (an email the recruiter can forward to whoever approves exceptions) and discuss it by phone. Either works; what matters is that the request is clear and the reasons are easy to repeat. Expect the recruiter to come back with a revised offer, sometimes after internal approval takes a few days. One round, sometimes two, is normal; long back-and-forth is not.

7. Deadlines, competing offers and counteroffers

Deadlines. Some offers come with short deadlines, sometimes called exploding offers. A reasonable request for more time, with a specific reason and date (“I’m finishing one other process and expect to know by Friday”), is often granted. Some companies say publicly that candidates may take their time; Anthropic’s careers FAQ, for example, says candidates can take time to think and finish other interview processes. If a deadline cannot move, decide with the information you have.

Competing offers. A real competing offer is useful and can be shared honestly, including its level and total compensation if you are comfortable. Tell recruiters about other processes and deadlines early, since many companies can speed up when they know. Compare offers on everything, not just money, and be prepared for a company to decline to match.

Accepting. Accept only when you intend to join. Accepting while planning to keep shopping, then reneging, damages your reputation in a small industry, may break terms you agreed to, and is unfair to the team that stopped interviewing others.

Counteroffers from your current employer. When you resign, your employer may offer more to keep you. Go back to why you started looking. If it was mostly money, a counteroffer might fix it; if it was the work, the team, growth or the manager, money rarely does. Decide which job you would choose if pay were equal, then check whether the difference changes that.

8. When the answer is no

Sometimes the company cannot or will not move, or moves less than you asked. How you respond still matters, both to the outcome and to the relationship. First, ask whether any other component has room: a sign-on instead of base, an earlier performance review, a later start date, a learning budget, relocation support. Recruiters can sometimes find flexibility in a component you did not ask about. Second, ask what would need to be true for the answer to change, for example demonstrating senior scope in the first six months and being considered for promotion; get any such commitment in writing if it matters to you. Third, go back to your walk-away point. If the final offer is above it and the role is one you want, accepting is a good outcome even if you did not get everything you asked for. If it is below, declining politely is equally legitimate: thank them, explain briefly, and leave the door open. Companies remember candidates who handled a no gracefully, and a declined offer today can become a better one in a year.

What you should not do is accept with resentment. Starting a job feeling you were treated unfairly colours the first months; if the offer is acceptable, accept it fully, and if it is not, decline. The decision is yours, and either answer is respectable.

9. Before you sign

Before accepting, make sure every agreed term is in the written offer: level and title, base, bonus terms, equity amount, vesting schedule and any cliff, sign-on and its repayment conditions, start date, location or remote arrangement, relocation. Read the restrictive terms too: repayment clauses, intellectual-property assignment, confidentiality, and any non-solicitation or non-compete clauses where they apply. Ask in writing about anything unclear. For unusual or high-stakes terms, such as complex equity at a startup or a broad non-compete, consider advice from an employment lawyer. Once signed, changes are much harder to make.

The strong version is not aggressive. It is warm, honest, specific and well organised, and it gives the recruiter exactly what they need to argue for the candidate internally: a real alternative, a scope-based reason, one clear request and a commitment to close. Whether the company meets the request in full or in part, the relationship is intact, and the candidate will start the job on good terms.

The recruiter’s position

It helps to understand the person on the other side. A recruiter is usually measured partly on filling roles with strong candidates, so once an offer is extended they generally want you to accept. They also work within constraints they do not control: bands set by compensation teams, fairness with current employees at the same level, and approval chains for exceptions. A good counter makes their job easier: it gives a reason they can write down, it is consolidated into one approval request, and it signals you will accept if it is met. A bad counter makes their job harder: vague, piecemeal, unsupported or adversarial. Treating the recruiter as a partner with constraints, rather than an opponent, is both more pleasant and more effective.

Why internal fairness limits base pay more than other components

Companies try to keep pay for people in the same role and level within a consistent range, both for fairness and because equal-pay laws, such as California’s Equal Pay Act described on the Labor Commissioner’s page, prohibit paying employees differently for substantially similar work on the basis of sex, race or ethnicity. As a result, base salary is often the least flexible component: raising it for one new hire creates pressure for everyone at that level. One-time payments such as sign-on bonuses, and sometimes initial equity grants, can be more flexible because they do not permanently change the pay structure. This is one reason flexibility on form (“base, equity or sign-on would all work”) often gets a better result than insisting on base.

Anchoring is robust, but not destiny

Research since Galinsky and Mussweiler has continued to find strong first-offer effects across many settings, and their study also showed how to weaken an anchor: by focusing on information inconsistent with it, such as your own alternatives and target. That is the deeper reason to write down your target and walk-away before the offer call. There are also situations where moving first is risky: when you know much less than the other side about what is reasonable, a first number can leave money on the table. In job offers, pay-transparency ranges and the recruiter’s answers reduce that asymmetry, which is why asking for the budgeted range early is sensible before stating any number yourself.

Negotiation and fairness

Not everyone negotiates, and people who do not can end up paid less for the same work. Pay-transparency laws, published ranges and structured levels are partly attempts to reduce that gap by making pay depend less on negotiation skill. For candidates, the practical implication is simple: negotiating politely and honestly is normal and expected, not greedy, and you should not feel you are doing something improper by asking. For future colleagues and managers, it is worth remembering that the quality of an offer is not a measure of a person’s worth, and supporting fair, transparent pay is part of building a good team.

Equity at AI companies

AI engineering offers frequently include equity in companies whose value is changing quickly, from large public companies to private startups and fast-growing private labs. The same principles apply as for any equity, with emphasis on understanding what you actually own and when you can sell it. For private companies, ask how equity is granted (options or shares), the vesting and exercise terms, whether there have been opportunities for employees to sell shares, and how the company thinks about future funding and dilution. Avoid making decisions based on headline valuations or on assumptions about future rounds; value private equity conservatively and make sure the job is one you want even if the equity turns out to be worth much less than hoped.

Location, remote work and pay

Many companies set pay bands by location, so the same level can pay differently in different cities or countries, and a remote role may be banded by where you live rather than where the team sits. If you are considering relocating, or working remotely from a different place, ask early which band applies and whether moving later would change your pay. This matters for comparing offers too: two offers that look different may be the same band applied to different locations, and an offer that requires relocation should be compared after the cost of moving and living there. As with every other component, ask the recruiter directly; it is a routine question, and the answer can change which offer is really better.

Ethics and reputation

The technology industry, and the AI community within it, is smaller than it looks. Recruiters move between companies, hiring managers talk, and the people you negotiate with may be your colleagues, managers or references for years. That is the strongest practical argument for honesty: no single concession is worth a reputation for bluffing, reneging or being difficult. It also cuts the other way: you are entitled to expect honesty from the company, and an employer that uses pressure tactics, misrepresents terms or retracts promises during the offer stage is showing you something about how it will treat you later.

How offer negotiations go wrong

Accepting on the call. Relief wins; the candidate says yes before seeing the details. Fix: thank them, ask for the offer in writing and a day or two to review.

Anchoring yourself low early. Giving a number in the first recruiter call based on current pay. Fix: defer, ask for the range, or give a researched range tied to the target level.

Bluffing. Inventing or inflating competing offers. Fix: share only real alternatives, with real details if you choose to share them.

Piecemeal asks. Requesting one change, then another, then another. Fix: one consolidated counter.

Ultimatums. “Match this or I walk” when you would not walk. Fix: state what would make you sign, honestly.

Ignoring level. Negotiating hard on base within a level that is too low. Fix: ask how level was decided and offer evidence if you have it.

Ignoring the non-money terms. Accepting a team, manager or role you did not check because the number was good. Fix: use the questions from beh8 before deciding.

Not reading the letter. Missing a repayment clause or a vesting cliff. Fix: check every term before signing.

A preparation checklist

  1. Before the first recruiter call: decide how you will answer the expectations question; check the posted range and the pay-transparency rules for the job’s location.
  2. During the loop: interview at the level you want, with stories whose scope supports it.
  3. Before the offer call: write down your BATNA, your walk-away point and your target, and the questions you will ask about the offer.
  4. On the offer call: thank them, show enthusiasm, ask questions, request the details in writing, set a date to respond.
  5. After the call: lay out offers year by year; weigh the non-money factors; prepare one consolidated counter with reasons.
  6. Before signing: confirm every term in writing and read the whole letter.

Where to find information without quoting rumours

The most reliable compensation information is official: ranges in job postings where laws require them, pay scales provided on request where laws grant that right, and what the recruiter tells you about the band and its components. Crowd-sourced compensation sites can be useful for a rough sense of ranges, but their data is self-reported, varies in quality and goes out of date; treat it as a starting point and ask the recruiter to confirm. People you trust who work at the company, or at similar companies, are another good source. This lesson deliberately quotes no figures, because any number written here would be out of date by the time you read it; check current sources on the day.

After you accept

Tell any other companies you are in process with promptly and politely, and thank the people who helped you. Resign from your current job professionally, giving the notice you owe and offering a good handover. If your current employer makes a counteroffer, decide using the reasoning above and communicate your decision quickly. Then, before you start, ask your new manager whether there is anything useful to read or learn, which is a good way to begin the relationship you just negotiated your way into.

Goal: prepare for an offer conversation before you have one. This is a written exercise; it takes about 40 minutes and needs no real offer.

  1. Expectations script. Write your exact answer to “What are your salary expectations?” for a recruiter screen: a polite deferral, a question about the budgeted range, and a fallback researched range tied to your target level. Look up the pay-transparency rules for the location of a role you are targeting on the relevant official page.
  2. BATNA. Write down your best alternative today (current job, another process) and what it is really worth to you, including non-money factors. From it, write your walk-away point and your target for a typical offer.
  3. Offer questions. List the questions you will ask on an offer call: level and how it was decided, each component, vesting schedule and cliff, sign-on repayment, start date, flexibility.
  4. Comparison sheet. Build a spreadsheet with a column per year (four years) and rows for base, bonus, equity vesting and sign-on, plus rows for level, team, manager and growth. Fill it with placeholders.
  5. Counter email. Draft a counter email using the five-part structure from this lesson (enthusiasm, one consolidated ask, reasons, flexibility, path to yes), with placeholders for amounts.
  6. Rehearse the call. Ask a friend to play the recruiter and make an offer; practise thanking them, asking questions and not accepting on the call.

What to look for: the hardest part for most people is step 6, not accepting on the spot. If you said yes or named a number during the rehearsal, repeat it until “thank you, could you send that in writing?” comes naturally.

Handle the call

You receive a verbal offer for a senior AI engineer role. The recruiter says the level is one below what you applied for, the equity vests over four years with a one-year cliff, there is a sign-on bonus that must be repaid if you leave within a year, and they need an answer in three days. You have another process at a final stage, expecting a decision in a week. Your current job is secure and you are reasonably happy there. What do you say on the call, and what do you do over the following week?

Answer

On the call: thank the recruiter warmly and say you are excited about the team. Do not accept or negotiate yet. Ask how the level was decided and what the next level expects; ask for the full offer in writing, including the vesting schedule, the sign-on repayment terms and any other conditions. Say honestly that you are at the final stage of another process with a decision expected in about a week, and ask whether the deadline can be extended to a specific date.

Over the following days: your BATNA is your secure, reasonably happy current job, which gives you a solid walk-away point; write it down with your target. Read the written offer. On level, if you have evidence of senior scope the interviews may have missed, send a short note to the recruiter with that evidence and ask whether the level can be reconsidered or assessed further; this is the biggest lever. Lay out the offer year by year, noting the one-year cliff (no equity if you leave before a year) and the sign-on repayment risk. Tell the other company about the deadline and ask whether they can move faster.

When you have the information, make one consolidated counter in writing: enthusiasm, then your asks (level reconsideration if appropriate, plus equity or sign-on adjustments), with reasons (scope, the other offer if it materialises, equity you would leave behind), flexibility on form, and a path to yes if true. If they will not extend the deadline and the other process cannot move, decide with what you have; because your current job is a good alternative, you can afford to decline an offer that is below your walk-away point. Never accept with the intention of backing out.
