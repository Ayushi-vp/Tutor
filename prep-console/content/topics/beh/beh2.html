Here is a pattern that recruiters and interview coaches see constantly. A candidate, call him Tomás (an illustrative example), downloads a list of “the 50 most common behavioural questions” and writes a polished answer to each one. He spends a week on it. In the actual interview, the first question is “Tell me about a time you had to change direction on a project because of new information.” That is not on his list. He hunts through his memory for the closest prepared answer, finds one about a deadline, and tries to bend it to fit. It comes out as half of one answer and half of another. The interviewer asks a follow-up, and he loses his place entirely.

Tomás did not fail for lack of experience. In his last two years he migrated a recommendation service to a new feature store, argued a research team out of a risky launch, and cleaned up after an embedding model change that broke search for a day. Any of those would have answered the question. The problem was his preparation was indexed by question wording, which is effectively infinite, instead of by stories, which are few, and themes, which are fewer still.

The fix has two parts, and this lesson teaches both. The first is a structure for telling one story well: STAR, which stands for Situation, Task, Action, Result. STAR is not a trick; it was designed on the interviewer’s side to collect complete evidence, so answering in it puts your evidence exactly where the interviewer is looking. The second is a story bank: a deliberately chosen set of 8–12 real stories, each written up once, each tagged with the themes it can serve, laid out in a matrix so you can see at a glance which themes are covered twice and which are not covered at all. With a bank, an unfamiliar question becomes a lookup: which theme is this, which of my stories serves it best, and what should I emphasise?

By the end of this lesson you will know how to time each part of a STAR answer, what makes an action section strong, how to write a result when there is no clean number, how to choose and write up stories, how to build the matrix, and how to re-angle one story for several questions without bending the truth. The worked example takes one question and answers it twice, weakly and strongly, with what the interviewer writes after each sentence.

Think of STAR as the format of a good lab report. A lab report has a fixed shape: background, method, results, discussion. The shape is not there to make science boring; it is there so a reader who has never met you can find the method in seconds and check whether the results follow from it. STAR does the same job for a behavioural answer. The interviewer, like the report’s reader, is looking for specific things: what the situation was, what you were responsible for, what you did, and what came of it. When those arrive in the expected order, the interviewer’s notes almost write themselves.

The analogy breaks in one important way. A lab report gives each section the space it needs; in a behavioural answer you have about two and a half minutes, so the sections compete. The skill is giving the action most of the time, because that is where the evidence is, and compressing everything else.

The story bank is the second idea. If STAR is the format of one report, the bank is the filing system for all of them. Think of a well-indexed database table: each row is a story, and an index lets you look stories up by theme. Behavioural questions come in perhaps ten recurring themes: conflict, failure, ownership, ambiguity, influence, impact, learning, prioritisation, communication and helping others. Each real story usually serves two or three of them. If you lay the stories out as rows and the themes as columns, you get a matrix, and two rules make it useful: every theme column should have at least two marks, and no single story should be the only answer for many themes.

It helps to see what the interviewer experiences in each case. When a candidate tells a story in STAR order, the interviewer’s notes fall into place without effort: a line of context, a line saying what the candidate owned, a run of “candidate did X because Y” lines, and a result. The interviewer can spend their attention on judging the decisions and choosing follow-ups. When a candidate tells the same story out of order (problem after solution, result in the middle, their own role never stated), the interviewer has to rebuild the timeline in their head while also taking notes. Some of the evidence gets lost, and the follow-ups go on reconstruction instead of depth. STAR is, in effect, a courtesy to the person scoring you, and courtesy to the scorer is rewarded.

The bank solves a different problem: finding the right story quickly. Without a bank, your memory has to search years of work for something that matches the question, under stress, in a few seconds. With a bank, the search space is ten stories you know well, and the theme of the question is the key you look them up by.

Put the two ideas together and preparation changes shape. Instead of writing fifty answers, you write eight to twelve story cards, once, carefully, each in STAR form with its numbers and lesson. You then practise telling each one from its card in fresh words, and you practise re-angling: telling the same true story so that it answers a different theme, by changing the first sentence and which actions you dwell on. In the interview, the work is a quick lookup: identify the theme, pick the best unused story for it, frame it, and tell it.

1. Where STAR comes from, and why that matters

STAR did not start as candidate advice. DDI (Development Dimensions International), a firm that builds hiring and assessment systems, says it brought the STAR method to market in the 1970s as part of its behavioural interviewing system, which it calls Targeted Selection. In DDI’s own description, STAR exists “to help interviewers collect complete information from candidates”: interviewers ask about the situation or task the candidate faced, the actions they took and, importantly, the results of those actions. DDI also lists the pitfalls STAR helps interviewers steer candidates away from: talking theoretically about what they “would do”, giving answers that sound good but lack substance, and taking credit for others’ work.

That origin tells you how to use it. An interviewer trained in this tradition is listening for each of the four parts and will ask for any that are missing. If your answer has no result, you will hear “And how did it turn out?”. If it has no clear personal action, you will hear “What did you do specifically?”. Every missing part costs a follow-up, and follow-ups spent filling gaps are follow-ups not spent on the deeper probing that earns top scores. Amazon’s candidate guidance makes the same point from the other side: STAR is “one way to structure your responses”, not the only way, and they say they do not expect rehearsed scripts. STAR is valuable because it matches the interviewer’s checklist, not because the letters are magic.

You will also meet variants: CARL (Context, Action, Result, Learning), STARL (STAR plus Learning), and Amazon’s own phrasing on its loop page, Context, Task, Actions, Outcome. They differ mainly in whether reflection gets its own letter. This lesson treats one sentence of learning as part of the result, which is what the interviewer’s rubric usually expects anyway.

2. Situation: the least you need to understand the stakes

The situation answers three questions in one or two sentences: where were you, roughly when, and why did this matter? “Last year I owned retrieval for an LLM assistant that drafts replies for about 120 support agents” does all three. The common mistake is to treat the situation as the place to establish credibility, with company history, team structure and the product roadmap. None of that is scoreable, and all of it delays the action.

A good test: after your situation, could a stranger understand why the task was hard or important? If yes, stop. If the interviewer needs more, they will ask, and you will have lost nothing. Aim for 10–20 seconds. For AI stories, one piece of context is often worth including because it explains the stakes: the scale (“30,000 tickets a month”) or the risk (“a wrong answer here meant a customer was told the wrong refund policy”).

3. Task: your piece, your goal, your constraint

The task is easy to confuse with the situation, but it is different and more important. The situation is what everybody in the story shared; the task is your part: what you were responsible for, what goal you were trying to reach, and what constraint made it non-trivial. “My job was to get the draft rejection rate under 20% within a month without adding latency” is a task. It tells the interviewer three useful things: you owned a measurable outcome, there was a deadline, and there was a trade-off.

The task also sets up the result. If the task names a goal (“under 20%”), the result can answer it (“we got to 17%”), which makes the story feel complete and the result credible. If you set your own task, for example because nobody had noticed the problem, say so: “Nobody owned evaluation for the assistant, so I took it on” is itself strong ownership evidence, and it belongs in the task.

4. Action: a sequence of decisions, each with a why

The action is where the score lives, and the best way to structure it is as a sequence of three to five decisions, each told in three layers:

  • What you did: “I pulled 300 rejected drafts and labelled why each failed.”
  • How, when the method shows skill: “I built a quick labelling sheet with five failure categories and had a teammate label 50 of the same ones so I could check we agreed.”
  • Why, the part most candidates skip: “because the team was about to spend a month fine-tuning, and I wanted evidence about where the failures actually were before we committed.”

The why is what turns an activity into judgement. “I added a re-ranker” is an activity; “I added a re-ranker because two-thirds of the failures were the right document ranked fourth or lower, which is exactly what a re-ranker fixes” is a decision, and it shows you understood the problem. Interviewers often follow up on the why anyway; giving it unprompted saves a follow-up and signals a habit of reasoning.

A good action section also contains at least one obstacle or tension: a disagreement, a trade-off, a surprise, a constraint you had to work around. Stories where everything went to plan contain little evidence, because there was nothing to decide. And it is told in the first person. When other people were involved, say exactly what they did and what you did: “Sam owned the fine-tuning experiment; I owned the eval set and the comparison.” That is both honest and scoreable.

Budget around 60% of the answer here, about 90 seconds in a two-and-a-half-minute answer. That sounds like a lot until you count: four decisions at about 20 seconds each is already 80 seconds.

5. Result: a number, a baseline, a time frame, and a lesson

The result proves the actions mattered. The strongest form has three parts: a metric, its baseline (the value before), and a time frame. “Draft rejection fell from 40% to 17% over the following month” has all three. Without the baseline, “17%” means nothing; without the time frame, the interviewer cannot tell whether it was a lasting change or a lucky week.

AI work often produces good numbers: evaluation scores, rejection or escalation rates, latency, cost per request, incidents per month. But not every story has a clean metric, and inventing one is worse than having none. When there is no number, say how you judged success and use an honest proxy:

  • Adoption: “two other teams started gating releases on the eval set”.
  • Avoided cost: “the launch review caught the regression before any customers saw it”.
  • Time saved, labelled as an estimate: “based on the ticket history, roughly a day a week of manual triage”.
  • Qualitative evidence from a named role: “the support lead said agents stopped rewriting drafts from scratch”.

Then add one sentence of learning: what you would do differently, or what you now do as a habit because of this. It must be specific to be credible. “I learned the importance of communication” is noise; “I now build the eval set before launch, because finding failure modes from user complaints is the expensive way” is evidence. Results can also be bad: if the project failed, say so plainly and let the learning carry the weight (beh5 is entirely about this).

6. Choosing which stories go in the bank

The bank is only as good as the stories in it. Start by collecting 15–20 candidates, then keep the best 8–12. Good raw material lives in places you have probably forgotten: performance reviews and self-assessments, design documents, postmortems and incident reports, launch announcements, sprint demos, notable code reviews, and messages where someone thanked you. If you keep a running list of accomplishments, the habit Julia Evans calls a brag document, this step takes an hour instead of a weekend.

Then test each candidate story against these criteria:

CriterionWhy it matters
Recent (ideally the last 2–3 years)Recent stories reflect your current level and you remember the details needed for follow-ups.
You were centralIf your part was small, the story produces little evidence however big the project.
Contains a real decision or tensionWithout something to decide, there is nothing to score.
Has a describable resultMeasured, or at least clearly judged; otherwise the story has no ending.

Aim for variety as well: different projects, different kinds of work (building, debugging, persuading, cleaning up), at least two failures or setbacks, and at least one story about helping someone else succeed. A bank of eight launches all told as triumphs will feel thin by the third interviewer.

7. Writing a story card

Each story gets a one-page card. Writing it is where most of the real preparation happens, because it forces you to recover the details, numbers and reasons that make the story scoreable. A useful template:

Title:        Embedding swap broke search (story C)
Hook:         I shipped an embedding model change that silently broke search for a day.
Themes:       Failure (primary), ownership, learning
S (1 line):   Owned the search service for an internal knowledge assistant, ~3k employees.
T (1 line):   Upgrade the embedding model to improve recall; no downtime.
A (decisions):
  1. Chose to swap models in place, without re-indexing in a shadow index -- why: time pressure (wrong call)
  2. Noticed recall alert next morning; rolled back within 40 min -- why: reversible, users affected
  3. Ran a postmortem; found vectors from two models mixed in one index
  4. Added a check that refuses queries if index and query model versions differ
R:            Search out ~1 day for ~30% of queries; zero recurrences in the 9 months since.
Learning:     Treat embedding model + index as one versioned artifact.
Follow-ups:   Why no shadow index? / Who noticed first? / What did you tell users? / Would you do it again?
Don't say:    Internal model names; the customer who reported it.
Length:       ~2 min 30 s

Notice what the card is not: it is not a script. It holds the skeleton (decisions, numbers, lesson) and the facts you need for follow-ups. You tell the story from the card in fresh words each time, which sounds natural and survives interruptions, because if the interviewer jumps to decision 3 you know exactly where you are.

The follow-up tree line deserves real effort. For each story, write the four or five questions an interviewer is most likely to ask, and answer them in a phrase each. The common ones are almost always the same: why did you choose that, what else did you consider, what did the other person say, what would you do differently, and how do you know it worked. Preparing these is what lets you go two or three levels deep calmly.

8. Building and using the matrix

With the cards written, build the matrix in the figure above: stories as rows, themes as columns, a mark wherever a story can genuinely serve a theme. Use your own list of themes, based on the company’s published values if it has them; a common starting set is conflict, failure, ownership, ambiguity, influence, impact, learning and prioritisation, plus communication and helping others. Then apply three checks.

  • Coverage: every theme column has at least two marks. In a loop, two interviewers may probe the same theme, and you want a different story for each.
  • Load: no story is the only option for several themes. If story A is your sole answer for conflict, ownership and impact, you will have to choose which theme to leave empty when two of them come up.
  • Honesty: each mark should be one you could defend if the interviewer pushes. A story where you were on the edges of a disagreement is not a conflict story.

Gaps are useful information. A thin column usually means one of two things: you do have a story but have not recognised it (many people do not see “I convinced the data team to change their schema” as an influence story), or you genuinely lack the experience, in which case you should know that before the interview rather than discover it during.

During a loop, keep track of which story you told to which interviewer. Several interviewers often probe overlapping themes; a quick note on paper between rounds is enough to avoid repeating yourself.

9. One story, many questions: re-angling

Re-angling is telling the same true story to answer a different theme. The facts do not change; three things do: the first sentence, which tells the interviewer which theme you are answering; which actions you dwell on; and which part of the result you stress.

Re-angling has one firm limit: you can choose emphasis, but you cannot add facts. If you find yourself wanting to say something that did not happen in order to make a story fit a theme, use a different story. Interviewers are good at noticing stretched stories, because the actions you describe will not actually demonstrate the theme. A conflict story where the disagreement lasted one meeting and nobody changed their mind is weak conflict evidence however you frame it.

10. Questions that do not fit the STAR shape neatly

Not every behavioural question is a clean “tell me about a time”. Several common shapes need a small adjustment, and recognising them saves you from forcing a story into the wrong form.

Superlatives: “What is the hardest technical problem you have solved?” or “your biggest failure”. Candidates freeze trying to find the objectively hardest or biggest. The interviewer does not need the record holder; they need a strong example. Pick a story from the bank that is clearly substantial, and if you like, say briefly why you chose it (“probably the hardest, because the cause was outside our system”). Do not spend a minute deciding out loud.

Two-part questions: “Tell me about a failure, and what you learned.” The second part is a hint that the interviewer weighs the learning heavily. Keep the STAR part a little shorter and give the learning more than one sentence: what you changed afterwards, and evidence that the change stuck.

Process questions: “How do you prioritise when everything is urgent?” or “How do you approach evaluating a new model?” These invite a general answer, and a general answer alone is weak, because anyone can describe a sensible process. The strong form is a short principle followed by a real example: “I rank by cost of delay and reversibility, and I make the ranking visible to the people affected. The last time this really mattered was…”, followed by a compressed STAR. The example is what makes the principle believable.

“Give me another example.” Interviewers sometimes ask for a second story on the same theme, either because the first was thin or to see whether the behaviour is a pattern. This is exactly why each theme column needs two marks. Tell the second story more briefly, and do not apologise for the first.

Negative-framed questions: “Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager and you were wrong.” The constraint is part of the question. Answering with a disagreement where you were right misses it, and interviewers notice. If you do not have one, say so honestly and offer the closest real example, such as a disagreement where the outcome was mixed.

Hypotheticals in disguise: “What would you do if a teammate kept missing deadlines?” As in beh1, answer the hypothetical with a clear plan, then anchor it in a real story if you have one. The bank helps here too: the matrix tells you whether you have a real story to anchor with.

11. Worked example: the same question, weak and strong

The question, from an interviewer assessing delivery and judgement: “Tell me about a time you had to ship something under a tight deadline.” Both answers are illustrative examples written for this lesson, using the same invented project.

Weak answer (about 2 minutes; no clear structure):

What the candidate saysWhat the interviewer writesSignal or noise
“Deadlines are always tight in AI, honestly, everything moves so fast.”(nothing)Noise: a generalisation before any story.
“One time we had a demo for a big customer and we needed the summarisation feature working.”“Demo, summarisation feature.”Weak signal: a situation, no task, no role.
“So we worked really hard, lots of late nights, and we cut some corners on testing.”“Late nights, cut testing. Who decided? Why?”Noise, and a possible concern: corners cut with no reasoning.
“Actually before that, I should say the model kept hallucinating dates, which was the main problem.”“Hallucinated dates (problem).”Out of order: the problem arrives after the solution.
“Anyway we got it done and the demo went fine.”“Demo ‘fine’.”Noise: unmeasured team result.
“I think it showed I can work under pressure.”“Self-assessment.”Noise: the candidate grades themselves instead of giving evidence.

No task, no personal decisions, a problem told after its solution, and a result of “fine”. The interviewer will now spend the follow-up time reconstructing the story, and the note “cut corners on testing, unclear why” may count against the candidate.

Strong answer (about 2 minutes 30 seconds; STAR, with timings):

What the candidate saysWhat the interviewer writesSignal or noise
(S, 12 s) “I was the only ML engineer on a contract-review product, and we had a pilot with a customer’s legal team starting in nine days.”“Sole ML eng, pilot in 9 days.”Signal: role, stakes, deadline.
(T, 12 s) “The summaries had to get key dates right, and on our test set they got them wrong 14% of the time, which legal users would never accept.”“Task: fix date errors (14%) before pilot.”Signal: a measurable goal and why it mattered.

(A, 20 s) “I told the PM on day two that this meant dropping the ‘summarise any document type’ claim for the pilot; we limited it to contracts.”“Cut scope early, told PM proactively.”Signal: managing scope and stakeholders under pressure.

(R, 20 s) “Date errors went from 14% to under 1% on the test set, the pilot started on time, and the customer extended it to two more teams.”“14% → <1%, on time, pilot expanded.”Strong signal: measured result with baseline, plus business outcome.

The likely follow-ups test the decisions the candidate highlighted: “Why not fine-tune?”, “How did the PM react to the scope cut?”, “What did you do about the other date errors?”. Because the candidate gave reasons in the first answer, these follow-ups become chances to go deeper rather than to repair gaps.

Why a story bank works: retrieval under pressure

Interviews are a poor environment for memory. Mild stress narrows attention, and searching your whole career for “a time you changed direction based on new information” in five seconds is genuinely hard. A story bank changes the search problem. Instead of an open-ended search over years of experience, you do a lookup over ten stories you have recently rehearsed, using the theme as the key. That is the same reason indexes make database queries fast: the expensive work of organising the data was done in advance. Rehearsing each story from its card a few times strengthens the cue from theme to story, which is why, in the interview, the right story tends to come to mind almost immediately.

Level-scaling: what changes in a senior story

The same STAR container holds stories of very different scope, and interviewers read the scope from specific signals. For senior and staff roles, three things matter more than the size of the project. First, problem definition: did the task arrive defined, or did you define it? (“Nobody had connected the rising refund rate to the assistant; I did.”) Second, second-order effects: did your decisions change what other teams did or could do? Third, durability: did something outlive the project, such as a process, a tool, a standard or a person you developed? A senior story often has a shorter action section about your own hands-on work and a longer one about how you got other people to act. If you are targeting a senior role, check every card for at least one of these three, and say it explicitly; interviewers do not award scope you did not mention.

The follow-up tree, and why depth matters more than polish

Structured interviews use planned follow-ups, and good interviewers go two or three levels into one moment of your story: “What did the researcher say? What did you say back? What did you do when they still disagreed?”. Depth matters for two reasons. It is where the outstanding anchors live, because the details of how you handled a moment show judgement and skill far more than the summary does. And it is how interviewers check that a story is real: genuine memories have detail at every level, while invented or borrowed ones run out. A practical consequence is that a slightly rough first answer followed by confident, specific follow-ups scores better than a polished first answer followed by vague ones. Spend at least as much preparation time on the follow-up tree as on the first answer.

Quantifying honestly

Numbers make results credible, but invented precision is easy to spot and damaging when caught. The honest options form a ladder: a measured value with a baseline (best); a measured value without a clean baseline, with the reason (“we didn’t have a pre-launch measurement, but escalations in the first month were 60% below the old system’s”); a labelled estimate with its method (“about 10 hours a week, from counting the tickets that went to manual review”); a proxy (“three teams adopted it”); and, finally, an honest “we didn’t measure it, and now I would measure X”. Every rung is acceptable if labelled. The only unacceptable option is a number that sounds measured but is not. AI work also has a specific trap: offline evaluation numbers are not production results. If you only have offline numbers, say so; it shows you know the difference, which interviewers value.

Confidentiality without vagueness

Many AI engineers work on things they cannot discuss in detail: unreleased models, private datasets, customer deployments. Vagueness is not the answer, because it destroys evidence; anonymisation is. Replace names with descriptions (“a large retail customer”, “our main production model, a fine-tuned open model of about 7 billion parameters”), replace absolute numbers with relative ones where needed (“cut cost per request by about a third”), and keep the decisions and reasoning fully specific, since those are rarely confidential. Note on each card what you must not say. Interviewers notice and respect discretion; they also notice, and mark down, candidates who share things they clearly should not.

The trade-off between rehearsal and naturalness

Too little rehearsal and you ramble; too much and you recite. The way through is to rehearse the structure, not the sentences. A useful drill is to tell each story three times in a row in different words, and at different lengths: 30 seconds, two minutes and five minutes. The 30-second version forces you to find the one decision that matters; the five-minute version forces you to recover detail for follow-ups; the two-minute version is the one you will use. After three tellings, the skeleton is fixed but the words are free, which is exactly the state you want to be in when the interviewer interrupts.

Common story-bank failures

The one-story loop. A candidate with one great project tells it to every interviewer, framed differently each time. In the debrief, five interviewers report the same story; the committee sees one data point and wonders whether the candidate has done anything else. Keep a note of which story went to which interviewer, and use the matrix to pick a different one when themes repeat.

Stale stories. Stories from five or more years ago are harder to remember in detail and often reflect a lower level than you are interviewing for. Use them only if they are uniquely strong, and say why you chose an older example.

Peripheral stories. A story about a famous launch where your role was small. The follow-ups expose it quickly, and the evidence is thin regardless. A smaller project where you were central is almost always better.

All successes. A bank with no failures leaves you with nothing honest to say to “tell me about a mistake”, and candidates in that position often offer disguised boasts (“I work too hard”), which interviewers recognise immediately. Include at least two real setbacks.

The over-stretched story. One story forced to serve a theme it does not contain, usually influence or conflict. The interviewer hears an answer that never actually demonstrates the theme and scores it borderline. A thin column in the matrix is better solved by finding another story than by stretching.

Keeping the bank alive between job searches

The cheapest time to write a story card is in the week the story happens, when the numbers, the names and the reasoning are fresh. Julia Evans’s brag document is one well-known version of this habit: keep a running document of what you did, including the small things and the reasons, and review it periodically. For behavioural preparation, add three fields to each entry: the decision you made, the number that changed, and the themes it might serve. A year of entries gives you far more candidate stories than you need, and preparation becomes selection rather than archaeology.

Calibrating with real practice

Two kinds of practice expose different problems. Recording yourself and transcribing (as in beh1’s lab) exposes structure problems: long setups, missing results, too much “we”. Mock interviews with someone who asks follow-ups expose depth problems: decisions you cannot explain, numbers you are unsure of, moments you cannot reconstruct. Ideally your mock interviewer has the story-bank matrix and deliberately asks a theme you have marked as thin. The goal is not to make every answer perfect, but to make sure that nothing in your bank surprises you.

Variation across companies

Companies differ in how literally they apply STAR. Amazon’s guidance explicitly explains STAR to candidates and maps questions to its Leadership Principles. DDI-trained interviewers at many large companies listen for the situation, action and result elements. Other companies run looser conversations that still have a rubric behind them. The story bank works for all of them, because what changes is how much the interviewer guides you, not what evidence they need. Where a company publishes its values, build your theme columns from them; beh9 does this for several companies with official public sources.

Goal: build the first version of your story bank and fill the matrix. Time: 60–90 minutes. You need a spreadsheet or paper.

  1. Mine (20 min). Go through your last two or three years: performance reviews, design docs, postmortems, launch notes, your calendar, notable pull requests. List 15–20 candidate stories, one line each.
  2. Select (10 min). Score each candidate 0–2 on: recent, you were central, real decision or tension, describable result, safe to tell. Keep the top 8–12, and make sure at least two are setbacks and one is about helping someone else.
  3. Write cards (30–40 min). For your top four stories, fill the card template from this lesson: title, hook, themes, S and T in one line each, three to five decisions with a why for each, result with baseline and time frame, one specific lesson, four likely follow-ups with short answers, and a “don’t say” line.
  4. Build the matrix (10 min). Rows are stories, columns are themes (conflict, failure, ownership, ambiguity, influence, impact, learning, prioritisation, communication, helping others; or the target company’s values). Mark each cell only if you could defend it under a follow-up.
  5. Find the gaps (5 min). Circle any column with fewer than two marks and any story carrying more than three themes alone. Write down what you will do: re-examine an existing story, find a new one, or accept the gap and prepare an honest answer.
  6. Re-angle once. Take one story and write the opening sentence for two different themes.

What to look for: a typical first matrix has strong columns for impact and ownership and empty columns for conflict, influence and failure, because people remember wins best. If yours looks like that, you have found where your interview risk is, before an interviewer finds it for you.

Fill the gap

Your matrix has two stories each for ownership, impact and ambiguity, but nothing for conflict. Your strongest project was leading the move from a fine-tuned classifier to an LLM-based router for customer intents. You remember that the data science lead initially wanted to keep the classifier because it was cheaper, and that you ran a two-week comparison before they agreed. You are interviewing for a senior AI engineer role next week. Can this be a conflict story, and if so, how would you open it and what would you emphasise?

Yes, if the disagreement was real and you handled it, but test it first: was there a genuine difference of view with reasons on both sides, did you engage directly with the other person, and did evidence decide it? From the facts given, it qualifies: the lead had a real argument (cost), you had a different one (presumably quality or flexibility), and a time-boxed comparison settled it.

Open with the disagreement, not the project: “When I proposed replacing our intent classifier with an LLM router, our data science lead disagreed, mainly on cost, and I think they had a fair point.” Then emphasise: how you understood their concern (cost per request at your volume), how you made the disagreement testable (agreed in advance on the metrics and the threshold that would decide it), what the comparison showed (accuracy on the long tail of intents, cost difference, latency), and how you resolved it, ideally a compromise such as routing only low-confidence cases to the LLM. For a senior role, stress that you agreed the decision criteria with them before running the test, and that the working relationship was good afterwards. Results: the decision, the measured outcome and the cost. Lesson: something specific, such as agreeing the evidence that would change your mind before arguing. If the honest answer is that the lead simply deferred to you without much discussion, this is not a conflict story, and you should keep looking.
