The interviewer says, “So, tell me a bit about yourself,” and the candidate (call her Mei, an illustrative example) starts at the beginning. She studied electrical engineering, took a first job in embedded systems, moved to a data analytics team, learned Python, joined a startup, worked on recommendation systems, moved to her current company, and three years ago started working on LLM features. It is all true and all relevant in some way. It also takes six and a half minutes. By the time she reaches the part the interviewer cares about, her current work on retrieval and evaluation for an LLM assistant, the interviewer has glanced at the clock twice and is rearranging the rest of the round in their head to fit two questions instead of three.

Worse, the interviewer now has a vague picture of who Mei is. Is she an analytics person who dabbles in AI, an embedded engineer who moved, or an AI engineer with an unusual background? The intro was meant to answer exactly that, and it did not. Whatever the interviewer decides in these first minutes will colour how they hear the rest. Google’s own guidance to its interviewers warns that in first encounters people make snap judgements and then hunt for evidence that confirms them; structured interviews exist partly to counter that, but no interviewer is immune.

The opening question is not small talk. It is the only answer in the round that you can prepare almost word for word, because it is asked in nearly every interview, and it shapes three things: how the interviewer understands your level, which of your experiences they choose to dig into, and how much time is left for the evidence that scores. A strong intro is one of the cheapest, highest-return pieces of preparation you can do.

This lesson builds that intro. It covers what interviewers are listening for in the opening, the present–past–future structure, how to write a one-sentence identity, how to turn highlights into compressed evidence with Google’s published “accomplished X, as measured by Y, by doing Z” formula, and how to plant hooks that lead the interviewer to your best stories. It then covers the related question, “walk me through your resume”, and the awkward questions that often follow: why you are leaving, gaps, career changes and short stays. The worked example is a weak and a strong intro for the same invented candidate, annotated with what the interviewer writes and timed to the second.

Think of your intro as the abstract of a paper. A good abstract does not tell the history of the field; it says, in a few sentences, what the paper is about, what the main results are, and why they matter. A reader decides from the abstract whether, and how, to read the rest. Your interviewer does the same with your intro: it tells them who you are, what your strongest evidence is likely to be, and where to dig.

The analogy breaks in a useful place. A paper’s reader can skip to any section; your interviewer can only ask about what you mentioned. So unlike an abstract, your intro should deliberately leave threads hanging: specific, interesting details that invite “tell me more about that”, each one leading to a story you have prepared. That is the idea of a hook.

The structure that works for almost everyone has three parts, in this order:

  1. Present (about 20–30 seconds): who you are now. One identity sentence (role, specialism, the kind of problem you solve), plus a line on what you are working on at the moment.
  2. Past highlights (about 40–60 seconds): two or three accomplishments that prove the identity, each with a number and how you did it. Only include earlier career history if it explains something important about who you are now.
  3. Future (about 15–20 seconds): why this role, at this company, is the logical next step. Then stop and hand back.

Why this order and not chronological? Because attention is highest at the start. If you begin with your degree, the interviewer spends their freshest attention on the least relevant thing you will say. Starting with the present puts your most relevant information where it will be heard best, and it immediately gives the interviewer a frame (“an AI engineer who owns retrieval and evaluation”) into which everything else fits. The past then becomes evidence for that frame rather than a story the interviewer has to assemble themselves.

A second analogy helps with the hooks. Think of the intro as the table of contents the interviewer will navigate by. Whatever you list there is what they can easily turn to. If your table of contents lists “dashboards, forecasting, a chatbot”, those are the chapters they will open, prepared or not. If it lists “an evaluation set built from real tickets that became the release gate for three teams”, that is the chapter they will open, and you have written it carefully. You are not controlling the interview; you are deciding which of your true chapters are easiest to find.

The intro is also the one answer that should be very well prepared. Most behavioural answers should be told from a story card in fresh words. The intro is different: it is short, it is asked in almost every interview, and the first sentence in particular benefits from being exact. Prepare it carefully, practise it out loud until it fits the time, and keep a few versions of different lengths.

1. What the interviewer is doing during your intro

Interviewers use the opening for several things at once, and knowing them tells you what to put in.

  • Judging communication. Can you summarise yourself clearly and concisely? This is the first sample of how you will explain things to colleagues and stakeholders. Google’s candidate guidance reminds candidates they have limited time and should “craft concise narratives while covering all the important information”.
  • Calibrating level. The scope you describe in the first minute sets expectations: “I own the evaluation and release process for our LLM features” frames you differently from “I work on some of the AI tickets”.
  • Choosing what to probe. Most interviewers have a list of competencies to assess and some freedom in which experiences to use. They will often pick up a thread from your intro, because it saves them time.
  • Checking motivation. Why this role, why now? A generic answer suggests you are applying everywhere; a specific one suggests you will stay and care.
  • Settling in. For both sides, the opening is a warm-up. A calm, prepared intro helps you as much as it helps them.

Notice what is not on the list: hearing your full history. The interviewer usually has your resume in front of them; Amazon’s guidance says interviewers will have a copy. They do not need you to read it aloud. They need you to tell them what it means.

2. The identity sentence

The first sentence is the most important one you will say, so it deserves to be written and rewritten. A strong identity sentence answers three questions: what role you do, what you specialise in within it, and what kind of problem you solve or outcome you produce. Compare:

Identity sentenceWhat the interviewer takes away
“I’m a software engineer with eight years of experience, passionate about AI.”Years and a feeling. Could be anyone.
“I’m an AI engineer working with LLMs, RAG, agents, fine-tuning and MLOps.”A list of buzzwords. Breadth claimed, nothing shown.
“I’m an AI engineer who takes LLM features from prototype to production; my specialism is retrieval and evaluation, making sure what we ship is measurably good.”A clear role, a specialism, and a value: measured quality. A frame for everything that follows.

The third version works because it is specific enough to be memorable and to be checked by the rest of the interview. It also contains a promise (“measurably good”) that the highlights can then keep. Write your sentence so that a recruiter without a technical background could repeat it to a hiring manager accurately. If it only makes sense to specialists, it is too dense; if it could describe a thousand other engineers, it is too generic.

Follow it with one line on what you are doing right now: “At the moment I’m leading the evaluation work for a support assistant that drafts about 30,000 replies a month.” This anchors the identity in a real, current thing.

3. Highlights as compressed evidence: the XYZ formula

The highlights are where most intros go wrong, usually by being claims (“I have strong experience with RAG”) rather than evidence. Google’s official interview advice offers a simple fix. It tells candidates that Google “loves data” and suggests this structure for describing achievements: “Accomplished [X], as measured by [Y], by doing [Z].” In an intro, that becomes:

  • X, what changed: “cut the rate at which support agents rejected AI drafts by more than half”,
  • Y, the measure: “from 40% to 17% over a month”,
  • Z, how you did it: “by rebuilding retrieval around an evaluation set I made from real tickets”.

Said aloud, that is about 15 seconds, and it is a complete micro-story: it has a problem, an action and a result. It is also a hook: the interviewer can hardly resist asking how you built the evaluation set or why retrieval was the problem, and you have a full story ready for either question.

Choose two or three highlights, not more. Choose them for three properties: they support your identity sentence; they are relevant to this role (Google’s guidance suggests cross-referencing your resume with the job description and building examples where they overlap); and each leads to one of your strongest stories. If you can, choose highlights that show different strengths, for example one about technical depth and one about influence or rigour. Two highlights that both say “I built a thing that worked” are less informative than one of those plus one that says “and I changed how the team decides what ships”.

4. Planting hooks deliberately

A hook is a specific, slightly incomplete detail that invites a question. “By rebuilding retrieval around an evaluation set I made from real tickets” is a hook: it hints at a decision (why real tickets?) and a story (what did you find?). “By using best practices” is not a hook; there is nothing to ask about.

Planting hooks is not a trick. Interviewers have to find evidence for their assigned competencies somehow, and they welcome a clear lead to follow. If your intro gives them a good one, they will often take it, and the conversation goes toward your strongest stories instead of whatever they might otherwise pick from your resume. If they do not take it, nothing is lost.

The practical step is to check every highlight against your story bank from beh2. For each highlight, you should be able to name the story card it leads to. If a highlight does not lead anywhere you have prepared, either prepare that story or swap the highlight.

5. The future: why this role

The last part answers the question the interviewer is silently asking: why are you here? It should be short (two sentences) and specific to the role, which means it can only be written after reading the job description and learning something about the team. A generic version (“I’m excited about AI and Company X is a leader in the space”) earns nothing; Amazon’s guidance on “Why Amazon?” makes the same point when it asks candidates to think about what excites them about “the role and the team (or teams) you’ll be meeting with”.

A strong version connects your trajectory to something concrete about the role: “I’ve spent the last two years on evaluation for a single product; this role owns evaluation across several products, and I want to work on that problem at that scale.” It tells the interviewer you understand the role and that the move makes sense for you, which suggests you will stay. beh8 goes much further into “why this company”; for the intro, two specific sentences are enough.

Then hand back. End with a clean stop, not a trailing “…so yeah, that’s me, I guess”. A simple “That’s the short version; happy to go deeper on any of it” is fine. It signals that you know the intro was a summary and invites the follow-up.

6. Walking through your resume

“Walk me through your resume” is a different request from “tell me about yourself”: it explicitly asks for history, usually in time order. Some interviewers, especially recruiters and hiring managers, use it to understand your trajectory and the reasons behind your moves. The danger is the same as with a chronological intro: too much time on the distant past.

A good walk-through has a shape: brief on early roles, fuller on recent ones. For each role, say three things: what you owned, one result (in XYZ form if you can), and why you moved on. A useful rule of thumb is one or two sentences for each role more than five years ago, and three or four sentences for each of the last two roles. Two to three minutes is usually right for the whole thing; offer to go deeper.

The most important part is the transitions. Each move should sound like a step toward something, not an escape from something. “I moved to the startup because I wanted to own a product end to end, not just one service” tells the interviewer you make deliberate choices. “I left because my manager was terrible” tells them how you will talk about them one day. When your path has a clear direction (say, from data analytics toward ML engineering toward AI engineering), name it explicitly: the interviewer should be able to see the thread without having to construct it.

Some interviewers will ask about a specific line on your resume instead. Every bullet on your resume is effectively a question prompt, so every bullet must be something you can discuss in depth. If your resume lists a tool, a paper or a project you cannot talk about for two minutes, either prepare to or remove it. In AI roles especially, resumes often list fashionable frameworks; an interviewer who asks “how did you use it and what went wrong?” and gets a vague answer will discount the whole resume.

7. The awkward questions: leaving, gaps, changes and short stays

These questions often follow the intro or the walk-through. They are not traps; interviewers mostly want to see that you can address them calmly. The rule for all of them is the same: one or two honest sentences, framed forward, then stop. Over-explaining is the most common mistake, because it sounds defensive and invites more questions.

QuestionStrong shapeAvoid
Why are you leaving?“I want to work closer to model evaluation, and this role is centred on it.”Criticising your employer, manager or colleagues; salary as the only reason.
What is this gap?“I took eight months to care for a family member; I kept current by implementing a few papers, and I’m ready to be back full-time.”Long justifications; sharing more personal detail than you are comfortable with.
Why the career change?A bridge: transferable skills, steps already taken, one result in the new area.Presenting the change as a hope rather than something already under way.
Why only a year there?A neutral, factual reason (team disbanded, company changed direction, a better-fit role came up), plus what you achieved in that year.Blaming; leaving it unexplained.
Were you laid off?“Yes, my team was part of a larger reduction in March. Here is what I shipped before it.”Shame or evasion. Layoffs are common and interviewers know it.

You are not obliged to share private matters such as health or family details. A brief “I took time off for a personal matter, which is resolved” is a complete answer. What matters to the interviewer is that you are ready now.

8. Adapting to the audience and the format

Keep one core intro, but tune it. A recruiter screen (often 20–30 minutes, early in the process) checks basic fit, level and motivation; keep it non-technical and make the why-this-role part especially clear. A hiring manager wants to see how your experience maps onto their team’s real problems, so pick the highlights closest to their work. A peer engineer wants substance; let one highlight go a level deeper into how. A panel often starts with a round of introductions where everyone has 30 seconds; use your shortest version.

That means keeping three lengths ready. The 30-second version (sometimes called an elevator pitch) is the identity sentence, one highlight and the why. The 60-second version adds a second highlight. The two-minute version adds a sentence of relevant history and a little more on how. Practise switching between them, because interviewers sometimes ask explicitly (“give me the one-minute version”) and sometimes signal it with their body language.

9. How an intro is judged: a simple rubric

Few companies publish a rubric for the opening, and many interviewers do not score it formally. But they do form a judgement, and it tends to follow the same dimensions as the rest of the round: clarity, evidence, relevance and motivation, plus whether you respected the time. They will almost certainly ask about one of the two highlights, which is exactly what the candidate wants. First impressions and why structure resists them

The tendency for information heard first to dominate overall judgement is called the primacy effect. Google’s re:Work guide describes the interview version directly: in first encounters people make snap, unconscious judgements and then, without realising it, hunt for evidence that confirms the initial impression. Structured interviewing tries to counter this with fixed questions and anchored rubrics, and it helps, but the effect does not disappear. Two practical consequences follow. A clear, evidence-based opening starts the interview with the bias working for you. And a weak opening is recoverable, because a well-trained interviewer will score later answers on their evidence, so if you stumble on the intro, put it behind you and give the next answer everything.

Level signalling without inflation

The intro is where many candidates either undersell or oversell their level. Underselling usually comes from describing tasks rather than ownership (“I worked on the retrieval part”). Overselling usually comes from claiming team outcomes as personal ones or using a title the evidence does not support. The safe and effective approach is to describe ownership and reach precisely: what you owned, who depended on it, and what changed beyond your own work. Interviewers for senior roles are listening specifically for reach (decisions that affected other teams, practices others adopted), so if you have it, say it in the intro rather than waiting for a follow-up that may not come. If you do not have it, an honest intro pitched at your real level is far better than one that the next 40 minutes will contradict.

Non-linear careers

Many people moving into AI engineering have non-linear paths: physics PhD to data science to AI engineering, mobile development to backend to LLM products, or a career outside software entirely. A non-linear path is a strength if you present it as a deliberate thread and a weakness if you present it as a list. The technique is to find the thread and name it: “Every role I’ve had has been about turning messy data into decisions; I started doing it with statistics, then with ML models, and now with LLM systems.” Then use the present–past–future structure as usual; the thread replaces chronology. Interviewers remember a thread far better than a sequence of jobs.

Why “passionate” does not work, and what does

Almost every candidate says they are passionate about AI, so the word carries no information. Interviewers, like any audience, discount claims they hear constantly and cannot verify. What does convey real interest is evidence of sustained voluntary effort: that you built an evaluation harness on weekends because you wanted to know whether a model was actually better, that you reproduced a paper’s results and found a discrepancy, that you changed your team’s practice because you were bothered by something. One such detail does more than any adjective. The same principle applies throughout the round: show, with specifics, rather than describe.

Different cultures of self-presentation

Norms for talking about yourself vary across cultures and individuals. Many strong engineers were raised or trained to see self-promotion as rude and default to “we” and understatement. Others default to confident claims. Structured interviews in large technology companies reward a particular style: specific, first-person, quantified and factual, without adjectives about yourself. If that feels like bragging, notice that it is actually the opposite: it removes self-assessment and replaces it with facts the listener can judge for themselves. “I cut rejections from 40% to 17%” is a fact; “I’m an excellent engineer” is a boast. Describing what you did accurately is not immodest.

Remote and recorded openings

On video calls, the opening carries extra load because body language is harder to read. Two adjustments help: pause briefly after the identity sentence, so the interviewer has time to register it, and watch for the small signals (looking down to type, unmuting) that mean they want to come in. Some companies use asynchronous recorded screens where you answer a prompt on camera with a fixed time limit; there your timed versions matter even more, because you cannot read the interviewer at all. Google’s guidance also notes that search and AI tools are not permitted during its interviews, which in practice means your intro and stories must come from your own preparation and memory.

Common intro failures

The chronology dump. Starting at university and walking forward. The most relevant content arrives last, when attention is lowest. Fix: present first; compress history into one sentence that supports your identity.

Reading the resume aloud. Listing employers, dates and titles the interviewer can already see. Fix: say what the resume means, not what it says.

The buzzword salad. A list of frameworks and trends (agents, RAG, fine-tuning, vector databases). It sounds broad and shows nothing. Fix: replace the list with two XYZ highlights; the skills will be evident from them.

Too long. Anything over about two and a half minutes without an invitation to continue. Fix: time yourself; most people underestimate their length by half.

Too humble. “I mostly just helped out on the AI stuff.” The interviewer believes you and levels you accordingly. Fix: describe what you owned precisely and without adjectives.

The negative turn. A dig at a current or former employer (“the leadership there didn’t really get AI”). It stays in the notes and colours everything after. Fix: talk about what you are moving toward.

The mismatch. An intro that does not match the resume (different dates, titles or emphasis). Interviewers notice and wonder which is accurate. Fix: check your intro against your resume line by line.

Measuring your own intro

Recording yourself is uncomfortable and extremely effective. Record the intro, time it, and transcribe it. Then check a few numbers: how many seconds until your identity sentence is complete (aim for under 15), how many numbers appear (aim for at least two), how many hooks lead to prepared stories (aim for two), and the total length (aim for 60–120 seconds). Read the transcript as the interviewer would and write the three-line note they would take. If that note does not say who you are, what your best evidence is and why you want this role, revise.

It also helps to try your intro on a non-technical friend and ask them to repeat back who you are in one sentence. If they cannot, the identity sentence is too dense or too vague. A recruiter will have to do exactly that when they describe you to a hiring manager.

How your intro gets retold

Your intro is not only heard; it is repeated. A recruiter summarises you to a hiring manager in a sentence or two. An interviewer’s written feedback often opens with a line of context (“Candidate is an AI engineer focused on retrieval and evaluation, currently leading eval for a support assistant”). A hiring committee reading the packet meets you first through those summaries. If your identity sentence is clear and memorable, it tends to be repeated almost word for word, which means the people who never met you get the frame you chose. If your intro had no clear identity, each person invents their own summary from fragments, and the summaries may not agree. This is one more reason to make the identity sentence simple enough that a non-specialist can repeat it accurately.

The same applies to your highlights. A highlight with a number and a method (“cut rejections from 40% to 17% by rebuilding retrieval around a real-ticket eval set”) survives retelling intact. A claim (“has strong RAG experience”) either disappears or, worse, gets repeated with a sceptical note (“claims strong RAG experience; did not see much depth”).

Keeping the intro current

Intros go stale quietly. The highlight that was your best two years ago may now be your third best, and the “why this role” part has to be rewritten for each application. Review the intro whenever you add a story to your bank, and rewrite the future part from scratch for every company, starting from the job description and whatever you can learn about the team. Google’s guidance to cross-reference your resume with the job description applies here too: the highlights you choose should be the ones where your experience and their needs overlap most.

When the interviewer skips the intro

Some interviewers skip “tell me about yourself” entirely and go straight to their first question, often because they have read your resume or are short of time. That is fine; do not force the intro in. Your highlights and identity can still come through in your first story. If the interviewer seems unsure of your background later in the round, a one-sentence version of your identity is always welcome as context.

Goal: record a 2-minute intro and score it against a rubric. Time: about 40 minutes. You need a way to record audio or video and a timer.

  1. Draft. Write your identity sentence (role, specialism, kind of problem). Write two highlights in XYZ form (accomplished X, as measured by Y, by doing Z), each linked to a story card from your bank. Write two specific sentences on why the target role is your next step.
  2. Record cold. Without reading, record yourself answering “Tell me about yourself.” Do not stop or restart.
  3. Score the recording 0–2 on each line of this rubric:
    • Identity: clear role, specialism and problem in the first 15 seconds.
    • Evidence: at least two highlights with numbers and how you achieved them.
    • Hooks: each highlight leads to a story you have prepared.
    • Relevance: highlights chosen for this role’s actual work.
    • Motivation: why this role is specific to the role, not generic.
    • Length: 60–120 seconds, with a clean hand-back.
    • Noise: no tool lists, adjectives about yourself or negative remarks.
  4. Write the interviewer’s note: three lines summarising what they would take away.
  5. Revise and re-record, then make 30-second and 60-second versions.

Keep the recording: comparing it with a version recorded after a week of practice is the clearest evidence of progress you will get.

What to look for: the first recording usually runs long and scores low on identity and evidence. A score of 11–14 out of 14 is a strong intro. The interviewer’s note is the real test: if it does not say who you are, your best evidence and why you want this role, revise.

Rebuild the intro

A friend applying for an AI engineer role sends you their intro: “Hi, I’m Alex. I have a master’s in data science and I’ve been at a logistics company for four years. I started as a data analyst doing dashboards in SQL and Tableau, then I moved into data science and built some forecasting models, and for the last year I’ve been working on an internal chatbot using GPT models and LangChain, which people really like. I’m very passionate about LLMs and I want to move into a full-time AI engineering role where I can keep learning.” The target team builds LLM-powered tools for operations staff. Diagnose it and rewrite it as a 90-second intro, using only facts plausible from what Alex said (mark invented numbers as placeholders for Alex to fill).

Answer

Diagnosis: it is chronological, so the most relevant work arrives last; there is no identity sentence; the highlights are claims with no numbers (“some forecasting models”, “people really like”); there are tool names but no outcomes; “passionate” and “keep learning” carry no evidence; and the motivation is about Alex’s wish to learn, not about this team’s work. The interviewer’s note would be: “Analyst → data science → internal chatbot. Unclear depth. Generic motivation.”

Rewrite (numbers in brackets are placeholders Alex must replace with real figures or drop): “I build LLM tools for operations teams. For the last year I’ve built and run an internal assistant at a logistics company that answers operations staff’s questions about shipments and procedures; about [N] people use it each week. The part I’m proudest of is [cutting the time to answer a routine shipment query from X minutes to Y], by connecting the assistant to our shipment database rather than relying only on documents. Before that I spent three years in data science and analytics at the same company, building forecasting models for [warehouse demand], so I know the operations data and the people who use it well. Your team builds LLM tools for operations staff, which is exactly the problem I’ve been solving inside one company, and I want to do it as my full-time focus on a team that specialises in it. Happy to go deeper on any of that.” This version leads with identity, turns the history into domain expertise (a real advantage for this team), gives evidence with placeholders for real numbers, plants two hooks (the database connection, the forecasting), and makes the motivation specific.
