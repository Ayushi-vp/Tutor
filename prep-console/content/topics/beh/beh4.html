AI engineering is full of honest disagreement, much of it about things that cannot be settled by looking something up. A researcher believes a new model is ready because it beats the old one on a benchmark; the engineer who runs it in production believes users will find it worse. A product manager wants to launch a feature this quarter; the engineer believes the hallucination rate is too high for a customer-facing use. Two engineers disagree about whether a language model used as a judge can replace human raters. A platform team wants to standardise on one serving stack; the team with the most traffic wants an exception. None of these people is being unreasonable. They hold different evidence, different incentives and different estimates of risk.

That is why almost every behavioural loop contains a version of “Tell me about a time you disagreed with a colleague.” Google’s own interview tips tell candidates to expect questions like “How would you address a conflict with someone you work with?”, and Amazon’s Leadership Principles include one devoted to it: “Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit”. The interviewer is not looking for someone who never disagrees, and not for someone who always wins. They want to know what happens on their team when you disagree: whether the disagreement makes the decision better or just makes the meeting longer; whether the relationship survives; whether, when the decision goes against you, you support it or quietly sabotage it.

Candidates find this question harder than most, for understandable reasons. The honest stories involve other people, sometimes people who behaved badly, and the temptation is either to make yourself the hero and the other person the villain, or to avoid the question with a story so mild it contains no conflict at all. Both fail. The first produces a red flag (“blamed colleague”); the second produces no evidence.

This lesson shows how to do it well. It covers what the question measures, the difference between task and relationship conflict and what research says about each, how to choose a story and describe the other side fairly, the single most useful move in technical disagreements (making them testable), who decides and what “disagree and commit” really means, when and how to escalate, and the specific conflicts AI engineers face. The worked example answers “Tell me about a time you disagreed with someone about how to evaluate a model” twice, weakly and strongly, annotated sentence by sentence.

A good mental model is code review done well. In a healthy review, the reviewer criticises the code, not the author. They say what concerns them and why. The author explains their reasoning; sometimes the reviewer is persuaded, sometimes the author changes the code, sometimes they agree to test it, and occasionally someone else is asked to decide. Then the change is merged and both people move on, without a grudge and without re-arguing it next week. A conflict answer in an interview is a story about a review like that, except the thing under review is a decision rather than a diff.

The analogy breaks in one place: in code review there is usually an agreed standard (tests pass, the style guide, the reviewer approves). Many real disagreements, especially in AI work, have no agreed standard; the two sides disagree partly because they trust different evidence. That is why the most useful skill in this topic is creating the standard: getting both sides to agree, before the argument is settled, on what evidence would settle it.

Read the stages as a checklist for your story. Did you first understand why the other person held their view? Was the disagreement about what is true (which model is better for users) or about what matters (shipping this quarter versus minimising risk)? If it was about facts, did you find a way to let evidence decide? If it was about goals, did you get it to someone who could weigh the goals? Who made the final call, and what did you do once it was made? A story that touches all of these, with your specific actions at each step, is a strong conflict story almost regardless of who turned out to be right.

It is worth being clear about what the interviewer imagines while you talk. They are not judging the decision you were arguing about; they usually know too little about your old project to say who was right. They are imagining you on their team, in their next disagreement: with their researcher who loves a benchmark, their product manager with a deadline, their staff engineer with strong opinions. Every detail of your story is evidence about how that future disagreement will go. A candidate who listened, tested, and committed predicts a disagreement that improves the decision. A candidate who pushed until a manager stepped in predicts a long meeting. This is why the same underlying events can produce a strong or a weak answer depending on which of your actions you describe.

Notice that “I convinced them” is not a stage. Persuasion often happens along the way, but interviewers value the process more than the victory, because the process is what will repeat on their team.

1. What the question really measures

Conflict questions come in many wordings: disagreeing with a colleague, a manager, another team, a stakeholder; a difficult coworker; pushing back on a decision; a time you were wrong. Underneath, interviewers are usually scoring some combination of these behaviours:

  • Engagement: you raised the disagreement directly, rather than avoiding it or complaining elsewhere.
  • Respect: you treated the other person as a reasonable colleague and described them fairly.
  • Evidence: you argued from data and reasoning, and looked for ways to let evidence decide.
  • Openness: you were willing to change your mind, and can show a time you did.
  • Judgement about process: you knew who owned the decision and when to escalate.
  • Commitment: you supported the decision once made, including when it went against you.
  • Outcome: the work went well, or at least you learned something specific, and the relationship survived.

Amazon’s principle compresses several of these into two sentences: leaders “are obligated to respectfully challenge decisions when they disagree, even when doing so is uncomfortable or exhausting… They do not compromise for the sake of social cohesion. Once a decision is determined, they commit wholly.” Notice that it rules out both failure modes: avoiding conflict to keep the peace, and fighting on after the decision.

2. Task, relationship and process conflict

Organisational researchers distinguish three kinds of conflict in teams. Task conflict is disagreement about the content of the work: which approach, which metric, whether something is ready. Relationship conflict is personal friction: irritation, dislike, clashing styles. Process conflict is disagreement about how work is organised: who does what, deadlines, procedures.

The distinction matters because the kinds behave differently. A 2012 meta-analysis by de Wit, Greer and Jehn combined 116 studies covering 8,880 groups. They found stable negative relationships between relationship and process conflict and group outcomes. For task conflict they did not find a strong negative relationship with performance, and it was more positively related to performance in some conditions: where task conflict was not entangled with relationship conflict, in top management teams, and where performance was measured as decision quality or financial results. In plain terms: arguing about the work is not inherently bad and can improve decisions, especially if it stays about the work; personal friction is reliably bad.

This gives you two practical rules. First, prefer task-conflict stories. They are the most common in engineering, they are the kind interviewers want to see you handle well, and they let you show reasoning. Second, show that you kept task conflict from becoming relationship conflict. Interviewers note candidates who say things like “I made sure we were arguing about the eval, not about each other” or “I asked for a call because the comment thread was getting sharp”. If you tell a relationship-conflict story (a genuinely difficult colleague), keep it entirely to observable behaviour and what you did, never to judgements about the person’s character.

3. Choosing the story

Good conflict stories share five properties. Check each candidate story from your bank against them.

Some kinds of story are risky even if true. The villain story, where the other person was incompetent or malicious, almost always reflects badly on the teller, because interviewers wonder what the other person would say. The vindication story, where you were right, nobody listened and things went wrong, can work only if you show that you raised it constructively and helped fix it without “I told you so”. The no-conflict story, where everyone agreed after a short chat, does not answer the question. And the escalation-first story, where you went to a manager before talking to the person, is usually a red flag.

4. Describing the other side fairly

The single sentence that most improves a conflict answer is a fair statement of the other person’s view. Compare “The research lead wanted to ship because the model scored higher” with “The research lead wanted to ship because the new model was three points better on our benchmark, which was the metric we had agreed to use, and delaying meant the team would miss a commitment to another group. That was a reasonable position.” The second version shows the interviewer that you understood the reasoning, that the disagreement was genuine, and that you are the kind of colleague who represents others fairly. Interviewers often write exactly that in their notes: “described opposing view fairly”.

A useful test is steelmanning: stating the strongest version of the other side’s argument, the version they would recognise and agree with. If you cannot do it, you probably did not understand their position at the time, which is worth admitting in your reflection. It also prepares you for a follow-up interviewers like to ask: “What would the other person say about this?”

5. Making a disagreement testable

The most effective move in a technical disagreement is to turn it from an argument about opinions into an agreement about evidence. It has three steps.

  1. Find the crux. What specific claim do you disagree about? Often it is narrower than the argument suggests. “The new model is worse” might really be “the new model is worse on long, multi-turn conversations, which are 30% of our traffic but 5% of the benchmark.”
  2. Agree on the evidence in advance. Before running anything, agree on what data would settle the crux and what result would change each person’s mind: “If the new model is at least as good on a sample of 300 real multi-turn conversations, rated by two people blind to which model wrote them, we ship.” Agreeing beforehand matters because once results are in, everyone is tempted to argue about the method.
  3. Run the cheapest test that produces that evidence, time-boxed. A one-week offline comparison, a small online test behind a flag, a shadow deployment that logs both models’ answers. The point is speed: a cheap test that settles the question beats a long debate.

This move is especially natural in AI work, because many disagreements are about evaluation and can be tested. It is also powerful evidence in an interview: it shows judgement, respect (you treated the other view as possibly right) and a bias toward action. The bank question in this track’s category puts it in one line: name the disagreement precisely, agree in advance on the evidence that would settle it, run the cheap experiment, and commit to the outcome either way.

6. Who decides, and what “disagree and commit” really means

Every decision should have an owner: the person accountable for it. In a launch, it might be the product manager or the engineering lead; in a model choice, perhaps the tech lead; in a research question, perhaps the research lead. Many conflicts last longer than they should because nobody is sure who decides. A strong story often contains a moment where the candidate clarified this: “I asked our manager who owned the launch call; it was the PM, so I made sure she had both views written down.”

Once the owner decides, the expectation at most companies is captured by the phrase disagree and commit. Jeff Bezos explained it in his 2016 letter to shareholders: if you have conviction in a direction without consensus, it is helpful to ask, “Look, I know we disagree on this but will you gamble with me on it? Disagree and commit?” He stressed that it is not one way: “If you’re the boss, you should do this too. I disagree and commit all the time.” His example was greenlighting an Amazon Studios original he was sceptical of, writing back that he disagreed and committed and hoped it would become the most watched thing they had made. He also spelled out what it is not: not privately thinking the team is wrong and not worth chasing, but “a genuine disagreement of opinion, a candid expression of my view, a chance for the team to weigh my view, and a quick, sincere commitment to go their way.”

For your story, this means two things must be visible: that you voiced your view clearly before the decision, and that afterwards your actions showed real commitment, such as executing well, defending the decision to others, or improving the plan you had argued against. A candidate who says “I disagreed but went along with it” has shown compliance, not commitment. One who says “once the PM decided to launch, I added the monitoring I had wanted as a condition, so if I was right we would know in a day, not a month” has shown both.

7. When to escalate

Some disagreements cannot be settled by evidence because they are not about facts. They are about goals: a team measured on launch dates and a team measured on incident rates will disagree about readiness however much data they share. Bezos’s letter calls this misalignment: “Sometimes teams have different objectives and fundamentally different views. They are not aligned. No amount of discussion, no number of meetings will resolve that deep misalignment. Without escalation, the default dispute resolution mechanism for this scenario is exhaustion. Whoever has more stamina carries the decision.” His advice is to recognise such cases early and escalate immediately.

Good escalation is a skill, and showing it is strong evidence. Its features are: you tried direct resolution first; you told the other person you thought it needed a decision from above, rather than going around them; you wrote down both positions fairly, ideally together, with the options and trade-offs; and you framed the request as “we need a decision on which goal wins here”, not “please tell them they are wrong”. Interviewers distinguish sharply between a candidate who escalated well and one who ran to the manager.

8. The difficult-colleague question

Some questions are explicitly about relationship conflict: “Tell me about a time you worked with someone difficult.” Here the disagreement is not about an approach but about behaviour: a colleague who misses commitments, dominates meetings, is dismissive in reviews, or does not share information. These stories are riskier, because it is easy to sound as if you are complaining, but they can be strong if told with discipline.

Four rules keep them safe. Describe behaviour, not character: “he reviewed my pull requests three or four days after I asked, and the team’s other reviews took a day” is a fact; “he was lazy” is a judgement. Assume and look for a reason: many difficult behaviours have causes the colleague would explain if asked, such as overload, unclear priorities, or a different understanding of their role. Strong stories often turn on discovering that reason. Show what you did directly with the person before involving anyone else: a private conversation, a concrete request, an offer to help. Show the outcome for the work and the relationship, including partial outcomes; not every difficult relationship becomes a friendship, and an honest “we never became close, but reviews came back within a day after that and the project shipped” is fine.

A useful sentence to have in such a story is the one where you checked your own part: “I also asked myself whether I was contributing to it, and realised I had been sending large pull requests late on Fridays.” Interviewers rarely hear it, and it signals exactly the self-awareness the question is probing.

9. Conflicts AI engineers actually face

It helps to recognise the common shapes, because each has a natural resolution move:

Disagreements about evaluation deserve a special note because they are so common. Offline evaluation measures a model on a fixed labelled dataset; online evaluation measures its effect on real users, for example with an A/B test. Using a language model to score outputs, LLM-as-judge, is a common cheaper substitute for human raters. Each is a proxy, and people disagree about which proxy to trust. The lm14 topic covers evaluation in depth; for an interview story, what matters is showing you treated the disagreement as a question about evidence and found a way to answer it.

The question, from an interviewer assessing collaboration and judgement: “Tell me about a time you disagreed with someone about how to evaluate a model.” Both answers are illustrative examples written for this lesson; the people, company and numbers are invented.

Expect follow-ups such as “What if the test had favoured ROUGE completely?” (the answer: you would have kept it and said so), “How did you choose 200?” (enough to see a difference of a few points in error rate, cheap enough to rate in a day) and “What would she say about how you handled it?”. Each is easy because the story is true and the candidate understood both sides.

Psychological safety and why it shows up in conflict stories

Google’s Project Aristotle studied 180 teams (115 engineering project teams and 65 sales pods) to find what made teams effective, and concluded that how a team works together matters more than who is on it. Of the five dynamics it identified, psychological safety came first: team members feel safe to take interpersonal risks, such as admitting a mistake, asking a question or offering a new idea, without fear of being embarrassed or punished. Disagreeing is one of those risks. This is why interviewers listen for how your behaviour in a conflict affected the people around you. A candidate who won an argument by making a junior colleague feel foolish has reduced their team’s willingness to disagree in future, which is a real cost even if they were right. Stories in which you made it easier for someone else to disagree with you, for example by asking for objections or crediting a dissenting view that turned out right, are strong senior-level evidence.

How task conflict turns into relationship conflict

The meta-analysis finding that task conflict is less harmful when it is weakly tied to relationship conflict points at a mechanism worth understanding. Disagreements about the work become personal through small signals: sarcasm in a comment thread, arguing in front of senior people, attributing a view to someone’s motives (“you just want to hit your launch date”), or repeating an argument after it has been decided. Each makes the other person feel attacked rather than disagreed with, and from then on they defend themselves rather than the idea. Practical counter-moves, which make good details in a story, include moving a heated written thread to a call, raising disagreements privately before meetings, separating the person from the proposal (“I think this plan has a risk” rather than “your plan is risky”), and explicitly acknowledging what is right in their view before saying what you disagree with.

Disagreeing upward and across power differences

Disagreeing with a manager, a much more senior engineer or a famous researcher is harder than disagreeing with a peer, because the costs of being wrong feel higher and deference is tempting. Interviewers for senior roles specifically want evidence that you will raise concerns upward, because silent agreement with a bad decision is expensive. The respectful version has a recognisable shape: do your homework so the concern is concrete; raise it privately first; ask questions that expose the reasoning (“what would we expect to see if the benchmark is not representative?”) rather than asserting; offer a way to test it; and accept the decision. The same story told from the other side, where you were senior and someone junior disagreed with you, is also strong evidence if you show you listened and changed your mind when they were right.

Written disagreement

Many engineering disagreements happen in writing: design documents, review comments, decision records. Writing has real advantages for disagreement: it forces each side to state its position precisely, it lets people respond without the pressure of a meeting, and it leaves a record of what was decided and why. Amazon’s candidate guidance mentions its culture of narrative memos read at the start of meetings, which is one formalised version. In stories, a written artefact often marks the turning point: “I wrote a one-page comparison of both options with the numbers, shared it with her first to make sure it represented her view fairly, then sent it to the decision owner.” That single sentence shows evidence, fairness and good escalation together.

The limits of “disagree and commit”

Commitment has limits, and a mature answer knows them. Committing to a technical or business decision you disagree with is expected; committing to something unethical, unsafe or illegal is not. If a decision would, say, ship a model you have evidence harms a group of users, or use data in a way that breaks the law or a promise to customers, the right move is to escalate through the appropriate channel (a security, privacy, legal or trust-and-safety function), not to commit. Interviewers occasionally probe this boundary with a hypothetical. A strong answer distinguishes clearly between “I think this is a worse choice”, where you commit, and “I think this causes real harm”, where you escalate and, if necessary, refuse.

Cultural differences in directness

Norms for disagreement vary between cultures, companies and individuals. Some teams expect blunt challenge in open meetings; others expect concerns to be raised privately and indirectly. Both can work, and an experienced engineer adapts. In a story, showing that you noticed and adapted to the other person’s style (“he was new and uncomfortable disagreeing in meetings, so I asked for his view one-to-one first”) is strong evidence of skill. In the interview itself, remember that the interviewer’s rubric usually rewards a direct but respectful style: you say clearly what you disagreed with and why, without either hedging it away or sounding combative.

How conflict answers go wrong

The villain. The other person is portrayed as incompetent, political or lazy. Even if accurate, the interviewer learns that you talk about colleagues this way and wonders what your former colleagues would say about you. Fix: describe their actions and reasoning neutrally; if their behaviour really was a problem, describe the behaviour and what you did about it, and stop there.

The non-conflict. “We had different ideas, talked it through and agreed.” No tension, no actions, no evidence. Fix: choose a story where the disagreement lasted and mattered, and where you did something specific to resolve it.

The manager as deus ex machina. The conflict ends because a manager decides, and the candidate’s own contribution stops at arguing. Fix: if a manager decided, show what you did to make that decision good (a fair write-up of both positions, a test that informed it) and what you did after.

The vindication. “I was right, they didn’t listen, it went wrong.” Fix: if you tell it, show that you raised the concern constructively, committed, and then helped fix the problem without blame; and reflect on what you could have done to be more persuasive.

The passive commit. “I disagreed but just did what I was told.” This shows compliance, possibly resentment. Fix: show the actions that made your commitment real.

The generic lesson. “I learned communication is important.” Fix: name a specific practice you adopted, such as agreeing on evidence in advance or raising concerns privately first.

The follow-ups to prepare

Conflict stories attract more follow-ups than most, because interviewers want to check balance and depth. Prepare short answers to these for each of your conflict stories: What exactly did the other person say? Why did they hold that view? What would they say about how you handled it? What would you have done if the test had gone the other way? Who made the final decision? How is your relationship with them now? What would you do differently? A candidate who answers these easily and generously almost always scores well; one who becomes defensive under them usually does not.

When you genuinely lack a good conflict story

Some candidates, often early in their careers or from very harmonious teams, cannot find a strong conflict story. Look harder first: code reviews where you pushed back, design discussions, disagreements with vendors, customers or other teams, disagreements in university projects or open-source work. Disagreements in which you were the one persuaded count too, and are often stronger. If you still have little, tell the best real story you have, honestly sized, and add how you would approach a larger disagreement. A small true story told well beats a large invented one, which follow-up questions are designed to expose.

Hypothetical conflict questions

Some interviewers ask conflict as a hypothetical: “What would you do if a teammate kept rejecting your pull requests for style reasons?” Answer with a clear plan that follows the stages in this lesson (understand their reasoning, find the crux, propose a standard such as an agreed style guide or linter, involve the team or lead if needed, commit) and then, if you can, anchor it in a real example. The plan shows reasoning; the example shows you have actually done it.

Goal: rewrite a weak conflict answer and prepare one of your own. Time: about 40 minutes.

  1. Rewrite the weak example. Take the weak answer in this lesson (the BLEU disagreement). Without inventing an entirely new story, rewrite it so that it: states the data scientist’s view fairly; shows the candidate making the disagreement testable; shows who decided; shows commitment; ends with a measured outcome and a specific lesson. Keep it under 2 minutes 30 seconds when read aloud.
  2. Pick your own story. From your story bank, choose your best conflict story. Check it against the five properties: real stakes, legitimate other side, your actions visible, a resolution, a relationship that survived. If it fails two or more, choose another.
  3. Steelman. Write the other person’s position in three sentences, as they would state it. Ask yourself: would they sign this?
  4. Map the stages. For your story, write one line for each: understand, facts or goals, testable or escalate, owner decides, commit and follow up. Mark any stage where you did nothing; that is a gap an interviewer will probe.
  5. Prepare follow-ups. Write one-line answers to: what would they say about you, what if the evidence had gone the other way, and how is the relationship now?
  6. Record and score your answer using beh1’s signal/noise method.

What to look for: in most first drafts the other person is described in one dismissive phrase and the resolution happens off-stage. A good final version spends about 15 seconds on their view, and most of the action time on how the disagreement was resolved.

Handle the escalation

You lead the AI features for a customer support product. The trust-and-safety lead wants every LLM-drafted reply reviewed by a human before sending, for at least six months. Your product manager wants to auto-send drafts for the 40% of tickets classified as simple, starting next month, to meet a cost target. You have been asked to “sort it out”. You think there is a middle path but have no authority over either person. What do you do, and how would you later tell this as an interview story?

Answer

First recognise the type: this is mostly a goals disagreement (risk versus cost), with some factual questions inside it. Talk to each person separately to understand their reasoning: what specific harms trust and safety fears (wrong refunds, unsafe advice, privacy leaks) and what the cost target really requires (is it 40% auto-send next month, or a cost reduction that could come other ways?). Then separate the facts you can test from the goals you cannot. Facts: how often drafts for “simple” tickets need edits, and how often edits fix something harmful rather than stylistic. You can measure that from existing human-reviewed tickets. Goals: how much risk is acceptable for how much saving.

Propose a decision rule both can evaluate, for example auto-sending only ticket categories where, over the last month of human review, harmful edits were below an agreed rate, starting with a small share and expanding weekly if monitored incident rates stay under a threshold, with instant rollback. Write the options and data in one page, check with both people that it represents their positions fairly, and take it to whoever owns the trade-off between cost and risk (perhaps the product director), jointly. Once decided, commit, and own the monitoring.

As an interview story: open with the disagreement and both positions stated fairly; spend the action time on separating facts from goals, measuring harmful-edit rates, the decision rule and the joint escalation; give the result (what was decided, what the monitoring showed, cost and incident numbers); and end with a lesson such as “when a disagreement is about goals, get it to the person who owns the trade-off early, with the data that makes the trade-off concrete.” This story shows influence without authority, good escalation and judgement about risk, so it can also serve beh7.
