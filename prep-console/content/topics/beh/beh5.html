“Tell me about a time you failed” is the behavioural question candidates dread most, and the one they most often answer badly in one of three predictable ways. Some offer a strength in disguise: “I’d say my biggest failure is that I care too much about quality and sometimes spend too long on details.” Some offer a failure that was entirely someone else’s: “The project failed because the data team kept changing the schema.” And some offer something so small it carries no weight: “Once I forgot to update a config value, but I caught it in review.” All three tell the interviewer the same thing: this candidate is not willing to look honestly at their own mistakes.

That matters a great deal in AI engineering, where failure is routine. Models regress on inputs nobody tested. Evaluation sets turn out not to represent real traffic. A prompt change that improved one behaviour silently breaks the output format another service depends on. An LLM feature that delighted the team in a demo goes unused by customers. A promising fine-tuning direction eats a month and produces nothing. The question for a hiring team is not whether you will fail; you will. It is what happens next: whether you notice quickly, own your part, limit the damage, find the real cause, fix the system so it stays fixed, and change your own behaviour.

Companies say this openly. Amazon’s candidate guidance tells candidates that not every story has to be a success, that failure is part of innovation, and that what matters is what you learned and how you grew; its principles describe leaders as “vocally self-critical, even when doing so is awkward or embarrassing” and as fixing problems “so they stay fixed”. Google’s engineering culture built a whole practice, the blameless postmortem, around learning from failure, and its Site Reliability Engineering book opens the chapter on it with a line worth remembering: “The cost of failure is education.”

This lesson teaches you to tell a failure the way a good postmortem is written, in the first person. It covers what these questions test, how to choose a failure, how to own your part without either blaming others or flagellating yourself, how to describe the response and the systemic fix, and how to prove the lesson stuck. It then covers the neighbouring questions about feedback and weaknesses, and finishes with “Tell me about an LLM feature that failed”, answered weakly and strongly, sentence by sentence.

The best model for a failure answer is a blameless postmortem, told aloud, in the first person. A postmortem, as Google’s SRE book defines it, is “a written record of an incident, its impact, the actions taken to mitigate or resolve it, the root cause(s), and the follow-up actions to prevent the incident from recurring”. That is almost exactly the structure of a strong failure answer: what happened and its impact, what you did about it, why it really happened, and what changed so it will not happen again. Add one thing a postmortem leaves out, your own part and what you personally learned, and you have the answer.

The analogy breaks in one important place, and it is worth understanding. A postmortem is blameless: the SRE book says it “must focus on identifying the contributing causes of the incident without indicting any individual or team”, because if people are shamed for mistakes they stop reporting them. In an interview you keep the blamelessness toward everyone else, but not toward yourself. You are not writing an organisational record; you are giving evidence about your own judgement. So you name your own decisions plainly (“I chose to ship without evaluating on production traffic”), and then, in postmortem style, explain the system that allowed that decision and how you changed it.

This proportion surprises many candidates, who assume the failure is the interesting part. It is not. Everyone fails; the failure only sets up the evidence. What the interviewer is scoring is in the second half: how quickly you noticed, how you limited the damage, whether you told the right people early, whether you found the real cause or stopped at the first one, whether you fixed the system or just the instance, and whether you are a different engineer now. A story where the failure is small but the response is excellent usually scores better than a dramatic failure with a thin response.

A final point about tone. Candidates often tell failure stories in a lowered voice, apologetically, as if confessing. That works against them, because it signals that failure is shameful, which is the opposite of the culture interviewers are hiring for. The right tone is the one an experienced engineer uses when walking colleagues through an incident review: calm, factual, a little curious about what happened, clear about what was wrong, and clearly more interested in what was learned than in what was suffered. Practise the story until you can tell it in that tone.

The same structure handles the neighbouring questions. “Tell me about a mistake” is a failure story narrowed to one decision. “Tell me about critical feedback you received” is a story where someone else detected the failure, in your behaviour, and the evidence is in how you responded. “What is your greatest weakness?” is a failure that is still in progress, where the evidence is the concrete steps you are taking. Learn the failure structure once and all four become manageable.

1. What failure questions test

Failure questions come in many forms: a time you failed, a mistake you made, a deadline you missed, a project that did not work, a decision you regret, something you would do differently, critical feedback you received, your greatest weakness. Underneath, interviewers are usually looking for these behaviours:

  • Accountability: you own your part plainly, without deflecting to others or to circumstances.
  • Self-awareness: you understand why you made the decision you did, and what was wrong with it.
  • Response under pressure: when it went wrong, you acted quickly and sensibly, and told the right people.
  • Depth of analysis: you found the real cause, not just the first one.
  • Systemic thinking: you fixed the conditions that allowed the failure, not just the instance.
  • Learning: you behave differently now, and can show it.
  • Risk judgement: you understand which risks were worth taking, and failing in a way that was cheap and recoverable is itself evidence of good judgement.

Amazon’s principles touch several of these: leaders “are vocally self-critical, even when doing so is awkward or embarrassing” (Earn Trust) and “ensure that defects do not get sent down the line and that problems are fixed so they stay fixed” (Insist on the Highest Standards).

2. Choosing the failure

The choice is most of the battle. A useful failure story has five properties: it is real (it actually failed, with consequences someone cared about); yours, at least in part (your decisions contributed); recoverable (you or the team limited the damage); learned from (you changed something lasting); and reasonably recent, so it reflects who you are now. Calibrate its size:

SizeExampleProblem
Too smallA typo caught in code review.No consequences, no judgement involved, no lesson worth hearing.
RightA model update that regressed answer quality for one customer segment for three days because your evaluation set did not include them.Real impact, your decision involved, recoverable, a clear systemic fix.
RightSpending six weeks on a fine-tuning approach without a clear stopping criterion, delaying a launch.A judgement failure with a cost; a strong lesson about how to run experiments.
Too big or wrong kindA deliberate shortcut that exposed customer data; anything involving dishonesty, harassment or breaking the law.Raises questions about integrity or legal risk that no lesson can answer in an interview.

There is a distinction worth keeping clear: failures of execution (a bug, a missed step) and failures of judgement (choosing the wrong approach, under-estimating a risk, not asking the right question). For mid-level roles either works. For senior roles, judgement failures are usually stronger, because they show you reflecting on decisions at the level you will be operating at. A senior candidate whose only failure is a bug they once shipped may seem to lack self-awareness about bigger choices.

3. Owning your part: the language of accountability

How you describe your part is scored very closely. There are two ways to get it wrong. Deflection puts the cause elsewhere: “the requirements changed”, “the data was bad”, “QA didn’t catch it”. Even if those things were true, the interviewer hears someone who does not see their own role. Over-ownership is the opposite and rarer: dramatic self-criticism (“it was a disaster and entirely my fault, I was so careless”). It sounds unbalanced and, oddly, it also avoids analysis, because if it was all your carelessness there is no system to fix.

Plain ownership sounds like a factual report about yourself: “I decided to…”, “I didn’t check…”, “I assumed… and that was wrong because…”. It includes the reasoning at the time, which makes the mistake understandable without excusing it: “I assumed the benchmark covered our main use case, because it had for the previous two releases.” If others were involved, describe their part factually and briefly, without adjectives, and come back to yours. The SRE book’s rule for postmortems, that you “assume that everyone involved in an incident had good intentions and did the right thing with the information they had”, is a good rule for how you describe others in the story.

4. The response: detect, mitigate, communicate

The middle of a failure answer is what you did once it went wrong, and it carries a lot of evidence. Three things matter.

Detection. How did you find out, and how long did it take? “A customer complained after two weeks” and “our quality monitor alerted within an hour” describe very different situations, and the detection delay is often the most important part of the failure. If you found out late, say so, because the fix will probably include faster detection.

Mitigation. What did you do to limit the damage before the root cause was fixed? In AI systems that is often a rollback to the previous model or prompt, turning off a feature flag, routing traffic to a fallback, or adding a temporary filter. Good mitigation is fast and reversible; describing it shows you think about users first.

Communication. Who did you tell, and when? Telling your manager, the affected teams and, where relevant, customer-facing staff early, before they hear it from someone else, is strong evidence of trustworthiness. “I told my manager and the support lead within the hour, with what we knew and didn’t know yet” is a sentence interviewers note.

5. From root cause to a systemic fix

Most failures have a trigger (the immediate thing that went wrong) and deeper causes (why that thing was possible). A common technique for finding them is the five whys: keep asking why until you reach something you can fix in the system rather than in a person. For example: the new model gave wrong refund amounts (trigger). Why? It had been fine-tuned on old policy documents. Why? The training data snapshot was six months old. Why? Nobody owned data freshness for fine-tuning. Why? The pipeline was built for a one-off experiment and became production without a review. Each step points at a better fix than the last.

The SRE book’s principle applies: “You can’t ‘fix’ people, but you can fix systems and processes to better support people making the right choices.” Fixes can be ranked by how reliably they prevent recurrence:

Fix typeExampleStrength
Personal resolution“I’ll be more careful.”Weakest: depends on memory under pressure.
Documentation or checklistA launch checklist item: check training data date.Better: helps everyone, but can be skipped.
Automated check or guardrailThe training job refuses data older than 30 days; the release gate fails if accuracy on a production-sampled set drops.Strong: works without anyone remembering.
Design changeRetrieval of current policies at answer time instead of training them into the model.Strongest: the failure becomes impossible rather than detected.

In an answer, name the strongest fix you actually put in place, and if it was only a personal resolution, say what you would build now. Interviewers for senior roles in particular listen for the move from instance to class: you did not just fix this bug, you made this kind of bug impossible or immediately visible.

6. The lesson, and proof it stuck

The lesson is where many otherwise good answers fade. “I learned the importance of testing” could be said by anyone and proves nothing. A strong lesson is specific (a concrete practice, not a value), transferable (it applies beyond this one situation) and evidenced (you can show you have used it since). Compare:

  • Weak: “I learned to be more careful with evaluation.”
  • Strong: “I now build the evaluation set from a sample of production traffic before any launch, not from the data we trained on. The next feature I shipped, a ticket classifier, I did that from day one, and it caught a whole category of tickets the team hadn’t thought of.”

The second sentence of the strong version, the later application, is the most powerful line in any failure answer. It turns a claim about learning into evidence of learning. Look for one for every failure story in your bank.

8. Missed deadlines and blown estimates

A very common variant is “Tell me about a time you missed a deadline” or “a project that took much longer than expected.” AI projects are notoriously hard to estimate, because whether a model approach will reach the required quality is often unknown until you try it, so most AI engineers have a story here. The structure is the same as any failure, with three specific points interviewers listen for.

First, when you raised it. The single most important fact in a missed-deadline story is how early you told people the date was at risk. “I told the PM in week two, when the first evaluation came back at 70% against a 90% target, that the date was unlikely” is very different from “we realised the week before launch”. Late surprise is what damages trust; early warning with options preserves it.

Second, the options you offered. A strong story shows that you did not just report the slip but gave the decision owner choices: cut scope (launch for one ticket category instead of all), lower the bar with safeguards (launch with human review), add people or time, or stop. That turns a failure of estimation into evidence of judgement.

Third, what you changed in how you estimate. Good lessons here are concrete: running a cheap feasibility test (a day of prompting or a small evaluation) before committing to a date; giving estimates as ranges with the main uncertainty named (“two to six weeks, depending on whether retrieval alone gets us to 90%”); or setting checkpoints where the plan is revisited. A candidate who says “I learned to pad my estimates” has learned the wrong thing; one who says “I now separate the research question from the engineering work and estimate them differently” has learned the right one.

9. Feedback questions

Two feedback questions are common: “Tell me about critical feedback you received” and “Tell me about a time you gave someone difficult feedback.” Both are close relatives of the failure question.

For receiving feedback, the evidence is in your response. A strong story shows that you listened without defending yourself, asked questions until you understood the specific behaviour and its effect, decided what to change, changed it, and followed up with the person to check it worked. Choose feedback that was about something significant (how you ran a project, how you communicated risk, how you treated colleagues in reviews), not cosmetic, and ideally feedback you initially disagreed with or that stung, because that is where your response shows.

For giving feedback, research is a useful guide. Kluger and DeNisi’s 1996 meta-analysis of 607 effects found that feedback improved performance on average, but in over a third of cases it made performance worse. The pattern they described is that feedback which shifts attention away from the task and toward the person (their self-image, how they compare with others) tends to hurt. In practice that means good feedback is about specific behaviour and its effect on the work, given privately and promptly, with an offer to help. A strong “gave feedback” story shows exactly those features, how the person reacted, and what changed.

10. “What is your greatest weakness?”

This is a failure question about the present. Interviewers want self-awareness: can you see your own limitations accurately, and are you doing something about them? The structure has three parts: a real weakness that is not fatal for the role; a brief example of its effect; and the concrete steps you are taking, with evidence they are working. For example: “I tend to go deep into a technical problem before checking it’s the right problem. On one project that cost a week building a retrieval improvement the PM didn’t need. Now I write a one-paragraph problem statement and get it confirmed before I start anything longer than a day, and in the last quarter that caught two misunderstandings early.”

Avoid humblebrags (“I’m a perfectionist”), weaknesses that are core requirements for the role (an AI engineer who says “I don’t really like evaluating models”), and weaknesses with no plan. A weakness with a credible improvement plan is a mild positive; a weakness without one is a mild negative; a humblebrag is a clear negative.

The strong answer is about the same failure (a team was pressured, requirements were vague; those may well have been true), but it spends its time on the candidate’s own decision, a same-day response, a root cause, a fix at the design level and proof of learning. The interviewer’s likely follow-ups, “Why didn’t you check data freshness before launch?” and “How did the sales leads react?”, are easy to answer from the same honest story.

Why blamelessness works, and where it came from

The SRE book explains that blameless culture “originated in the healthcare and avionics industries where mistakes can be fatal”, environments where every mistake is treated as an opportunity to strengthen the system. The mechanism is about information. If people are punished for mistakes, they hide them, and an organisation that cannot see its mistakes cannot fix the conditions that caused them; the book warns that blame risks a culture in which incidents and issues are “swept under the rug”. Google’s Project Aristotle points at the same dynamic from another angle: the top factor in effective teams was psychological safety, which includes feeling safe to admit a mistake. When you tell a failure story calmly and in detail, you also show the interviewer that you would contribute to that kind of team: someone who reports problems early and helps fix systems rather than assigning blame.

Hindsight bias, and judging decisions rather than outcomes

After something has gone wrong, it is very easy to believe it was obviously going to go wrong. Psychologists call this hindsight bias. It distorts failure stories in two directions: candidates either beat themselves up for not foreseeing something that was genuinely hard to foresee, or, when they want to defend themselves, they point out that nobody could have known. A mature answer separates the two questions explicitly: was the decision reasonable given what was knowable at the time, and was better information cheaply available? If you decided reasonably and got unlucky, say so, and describe what you would still change (often, a cheaper early test). If better information was available and you did not look for it, that is the real lesson, and it is usually a more valuable one.

How level changes the failure you should tell

The scope of your failure stories signals your level just as success stories do. An early-career engineer’s failure might be a bug or a missed edge case, with a fix such as a test. A mid-level engineer’s might be a project-level mistake: a wrong technical approach, an estimate badly missed, a risk not raised. A senior engineer’s strongest failures are usually about judgement and influence: not challenging a plan they had doubts about, letting a team build on an evaluation they did not trust, prioritising a technically interesting problem over the one that mattered. The systemic fixes scale too: from a test, to a team process, to a change in how several teams make decisions. Choose a failure whose scope matches the role, and make sure the fix you describe is at that level.

Near misses and negative results

Not every good failure story involves harm. A near miss, where a failure was caught just before it reached users, can carry all the same evidence, especially if the catch happened because of something you did, and if you then fixed the conditions that nearly let it through. Similarly, a negative result in research or modelling (an approach that did not work) can be a strong story if you designed the work so the result was clear, stopped at the right time, and the team used what you learned. The weak version of a negative-result story is one where the work dragged on with no stopping criterion; the strong version shows a criterion set in advance and a decision made against it, which is exactly the judgement interviewers want from someone spending expensive compute.

Failures you led versus failures you caused

Senior candidates are often asked about failures of teams they led or projects they owned, where no single decision of theirs was the cause. The accountability expected is different but no smaller. An owner is accountable for the outcome, including decisions others made under their leadership, and for the conditions they created: unclear goals, missing checkpoints, a culture where people did not raise concerns. A strong answer from a technical lead might be: “I owned the launch, and I didn’t create a point where anyone could stop it; three people had doubts about the evaluation and none of them felt it was their place to block. I now run a short pre-launch review where I ask each person directly whether they would ship it.” That kind of answer shows the candidate understands that leading a team means owning the system the team works in, which is precisely what senior failure questions are probing.

Receiving feedback well is a skill, not a temperament

Many candidates treat their reaction to criticism as a fixed part of their personality. It is better understood as a set of behaviours that can be practised: pausing before responding; asking for specific examples; separating the behaviour from your intent (“I meant to be thorough; the effect was that reviews took a week”); looking for the part that is true even if the rest is not; deciding on one change; and closing the loop with the person weeks later. Interviewers recognise this sequence, and candidates who describe it, with a real example, come across as people who will improve quickly on the job. That is a big part of what “learn and be curious” or “growth mindset” values are trying to capture.

How failure answers go wrong

The humblebrag. “I care too much”, “I work too hard”, “I’m a perfectionist”. Interviewers have heard these many times and score them as evasion. Fix: tell a real failure with real consequences.

The deflection. Every cause is outside the candidate: requirements, leadership, other teams, data. Fix: find and name your own decision; if your part was genuinely small, choose a different story.

The trivial failure. A typo caught in review. No consequences, no judgement, no lesson. Fix: choose a failure that cost something.

The catastrophe or the integrity problem. A failure so large, or of such a kind (dishonesty, a privacy breach through a deliberate shortcut), that the interviewer is left worried. Fix: choose a different story; some failures are better left for a conversation with a trusted mentor than an interview.

The ancient failure. Something from university or ten years ago. It suggests you have not failed (or not noticed) since. Fix: use something from the last few years.

No fix, or a personal fix only. “I learned to be more careful.” Fix: describe the system change you made, or would make now.

No proof of learning. The lesson is stated but never applied. Fix: find the later example where you did it differently.

Accepting an offered excuse. The interviewer says “sounds like that wasn’t really your fault” and the candidate agrees with relief. Interviewers sometimes do this deliberately. Fix: thank them and restate your part honestly.

Confidentiality and incidents

Production failures often involve details your former employer would not want shared: customer names, the size of an outage, internal system names, security details. You can tell a strong failure story without any of them. Describe the system generically (“our main customer-facing model”), give impact as a relative or rounded figure (“a few percent of requests for about three days”), and never share security vulnerability details. If an incident was publicly reported, you can refer to what was public, but be careful not to add anything that was not.

Building your failure stories

Your best raw material is postmortems, incident reports, retrospectives and project reviews you were part of, and your own memory of decisions you would make differently. For each candidate story, write the postmortem version first (trigger, impact, detection, mitigation, contributing causes, fixes) and then the first-person version (your decision, your reasoning at the time, what you did, what you changed, where you have applied it since). Having at least two failure stories in your bank matters, because some loops ask about failure twice, and because “tell me about another one” is a common follow-up when the first story is small.

The follow-ups to prepare

Failure stories attract probing follow-ups: Why did you make that decision at the time? What would you do differently? How did you find out? Who did you tell, and when? What did your manager say? How do you know the fix worked? Has anything similar happened since? Would you take the same risk again? Prepare short honest answers to each for every failure story. The last one deserves thought: sometimes the right answer is yes, with better safeguards, because the risk was worth taking and the failure was cheap, and saying so shows good judgement about risk rather than over-correction.

Goal: turn one of your own failures into a strong answer by writing its postmortem first. Time: about 40 minutes.

  1. Choose. List three candidate failures from the last three years. For each, mark: real consequences? my decision involved? recovered? lesson applied since? Pick the one with the most marks.
  2. Write the postmortem (10 min). In neutral, blameless language: trigger, impact (with numbers if possible), detection (how, how long), mitigation, contributing causes (use five whys), fixes.
  3. Write your part (5 min). In one or two sentences, the decision you made and your reasoning at the time. No adjectives about yourself or others.
  4. Rank your fix. Was it a personal resolution, a checklist, an automated guardrail or a design change? If it was only personal, write the stronger fix you would build now.
  5. Find the proof. Write one sentence about a later time you applied the lesson.
  6. Assemble and time it. Target about 150 seconds, with roughly 40 seconds on what happened and your part, and the rest on response, fix and lesson. Record it and check the split.
  7. Prepare follow-ups for: why did you decide that then; who did you tell; would you take the risk again.

If you can, tell the finished story to someone who worked with you at the time and ask whether it matches their memory. A story your former colleague would recognise is a story that will survive any follow-up.

What to look for: most first drafts spend over a minute on the failure and under 20 seconds on the fix and lesson. Flip that ratio, and check that no sentence blames anyone.

Repair the answer

A candidate’s failure story: “I fine-tuned a model to classify support tickets. In testing it got 94% accuracy, so we deployed it. In production it routed lots of billing tickets to the wrong queue and the billing team was furious. It turned out the test set was from before we launched a new pricing plan, so the model hadn’t seen those tickets. The data team should have given us more recent data. We retrained it and it was fine.” Identify what is weak, and rewrite the key sentences.

Answer

Weaknesses: the candidate’s own decision is invisible (“so we deployed it”); the cause is placed on the data team; there is no detection detail (how long did misrouting go on, how was it found?), no mitigation, no communication with the billing team, no measured impact, a fix that only addresses the instance (“retrained it”) and no lesson at all. “Furious” describes another team’s emotion without describing what the candidate did about it.

Rewritten key sentences: “I decided to deploy on the strength of a 94% test accuracy without checking whether the test set reflected current tickets; it was from before the new pricing plan launched.” “The billing team noticed after four days that about a quarter of billing tickets were going to the general queue; I switched billing-related keywords back to the old rules-based router that afternoon and apologised to the billing lead, with a plan.” “The root cause was that our evaluation data had no freshness check, and nothing in our process asked whether the product had changed since the data was collected. I retrained on the last month of tickets, added a weekly evaluation on a fresh sample from production, and added a release-checklist question: what has changed in the product since this data was collected?” “Misrouting dropped back below 2%. Since then I always evaluate on the most recent month of real traffic before deploying, which is how we caught a similar shift when the refund policy changed.” This version owns the decision, responds quickly, fixes the system and proves the lesson.
