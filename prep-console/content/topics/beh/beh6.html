Imagine two engineers on the same team, both asked in their interviews for “the most impactful thing you’ve worked on”. Both worked on the same LLM support assistant. The first says: “I implemented the retrieval service and the caching layer. I completed all my tickets on time and the assistant launched in March.” The second says: “After launch I noticed that a third of conversations were about order status, which the assistant couldn’t answer, so agents had to take over. Nobody owned that gap, so I proposed connecting it to the order system, ran a two-day prototype to show it would work, and after it shipped, agent hand-offs dropped from 34% to 21%.” Same team, same project. The first engineer describes tasks. The second describes an outcome they took responsibility for, a problem they defined, and a measured change.

Interviewers hear the difference immediately, and it shapes their view of level more than almost anything else in a behavioural round. Three themes are tangled together here, and they come up in nearly every loop. Ownership: do you treat outcomes as yours, or only the tasks you are assigned? Ambiguity: what do you do when the problem, the goal or the path is unclear? Impact: can you show that your work changed something people care about, and do you choose work that will?

These themes matter especially for AI engineers. AI work is unusually ambiguous: success metrics are often undefined (“make the assistant better”), feasibility is unknown until you try (will retrieval get us to 90% accuracy, or do we need fine-tuning?), and data and requirements shift under you. It is also work where ownership gaps are common: the evaluation set nobody maintains, the drift nobody monitors, the cost nobody watches. Engineers who notice those gaps and close them are exactly who hiring managers want.

This lesson covers what interviewers look for in each theme and how to tell stories that show it. It explains Amazon’s widely quoted ideas about decision speed, one-way and two-way doors and deciding with around 70% of the information, from Jeff Bezos’s own shareholder letters; how to reduce ambiguity step by step; how to trace and honestly attribute impact; and how prioritisation questions fit in. The worked example answers “Tell me about a time you had to work on something with no clear requirements”, weakly and strongly, with the interviewer’s notes and timings.

A helpful picture is the difference between a contractor and an owner of a house. A contractor builds what is on the plan, to spec, and leaves. If the plan has a flaw, that is the architect’s problem. An owner lives there. If the roof leaks after the builders leave, it is the owner’s problem, so the owner checks the gutters, notices the damp patch early and gets it fixed, even though nobody assigned that job. Interviewers asking about ownership want to know whether you think like the owner of what you build. Amazon’s Ownership principle describes the same thing: owners “think long term”, “act on behalf of the entire company, beyond just their own team”, and “never say ‘that’s not my job’”.

The analogy breaks in a useful way: a homeowner has authority over the house, while at work you usually do not own the neighbouring systems. Ownership at work therefore often means making sure a problem gets fixed, by the right person, rather than fixing it yourself. That distinction will matter later.

Ambiguity is the second theme. A good picture is a funnel. A vague request goes in at the top (“can we use AI to help the support team?”); a clear, tested plan comes out at the bottom. Each step narrows the uncertainty a little, at the lowest cost that will do it.

Impact is the third theme, and the simplest to state: what measurably changed because of your work? The subtle parts are choosing the right measure (something people actually care about, not a vanity number), and being honest about attribution (how you know your work caused the change).

One more framing helps candidates who find these questions uncomfortable. Many engineers think of ambiguity as a temporary defect in a project, something that a better manager or a clearer spec would have prevented. In AI engineering it is closer to the normal state. Nobody can tell you in advance whether retrieval alone will reach the accuracy a use case needs, how users will phrase their questions, or which failure modes a model will show on real traffic. The engineers who do well are not the ones who are given clearer specs; they are the ones who are good at making things clear, quickly and cheaply. When an interviewer asks about ambiguity, they are asking whether you are one of those engineers. Treat the question as an invitation to describe a skill, not to apologise for a messy project.

The three themes connect. Owners notice problems; noticed problems usually start ambiguous; handling the ambiguity well is how you reach impact. That is why one good story often serves all three themes, and why your story bank should contain at least two stories in which you defined a problem nobody had defined, reduced its uncertainty cheaply, and produced a measured result.

2. Ownership: outcomes, not tasks

The core of ownership is a shift in what you consider your job. A task-oriented engineer’s job ends when the ticket is closed. An outcome-oriented engineer’s job ends when the thing the ticket was for actually works for the people it was for, and keeps working. In stories, ownership shows up in a few recognisable moves:

  • Noticing an orphaned problem: something hurting outcomes that nobody owns. In AI teams, typical orphans are the evaluation set nobody updates, the drift nobody monitors, the cost nobody tracks and the failure category nobody has looked at.
  • Following through after launch: watching the real behaviour of what you shipped and acting on what you see, without being asked.
  • Thinking long term: choosing the slightly slower path that avoids a future problem, and explaining why.
  • Acting beyond your team: fixing or reporting something in a neighbouring system because it hurts the overall outcome.
  • Saying no: declining or stopping work that would not serve the outcome, even when it is interesting.

Ownership has a boundary that good stories respect. Taking ownership of a problem that sits in another team’s system does not mean silently changing their code. It means making sure the problem gets fixed: telling the owners with evidence, offering help, agreeing who will do what, and checking that it happened. A story where you quietly “fixed” another team’s service without telling them can read as disrespectful or risky. A story where you noticed, proposed, agreed ownership and followed up reads as mature. One sentence often makes the difference: “I checked with the platform team first; they didn’t have capacity, so we agreed I’d make the change and they would review it.”

3. Ambiguity: reducing uncertainty cheaply

AI work is full of ambiguity, and it helps to name the kinds, because each has a different remedy:

Kind of ambiguityExampleRemedy
Unclear problem“Use AI to help the support team.”Talk to users and look at data; write a problem statement.
Unclear success“Make the answers better.”Agree a measurable target with the owner, and a guard metric.
Unclear feasibility“Can an LLM do this reliably?”A cheap feasibility test: a day of prompting on 50 real examples.
Unclear data“We probably have the data somewhere.”Find it, sample it, and measure its quality before planning around it.
Moving requirementsPriorities change mid-project.Checkpoints; reversible decisions; keep the plan cheap to change.

The common thread is cheapness. The goal is to reduce uncertainty with the smallest investment that will do it, before committing to anything expensive. A one-page problem statement takes a day and can prevent a month of building the wrong thing. A feasibility test on 50 real examples can show in two days whether prompting alone gets close to the target or whether fine-tuning will be needed. Stories that show this sequence (clarify, define success, test cheaply, then commit) are strong ambiguity evidence. Stories that jump from a vague ask to a large build, or that stall waiting for someone to provide clarity, are weak.

A problem statement is worth describing in detail because it is such a powerful move. In a few sentences it says who has the problem, what it costs them now (with a number if possible), what success would look like and how it will be measured, and what is out of scope. Writing it forces the ambiguity into the open: you will discover what you do not know. Sharing it with the stakeholder and asking “is this the problem?” often reveals that it is not quite, which is the cheapest possible time to find out.

4. Deciding at the right speed: one-way and two-way doors

Ambiguity stories always involve a decision made with incomplete information. The most useful framework for these comes from Jeff Bezos’s shareholder letters. In his 2015 letter he distinguished two kinds of decision. Some are “consequential and irreversible or nearly irreversible – one-way doors – and these decisions must be made methodically, carefully, slowly, with great deliberation and consultation”; he called these Type 1. “But most decisions aren’t like that – they are changeable, reversible – they’re two-way doors”; these Type 2 decisions “can and should be made quickly by high judgment individuals or small groups”. He warned that large organisations tend to use the heavy Type 1 process for everything, with the result of “slowness, unthoughtful risk aversion, failure to experiment sufficiently”.

In his 2016 letter he added a rule of thumb: “most decisions should probably be made with somewhere around 70% of the information you wish you had. If you wait for 90%, in most cases, you’re probably being slow.” The condition attached matters as much as the number: “you need to be good at quickly recognizing and correcting bad decisions.” Being fast is only safe if you can tell quickly when you were wrong and reverse.

For AI engineers, the framework translates directly:

  • Two-way doors: a prompt change behind a feature flag, a new model version in a shadow deployment or for 5% of traffic, a retrieval parameter, which evaluation examples to label first. Decide quickly, monitor, roll back if needed.
  • One-way doors, or close to them: deleting raw data you would need to retrain; sending user data to a new third party; a multi-year vendor or hardware commitment; a public launch that sets user expectations; a data schema many teams will build on. Slow down, consult, and test cheaply first.

A strong ambiguity story often contains the move of turning a one-way door into a two-way door: instead of a big-bang launch, a staged rollout with a rollback; instead of migrating every team at once, one team first. That single move shows you understand risk, and it is the most common way experienced engineers make fast progress safely.

5. Impact: measuring what changed

Impact needs a number, or an honest substitute, and the right number. A useful tool is the impact chain: the path from your action to the outcome someone cares about.

System metrics (evaluation accuracy, retrieval recall, latency) are the easiest to attribute but matter least to stakeholders. Business results (cost, revenue, retention) matter most but are hardest to attribute. In between are user metrics such as draft acceptance, resolution time or task completion, often the best place to anchor an AI impact story. The strongest attribution comes from an A/B test (users randomly assigned to old or new), or from a staged rollout where you can compare groups. A simple before-and-after comparison is weaker, because other things change at the same time; if that is all you have, say so.

Watch for vanity metrics: numbers that look impressive but do not reflect value. “The assistant handled 2 million messages” says nothing about whether those messages helped anyone. “Agent hand-offs fell from 34% to 21%” does. Interviewers notice the difference, and choosing a meaningful metric is itself evidence of judgement.

6. Prioritisation: impact judgement in advance

Prioritisation questions (“How do you decide what to work on when everything is a priority?”) are impact questions asked before the fact. A strong answer has three elements: a way to compare options, a way to agree the ranking, and honesty about what you are not doing. Compare options by the outcome each would move and the cost of delay (what is lost for each week it waits), adjusted for effort and reversibility. Agree the ranking with whoever owns the priorities rather than deciding alone, because the most common prioritisation failure is a correct ranking that the stakeholder never agreed to. And say explicitly what you are deprioritising and why, so nobody is surprised. The bank question on this theme puts it neatly: show the judgement, not the busyness. Follow the principle with a real example; as with all process questions, the example is what makes it believable.

7. Requirements that change mid-project

A specific kind of ambiguity question is “Tell me about a time the requirements changed in the middle of a project.” The evidence interviewers want here is less about the change itself, which is normal, and more about two things: how much the change cost you, and how you handled it with the people involved.

How much a change costs depends largely on decisions made before it happened. A project built in small, reversible steps with frequent checkpoints absorbs a change cheaply: you lose a week, not a quarter. A project that committed early to irreversible choices (a large fine-tuning run, a data schema others built on, a public announcement) absorbs the same change expensively. So a strong answer often includes a sentence about why the change was cheap (“because we had launched to one team behind a flag, switching the target user group cost us four days”) or, honestly, why it was expensive and what you now do differently.

On the people side, interviewers listen for whether you understood why the requirement changed (a new customer, a competitor, a leadership decision, something learned from users) rather than treating it as arbitrary, whether you made the cost of the change visible to whoever requested it (“this will move the launch by two weeks; here are two options that don’t”), and whether, once the change was decided, you committed to it rather than relitigating it. That last point connects directly to beh4’s disagree and commit: a change you disagreed with is still a change you deliver well.

8. Ownership after launch: what AI owners watch

Some of the strongest ownership evidence comes after launch, because that is when most people’s attention moves elsewhere. AI systems are particularly likely to change behaviour after launch without any code change: user behaviour shifts, the documents behind retrieval go stale, an upstream model provider updates a model, traffic grows into parts of the input space nobody tested. An owner sets up ways to see this and acts on it. The ae3 topic covers monitoring in depth; for stories, the moves interviewers recognise are these.

  • Watching quality, not just uptime: sampling real conversations each week, tracking a quality metric on fresh production data, or tracking user signals such as edits, thumbs-down or hand-offs.
  • Watching cost: per-feature and per-request spend, with an alert when it moves sharply.
  • Reading the failures: regularly reviewing the worst-rated or escalated cases and grouping them into categories, which is how orphaned problems are usually found.
  • Closing the loop: turning a discovered failure category into an evaluation case so it is checked on every future change.

A story told from this angle often begins weeks after the official end of the project: “Three weeks after launch, in my weekly read-through of escalated conversations, I noticed…”. That opening alone signals ownership, because it shows a habit nobody assigned.

9. How level changes these stories

These three themes are the ones where level shows most clearly. At mid-level, a strong ownership story might be following through on your own feature after launch; a strong ambiguity story might be turning a vague ticket into a clear plan; impact is a measured change in your area. At senior level, ownership extends beyond your team (you noticed a problem in how several teams evaluate models and fixed the shared process), ambiguity is about defining problems others had not seen (you identified that agent hand-offs were the real cost, not answer quality), and impact is measured at the user or business level with honest attribution. At staff level and above, the stories are about choosing which problems an organisation should work on and changing how decisions are made. Choose stories that match your target level, and say the scope explicitly.

Note how the strong answer serves three themes at once: ambiguity (the funnel), ownership (taking on the vague ask, fixing the runbook template across teams) and impact (a measured result on an agreed metric). That is typical of the best stories in a bank, and it is why it should be told with different emphasis depending on which theme the question targets.

Bias for action versus recklessness

Amazon’s Bias for Action principle says “Speed matters in business. Many decisions and actions are reversible and do not need extensive study. We value calculated risk taking.” The word candidates often overlook is calculated. Interviewers asking about decisions under uncertainty are checking two things at once: that you do not stall waiting for certainty, and that you do not take irreversible risks casually. The way to show both is to make your reasoning about reversibility explicit in the story: “this was a two-way door because the change was behind a flag and we could roll back in minutes, so I decided the same day” or “deleting the old logs was a one-way door, so I waited for the legal review even though it cost a week”. Candidates who describe every fast decision as admirable, including irreversible ones, signal poor risk judgement.

Owning outcomes you do not fully control

Most meaningful outcomes depend on other people: a model’s usefulness depends on the product design, a feature’s adoption depends on the users’ managers, a cost saving depends on another team’s migration. Ownership of such outcomes cannot mean controlling them. It means influencing every lever you can reach, making dependencies visible early, and reporting honestly on the outcome even when the shortfall is in someone else’s area. Interviewers listen for a mature version of this: “I owned adoption, so when usage stalled I sat with three support leads to understand why, and found their dashboards didn’t show the assistant’s suggestions. I couldn’t change their dashboards, but I made the case to the team that could.” That is ownership without control, and it is exactly what senior roles need (beh7 goes further into influence).

Goodhart’s law and owning the measurement

A frequently quoted observation, known as Goodhart’s law, holds that when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure, because people optimise the number rather than the thing it stood for. AI systems are especially prone to this, since they can be tuned directly against a metric. An assistant tuned to raise “resolved without hand-off” might learn to discourage users from asking for a human; a summariser tuned on an automatic overlap score might copy more text without becoming more useful. A true owner owns the measurement as well as the number: they read real conversations alongside the dashboard, add guard metrics for the obvious side effects, and say openly when a reported gain looks suspicious. A story in which you questioned your own good-looking metric is unusually strong evidence of ownership and integrity.

Attribution under real-world conditions

Interviewers who probe impact often ask, “How do you know your change caused that?” The rigorous answer is a randomised comparison, but many teams cannot run one for every change. Intermediate options, in decreasing strength, are: a staged rollout where you compare groups that got the change earlier and later; a holdout group kept on the old version; a before-and-after comparison with a check that nothing else changed at the same time; and, weakest, a before-and-after with no checks. Each is acceptable if you name it. AI features also suffer from novelty effects (users try the new thing because it is new, then usage falls) and seasonality (support volume changes with the calendar), so a week of good numbers is weak evidence. Mentioning the method and its limits, briefly, makes your impact claim more credible, not less.

Ownership includes saying no

Ownership is often illustrated with stories of taking more on, but some of the strongest ownership stories are about taking something off. An owner of an outcome sometimes concludes that a requested feature would not serve it, that a project should be stopped, or that the team should not take on a new commitment because it would endanger an existing one. Saying so, with evidence and a proposed alternative, is harder than saying yes, and interviewers know it. The shape of a good “no” story is: the request and who made it; the evidence that it would not serve the outcome (usage data, an evaluation, the cost); the alternative you proposed; how the decision was made; and the result. As with conflict stories, it must not be a story about overruling someone, but about helping the decision owner make a better call.

When ownership becomes heroics

A common ownership story is the heroic rescue: the engineer who worked all weekend to save a launch. Interviewers have mixed feelings about these. The effort shows commitment, but repeated heroics often indicate a process failure that the hero is masking, and a team that depends on heroics is fragile. If you tell a heroic story, add the second half: what you changed afterwards so that it would not need a hero next time. That turns a story about effort into a story about ownership of the system, which is what senior interviewers are looking for.

How these answers go wrong

The ticket-taker. “I implemented the components I was assigned, on time.” Good execution, no ownership. Fix: find the moment where you went beyond the assignment: noticed a gap, followed up after launch, changed the plan for the better.

Waiting for clarity. An ambiguity story in which the candidate asked for requirements, waited, and then executed. Fix: show what you did to create clarity: data gathering, a problem statement, a cheap test.

The leap. An ambiguity story where the candidate jumped from a vague ask straight into a large build, and it worked out. It may have been luck; interviewers wonder. Fix: show the cheap steps you took first, or reflect honestly on the risk you took.

The vanity metric. “Handled 2 million queries”, “10,000 users”. Fix: choose a metric tied to value, such as a resolution rate, time saved or errors avoided, with a baseline.

The unattributed claim. “Revenue went up 20% after we launched.” The interviewer asks how you know, and there is no answer. Fix: claim the link you can support and label the rest.

The silent fixer. An ownership story where the candidate changed another team’s system without telling them. Fix: include the agreement with the owning team.

Preparing impact numbers

Before any loop, go through each story in your bank and write down, for each, the impact chain: action, system metric, user metric, business result, with numbers where you have them and a note on how each was measured. Where you lack a number, decide on the best honest proxy and how you will phrase it. This takes an hour and pays off in every round, because impact questions and impact follow-ups (“what was the result?”, “how did you measure it?”) come up constantly. It also exposes stories that have no measurable result at all, which you may want to replace.

Common AI-engineer ownership stories

If you are short of ownership stories, look for these moments in your work, which AI engineers often have but do not recognise as ownership: building or maintaining an evaluation set nobody asked for; adding monitoring for quality drift or cost after launch; noticing a data quality problem upstream and getting it fixed at the source; writing the runbook for a model service; stopping a model launch because of a risk others had not seen; or cleaning up after an experiment that turned into production without anyone owning it. Each is ownership of an outcome beyond a task, and each usually comes with a measurable result.

Adapting to company language

Companies phrase these themes differently. Amazon’s principles include Ownership, Bias for Action, Dive Deep and Deliver Results; Google’s guidance talks about navigating complexity and ambiguity and about data-driven impact; other companies use words like “drive”, “accountability” or “act like an owner”. The underlying evidence is the same. Read the target company’s own language (beh9 covers several companies’ published values) and use their terms in your preparation, so you recognise which theme a question targets, but tell the stories in plain language.

Goal: build impact chains for your stories and practise the ambiguity funnel. Time: about 45 minutes.

  1. Impact chains (20 min). For four stories in your bank, fill in: your action; the system metric it moved (before → after); the user metric (before → after); the business result; and for each link, how it was measured (A/B test, staged rollout, before-and-after, estimate). Mark the furthest-right link you can honestly support.
  2. Find the vanity metrics. Circle any number that does not reflect value (volume, users, messages). Replace each with a value metric, or note that you need one.
  3. Ambiguity funnel (15 min). Pick your best ambiguity story and write one line for each layer: the vague ask; how you defined the problem; the success metric and who agreed it; the cheapest feasibility test; the reversible first decision; the measurement. Mark any layer you skipped. If you skipped one, add a sentence of reflection on what it cost or would have saved.
  4. Doors. List three decisions from your stories and label each a one-way or two-way door. For each, write one sentence on whether your decision speed matched.
  5. Record the ambiguity story and check the first action starts within about 30 seconds.

Keep the impact chains with your story cards so they are in front of you before each loop.

What to look for: most people find that their impact numbers stop at the system metric (accuracy, latency) and that their ambiguity stories skip the problem statement. Extending one chain to a user metric, and adding the problem-definition step to one story, will noticeably strengthen both answers.

Make the decision visible

You are an AI engineer at a mid-sized company. Your manager asks you to “reduce our LLM costs, they’re getting out of hand” and gives you two weeks. Monthly spend has doubled in three months. You have options: switch the default model to a smaller one, add caching, shorten prompts, or negotiate a committed-use contract with the provider. Walk through how you would handle the ambiguity and which decisions are one-way or two-way doors, then describe how you would tell it as an interview story afterwards.

Answer

Start by reducing ambiguity cheaply. Define the problem: “out of hand” relative to what? Agree a target with the manager (for example, back to last quarter’s monthly spend without dropping below an agreed quality bar) and a guard metric for quality. Then spend a day on data: break spend down by feature, model and request type. Typically a few features or request types dominate, and the breakdown often shows something specific, such as a retry loop or very long contexts on one endpoint.

Classify the options. Caching and prompt shortening are two-way doors: implement them behind flags for the heaviest endpoints, measure cost and quality, and roll back if quality drops. Switching the default model to a smaller one is mostly a two-way door technically, but needs an evaluation on production-sampled data first, and a staged rollout, because quality regressions may be subtle. A committed-use contract is close to a one-way door, since it commits money for a long period based on a forecast that your other changes are about to alter. Do it last, if at all, after the usage pattern has settled, and with finance involved.

As an interview story (ambiguity, ownership, impact): open with the vague ask and the doubled spend; spend the action time on defining the target and guard metric, the spend breakdown and what it revealed, the reversible changes and their staged rollout, and the deliberate decision to delay the contract; give the result (spend change with baseline, quality metric unchanged within the agreed margin); and finish with a lesson such as “break the cost down before choosing a fix, and keep the irreversible commitment for last”.
