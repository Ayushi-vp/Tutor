An AI engineer’s most important work often depends on people they cannot instruct. You build an evaluation suite that would catch regressions before release, but each product team decides whether to run it. You find that the research team’s benchmark does not reflect real traffic, but they own their release criteria. You believe the company should stop sending customer data to a third-party model, but that decision belongs to product, legal and security. You know a junior colleague is stuck, but you are not their manager. In each case, being right is not enough. Something only changes if you can get other people to act.

This is what interviewers mean by leadership when they ask individual contributors about it, and it is why the theme comes up in almost every loop, not only for managers. Google’s guide to structured interviewing lists leadership as one of the attributes it assesses and describes the individual-contributor version as “emergent leadership”: taking initiative, working collaboratively across teams and driving your own development. Its candidate tips ask applicants to think about how they have used communication and decision-making skills to mobilise others, “by helping a team succeed even when you weren’t officially the leader”. Amazon’s list of example behavioural questions includes “Share an example of when you took the lead on a project” and “What did you do to motivate a group or encourage collaboration on a team project?”.

Candidates find these questions hard for two opposite reasons. Some have never had a leadership title and conclude they have no leadership stories, when in fact they have led projects, set standards and helped colleagues. Others tell leadership stories that are really stories about being right and pushing until others gave in, which interviewers score as stubbornness rather than influence.

This lesson explains what influence without authority actually consists of: mapping the people involved and their incentives, building a case in their terms, lowering the cost of saying yes, and earning the credibility that makes people listen. It covers leading a project when you are not anyone’s manager, mentoring stories, the specific influence problems AI engineers face between research, product and engineering, and how scope changes with level. The worked example answers “Tell me about a time you got another team to adopt something you built”, weakly and strongly, sentence by sentence.

A good model is the maintainer of an open-source library. A maintainer cannot order anyone to use their library, contribute to it or follow its conventions. Yet good maintainers shape what thousands of people do. They do it by solving a real problem people have, making adoption easy (clear documentation, sensible defaults, migration guides), being reliable (releases that do not break things, responses to issues), listening to users and changing the library when users are right, and giving visible credit to contributors. Influence without authority inside a company works the same way. You are the maintainer of an idea, a tool or a standard, and the other teams are your users.

The analogy breaks in one place: inside a company, there usually is someone with authority over the decision, even if it is not you. A product manager decides priorities, a research lead decides release criteria, a director can settle a disagreement between teams. So influence at work has an extra lever the open-source maintainer lacks: making sure the right decision-maker sees a fair, clear case at the right time.

Seen this way, influence is less a personality trait than a set of habits anyone can practise.

The levers of influence, roughly in the order you build them, are shown below.

The order matters. Credibility is built before you need it, through delivering reliably and being straight with people. Understanding the other team’s incentives comes before you design your proposal, because a proposal that ignores what they are measured on will be politely ignored. Evidence persuades when it speaks to their concerns, not yours. A written case lets people consider it on their own time and forward it to whoever decides. Lowering the cost of saying yes (doing the hard first part yourself, offering a small pilot) turns “maybe later” into “sure, let’s try”. And allies and shared credit let the change spread beyond the people you personally persuade.

It is worth noticing what this model leaves out. It has no place for seniority, volume or persistence. Seniority helps, because it brings credibility, but people with titles fail to influence all the time when they ignore the other levers. Volume and persistence (repeating your case louder and more often) mostly produce resistance: the other side starts defending their position rather than considering yours. Persistence has a role only in the gentler sense of patience: keeping a good idea alive until the evidence or the timing makes it easy to accept. Candidates whose stories rely on persistence alone often describe themselves, without meaning to, as someone colleagues learned to avoid.

Interviewers listen for these moves. A story in which you understood the other team’s concerns, offered a pilot, did the integration work yourself and credited their champion is unmistakably a leadership story, whatever your title was.

1. The questions and what they test

Leadership questions take several forms, and it helps to recognise them:

Question formWhat it tests

Tell me about influencing someone without authority.Persuasion through evidence, incentives and trust, across teams.

The behaviours interviewers score are fairly consistent across them: initiative (you started something), clarity (people knew what to do), respect for others’ expertise and decisions, effective persuasion, enabling others rather than doing everything yourself, resilience when people resisted, and a result that came about through other people’s actions. Google’s manager research, Project Oxygen, is a useful reference even for individual contributors: among the ten behaviours it found in effective managers are being a good coach, empowering the team without micromanaging, communicating effectively, having a clear vision and strategy, and collaborating across the company. Most of these are things an individual contributor can show too.

2. Map the people before you make the case

Influence starts with understanding who is involved. For any change you want, there are typically four kinds of people: those who decide (the product manager who owns the roadmap, the research lead who owns release criteria), those who are affected (the teams who would have to change their workflow), those who can block (security, legal, a platform team whose approval is needed), and potential allies (people who already feel the problem). A simple stakeholder map places each person by how much they can influence the outcome and how much it affects them.

For the people who matter most, find out what they are measured on and worried about. A research lead may be measured on model quality metrics and publication; a PM on launches and user metrics; a platform team on reliability and cost. Your proposal will land much better if it helps their measures, or at least does not hurt them. “This evaluation gate will slow your releases” is a threat to a research team’s incentives; “this evaluation gate catches the regressions that caused the last two rollbacks, which cost your team three weeks” is help.

3. Build the case in their terms, and write it down

A persuasive case usually has the same parts: the problem, with evidence in the other side’s terms; the proposal; the alternatives you considered and why you prefer this one; the costs and risks, stated honestly; and a specific, small ask. Writing it down, as a short design document or one-page proposal, has big advantages over arguing in meetings. People can read it when they have time, think rather than react, forward it to the person who decides, and respond to specifics. It also forces you to state the case precisely, which often improves it.

Evidence persuades best when it is concrete and close to the reader’s experience. For an AI team, that might be: five real cases where the current evaluation passed a model that then failed in production, each with what it cost; a chart of rollback frequency; a quote from a support lead about a bad week. Abstract arguments (“best practice says we should evaluate on production data”) persuade far less than “here are the three regressions in the last quarter that this would have caught”.

Address objections before they are raised. If you know the research team worries about release delays, state the expected added time and how you will keep it small. Showing you have understood the other side’s concerns is itself persuasive; it tells them you are proposing something workable, not something that serves only you.

4. Lower the cost of saying yes

Many good proposals fail not because people disagree but because saying yes would cost them time they do not have. The most effective influence move is often to absorb that cost yourself:

  • Do the hard first part: build the integration, write the migration, set up the first evaluation run for them.
  • Offer a pilot: one team, one model, one month, with a clear way out. A pilot turns a big decision into a small, reversible one (a two-way door, in beh6’s terms).
  • Start with the willing: find the team that already feels the problem most, make them successful, and let their success persuade the next team. Peers persuade peers better than you can.
  • Make the default easy: a template, a one-line configuration, a shared library, so that doing the right thing is the path of least resistance.

In interview stories these moves are very recognisable, and they distinguish influence from persuasion. “I convinced them” tells the interviewer little. “I wrote the integration for their pipeline myself so that adopting it was a one-line change, and offered to run it in shadow mode for two weeks before they relied on it” tells them you know how change actually happens.

5. Credibility: built before you need it

People listen to those they trust, and trust at work comes from a few sources: expertise (you clearly know the subject), a record of delivering (you have done what you said before), dependability (you do what you say, on time), straightness (you say what you know and what you do not, and you report problems early), and generosity (you help others and give credit). Google’s Project Aristotle found dependability to be one of the five dynamics of effective teams, alongside psychological safety, structure and clarity, meaning and impact.

The key point for stories is that credibility is usually built before the influence moment. A strong influence story often contains a sentence about it: “I’d spent my first two months fixing the flaky parts of their data pipeline, so when I proposed the new evaluation gate they already trusted that I wasn’t going to slow them down.” That sentence shows the interviewer you understand influence as a long game.

6. Leading a project without being a manager

Many engineers lead projects as a tech lead or project owner without managing anyone. Leading in that role is mostly about making four things clear and keeping them clear:

  • Goals: what the project is for and how success will be measured (beh6’s problem statement).
  • Roles: who owns which part, and who decides what.
  • Decisions: making sure decisions are made by the right people at the right time, and recorded, often in a simple decision log, so settled questions stay settled.
  • Progress and risk: a regular, honest view of where things stand, with problems raised early.

Then the lead’s main job is to unblock: chasing dependencies, resolving disagreements (beh4), getting decisions from people outside the team, and protecting the team from distractions. A common weak project-leadership story shows the lead doing most of the technical work personally. A strong one shows the lead making it possible for everyone else to do their best work, and still contributing technically where it mattered most.

7. Developing others

Mentoring and developing colleagues is leadership, and for senior individual contributors it is usually an explicit expectation. Being a good coach is the first of Project Oxygen’s ten manager behaviours, and Amazon’s Hire and Develop the Best principle says “Leaders develop leaders and take seriously their role in coaching others.”

A strong mentoring story is specific about three things. The starting point: what the person struggled with, described respectfully (“she was strong technically but tended to take on vague tasks without clarifying them, and lost days as a result”). The actions: what you did, ideally things that built their judgement rather than solving problems for them, such as asking questions instead of giving answers, giving a stretch assignment with support, specific feedback after each attempt, and stepping back as they improved. And the change: something visible in their work, such as leading a project on their own, a promotion, or a manager’s comment. Avoid mentoring stories that make the other person sound incompetent or that centre on how much you knew.

8. Influence in AI work: research, product and engineering

AI engineers often sit between groups with different cultures and incentives, which makes influence both harder and more valuable. Researchers tend to value novelty and benchmark results; product teams value user outcomes and launch dates; platform and infrastructure teams value reliability and cost; security, privacy and legal teams value risk reduction. Common influence situations include persuading researchers to evaluate on production-like data, persuading product to invest in evaluation and monitoring rather than features, persuading platform teams to support a new serving pattern, and persuading leadership to accept a slower but safer launch.

The same levers apply in each case, but translation is the special skill: expressing an engineering concern in the other group’s currency. To a researcher, a production regression is a threat to the credibility of their result. To a product manager, missing evaluation is a risk to the launch date and to user trust. To leadership, a safety gap is a reputational and legal risk with a probability and a cost. Candidates who show they can translate like this demonstrate exactly the cross-functional leadership AI teams struggle to find.

9. Motivating a group

Amazon’s example question “What did you do to motivate a group or encourage collaboration on a team project?” asks about a different kind of leadership: not changing a decision, but changing energy and focus. Typical situations are a team demoralised after a failed launch, a long project in its tedious middle, a group from several teams that does not yet feel like a team, or a crunch before a deadline.

The behaviours that work in such stories are concrete, not inspirational speeches. Connecting the work to its purpose: showing the team real user conversations the assistant had improved, or the support lead’s message about time saved. Meaning and impact are two of the five team dynamics in Google’s Project Aristotle, and engineers often never see the effect of their work. Making progress visible: a weekly evaluation chart that shows the error rate falling, so a long slog has visible milestones. Clearing friction: removing the flaky test, the slow review cycle or the unclear ownership that is quietly draining the team. Recognising contributions specifically: naming what someone did and why it mattered, in front of people whose opinion they value. Protecting the team: pushing back on a new request so the team can finish what matters.

A strong motivation story names the starting state with evidence (“two people had asked to move teams; velocity had halved”), describes two or three of these specific actions, and gives a change you could observe: delivery recovered, people stayed, the team asked to keep a practice you introduced. Avoid stories that amount to “I stayed positive”; positivity is not an action anyone can score.

10. Leading in an incident

Incidents create some of the clearest leadership moments, because roles are unclear, pressure is high and someone has to organise people quickly. In many engineering organisations an incident has a designated coordinator, sometimes called the incident commander, whose job is not to fix the problem personally but to make sure it gets fixed: assigning who investigates what, keeping a running record, deciding when to roll back, and communicating with stakeholders. Google’s SRE book describes this kind of role-based incident management in its chapter on managing incidents.

An AI engineer might lead in an incident such as a model producing harmful or wrong outputs at scale, a provider outage, or a cost spike. A strong story shows the candidate creating order: separating investigation from communication, making a fast reversible decision (roll back first, investigate second), keeping stakeholders informed at regular intervals, and running the follow-up postmortem (beh5). It is a leadership story precisely because the candidate’s main contribution was coordination, which interviewers recognise as a skill many strong individual engineers lack.

11. Worked example: getting another team to adopt something

The question, from an interviewer assessing influence and leadership: “Tell me about a time you got another team to adopt something you built.” Both answers are illustrative examples written for this lesson; the people, company and numbers are invented.

What Project Oxygen implies for individual contributors

Google’s Project Oxygen analysed more than 10,000 data points from performance reviews, feedback surveys and interviews, and found that teams with highly effective managers were more likely to achieve better results, be happier and have lower turnover. Its ten behaviours are written for managers, but several are the core of individual-contributor leadership too: being a good coach, empowering others without micromanaging, communicating effectively, having a clear vision and strategy, having key technical skills, collaborating across the company and making strong decisions. When you prepare leadership stories, it is worth checking which of these each story shows. A senior engineer whose stories show coaching, a clear technical vision and cross-company collaboration is showing the same behaviours Google found in its best managers, which is exactly the signal a senior or staff interviewer wants.

Influence versus manipulation

Interviewers occasionally probe the ethics of influence, and it is worth having a clear view. Legitimate influence changes someone’s decision by improving their information, aligning the proposal with their real interests, and making action easier, all openly; if they later learned exactly how you persuaded them, they would not feel tricked. Manipulation works through hidden information, misrepresented evidence, manufactured urgency or exploiting someone’s position. Besides being wrong, manipulation is ineffective over time, because influence depends on credibility and manipulation spends it. A useful test when telling a story: would the person you influenced be comfortable hearing your account of it? If yes, it was influence.

When influence fails

Not every attempt succeeds, and stories of failed influence can be strong evidence if told well. Influence fails for recognisable reasons: the proposal did not serve the other side’s goals; the evidence was in your terms, not theirs; the ask was too big; the timing was wrong (they were mid-crisis); you went to the decision-maker too late, or too early, before the affected team was on board; or the other side was simply right. A mature failed-influence story identifies which reason applied, shows that you accepted the decision and committed (beh4), and names what you do differently now. Sometimes the best ending is that you were persuaded instead, which shows the openness that makes people willing to listen to you next time.

Writing scales influence

Spoken persuasion reaches the people in the room; writing reaches everyone the document is forwarded to, including the people who decide. Several large companies formalise this. Amazon’s candidate guidance explains that it uses narrative memos instead of slide decks, read silently at the start of meetings, and that these are “a big part of how we share ideas and make decisions”. Engineering organisations widely use design documents for the same purpose. For senior and staff candidates especially, a story that includes a well-received written proposal shows a scalable form of influence. It also gives you concrete detail for follow-ups: what the document argued, who reviewed it, what changed after review.

Influencing upward

Influencing your own manager or leadership is a special case. You have a relationship and regular access, but they hold authority over you, and disagreeing with them carries more perceived risk. The same levers work, with two adjustments. First, framing in their terms means framing in terms of what they are accountable for: their team’s goals, their commitments to their own manager, the risks they will be asked about. Second, timing and channel matter more: raise ideas in a one-to-one before a group setting, bring a written proposal they can forward, and give them options rather than a single demand, so the decision remains theirs. A story about successfully changing a manager’s plan, told with respect for their position and with the outcome, is strong evidence for senior roles, because it shows you will improve decisions above you rather than just execute them.

Leading through others’ credit

One of the clearest markers of senior leadership is comfort with other people getting the visible credit. In the worked example, the candidate let the other team’s tech lead present the pilot results. That choice made the change more likely to succeed (peers trust peers), made the other lead an owner rather than a recipient, and built a relationship for future work. Interviewers notice this, but only if you tell them, which creates a small paradox: you need to describe, briefly and factually, how you deliberately shared credit, without making the story about your generosity. “Their lead presented the results, which helped it spread” does it in a sentence.

Staff-level leadership: choosing the problem

At staff level and above, the hardest leadership work is choosing what an organisation should work on, not executing a chosen project. Stories at this level show a candidate noticing a pattern across teams (every team building its own evaluation tooling, inconsistent safety reviews, duplicated retrieval infrastructure), making the case for a shared direction, aligning several leaders with different incentives, and then letting others lead the pieces. The evidence is about reach and durability: how many teams changed course, and whether the direction outlived the candidate’s direct involvement. If you are interviewing at this level, make sure at least two of your stories show this shape, and make the scope explicit.

How leadership answers go wrong

Leadership as title. “I haven’t been a manager, so I don’t have a leadership story.” Fix: look for times you led a project, set a standard others adopted, unblocked a cross-team problem or helped a colleague grow.

“I told them.” Influence described as instruction or broadcasting: presentations, emails, insisting. Fix: describe how you understood their needs, built evidence in their terms and lowered their cost.

Authority as a substitute. The story resolves when a manager mandates the change. That may be appropriate, but it is not influence. Fix: if escalation was needed, show what you did before and after it, and why it was the right call (beh4).

The solo hero. A project-leadership story where the lead did all the work. Fix: show how you organised others, and what they achieved.

No resistance. Everyone agreed immediately. Fix: choose a story with real resistance, or describe the indifference you had to overcome.

Credit hoarding. “I drove adoption across the organisation” with no mention of anyone else. Fix: name the roles of the people who helped, and the champion in each team.

Finding leadership stories you already have

Many engineers have more leadership stories than they think. Look for: a time you noticed a problem across teams and organised a fix; a design document you wrote that changed a plan; a standard, template or tool others adopted; an onboarding guide or training you created; a new team member you helped get productive; a project where you coordinated people from several teams; an incident you coordinated; a time you convinced someone senior to change course. Each can be a leadership story if you tell your part in it: the initiative, the organising, the persuasion and the result through others.

Calibrating scope to level

Interviewers read level from the reach of your leadership. For mid-level roles, leading a project within your team or mentoring a new hire is solid evidence. For senior roles, they look for influence beyond your team: standards other teams adopted, cross-team projects, technical direction for your team. For staff roles, they look for direction across several teams, alignment of senior stakeholders and leaders you developed. If your target level is senior or above, make sure your leadership stories cross team boundaries and make the reach explicit with numbers: teams, people, releases, incidents.

Leadership questions in AI-specific loops

AI teams often add leadership questions shaped by their own pain points: getting research results into production, establishing evaluation and safety practices, and coordinating between model providers, platform teams and product teams. Expect questions such as “How did you get a research prototype into production?” or “How did you establish an evaluation process the team actually follows?”. Both are influence questions in disguise: researchers and product teams rarely report to the engineer making the change. Having one story ready for each is worth the preparation time.

The follow-ups to prepare

Leadership stories attract follow-ups about the people side: Who resisted, and why? What did they say? What would you have done if they had said no? How did you decide who to involve first? Who else deserves credit? What happened after you moved on; did it last? How did the people you led feel about it? Prepare honest, specific answers for each leadership story. The last two are especially telling: a change that lasted after you left and a team that would work with you again are the best evidence of real leadership.

Goal: prepare one influence story and check it uses the real levers of influence. Time: about 40 minutes.

  1. Find candidates. List three times you got people who did not report to you to do something differently: adopt a tool, change a process, reprioritise, accept a design. Pick the one with the most genuine resistance.
  2. Draw the stakeholder map. Place each person involved by influence and by how much the change affected them. Mark who decided, who could block and who were allies.
  3. Write their position. For the main resistant party, write in two sentences what they were measured on and why the change was unattractive to them at first.
  4. Check the levers. For each of the six levers (credibility, their incentives, evidence in their terms, a written case, lowering the cost of yes, allies and credit), write what you did, or “nothing”. A strong story has at least three.
  5. Find the number. Write the adoption result and its effect: teams, releases, incidents, time.
  6. Draft and record a STAR answer of about two and a half minutes. Check that no sentence says “I convinced” or “I told” without saying how.

Finally, ask yourself whether the person you influenced would recognise your account of it. If not, revise until they would.

What to look for: if your levers column shows only “evidence” and “a presentation”, your story is persuasion, not influence. Look for what you did to lower the other side’s cost or to make the win theirs; if you did nothing like that, consider a different story.

Plan the influence

You are an AI engineer on a product team. You believe the company should route all LLM calls through a shared gateway that logs prompts and responses (with personal data redacted), enforces cost limits and allows model switching. Four product teams call model providers directly from their own code, each in a different way. You have no authority over them, and your manager supports the idea but will not mandate it. Plan how you would get the teams to adopt the gateway, and say how you would tell it later as an interview story.

Answer

Map the people: the four teams’ tech leads (affected, must change code), their PMs (care about launch speed), security and privacy (would strongly want logging and redaction: likely allies or even sponsors), finance or whoever watches LLM spend (ally), and a platform team that might eventually own the gateway (needed for long-term support). Learn each team’s incentives and blockers: likely fear of added latency, fear of a dependency on an unproven service, and migration effort.

Build credibility and evidence: measure the current state (spend per team, incidents caused by provider outages or unexpected model changes, absence of logs in any recent incident investigation). Write a short design document with options (do nothing, shared library, gateway), the trade-offs (added latency, a new single point of failure and how you would mitigate it), and a small ask. Get security or privacy to review it; their support carries weight.

Lower the cost of yes: build the gateway to be a drop-in replacement for the provider client library, keep the added latency to a few milliseconds and measure it, offer to make the migration change for the first team yourself, and run in pass-through mode first. Start with the team that suffered most from a recent incident. Let their tech lead share the result. Then agree with the platform team who will own it long-term, so other teams are not depending on one person.

As an interview story: open with the problem and the lack of authority; spend the action time on understanding each team’s concerns, the written case with security’s support, the drop-in design and first migration, and the champion-led spread; give a result (teams migrated, spend visibility, an incident resolved faster because logs existed, cost saved by limits); credit the first team’s lead and the platform team; and end with a lesson such as “find the ally whose goals your proposal serves most, and do the first migration yourself.”
