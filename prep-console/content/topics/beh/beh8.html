Two candidates have performed equally well in a loop for an AI engineer role at a health-technology company. Near the end of the hiring manager’s interview, each is asked, “So why do you want to work here?” The first says: “I’ve always admired the company, you’re a leader in AI, and I love your mission of improving healthcare. I think it would be a great place to grow.” The second says: “I read your engineering blog post about evaluating clinical summaries with physician reviewers, and that’s the problem I’ve been working on from the other side: for the last year I’ve built evaluation for a support assistant, where errors are annoying but not dangerous. I want to work where evaluation has to be that rigorous, and I think the release-gating approach I built would carry over.” The hiring manager will remember the second candidate. (Both candidates, and the company, are illustrative examples.)

“Why this company?” and “why this role?” are among the most predictable questions in any loop, and among the most often wasted. Candidates prepare their technical stories carefully and then improvise motivation, which produces generic praise that could be said to any employer. Interviewers ask the question for real reasons. They want to know whether you will stay and care: people who chose a role for specific reasons tend to be more engaged than people who applied everywhere. They want to see whether you did your homework, because research before a decision is a habit they want on the job. And they want to check whether what you want matches what the role actually offers, since a mismatch means an unhappy hire.

The end of the interview has a second, related moment: “Do you have any questions for me?” This is not politeness. Google’s guidance to candidates puts it directly: “Interviewing is a two-way street, so come prepared with questions… This is your opportunity to interview us as well. Both sides need to be a fit!” Your questions are evidence about how you think, and the answers are your best evidence about whether you want the job. Many candidates ask nothing, or ask something answered on the careers page, and leave knowing no more than when they arrived.

This lesson covers both moments. It explains what interviewers listen for, how to research a company using official sources only (and why that matters), how to build a 45–60 second “why us” from three ingredients, and the related questions about why you are leaving and why AI. It then turns to your questions: what to ask each kind of interviewer, AI-specific questions that reveal how a team really works, what not to ask, and how to use the answers to decide. The worked example is a weak and a strong answer to “Why do you want to work here?”, annotated sentence by sentence.

Think of a good “why us” answer as the intersection of three circles. The first circle is your trajectory: what you have done and what you want to do next. The second is the role’s actual work: what this team builds, the problems it faces, what the job description says the person will do. The third is specific, verified facts you learned about the company and team: a blog post, a paper, a product feature, something the recruiter told you. A strong answer sits where all three overlap.

Each circle on its own produces a recognisable weak answer. Only your trajectory: “I want to grow and learn new things” (true of every job). Only the role: a recitation of the job description back to the interviewer. Only facts about the company: “You were founded in 2012, you have 3,000 employees and you recently launched an AI product”, which shows research but not motivation. Two circles are better; all three make an answer that could only be said by you, to this company, for this role.

The analogy also tells you what preparation looks like. You already have the first circle from your story bank and intro. The second comes from reading the job description closely and asking the recruiter what the team actually does. The third, verified specifics, is the one most candidates skip, and it is the one that takes real effort: reading what the company has published about its work and using the product.

Why does the third circle matter so much? Because it is the only one an interviewer can check, and the only one that costs effort. Anyone can claim to be motivated and anyone can read the job title; only someone who actually spent time on the company’s published work can say something specific and true about it. Interviewers treat that effort as a proxy for how you will approach decisions on the job: an engineer who researched a team before asking to join it is likely to research a problem before proposing a solution. This is also why a single precise fact beats a list of vague ones. “Your post on physician review of summaries” is worth more than “you do a lot of interesting things in healthcare AI”, because it proves you read something, and invites a real conversation about it.

There is a second, quieter benefit. Doing the research changes your own view. Sometimes it makes you more excited about the role; sometimes it reveals that the work is not what you want. Either outcome is valuable before you spend a day in interviews.

The questions you ask at the end work the same way. A question that sits in the same overlap (“Your blog described physician review for clinical summaries; how does that scale as the number of summary types grows?”) shows research, technical thinking and genuine interest all at once. It also gets you information you need, which is the other half of the point.

1. What interviewers are judging

Motivation questions take several forms: why this company, why this role, why now, why are you leaving, why AI, what are you looking for in your next role. Interviewers use them to judge four things.

  • Motivation fit: whether what drives you matches what the role offers. Someone who wants to do research will be unhappy in a role that is mostly integration work, however enthusiastic they sound.
  • Likelihood of staying and caring: a specific reason to choose this role predicts engagement better than general enthusiasm.
  • Research habits: did you find out about the work before asking to join it? Preparing well for a decision is a habit teams want.
  • Honesty: interviewers have heard every flattering formula; an answer that sounds true is worth more than one that sounds polished.

Amazon’s candidate guide describes “Why Amazon?” as a common question whose answer “helps us get a better sense of who you are and what motivates you”, and suggests thinking about “what excites you about the role and the team (or teams) you’ll be meeting with”. Note the emphasis on the role and the team, not the company in general.

2. Researching the company: official sources only

The third circle needs facts, and the quality of the facts matters. Use sources the company itself has published or that come from the people doing the work:

SourceWhat it gives you
Job descriptionThe responsibilities and requirements, often with hints about current problems (“help us scale our evaluation infrastructure”).
Engineering blog and conference talksHow the team builds things, what they are proud of, what problems they have written about.
Research papers, model cards, technical reportsFor AI companies: what models they build, how they evaluate them, the limitations they acknowledge.
Product documentation and the product itselfWhat the product actually does, and where it is strong or weak. Use it before the interview.
Responsible-AI, safety or privacy pagesThe practices and commitments the company states publicly.
Annual reports, shareholder letters, official announcementsCompany direction and priorities, for public companies.
The recruiterWhat the team works on, how the loop is structured, what the hiring manager cares about. Ask directly.
Current or former employeesDay-to-day reality, if you can reach them; treat as one person’s view.

Two rules keep this honest. First, only state as fact what you have verified, and say where you learned it: “I read in your engineering blog that…” is safe; “I heard you’re rebuilding your whole platform” is a rumour, and repeating it can embarrass you or the interviewer. Second, do not over-claim: reading one blog post does not make you an expert on the team, and interviewers can tell when a candidate is stretching a single fact. It is fine to say what you do not know and ask about it; that is often the best transition into a good question.

3. Building the answer: three parts in under a minute

A strong “why us” answer usually has three parts and takes 45–60 seconds.

  1. The specific thing (about 15 seconds): what about this team’s work draws you, named concretely. “Your team owns evaluation and release for every model the company ships” or “the post your team wrote about grounding answers in contracts”.
  2. Why it matters to you (about 20 seconds): the connection to your trajectory, ideally to a story in your bank. “I built evaluation for one product and found it was the part that decided whether we could ship; I want to work on it at a larger scale.”
  3. What you would bring (about 15 seconds): the contribution, not a list of skills. “The approach I used, gating releases on production-sampled evaluation sets, would apply directly to the multi-product setting.”

Then stop. The answer is short because it does not need to prove everything; it needs to sound specific and true, and to invite a follow-up conversation about the team’s work, which is usually where the interviewer goes next.

Notice what is not in the structure: prestige (“it’s a top company”), perks, compensation, and flattery (“you’re the leader in AI”). Those may be real considerations for you, and that is fine, but as the main answer they say nothing about fit. Mission statements are a special case: quoting the company’s mission back to it is flattery unless you connect it to something concrete you have done or seen.

4. The related motivation questions

Why this role (versus another role at the company)? Focus on the work itself: what the role does day to day, and why that suits your trajectory. If you are applying for an AI engineer role rather than a research scientist role, for example, be able to say why building and shipping is what you want.

Why are you leaving your current job? beh3 covers this: one or two honest, forward-looking sentences about what you are moving toward, never criticism of your employer. It should connect naturally to your “why us”.

Why AI engineering? The bank question on this theme gives the shape: “a concrete arc: a problem you hit, what you built, what it taught you, and what you want to do next. Specific beats enthusiastic.” Interest in AI is near-universal among candidates, so the only thing that distinguishes you is the specific path that led you here.

What are you looking for in your next role? Answer honestly with two or three specific things (the kind of problem, the kind of team, the kind of growth), and make sure they are things this role offers. If they are not, that is worth knowing before you accept.

5. Questions to ask: what they are for

Your questions do two jobs at once. They are evidence: a thoughtful, specific question shows research, technical judgement and real interest; Google’s guidance says having questions prepared “shows you care about understanding Google and the position… and is a testament to the research you’ve done”. And they are information: the answers are the best data you will get about whether you want to work there. Of the two, the second matters more in the long run. A job that looked good from outside and turns out to be constant firefighting under an unclear manager is a costly mistake, and the interview loop is your chance to find out.

Amazon’s guide notes that interviewers try to leave a few minutes at the end for questions, and that if time runs out you can follow up with your recruiting contact. In practice you usually have five minutes or less. Prepare three to five questions for each interviewer so you can choose the best one or two for the time available, and do not use the time on things you could have read online.

6. What to ask each kind of interviewer

Different people can answer different questions well. Tailor your questions to the interviewer’s role.

InterviewerGood questions
RecruiterHow is the loop structured and what does each round assess? What level is this role, and how is level decided? Is there team matching after the loop? What is the timeline?
Hiring managerWhat does success look like at six months and a year? What is the biggest challenge the team faces right now? How are priorities set and decisions made? How do people on the team grow?
Peer engineerWhat does a typical week look like? How do code review, deployment and on-call work? What do you wish you had known when you joined? What would you change?
Researcher or PMHow do research results get into production? How do engineering, research and product disagree, and how are disagreements settled? How is success of an AI feature measured?
Skip-level or leaderWhere is the team’s work heading in the next year or two? How does this team’s work connect to the company’s priorities? What would make you consider this hire a great success?

7. AI-specific questions that reveal how a team works

For AI engineering roles, a handful of questions are unusually revealing because the answers expose whether the team has mature practices or is improvising.

  • “How do you decide a model or prompt change is ready to ship?” A good answer mentions an evaluation set, ideally drawn from real usage, and some form of staged rollout. “We try it and see” tells you a lot.
  • “What happens when a model provider updates a model you depend on?” Reveals whether they pin versions, re-evaluate, and monitor.
  • “How do you know an AI feature is working for users after launch?” Reveals monitoring and the metrics they trust.
  • “How does work move from research or prototype to production here?” Reveals how the groups collaborate, and where friction is.
  • “What was the last significant incident with an AI feature, and what changed afterwards?” Reveals whether they learn from failure (beh5).
  • “What is hardest about this work right now?” An honest, specific answer is a good sign; a vague one is a mild warning.

These questions also showcase your own knowledge without boasting: asking how a team handles provider model updates tells the interviewer you know that is a real problem.

8. What not to ask, and how to ask well

Avoid questions answered on the company’s website or in the job description (they show no research), “How did I do?” or “Did I pass?” (interviewers usually cannot say, and it puts them in an awkward spot), detailed compensation questions with interviewers (the recruiter handles those; beh10 covers when), and questions framed as accusations (“Why is your app so slow?”). Avoid long lists: two good questions with real follow-up beat five in rapid succession.

How you ask matters as much as what. Ask open questions (“how”, “what”, “tell me about”), not yes/no. Listen and follow up: “You mentioned evaluation is mostly manual right now; is automating it part of this role?” turns a scripted question into a conversation and shows you were listening. Take brief notes, because you will want to compare answers later. And watch the clock: if the interviewer has given you five minutes, do not take fifteen.

9. Using the answers to decide

Keep notes on what each interviewer said and compare them after the loop. Useful signals include: whether success in the role is defined clearly and consistently; whether the team has evaluation, monitoring and on-call practices or lives in constant firefighting; whether decisions are made transparently and by clear owners; whether people describe the same priorities; and whether people seem to enjoy working together. Google’s team effectiveness research is a handy checklist here: psychological safety, dependability, structure and clarity, meaning and impact are all things you can ask about.

Some answers are red flags: nobody can say what success looks like; three interviewers give three different top priorities; every story involves an emergency; questions about evaluation get a shrug; the hiring manager speaks badly of other teams. None is decisive alone, but together they predict your experience. If you get an offer, you can usually ask follow-up questions of the hiring manager or recruiter before deciding, and you should.

10. “What other companies are you talking to?”

Recruiters, and occasionally interviewers, ask whether you are interviewing elsewhere. The question is legitimate: they want to understand your timeline and how competitive the process is. You are not obliged to name companies, and naming them rarely helps you at this stage. A good answer is honest and general: “Yes, I’m in process with a few companies, mostly for AI engineering roles focused on evaluation and production LLM systems, and I expect to be making decisions in the next three or four weeks.” That tells the recruiter what they need (you are actively looking, what you are looking for, and your timeline) and connects your search to your stated motivation.

Two things to avoid. Do not pretend you are not looking elsewhere if you are; if an offer timeline later forces you to say so, the earlier answer looks dishonest. And do not inflate your other processes to create pressure; recruiters talk to many candidates and can usually tell. If you have a real deadline from another company, telling the recruiter early is in your interest, because many companies can speed up their process when they know (beh10 covers this in the offer stage).

11. Worked example: “Why do you want to work here?”

The candidate and company below are illustrative examples written for this lesson. The company, “Northwind Health”, is fictional; imagine it publishes an engineering blog describing how physicians review AI-generated clinical note summaries before release. The role is an AI engineer on its clinical summarisation team.

The strong answer is barely longer than the weak one. It names something real and checkable, connects it to the candidate’s own record, says what they would contribute, and raises a natural question (how physician review scales) that the hiring manager will probably want to talk about. That last move matters: a good “why us” answer often turns into the most engaged conversation of the interview.

A good question for the end of the same interview might be: “When a summary type passes physician review, how do you monitor it after release, given that the notes it sees keep changing?” It sits in the same overlap of research, technical judgement and interest, and the answer tells the candidate whether the team’s rigour continues after launch.

Motivation fit and why companies care

Companies care about motivation for practical reasons. Hiring is expensive, the first months in a role are mostly cost, and an engineer who leaves after a year because the work was not what they wanted is a loss for both sides. Specific motivation is a reasonable, if imperfect, predictor that the match is good: if you can say exactly what about the work attracts you, and the role really offers it, you are more likely to stay and do your best work. Generic enthusiasm predicts little, because almost everyone expresses it. This is why interviewers push on vague answers (“what specifically about our team?”): they are trying to find out whether there is a real match underneath.

Honesty when the reasons are mixed

Real motivations are usually mixed: the work is interesting, the pay is better, the location suits you, a friend works there, you need to leave your current job. You do not need to list them all, and you should not pretend the interesting work is the only reason if it is not. The honest approach is to lead with the reasons that are both true and relevant to the role, which for any role worth taking should include something about the work, and to leave out reasons that are true but irrelevant to the interviewer. What you must avoid is inventing reasons, especially fabricated specifics (“I’ve followed your work for years”) that a single follow-up question would expose.

When the company is controversial or in the news

AI companies are often in the news, sometimes for difficult reasons: safety debates, legal cases, reorganisations, layoffs. Do not raise news stories you have not verified, and do not offer opinions on controversies unless asked. If an interviewer raises a public topic, or you genuinely need to understand something to decide (for example, how a reorganisation affects the team), ask a calm, open question in the right setting, usually with the hiring manager or recruiter: “I saw the announcement about the reorganisation; how does it affect this team’s plans?” This gets you real information without putting the interviewer on the defensive.

Why your questions are evaluated

Interviewers form impressions from your questions even when they are not formally scored. Specific questions grounded in research signal preparation. Questions about how decisions are made, how quality is measured and what is hard signal the kind of engineer who thinks about the system around the code. Questions only about perks, remote work or promotion speed, asked first, can signal priorities that worry an interviewer, even though they are legitimate concerns; ask them of the recruiter, or later. And the way you listen to answers (following up, connecting to what you heard earlier) is evidence of the collaboration skills the whole loop is trying to measure.

Startups and large companies call for different questions

The same question can matter very differently depending on the company’s size and stage. At a large company, the biggest unknowns are usually about the specific team: who the manager is, what the team owns, how it fits into the larger organisation, and how much of your time will go to process. At a startup, the unknowns are about the company itself: how long its funding lasts, how it makes or plans to make money, whether the product has real users, and how decisions are made when the founders disagree. Questions such as “How do you think about runway and the next funding milestone?” or “What would have to be true in a year for this to be going well?” are normal and appropriate at a startup, usually with a founder or the hiring manager. Asking them calmly signals the maturity that startups need, and the answers can be decisive for your choice.

Judging the manager

Of everything you can learn in an interview loop, the most predictive for your day-to-day experience is probably who your manager will be and how they work. Google’s manager research found that teams with highly effective managers achieved better results, were happier and had lower turnover. You usually meet the hiring manager once, so use the time. Questions that reveal management style include: “How do you give feedback to people on your team?”, “Tell me about someone on your team who grew a lot; what did you do to help?”, “How do you decide what the team works on when there is more to do than people?”, and “What would you want from me in the first month?”. Specific, example-based answers are a good sign; vague answers, or answers that are all about the manager’s own achievements, are worth noting.

Team matching and choosing between teams

At some large companies, candidates pass a general loop and then talk to several teams in a team matching stage before an offer is finalised. These conversations are interviews in both directions: the team is checking fit, and you are choosing where you will spend the next few years. Everything in this lesson applies with more weight. A specific “why this team” answer helps you get matched where you want, and the questions you ask (about the manager’s style, the team’s practices, the work you would own) are your main source of information for the choice. Ask the recruiter in advance how matching works and how many teams you can talk to.

How these answers go wrong

The flattery answer. “You’re the best company in AI.” Says nothing about you. Fix: name the specific work and connect it to your trajectory.

The mission quote. Reciting the mission statement. Fix: if the mission genuinely matters to you, connect it to something concrete you have done or seen.

The prestige and perks answer. “It would be great for my career”, “the benefits are amazing”. Fix: keep those private; lead with the work.

The rumour. Repeating forum gossip or guesses about internal plans. Fix: use only official sources, and say where you learned it.

The over-claim. “I know your stack inside out” after reading one post. Fix: say what you read and ask about what you do not know.

No questions. “No, I think you’ve covered everything.” Fix: always have at least two prepared per interviewer.

The website question. “What does your company do?” Fix: research first; ask about what research could not tell you.

The interrogation. Five rapid questions with no follow-up. Fix: fewer questions, listen, follow up.

A preparation routine

For each company, budget about two hours. Read the job description twice and underline the responsibilities and any hints about current problems. Read two or three recent engineering blog posts or talks from the relevant team or area, and for AI companies their model documentation or responsible-AI pages. Use the product for twenty minutes. Write a 45–60 second “why us” in the three-part structure, checking every fact against its source. Write three to five questions for each interviewer you know about, at least one of them following from your research. Finally, check that your “why us” is consistent with your intro’s future part (beh3) and your answer to why you are leaving.

Following up after the interview

If you ran out of time for questions, or thought of one later, Amazon’s guidance explicitly suggests following up through your recruiting contact. A short, specific follow-up to the recruiter (“I didn’t get to ask about how the team handles provider model updates; is there someone I could ask, or could you pass it on?”) is perfectly normal. Thank-you notes vary by company and culture; a brief, sincere note to the recruiter is never wrong, but do not expect it to change a decision. What matters more is collecting the answers you need before you have to decide on an offer, which beh10 covers.

Using the questions to protect yourself

It is easy to treat the end of the interview as a formality while you are anxious about passing. Try to remember that you are also deciding. A role at a famous company on a team with no evaluation practice, unclear ownership and a manager who cannot describe success can be a worse career move than a role at a smaller company with a clear mandate and good practices. The questions in this lesson are designed to surface those differences, and asking them calmly and specifically also happens to make you a more impressive candidate.

Goal: prepare a “why us” answer and a question set for one real target company. Time: about 90 minutes, mostly research.

  1. Research (45 min). Read the job description and underline responsibilities and problems. Read two or three recent official posts, talks or documents from the relevant team. Use the product. For each fact you might use, write the source next to it.
  2. Three circles (10 min). Write three bullets in each circle: your trajectory, the role’s work, verified specifics. Draw lines where items connect across circles.
  3. Write the answer (15 min). Three parts: the specific thing (15 s), why it matters to you (20 s), what you would bring (15 s). Remove every adjective about the company.
  4. Questions (15 min). Write three questions each for the recruiter, hiring manager and a peer engineer, including at least two of the AI-specific questions in this lesson and at least one that follows from your research.
  5. Record and time the answer. Aim for 45–60 seconds. Ask yourself: could another candidate say this word for word to this company? If yes, make it more specific.
  6. Consistency check. Read your intro’s future part and your “why are you leaving” answer next to this one. They should tell one story.

Save the research notes with sources; you will reuse them for the offer decision.

What to look for: the most common gap is the verified-specifics circle. If your answer contains no fact with a source, go back to step 1.

Rewrite and question

A candidate is interviewing for an AI engineer role on a team that builds an LLM-powered coding assistant at a developer-tools company. Their prepared answer is: “I use your products every day and I think they’re great. AI is the future of software development and I want to be part of it. Also your company has a great culture and I’d love to work with such talented people.” Rewrite the answer using only things the candidate could plausibly verify (mark any facts they must check), and write three questions for the hiring manager and two for a peer engineer.

Answer

The original has one useful seed (“I use your products every day”) buried under flattery and generic claims. A rewrite: “I use your editor every day, and when the AI completion feature launched I paid attention to where it helped me and where it didn’t: it’s strong on boilerplate and weaker when the answer depends on code in other files. [Check: is cross-file context something the team has written about?] Your team’s post on [the topic, verified] described how you evaluate suggestions on real repositories, which is the part of this work I find most interesting. For the last year I’ve built retrieval for an internal LLM tool over our codebase, so I’ve dealt with exactly the problem of getting the right context into a limited window. I’d like to work on it at your scale, and I think my experience measuring retrieval quality on real queries would carry over.” Every claim is either the candidate’s own experience or marked for verification.

Hiring manager: “What would a great first six months look like in this role?”; “How do you decide a change to the assistant is ready to ship: what evaluation does it have to pass?”; “What is the hardest unsolved problem the team is working on right now?”. Peer engineer: “What does the path from an idea to a shipped change look like day to day, including evaluation and rollout?”; “When a model provider or your own model changes, how do you find out whether suggestions got better or worse for users?”. These questions will give the candidate real information about the team’s practices, and each one shows the kind of thinking the role needs.
