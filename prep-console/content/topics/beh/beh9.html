Most exams come with a syllabus. Behavioural interviews at many large companies do too, and most candidates never read it. Amazon publishes its 16 Leadership Principles and states that its interview conversations are guided by them. Netflix publishes a long culture memo listing the values it expects. Meta publishes six core values. Microsoft publishes a culture page centred on a growth mindset. AI companies publish theirs as well: Anthropic’s careers page lists seven principles. These are not decoration. At companies that use them in hiring, the values are the competencies on the interviewer’s rubric, the columns their notes are sorted into after you leave.

Candidates who ignore this tend to make two mistakes. Some prepare a generic set of stories and are surprised when an Amazon interviewer asks, back to back, for a time they dove deep into data, a time they disagreed and committed, and a time they did more with less, and three of their stories turn out to be about the same thing. Others go too far the other way: they memorise value names and sprinkle them through their answers (“and that really shows my Customer Obsession”), which interviewers find tiresome and which adds no evidence.

There is also a misinformation problem. Interview-preparation websites publish confident lists of what each company “really” looks for, some outdated, some invented. Google’s “Googleyness” is the classic case: the word is widely used, and Google employees use it themselves, but the many detailed checklists online are not Google’s, and Google’s current candidate pages describe its process quite differently. Preparing from unofficial sources can mean preparing for the wrong test.

This lesson reads the official sources for several companies that publish them: Amazon in depth, then Google, Meta, Netflix, Microsoft and Anthropic. For each, it covers what the company says it looks for, how that turns into questions, and which of your stories serve it. It then shows why the frameworks converge on the same few competencies, so one story bank can serve all of them with relabelled columns. The worked example answers a classic Amazon-style question, “Tell me about a time you had to dive deep to find the root cause of a problem”, weakly and strongly, annotated sentence by sentence.

Think of a company’s published values as a rubric written in the company’s dialect. Underneath, most rubrics measure similar things: do you own outcomes, use good judgement, tell the truth, disagree well, learn, care about users and deliver. Each company describes those things in its own words, with its own emphasis and its own priorities. Amazon says “Ownership” and “Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit”; Netflix says “Judgment” and “Candor”; Meta says “Be direct and respect your colleagues”; Microsoft says “growth mindset”. Your job is to be fluent in the dialect without forgetting that the underlying language is evidence.

The analogy breaks in one place: dialects differ in emphasis, not only in words. Amazon’s list includes Frugality, which few others name; Netflix stresses Courage and Selflessness; Meta stresses speed and long-term impact together; Anthropic stresses safety and simplicity. Where a company names something distinctive, assume its interviewers will look for it, and make sure you have a story.

So preparation has three steps. First, read the company’s official values and any official interview guidance, carefully and in full. Second, map them onto your story bank: rename the matrix columns to the company’s values, check which stories fit each, and find stories for any distinctive values you lack. Third, practise telling each story with the emphasis that value wants, in plain language, without announcing the value. The interviewer will do the mapping; your job is to give them evidence they cannot miss.

A useful habit while reading any values page is to look for the verbs. The value’s name is a slogan; its description usually contains specific behaviours, and those behaviours are what an interviewer can hear in a story. Amazon’s Dive Deep, for instance, names four: operating at all levels, staying connected to details, auditing frequently, and being sceptical when metrics and anecdote differ. Meta’s Move fast names acting with urgency and “methodically removing barriers”. Netflix’s Candor names receiving and giving feedback and admitting mistakes openly. Each verb is a candidate rubric line, and each suggests a story: a time you audited something nobody had checked, a time you removed a barrier slowing your team, a time you admitted a mistake publicly. Reading values this way turns a page of corporate prose into a short list of things to prepare evidence for.

One caution before the details. Official pages describe what a company says it values; individual interviewers and teams vary, and no page tells you exactly which questions you will be asked. Use the frameworks to make sure your preparation covers the right ground, not as a script for the day.

The rest of this lesson goes company by company, starting with Amazon, whose framework is the most explicit and most widely copied.

1. Why companies publish values, and how interviews use them

A company that publishes its values gains a shared language for decisions and, in hiring, a published competency model. Structured interviewing (beh1) needs a list of competencies to write questions and rubrics against; values are a natural source. The connection is explicit at Amazon. Its interview-loop page says its conversations “are guided by our Leadership Principles”, and its SDE II preparation page explains that the loop covers “technical competencies and non-technical competencies that are based off of our Leadership Principles, which different interviewers will be assigned to evaluate”, and that “each interviewer will typically ask two or three behavioral-based questions about successes or challenges and how you handled them using our Leadership Principles.” Netflix’s memo describes its values as “the values we value” and says the company works hard to keep people accountable for them. Other companies are less explicit, but where values are published and specific, it is reasonable to assume they shape what interviewers listen for.

Two practical consequences follow. Different interviewers in the same loop may be assigned different values, which is another reason you need several distinct stories (beh2). And the values tell you what kind of story to prepare: a company that names Frugality will probably ask about doing more with less; one that names Candor will probably ask about feedback.

2. Amazon: the 16 Leadership Principles

Amazon’s official page lists sixteen principles: Customer Obsession; Ownership; Invent and Simplify; Are Right, A Lot; Learn and Be Curious; Hire and Develop the Best; Insist on the Highest Standards; Think Big; Bias for Action; Frugality; Earn Trust; Dive Deep; Have Backbone, Disagree and Commit; Deliver Results; Strive to be Earth’s Best Employer; and Success and Scale Bring Broad Responsibility. Each has a short description, and the descriptions are worth reading word by word because they contain the specific behaviours interviewers listen for. A few examples, quoted from the page:

  • Dive Deep: “Leaders operate at all levels, stay connected to the details, audit frequently, and are skeptical when metrics and anecdote differ.”
  • Are Right, A Lot: “They seek diverse perspectives and work to disconfirm their beliefs.”
  • Earn Trust: “They are vocally self-critical, even when doing so is awkward or embarrassing.”
  • Insist on the Highest Standards: “Leaders ensure that defects do not get sent down the line and that problems are fixed so they stay fixed.”
  • Frugality: “Accomplish more with less. Constraints breed resourcefulness, self-sufficiency, and invention.”

Prepare at least one story per principle, and two for those that come up most for your role (Ownership, Dive Deep, Have Backbone, Deliver Results, Earn Trust and Customer Obsession are good bets for engineers, though no official list says so).

3. Google: structured rubrics, attributes and “Googleyness”

Google’s official candidate material describes its process rather than a list of values. Its hiring-process page says interviews use structured interviewing: “Every candidate is assessed using clear rubrics. We use the same rubrics for everyone being considered for that role”, with open-ended, role-related questions and no brain teasers. Its re:Work guide on structured interviewing describes three helpful categories of attributes: role-related knowledge, problem solving, and leadership (for individual contributors, “emergent leadership”: taking initiative, working collaboratively across teams, and driving self-development). It describes both behavioural questions (“Tell me about a time…”) and hypothetical ones (“Imagine if…”), each with predetermined follow-ups. Google’s interview tips add what to prepare: data about your impact (the XYZ formula from beh3), how you navigate complexity and ambiguity, how you work with people and teams including conflict and influence, your leadership style, and questions for the interviewer.

And “Googleyness”? The word is part of Google’s culture: Google’s own blog has employees describing it (one, in a “My Path to Google” post, said that for them it narrows down to “respect and helping others”). Laszlo Bock, Google’s former head of People Operations, described it in his 2015 book Work Rules! in terms that include intellectual humility, conscientiousness, comfort with ambiguity and having taken courageous or interesting paths. But Google’s current candidate pages do not define it, and the many detailed checklists on preparation websites are not official. The safe approach is to prepare for what Google’s own pages describe (structured behavioural and hypothetical questions on collaboration, ambiguity, leadership and impact, plus genuine respect and helpfulness toward colleagues) and to treat any unofficial list with caution.

Two other official notes are worth knowing. Google’s pages state that AI tools are not permitted during its interviews. And its re:Work guide says interviewers document what poor, borderline, solid and outstanding answers look like, which is the rubric model from beh1.

4. Meta: six core values

Meta’s careers site publishes six core values. In its own words: Move fast (“We build and learn faster than anyone else. Acting with urgency, we don’t wait until next week to do something we could do today”); Build awesome things (shipping things that are “not just good, but also awe-inspiring”); Be direct and respect your colleagues (being “straightforward and willing to have hard conversations”, while respectful, recognising that “many of the world’s leading experts work here”); Focus on long-term impact (extending “the timeline for the impact we have, rather than optimizing for near-term wins”); Live in the future; and Meta, Metamates, Me.

For preparation, notice the tensions the values hold together: speed and long-term impact, directness and respect. Stories that show both sides of a tension are strongest: shipping fast on a reversible change while protecting a long-term asset; giving direct feedback in a way that kept the relationship. The conflict and feedback lessons (beh4, beh5) and the decision-speed material in beh6 map directly onto these values.

5. Netflix: the culture memo

Netflix publishes a long culture memo (its first culture deck dates from 2009). It describes four core principles (the Dream Team; People Over Process; Uncomfortably Exciting; Great and Always Better) and lists eight values: Selflessness, Judgment, Candor, Creativity, Courage, Inclusion, Curiosity and Resilience, each with a one-sentence description. Judgment, for example, is described as looking “beyond short term fixes in favor of long term solutions”, making “wise decisions despite ambiguity” and using “data to inform your intuition”. Candor includes willingly receiving and giving feedback and admitting mistakes openly.

The memo also describes decision practices that make good interview material because they match behaviours interviewers elsewhere look for. Managers practise context not control. Significant decisions have an informed captain who is expected to seek out different opinions, which Netflix calls farming for dissent, and once a decision is made, people disagree then commit. If you interview at Netflix, expect questions about feedback you gave or received, decisions you made with limited information, and times you sought out or acted on dissent. The memo also describes high performance expectations, including the keeper test, which is worth understanding before you decide whether the culture suits you.

6. Microsoft: growth mindset

Microsoft’s careers culture page centres on four themes: growth mindset, customer obsessed, diverse and inclusive, and One Microsoft. The page describes a growth mindset as seeing potential “as something to be nurtured, not pre-determined”, and emphasises curiosity, continuous learning, “taking smart risks and learning from mistakes”, and seeking feedback. For interviews, that suggests preparing especially strong learning and failure stories (beh5): a time you learned something new quickly, a mistake and what you changed, feedback you acted on. “One Microsoft” suggests collaboration across teams, which beh7’s influence stories cover.

7. AI companies: the example of Anthropic

AI companies increasingly publish values too, and they are worth reading closely because they often reveal what matters about how the work is done. Anthropic’s careers page lists seven principles: Act for the global good; Hold light and shade; Be good to our users; Ignite a race to the top on safety; Do the simple thing that works; Be helpful, honest, and harmless; and Put the mission first. Two are especially concrete for an engineer. “Do the simple thing that works” describes taking “an empirical approach to problems” and caring “about the size of our impact and not the sophistication of our methods”, identifying “the simplest solution and iterate from there”. “Hold light and shade” describes taking both AI’s potential risks and benefits seriously.

The same page describes the interview process in general terms: interviews over video, live coding where you can look things up, questions about your experience and what motivates you, and time to ask your own questions, described as “a two-way conversation”. For an AI engineer, the principles suggest preparing stories that show empirical, simple-first engineering (a measured baseline before anything complex) and stories that show you take risks and safety seriously in your work. The general point applies to any AI company: read its own published principles and process description, and prepare evidence for what it says, not for what forums claim.

8. Reading a value description as a rubric

To see how much a value description gives away, take Amazon’s Earn Trust in full: “Leaders listen attentively, speak candidly, and treat others respectfully. They are vocally self-critical, even when doing so is awkward or embarrassing. Leaders do not believe their or their team’s body odor smells of perfume. They benchmark themselves and their teams against the best.” Broken into behaviours an interviewer could hear in a story, that is: listening attentively (you changed your view after hearing someone out); speaking candidly (you told someone an uncomfortable truth); treating others respectfully (your description of colleagues is fair); being vocally self-critical (you admitted a mistake publicly); not believing your own team is beyond criticism (you found and fixed a weakness in your own team’s work); and benchmarking against the best (you compared your system to a stronger external baseline and acted on the gap).

That list doubles as a preparation checklist. For an Earn Trust question, the strongest story will show two or three of those behaviours at once, for example a time you discovered your own team’s evaluation was flattering your model, said so openly in a review, and benchmarked against a stronger baseline to fix it. It also tells you what to avoid: a trust story in which you described another team unfairly contradicts the principle while appearing to illustrate it. The same exercise works on any company’s values, and it takes about ten minutes per value. Doing it for the five or six values most relevant to your role is one of the highest-return hours of company-specific preparation.

9. One story bank, many companies

The frameworks overlap far more than their wording suggests. Almost all of them want evidence of ownership and delivery, judgement under uncertainty, candour and constructive disagreement, learning from mistakes, and care for users. Some add distinctive emphases: Amazon’s Frugality, Netflix’s Courage and Selflessness, Meta’s speed, Anthropic’s safety and simplicity, Microsoft’s growth mindset. So the efficient preparation is:

  1. Build one story bank of 8–12 stories (beh2) around the shared competencies.
  2. For each target company, copy the matrix and relabel its columns with that company’s values.
  3. Re-check which stories fit each column; most will map directly.
  4. For distinctive values with no story, look for the closest real example and add it.
  5. Practise re-angling: the same story told with the emphasis each company’s value wants.

Throughout, use the company’s language to prepare and to recognise what a question is aiming at, but answer in plain language. The interviewer is trained to map evidence onto their values; announcing the value (“this shows my Bias for Action”) does their job badly and adds no evidence.

10. Worked example: “Tell me about a time you had to dive deep”

The question, as an interviewer assigned Amazon’s Dive Deep principle might ask it: “Tell me about a time you had to dive deep to find the root cause of a problem.” Both answers are illustrative examples written for this lesson; the company, people and numbers are invented.

Note that the same story would serve other companies’ values with different emphasis: Netflix’s Judgment (using data to inform intuition), Anthropic’s “Do the simple thing that works” (a simple grounding check rather than a new model), or Meta’s “Focus on long-term impact” (fixing the measurement so future changes are caught).

Why values converge

It is not a coincidence that company values overlap. Structured interviewing research and long experience point to a similar set of behaviours that predict good engineering work: taking responsibility, judging well under uncertainty, telling the truth, collaborating through disagreement, learning, and caring about the people who use the product. Companies express these in their own language and add emphases that reflect their business and history: a retailer with thin margins names Frugality; an entertainment company competing for creative talent names Creativity and Courage; a company building powerful AI names safety. Understanding the shared core lets you prepare efficiently; understanding the emphases tells you where to add stories.

Values in tension, and why that is useful

Many value lists contain deliberate tensions: Meta’s speed and long-term impact; Amazon’s Bias for Action and Insist on the Highest Standards; Netflix’s freedom and high performance. These are not contradictions; they describe judgement about when each applies. Interviewers sometimes probe them directly (“tell me about a time you had to choose between speed and quality”). The strongest stories show you recognising the tension and resolving it with a principle, typically the reversibility rule from beh6: move fast where mistakes can be undone, hold the standard where they cannot. A candidate who can articulate that is showing exactly the judgement value lists are trying to describe.

Reading values as a candidate, not just as an interviewee

Published values tell you what a company will expect of you, not only what it will ask. Netflix’s memo, for example, describes the keeper test and a performance culture modelled on “a professional sports team, not a family”; some people find that energising and others find it stressful. Amazon’s principles describe relentlessly high standards and frugality. Anthropic’s principles put the mission first. Reading values carefully is therefore part of deciding whether you want the job, not only part of getting it, and it gives you good material for the questions you ask (beh8): “How does the team apply ‘disagree then commit’ when the informed captain is junior?” is both a sign of preparation and a real question.

When official sources are thin

Not every company publishes values or interview guidance, and some publish only general statements. In that case, use the next best official sources: the job description (it often lists behavioural expectations), the company’s engineering blog (what it chooses to celebrate tells you what it values), talks by its leaders, and the recruiter, who can usually tell you what each round assesses if you ask. What you should avoid is filling the gap with confident lists from preparation websites. They may be accurate, outdated or invented, and you cannot tell which. Prepare the shared competencies well, and you will be ready for most of what an unpublished framework contains.

Values and level

The same value is judged at different scopes depending on the level of the role, just as in beh1. A mid-level engineer’s Ownership story might be following through on their own feature; a senior engineer’s might be fixing a problem that spanned several teams. Some values carry most of their weight at senior levels: Amazon’s Think Big and Hire and Develop the Best, Netflix’s description of leaders as strong developers of talent, Meta’s Focus on long-term impact. If you are interviewing at a senior or staff level, expect those values to be probed harder, and prepare stories where your scope is explicit: the number of teams affected, the people you developed, the direction you set. A senior candidate with only task-level stories for these values will often be offered a lower level even if every story is well told.

The risk of pandering

Knowing a company’s values creates a temptation to perform them: to describe yourself in their words, to claim every value, to bend stories toward whichever value you think the interviewer wants. Interviewers notice, and it can backfire twice: it adds no evidence, and it can make the candidate seem less authentic, which some value systems (Netflix’s Candor, Anthropic’s honesty) explicitly prize. The disciplined approach is to let the values shape your preparation and your story selection, and then to tell the truth plainly. If one of their values genuinely does not describe you, that is useful information for both sides.

How company-specific preparation goes wrong

The value-name salad. Answers that mention the company’s values by name (“this shows Ownership and Bias for Action”) instead of showing them. Fix: never name the value; give evidence the interviewer can map.

Forum-driven preparation. Preparing from unofficial lists of “what Google really looks for” or leaked question banks. They are often outdated or wrong, and memorised answers to leaked questions sound memorised. Fix: official pages first; shared competencies second.

One story for every value. At Amazon especially, where interviewers are assigned different principles, a candidate with three stories will repeat them. Fix: at least one story per principle, two for the most common.

Ignoring the distinctive values. Preparing generic stories and having nothing for Frugality, Courage or safety. Fix: read the full list and fill the gaps that are specific to the company.

Stretching a story to fit. Telling a delivery story as a Frugality story because it happened to be cheap. Fix: only map a story where the behaviour is genuinely the point of the story.

A per-company preparation checklist

  1. Read the official values page in full; copy each value’s description into your notes.
  2. Read the official interview guidance (Amazon’s loop and role pages, Google’s hiring and interview-tips pages, the company’s careers FAQ).
  3. Relabel your story-bank matrix columns with the values; mark fits; identify gaps.
  4. Find or prepare stories for the gaps, especially distinctive values.
  5. For each value, practise the opening sentence of the best story with that value’s emphasis.
  6. Prepare one honest answer to “which of our values fits you least?”.
  7. Note one or two questions about how the values work in practice, for the end of your interviews.

Recognising the value behind a question in the moment

In the interview itself, a quick internal translation helps. When the question arrives, ask yourself which value it most likely targets (“a time the data told a different story from the metrics” is almost certainly Dive Deep or Judgment), pick the best unused story for that value from your matrix, and choose the opening sentence that puts the relevant behaviour first. This takes a few seconds and is worth a short pause; it is fine to say “let me think of the best example”. If a question seems to target two values at once, choose the story that shows both, or ask a brief clarifying question.

Keeping up with changes

Values and interview processes change. Amazon added its two newest principles, Strive to be Earth’s Best Employer and Success and Scale Bring Broad Responsibility, in 2021; Meta replaced its earlier values with the current six in 2022; Netflix has revised its culture memo several times; Anthropic’s careers page describes its interview process in some detail and can change. Always read the current official page shortly before your interviews, and be wary of any guide that quotes values you cannot find on the company’s own site.

When you interview at several companies

Candidates interviewing at several companies at once can keep one master story bank and one matrix per company. The extra work per company is small once the bank exists: an hour to read the values and relabel, and perhaps one or two new stories. Track which stories you have told in which loop, since a company may see feedback from several rounds and you want variety there; different companies, of course, will not compare notes.

Goal: map your story bank onto one company’s published values and fill the gaps. Time: about 45 minutes.

  1. Choose a company you are targeting, or Amazon if you have none, because its framework is the most explicit.
  2. Read the official page. Copy each value and its description into a sheet. For Amazon, add notes from its interview-loop page.
  3. Relabel your matrix. Columns become the company’s values; rows remain your stories (beh2). Mark each cell only where the behaviour is genuinely central to the story.
  4. Find gaps. Circle values with no story, and values with only one. For each gap, write the closest real example you can think of, or note that you have none.
  5. Re-angle. Take your strongest story and write its opening sentence for three different values.
  6. Remove the labels. Read one full answer aloud and delete every mention of a value name. Check that the behaviour is still obvious from what you did.
  7. Prepare the honest answer to “which of our values fits you least?”, with one sentence on how you are working on it and one example that shows the effort is real.

Repeat the exercise for every company where you have a loop scheduled, ideally a week before, so there is time to find stories for the gaps.

What to look for: most engineers find their Amazon gaps are Frugality, Think Big and Hire and Develop the Best. If one value column is empty, that is the question most likely to catch you off guard, so prepare it first.

One story, three companies

Your strongest story: you noticed your team’s RAG assistant was answering from outdated policy documents, built a freshness check and a nightly evaluation on recently changed policies, and wrong-policy answers dropped from about 8% to under 1%. You have loops at Amazon, Meta and Netflix next month. For each company, name the value or values this story best serves, write the opening sentence you would use, and say which part of the story you would emphasise.

Answer

Amazon: Insist on the Highest Standards (“problems are fixed so they stay fixed”) or Dive Deep, possibly Ownership. Opening for Highest Standards: “Our assistant’s overall accuracy looked fine, but I found it was quoting policies that had been changed, and I wanted to make sure that kind of error couldn’t reach customers again.” Emphasise the nightly evaluation and freshness check as the mechanism that keeps the defect from recurring, and the measured drop.

Meta: Focus on long-term impact, perhaps Move fast. Opening: “I could have patched the few wrong answers we saw, but I built something that would catch every future policy change.” Emphasise choosing the durable fix over the quick one, while noting how quickly you shipped it (speed and long-term impact together).

Netflix: Judgment (“long term solutions”, “data to inform your intuition”) or Curiosity. Opening: “I had a hunch our assistant was going stale as policies changed, so I measured it before arguing for a fix.” Emphasise using data to test an intuition, and the judgement in choosing a check rather than a rebuild.

In every version the facts are identical; only the opening and emphasis change. And in none of them do you name the value: the interviewer will.
