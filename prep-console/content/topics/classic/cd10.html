Type a few words into a search box and, in a fraction of a second, you get ten links out of billions of pages, ranked so the useful ones are first, and most of them reflect what those pages say today rather than last year. Behind that box sit two very different machines. One reads the web: it downloads pages, finds links in them, downloads those, and keeps going forever, revisiting pages as they change. The other answers questions: it has organised everything that was read into an index that can find every page containing a set of words and rank them, faster than the user can blink.

The reading machine — the crawler — is the subject of most interviews, and it is harder than it looks. A naive crawler is a loop: take a URL from a list, download it, add its links to the list. Run that at the scale of the web and everything goes wrong. It hammers small websites with hundreds of requests a second until their owners block it or their servers fall over. It downloads the same page hundreds of times under slightly different URLs. It gets lost in an online calendar that has a "next month" link forever. It spends its whole budget re-fetching pages that never change while news pages go stale. And it collapses on surprising bottlenecks: when Larry Page and Sergey Brin built the first Google crawler in 1998, they named DNS lookups — turning host names into addresses — as a major performance stress.

The design that works is built around a single component, the URL frontier, which decides both what to fetch next (priority) and when each website may be contacted again (politeness). Around it sit duplicate detection at two levels (the same URL, and the same content under different URLs), respect for each site's robots.txt rules, a recrawl scheduler that spends a limited budget where change and importance are highest, and an indexing pipeline that turns pages into an inverted index and a link graph for ranking.

This lesson follows the interview flow: requirements; estimates (pages per second, connections in flight, bandwidth, storage, URL dedup memory, index size); the API between components and the data model; the high-level design and the critical path of one fetch; deep dives on the frontier and politeness, on deduplication, and on recrawl scheduling, indexing and PageRank; then failure modes and trade-offs. The real case is Brin and Page's original Google paper, and the lab builds a polite frontier with URL normalisation, a Bloom filter and SimHash.

The mental model: a library that sends out readers

Imagine a library that wants a copy of every newsletter in the country. It employs readers who visit publishers, copy their newsletters, and note any other publishers mentioned inside. A head librarian keeps the list of publishers to visit and decides who goes where. Two rules keep the library welcome: never send more than one reader to the same small publisher at a time, and come back to each publisher only as often as they actually publish. The librarian also keeps a card index of what has already been copied, so two readers never copy the same newsletter, and notices when a "new" newsletter is really an old one with a different date on the cover. Back at the library, cataloguers build an index — for every word, which newsletters contain it — and keep track of which publishers are mentioned most by respected others, to decide which newsletters to recommend first.

The head librarian's list is the URL frontier. "One reader per small publisher at a time" is politeness. Publishers who post a sign on the door saying "do not copy the back room" are using robots.txt. The card index is URL deduplication; spotting a re-dated newsletter is near-duplicate detection. Coming back as often as they publish is recrawl scheduling. The word index is the inverted index, and "mentioned by respected others" is PageRank.

The analogy breaks on scale and hostility. There are hundreds of millions of publishers; some publish millions of pages and some one; some build infinite corridors of pages to trap readers; and every visit involves a slow phone call (a DNS lookup) to find out where the publisher lives. The design is mostly about making those differences cheap.

Two systems with different clocks

The crawl and index pipeline runs continuously in the background and is measured in pages per second, freshness and coverage. The query service answers users and is measured in milliseconds of latency. They meet only at the index: the pipeline publishes new index segments, and the query service searches whatever segments are live. Keeping them apart means a slow crawl never slows a search, and a query spike never slows the crawl.

Three things a beginner should hold on to

First, throughput comes from breadth, not speed: because each host must be contacted slowly, a fast crawler is one that talks to thousands of hosts at the same time. Second, most URLs and much content are duplicates, so cheap duplicate checks before fetching and before indexing save most of the work. Third, the crawl is never finished: the web changes constantly, so the real question is how to spend a fixed fetch budget to keep the most important pages freshest.

1 · Requirements

Functional. Starting from seed URLs, discover and download web pages; extract text and links; avoid downloading the same page repeatedly; store pages; build an index so that a query returns relevant pages ranked by relevance and importance; revisit pages to keep the index fresh. Out of scope, said aloud: query understanding and spelling correction, ads, image and video search, and the detailed ranking model beyond link analysis and text relevance.

Non-functional. Politeness: never overload a site; obey robots.txt. Robustness: survive malformed HTML, slow or malicious servers, spider traps and enormous files. Scale: billions of pages. Freshness: important pages re-fetched quickly after they change. Extensibility: new content types and processing stages can be added. Queries return in a few hundred milliseconds at most.

The clarifying questions: how many pages, and how fresh must they be? A one-off research crawl of a billion pages over a month is a different system from keeping ten billion pages fresh continuously. Also ask whether JavaScript must be executed to see content (rendering pages in a headless browser costs orders of magnitude more than fetching HTML), and whether the crawl is general web or focused on particular domains.

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Assume we keep an index of 10 billion pages, re-fetched on average once every 30 days, with an average page of 100 KB of HTML.

Fetch rate. 10B ÷ (30 × 86,400 s) = 10B ÷ 2.59M s ≈ 3,900 pages per second, sustained. Discovery of new pages and failures add some margin: plan for about 5,000.

Connections in flight. A fetch — DNS lookup, connect, TLS, request, download — takes perhaps 0.5-2 seconds. By Little's law (in flight = rate × time each takes), 3,900 per second × 1 s ≈ 4,000 concurrent fetches. With politeness of about one request per second per host, that is also about 4,000 different hosts being fetched at any moment. For comparison, Brin and Page's 1998 crawler ran about three crawler processes with roughly 300 connections each and peaked at just over 100 pages per second.

Bandwidth. 3,900 × 100 KB = 390 MB/s ≈ 3.1 Gbit/s inbound, less with compressed transfer. Comfortable for a data centre; not the bottleneck.

Storage. 10B × 100 KB = 1 PB of raw HTML. Compression of 3-4× is typical for HTML (the original Google repository compressed 147.8 GB to 53.5 GB, about 2.8×, with a fast compressor), so about 300 TB of compressed current copies; keep a few historical versions for change detection and the store is around 1 PB. Extracted text is much smaller, perhaps 10 KB per page: 100 TB.

URLs seen. The crawler discovers far more URLs than it fetches — the 1998 Google crawl saw 76.5 million URLs while fetching 24 million pages, about 3 to 1. Say 30 billion URLs. Stored in full at about 100 bytes each, that is 3 TB; a Bloom filter answering "have I seen this URL?" with a 1% false-positive rate needs about 9.6 bits per URL: 30B × 9.6 bits ≈ 2.9 × 1011 bits ≈ 36 GB, which fits in the memory of a few machines.

DNS. At 3,900 fetches per second over hundreds of millions of hosts, an uncached DNS lookup for every fetch would mean thousands of lookups per second, each taking tens to hundreds of milliseconds. A local DNS cache (each 1998 Google crawler kept its own) turns most into memory lookups.

Index size. An inverted index with positions is often a substantial fraction of the text it indexes — say 30-50% of the 100 TB of text, around 40 TB — sharded by document across many machines, replicated for query throughput.

3 · Interfaces and data model

A crawler has no public API; its interfaces are between internal components:

frontier.add(urls[], priority, source)          -- after normalisation and seen-check
frontier.next() → (url, host, not_before)        -- next URL whose host may be fetched now
fetcher.fetch(url, if_modified_since?, etag?)   → status, headers, body | 304 | error
parser.parse(body) → text, links[], canonical?, meta robots, fingerprints
indexer.add(doc_id, terms[], positions, signals)
search(query, k) → top-k [(url, title, snippet, score)]
url_state(url_hash PK, url, host, last_fetch, last_change, etag, last_modified,
          change_rate_estimate, importance, next_fetch_due, status)
host_state(host PK, robots_rules, robots_fetched_at, crawl_delay_s, next_allowed_at,
           error_rate, ip_addresses, dns_expires_at)
content_store(content_hash PK, compressed_body, fetched_at)      -- immutable blobs
docs(doc_id PK, url, content_hash, simhash, title, lang, pagerank)
postings(term → [(doc_id, positions[])])                         -- inverted index segments
links(src_doc_id, dst_url_hash, anchor_text)                     -- link graph for ranking

Two choices to call out. Content is stored once by content hash, so identical pages fetched under different URLs cost one copy. And anchor text — the words of links pointing at a page — is stored with the link graph, because it often describes a page better than the page itself; the original Google paper indexed over 259 million anchors from its 24-million-page crawl for exactly this reason.

4 · High-level design and the critical path of one fetch

Walk one URL through the sequence diagram. The frontier hands a fetcher https://news.example.org/world/, whose host's next-allowed time has just arrived. The fetcher checks the host's cached robots.txt (fetched less than 24 hours ago; the path is allowed) and the cached IP address (no DNS lookup needed). It sends a conditional GET with the stored ETag and If-Modified-Since. The server returns 200 with 140 KB of HTML. The fetcher records the fetch time and updates the host's next-allowed time (now + 1 s). The page goes to the content store and the parser. The parser extracts text and 85 links; each link is normalised and checked against the seen-URL filter, and the 12 genuinely new ones go to the frontier with a priority derived from the page's importance. The content's exact hash and SimHash are checked against recent fingerprints for that site: it is not a duplicate, so it goes to the indexer, which adds its terms to the next index segment. The page's change-rate estimate is updated (it changed since last time), so its next fetch is scheduled sooner.

5 · Deep dive A: the URL frontier and politeness

The frontier must satisfy two competing goals: fetch the most valuable URLs first, and never contact a host more often than it allows. A single priority queue fails the second: the top thousand URLs might all be on one big site, and a fetcher pool working down that list would hit it a thousand times at once. A single FIFO queue per host fails the first: it has no notion of importance across hosts.

The classic design (from the Mercator crawler, described in standard information-retrieval textbooks) uses two layers. Front queues hold URLs by priority — say ten queues from most to least important — so higher-priority URLs are selected more often. Back queues hold URLs per host, each a FIFO of URLs for one host, with a rule that each back queue contains only one host and each active host has exactly one back queue. A heap keyed by each back queue's next allowed fetch time decides which host is ready: a fetcher pops the earliest host, takes the head URL of its queue, fetches it, and pushes the host back into the heap with time = now + its politeness delay. When a back queue empties, it is refilled from the front queues with the next URL for a new host. The lab implements the back-queue half with a heap and asserts that no host is ever fetched twice within its delay.

The politeness delay. A common default is about one request per second per host, or a multiple of the time the last fetch took (a slow server gets a longer gap). Some sites state a Crawl-delay in robots.txt; it is a widely used but non-standard directive (RFC 9309 does not define it), and many crawlers honour it anyway. Large sites often allow much faster crawling, and some publish rate preferences through webmaster tools. Politeness is per host, but several host names can share one server (one IP address), so politeness should also cap requests per IP address.

robots.txt is fetched before crawling a host and cached. RFC 9309 sets the rules: match the crawler's user-agent group (case-insensitively) or fall back to *; within a group, the most specific (longest) matching rule wins between Allow and Disallow; follow at least five redirects to reach the file; if the server returns a 4xx status, the crawler may access anything; if it returns a 5xx or the network fails, the crawler must assume complete disallow; do not use a cached copy for more than 24 hours unless the file is unreachable; and parse at least 500 KiB of it.

Skew is the real constraint. In aggregate, 4,000 hosts busy out of hundreds of millions looks like enormous headroom. But pages are concentrated: a few very large sites hold a large share of all pages, and each is still limited by its own per-host rate. In the lab, the one big site holds 201 of 2,431 URLs and dictates the 200-second length of the whole simulated crawl while 50 small sites finish early. The drill works this through at web scale. The fixes are per-host negotiated rates, sitemaps and conditional requests for big sites, and refreshing big sites selectively rather than completely.

6 · Deep dive B: deduplication of URLs and content

URL normalisation collapses different spellings of the same address: lower-case the scheme and host; drop default ports (:80 for http, :443 for https); remove the fragment after #, which never reaches the server; collapse duplicate slashes and resolve . and .. segments; sort query parameters; strip known tracking parameters such as utm_source; and honour a page's declared canonical URL when present. In the lab, HTTP://Example.com:80//a//b?utm_source=x&b=2&a=1#top becomes http://example.com/a/b?a=1&b=2.

Seen-URL check. Every normalised URL extracted from every page is checked before entering the frontier. At tens of billions of URLs, a Bloom filter does this in memory: hash each URL with k hash functions to k positions in a bit array; to add, set those bits; to test, check whether all are set. If any is zero the URL is definitely new; if all are set it was probably seen. The size formula: for a false-positive rate p, about −ln(p) ÷ (ln 2)² bits per item — 9.6 bits for 1% — with k ≈ 0.7 × bits per item, about 7. A false positive means occasionally skipping a URL that was not actually seen, which a crawler can tolerate; the alternative of keeping every URL in a database is used for the authoritative per-URL state (last fetch, change rate), sharded by URL hash.

Exact content duplicates. Many URLs serve identical bytes — mirrors, printer-friendly versions, the same page with and without www. A hash of the content (after stripping volatile parts like whitespace) catches them: store the content once, point every URL at it, and index it once.

Near-duplicates are the larger problem: the same article with a different advert, timestamp, comment count or navigation. SimHash builds a 64-bit fingerprint in which similar documents differ in few bits. For each word (or short phrase) in the document, hash it to 64 bits; keep 64 counters, adding 1 for each bit that is 1 and subtracting 1 for each bit that is 0 (weighted by word importance if desired); the fingerprint's bit i is 1 if counter i ended positive. Changing a few words shifts a few counters a little, so only a few fingerprint bits flip. Two documents are near-duplicates if their fingerprints differ in at most a few bits (a Hamming distance of 3 is a commonly cited threshold for 64-bit SimHash on web pages). In the lab, appending "| Advertisement | Updated 10:42" to a short news blurb moves its fingerprint 7 bits, while an unrelated recipe is 34 bits away — on short texts fingerprints move more, which is why thresholds are tuned on real data. To find near-duplicates among billions of fingerprints without comparing all pairs, split each fingerprint into blocks and index on each block: two fingerprints within 3 bits must agree exactly on at least one of 4 blocks, so only candidates sharing a block are compared.

7 · Deep dive C: recrawl scheduling, the index and PageRank

Recrawl. The crawl budget (about 3,900 fetches per second here) must be divided among 10 billion pages that change at very different rates. Estimate each page's change rate from its history — if the last four fetches found changes in three, it changes often — and weight by importance (incoming links, traffic, PageRank). Schedule fetches so important, fast-changing pages are revisited often, and stable or unimportant pages rarely. Make unchanged revisits cheap: send If-None-Match with the stored ETag or If-Modified-Since with the stored date, and an unchanged page returns 304 Not Modified with no body. Use sitemaps' lastmod dates as hints for which URLs changed. New pages enter with an estimate borrowed from similar pages on the same site.

The inverted index. For each term, store a posting list of the documents that contain it, with positions (for phrase queries) and per-document signals. A query for storm schools intersects the posting lists of both terms, scores the matches (term frequency, how rare each term is across the collection, whether terms appear in the title or in anchor text, proximity) and returns the top results. Indexes are built in batches: each indexer writes immutable segments for a slice of documents, which are merged in the background; deletions are recorded as tombstones until a merge removes them. The original Google indexer kept up with crawling at about 54 pages per second in 1998, and used parallel sorters to turn per-document word lists into per-word posting lists.

Sharding the index. Shard by document: each shard holds a full inverted index for its subset of pages, every query goes to every shard, each returns its local top k, and a coordinator merges. Sharding by term would put the posting list for "the" on one overloaded machine and force huge lists across the network for multi-term queries. Replicate each shard for query throughput and availability.

PageRank adds a query-independent importance score from the link graph. From the original paper: PR(A) = (1 − d) + d × (PR(T1)/C(T1) + … + PR(Tn)/C(Tn)), where T1…Tn link to A, C(T) is the number of links going out of T, and the damping factor d is usually 0.85. The intuition is a random surfer who follows a random link with probability d and otherwise jumps to a random page; a page's PageRank is how often the surfer is there. A worked example on three pages — A links to B and C, B links to C, C links to A — using the normalised form with (1 − d)/N so ranks sum to 1, and starting from 1/3 each: after many iterations the ranks settle at about A 0.39, B 0.21, C 0.40: C collects links from both A and B, and A inherits all of C's rank. At web scale, each iteration is a pass over the edge list: every page sends its rank divided by its out-degree along each link, and each page sums what it receives — a MapReduce-style join and sum, repeated a few dozen times until changes are negligible. The 1998 paper reports computing PageRank for 26 million pages in a few hours on a medium-sized workstation.

8 · Bottlenecks, failure modes and trade-offs

DNS: a slow or overloaded resolver silently throttles everything; cache aggressively per fetcher, run local resolvers, and prefetch for hosts about to become ready. Spider traps: calendars, session ids and auto-generated paths create infinite URLs; cap URLs per host and path depth, detect repeating patterns and near-duplicate floods. Hostile or broken servers: slow-drip responses, enormous files, malformed HTML; enforce timeouts, maximum sizes and robust parsers. Frontier imbalance: too few ready hosts leaves fetchers idle while big hosts' queues are full; keep many hosts active and monitor ready-host counts. Robots.txt errors: a site whose robots.txt starts returning 5xx must be treated as fully disallowed, which can look like a sudden coverage drop. Index publication: new segments go live atomically; a bad segment must be rollback-able without stopping queries.

Trade-offs to close on: freshness versus coverage within a fixed fetch budget; politeness versus speed (and negotiating per-host rates with big sites); Bloom-filter memory versus occasionally skipping new URLs; near-duplicate thresholds (too strict wastes index space, too loose merges distinct pages); rendering JavaScript (complete content, 10-100× cost) versus fetching HTML only; and document-sharded indexes (simple, balanced) versus term-sharded ones. The sentence that ties the crawler together: throughput comes from breadth across hosts, and the frontier exists to turn a priority order into a polite schedule.

Tail latency when a query fans out to every shard

A document-sharded index sends every query to every shard and waits for all of them. If each shard answers within its p99 latency 99% of the time, a query touching 100 shards meets at least one slow shard with probability 1 − 0.99100 ≈ 63%. So the system's tail is set by the slowest shard, not the typical one. The remedies, described in Google's "The Tail at Scale", are hedged requests (send a second request to another replica of a shard if the first has not answered within, say, its p95 time, and use whichever returns first), tied requests that cancel the duplicate once one starts, and hard per-shard deadlines that return partial results rather than waiting. The cost is a few percent extra load for a large drop in tail latency.

Freshness as an optimisation problem

Suppose a page changes on average once every T days and is fetched every I days. If changes arrive randomly, the expected fraction of time the stored copy is stale grows with I/T: fetching a daily-changing page weekly leaves it stale most of the time, while fetching a yearly-changing page weekly wastes almost every fetch. Research on crawl scheduling shows a counter-intuitive result: if you can only afford a fixed number of fetches, spending them on pages that change extremely often can be a poor investment, because they go stale again almost immediately; moderate-change, high-importance pages often give more freshness per fetch. A practical scheduler assigns each page an interval from its estimated change rate and importance, subject to per-host budgets, and lets measured outcomes (fraction of fetches that found changes) tune the model.

Distributing the crawler

A single frontier becomes a bottleneck at thousands of fetches per second, so crawlers are partitioned, usually by host: hash the host name to choose which crawler node owns it. All of a host's politeness state, robots.txt, DNS cache entry and URL queue then live on one node, so politeness needs no coordination. Links discovered on one node that point to hosts owned by another are forwarded in batches. Partitioning by host also makes failure recovery simple: when a node dies, its hosts are reassigned and its frontier is reloaded from durable per-URL state. Partitioning by URL hash would scatter one host's URLs across nodes and make politeness a distributed-locking problem.

Rendering, the modern complication

Many sites build their content with JavaScript, so the HTML a plain fetch returns may be nearly empty. Rendering means running the page in a headless browser, executing scripts and waiting for network requests, which costs seconds of CPU per page instead of milliseconds. Large crawlers therefore run two stages: fetch and index the raw HTML immediately, then queue pages that appear to need rendering (little text, heavy scripts) for a separate, much smaller rendering fleet, and update the index when rendering completes. The rendering queue is itself budgeted by importance.

Crawler traps and adversaries

Not every site is friendly. Some generate infinite URL spaces by accident (calendars, faceted search with every filter combination); some do it on purpose to waste crawlers' budgets or to inject spam into indexes; some serve different content to crawlers than to users (cloaking). Defences combine budgets (maximum pages per host per cycle, maximum depth), pattern detection (repeating path segments, monotonically growing parameters), content checks (near-duplicate floods, link farms where many pages link only to each other) and comparisons of crawler-fetched and browser-rendered content. Link-analysis scores like PageRank are themselves targets of manipulation, which is why modern ranking combines many signals.

Index updates without downtime

Rebuilding a whole index takes hours or days, so production systems use a tiered approach: a large base index rebuilt periodically, plus small, frequently built increments for new and changed pages, searched together at query time. Each piece is a set of immutable segments; publishing means atomically switching which segments are live on each replica, and rolling back means switching back. This is the same log-structured idea used by Lucene and many databases: write new immutable files, merge in the background, never update in place.

Measuring the crawler

Useful metrics go beyond pages per second: freshness (sampled important pages checked against the live web: what fraction is stale?), coverage (how many pages users visit, or that appear in other indexes, are missing from ours?), politeness incidents (complaints, robots violations: must be zero), duplicate rate at URL and content level, 304 rate for revisits (how much budget conditional requests save), DNS latency, and fetch state distribution (time spent resolving, connecting, downloading).

The real case: the original Google crawler and index

Brin and Page's 1998 paper, "The Anatomy of a Large-Scale Hypertextual Web Search Engine", describes a crawler that already has this lesson's shape. A single URLserver sent lists of URLs to a few crawler processes ("we typically ran about 3"), all written in Python. "Each crawler keeps roughly 300 connections open at once," the paper says, which was necessary to fetch pages fast enough; at peak, the system crawled over 100 pages per second using four crawlers, about 600 KB per second of data. "A major performance stress is DNS lookup," so each crawler maintained its own DNS cache. Each connection moved through states — looking up DNS, connecting, sending the request, receiving the response — managed with asynchronous I/O and queues.

The numbers from that crawl are worth knowing: 24 million pages fetched out of 76.5 million URLs seen, 147.8 GB of fetched pages compressed to a 53.5 GB repository, 1.6 million 404s, and over 259 million anchors indexed for anchor-text ranking. The whole crawl of 26 million pages took about nine days including problems — "disks filled up, name servers crashed" — but once running smoothly it downloaded the last 11 million pages in 63 hours, about 48.5 pages per second, with the indexer keeping pace at about 54 pages per second and four machines sorting the index in about 24 hours. PageRank, with d usually set to 0.85, could be computed for 26 million pages in a few hours on a medium-sized workstation.

The paper is also candid about operating a crawler in public. Running one that connects to more than half a million servers and generates tens of millions of log entries "generates a fair amount of email and phone calls". Some webmasters did not know about the robots exclusion protocol and expected a "this page is copyrighted" notice to stop indexing; once, the crawler tried to crawl an online game and sent garbage messages into the middle of play. The authors' conclusion — that it is "virtually impossible to test a crawler without running it on large part of the Internet", that hundreds of obscure problems occur on one page out of the whole web, and that significant resources must go to reading email and fixing problems as they come up — remains the most honest description of crawler operations.

The robots exclusion protocol the paper mentions was finally standardised in 2022 as RFC 9309, which codifies the behaviours this lesson uses: longest-match rules, five redirects, 4xx means no restrictions, 5xx means disallow everything, caching for no more than 24 hours, and at least 500 KiB parsed. It also states plainly that robots.txt "is not a substitute for valid content security measures".

Failure modes and what to monitor

Fetch rate and its breakdown by state (DNS, connect, TLS, download) — a shift towards DNS time is the classic early warning. Ready hosts in the frontier: if it falls, fetchers idle even though queues are full. Per-host error and 5xx rates, with automatic backoff for struggling hosts. robots.txt fetch failures, since they force disallow. Politeness violations, which must be zero. URL explosion per host, to catch traps. Duplicate and near-duplicate rates. Freshness on a sampled set of important pages. Index build lag and query tail latency per shard.

Goal: build the core of a polite frontier — URL normalisation, a Bloom filter for seen URLs, per-host queues scheduled by a heap — and a SimHash near-duplicate check, on a simulated web. Python 3.12, standard library, runs instantly, no network access.

"""A polite URL frontier: URL normalisation, a Bloom filter for seen URLs, per-host queues
scheduled by a heap of next-allowed fetch times, and SimHash near-duplicate detection.
Python 3.12 stdlib; the 'web' is simulated, no network access."""
import hashlib, heapq, random, re
from collections import defaultdict, deque
from urllib.parse import urlsplit, urlunsplit

def normalise(url):
    p = urlsplit(url.strip())
    host = (p.hostname or "").lower()
    port = f":{p.port}" if p.port and p.port not in (80, 443) else ""
    path = re.sub(r"/+", "/", p.path or "/")
    query = "&".join(sorted(q for q in p.query.split("&") if q and not q.startswith("utm_")))
    return urlunsplit((p.scheme.lower(), host + port, path, query, ""))   # drop #fragment

class Bloom:
    def __init__(self, bits=1 << 20, k=7):
        self.bits, self.k, self.arr = bits, k, bytearray(bits // 8)
    def _idx(self, s):
        h = hashlib.sha256(s.encode()).digest()
        return [int.from_bytes(h[4 * i:4 * i + 4], "big") % self.bits for i in range(self.k)]
    def add(self, s):
        for i in self._idx(s): self.arr[i // 8] |= 1 << (i % 8)
    def __contains__(self, s):
        return all(self.arr[i // 8] >> (i % 8) & 1 for i in self._idx(s))

def simhash(text, bits=64):
    v = [0] * bits
    for w in re.findall(r"\w+", text.lower()):
        h = int.from_bytes(hashlib.md5(w.encode()).digest()[:8], "big")
        for i in range(bits): v[i] += 1 if h >> i & 1 else -1
    return sum(1 << i for i in range(bits) if v[i] > 0)

class Frontier:
    def __init__(self, delay=1.0):
        self.delay, self.queues, self.ready, self.seen = delay, defaultdict(deque), [], Bloom()
    def add(self, url, now=0.0):
        u = normalise(url)
        if u in self.seen: return False
        self.seen.add(u)
        host = urlsplit(u).hostname
        if not self.queues[host]: heapq.heappush(self.ready, (now, host))   # host becomes schedulable
        self.queues[host].append(u)
        return True
    def next(self):
        t, host = heapq.heappop(self.ready)
        url = self.queues[host].popleft()
        if self.queues[host]: heapq.heappush(self.ready, (t + self.delay, host))  # politeness gap
        return t, url

Work it out

You must crawl 20B pages and refresh them monthly. Politeness allows 1 request/second/host. Your corpus spans 200M distinct hosts, but the distribution is extreme: the top 1,000 hosts hold 40% of all pages.

(a) What crawl rate is needed and is politeness the binding constraint? (b) What happens to the top 1,000 hosts? (c) How do you fix it?

Answer

(a) 20B ÷ 2.6M seconds = 7,700 pages/s. At 1 req/s/host, sustaining that needs 7,700 hosts being fetched at any instant — out of 200M available. That is 0.004% of hosts active at once, so in aggregate politeness is not binding at all; bandwidth (~6 Gbps at 100 KB per page) and the frontier's throughput are the practical limits. But the aggregate is misleading, which part (b) shows.

(b) The top 1,000 hosts. They hold 40% of 20B = 8 billion pages. At 1 request/second/host, those 1,000 hosts can collectively yield 1,000 pages/s, so crawling 8B pages from them takes 8 × 10⁹ ÷ 1,000 = 8 million seconds ≈ 92 days.

Your monthly refresh target is impossible by a factor of three for the pages that matter most — and these are the large, high-authority sites whose freshness users notice. Meanwhile the long tail of 200M small hosts finishes almost immediately and then sits idle. The politeness constraint is not a global limit, it is a per-host limit, and the page distribution is so skewed that a handful of hosts dominate the schedule entirely. This is the real insight of the question.

(c) The fixes, in order of value:

  • Negotiate a higher rate with large hosts. Major sites publish sitemaps, many state a (non-standard) Crawl-delay, and they often welcome faster crawling because they want to be indexed. Ten requests/second to a site serving millions of users daily is negligible load. This alone turns 92 days into 9. Recognising that politeness is a per-host negotiated parameter rather than a universal constant is the key move.
  • Stop refreshing everything monthly. The uniform target is the actual error. Allocate crawl budget by predicted change rate and importance: a large site's homepage and section pages hourly, its active content daily, its decade-old archive annually. Most of those 8 billion pages have not changed since the last crawl, so re-fetching them is wasted budget.
  • Use the cheap signals before spending a fetch. Sitemaps with lastmod, conditional requests with If-Modified-Since, and ETag headers let a host tell you nothing has changed for a fraction of the cost of a full fetch — a 304 response is a few hundred bytes and still counts against politeness, but it is far cheaper in bandwidth and parsing, and many large sites will permit a higher rate for conditional requests.
  • Shard the frontier by host so these 1,000 hosts have dedicated, continuously-active per-host queues rather than competing for slots in a shared structure. Their crawlers should never be idle.

The transferable lesson: an averaged capacity calculation hid a constraint that only appears when you look at the distribution. 0.004% host utilisation looked like enormous headroom while the pages that mattered were three times over budget. Any system with a skewed key distribution — hot shards, celebrity fan-out, popular cache keys — has this same shape, and checking the tail rather than the mean is the habit worth carrying into every capacity question.

Compute it

Three pages: A links to B and C; B links to C; C links to A. Using the normalised PageRank PR(p) = (1 − d)/N + d × Σ PR(q)/C(q) with d = 0.85 and N = 3, start from 1/3 each and compute two iterations by hand. Which page ends up highest, and why?

Answer

(1 − d)/N = 0.15 ÷ 3 = 0.05. Out-degrees: C(A) = 2, C(B) = 1, C(C) = 1.

Iteration 1 (from 0.333 each): A receives from C: 0.333/1 → PR(A) = 0.05 + 0.85 × 0.333 = 0.333. B receives from A: 0.333/2 = 0.167 → PR(B) = 0.05 + 0.85 × 0.167 = 0.192. C receives from A (0.167) and B (0.333) = 0.5 → PR(C) = 0.05 + 0.85 × 0.5 = 0.475.

Iteration 2: PR(A) = 0.05 + 0.85 × 0.475 = 0.454. PR(B) = 0.05 + 0.85 × (0.333/2) = 0.192. PR(C) = 0.05 + 0.85 × (0.333/2 + 0.192) = 0.05 + 0.85 × 0.358 = 0.355.

The values oscillate between A and C at first, then settle (after a few dozen iterations) near A ≈ 0.39, B ≈ 0.21, C ≈ 0.40, summing to 1. C is highest because it receives links from both other pages; A is close behind because C, an important page, gives all of its rank to A. B is lowest: it is linked only by A, which splits its rank between two links. The example shows the two ideas of PageRank — links from important pages count more, and a page's vote is divided among its outgoing links.
