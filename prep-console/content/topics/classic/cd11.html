You are standing outside a train station in a city you have never visited. You open a maps app and type "coffee". In under a second you see a map of the streets around you, a dozen pins for cafés within walking distance sorted by how close and how good they are, and when you tap one, a blue line through the streets with "7 min" beside it. Then you change your mind, ask for a route to a museum across town by car, and the app says 23 minutes, warns of a slowdown on the ring road, and picks a different way than the shortest one.

That one interaction touches three very different systems. The first answers what is near this point? over hundreds of millions of places, and it must do it without scanning all of them. The second draws the map: the whole planet at every zoom level, from the outline of continents to individual building footprints, delivered to a billion phones fast enough that panning feels instant. The third answers how do I get there, and how long will it take? over a road network with hundreds of millions of road junctions, where the cost of each road changes minute by minute as traffic builds and clears.

Each of these breaks the obvious approach. Storing places in a database with latitude and longitude columns and asking for rows "between" two values in each direction forces a scan of a band around the whole planet. Drawing the map on a server for each request would melt any data centre. Running the textbook shortest-path algorithm on a continent takes seconds per query and would need thousands of machines. And a route that ignores traffic, or an arrival time computed from speed limits, is wrong exactly when people care most.

This lesson works through the design in interview order: requirements; estimates done by hand (search queries per second, tile counts and bandwidth, routing load, graph size); the API and data model; the high-level design; then three deep dives. The first is proximity search: geohash precision versus cell size worked by hand (building on the cell lookup taught in cd5), then a quadtree that adapts to density, with split and radius search traced step by step. The second is the tile pyramid: how many tiles each zoom level has, what they cost to store and serve, and why vector tiles changed the maths. The third is routing: Dijkstra, then A*, then contraction hierarchies, and how live traffic becomes an ETA. The real case is the original contraction-hierarchies paper and Google's published ETA model; the lab builds a point quadtree with radius search and checks it against brute force.

The mental model: a library, an atlas and a taxi dispatcher

Think of three old professions. A librarian who files every shop in town on index cards by neighbourhood can answer "cafés near the station" by pulling the station's drawer and the drawers next to it, instead of reading every card. An atlas printer does not print one enormous map; they print a world page, then continent pages, then country pages, then city pages, each page the same size but covering less ground in more detail. You turn to the page you need. A taxi dispatcher who has driven the city for twenty years does not consider every side street for a cross-town trip: they know you get onto the main road, take the ring road, and only think about small streets at the very start and end. They also listen to the radio for jams.

Those three map directly onto the design. The librarian's drawers are cells — geohash rectangles or quadtree squares — that let a proximity query read a handful of buckets instead of the whole table. The atlas pages are tiles: square images or vector data files, one tile at zoom level 0 showing the whole world, four at zoom 1, sixteen at zoom 2, each level splitting every tile into four. The dispatcher's knowledge of which roads matter is what contraction hierarchies precompute: an ordering of road junctions by importance plus shortcut edges, so a route search climbs quickly from local streets to the motorway network and never wanders through every alley. The radio is live traffic: speeds reported by phones on the road, which change the cost of each road segment.

Where the analogies break is the useful part. The librarian's drawers have fixed borders, so a café across the street from the station can be filed in a different drawer — you must always check neighbouring drawers. Real density is wildly uneven: one drawer for central Tokyo would overflow while a drawer for the Sahara holds nothing, which is why adaptive structures like quadtrees exist. And the dispatcher's intuition is fuzzy, while a routing engine must return the provably shortest path (by its cost model) every time.

Three systems with different shapes

The three systems differ in nearly every property, and seeing that early saves a lot of confused design. Places search is a read-heavy index over a few hundred million small records that change slowly (a shop opens, a phone number changes); queries must return in tens of milliseconds and are dominated by "near this point" plus filters. Tiles are a huge, slowly changing set of static files: the hard part is generating them and the cost of serving bytes, and nearly all serving happens at CDN edges, not in our data centre. Routing is compute-heavy: a fixed graph that fits in one machine's memory, queried thousands of times per second, whose weights change every minute from a stream of location reports. Each gets its own storage, scaling strategy and failure modes.

The shape of the hour

In a 60-minute interview you will not build all three in depth. Name all three in the requirements, size them in the estimates, draw them in the high-level design, then let the interviewer pick, or pick yourself: proximity search is the most common focus ("design Yelp's nearby search"), routing and ETA the most impressive if you can explain it. 1 · Requirements

Functional. (1) Show the map: pan and zoom anywhere on Earth. (2) Search nearby places: "restaurants within 1 km", with filters (category, open now, rating) and ranking. (3) Get directions between two points for driving (walking and transit are variations), with alternatives. (4) Show an ETA that accounts for live traffic, and update it during navigation. (5) Place details: name, address, hours, photos, reviews. Out of scope, said aloud: creating the base map data (surveying, satellite imagery processing), public transit timetables, offline maps, and the business side of listings.

Non-functional. Nearby search in under about 100 ms at the 99th percentile (p99 — 99% of requests are faster). Tiles must appear instantly when panning, which means they come from a CDN edge near the user. Routes within about a second, even for cross-country trips. ETAs should be accurate — say, within 10% for most trips — and traffic fresh within a minute or two. High availability for reading the map and searching; it is acceptable for traffic to degrade to historical speeds if the live pipeline fails. Location data from users must be anonymised and minimised.

Clarifying questions. Is this primarily proximity search (Yelp-style) or the full maps product? How many places and users? Do places change often (a static directory versus moving objects like drivers, which cd5 covers)? Is routing for cars only? These answers decide which deep dive the hour will spend on.

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

State round assumptions. 100 million daily active users (DAU). 200 million places worldwide. Each user does 5 nearby searches, views about 100 tiles and requests 1 route per day. A day is 86,400 seconds, call it 100,000 for easy maths when a rough answer is enough, and peak traffic is about 3× the average.

Search QPS. 100M × 5 = 500 million searches per day. 500,000,000 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 5,800 queries per second (QPS) on average, about 17,000 per second at peak. A single in-memory index server can handle a few thousand such queries per second, so this is a handful of replicas per region — the load is not the hard part; the index design is.

Places storage. A full place record (name, address, category, hours, phone, rating, a few attributes) is about 1 KB: 200M × 1 KB = 200 GB, a normal sharded database. The geo index needs much less: per place an 8-byte id, two 4-byte coordinates (latitude and longitude stored as integers in units of 10-7 degrees, about 1 cm precision), a category code and a ranking score — say 32 bytes. 200M × 32 B = 6.4 GB. The whole world's index fits in one server's RAM, which is a decisive fact: replicate it rather than shard it, unless memory or rebuild time push otherwise.

Tile traffic. 100M × 100 = 10 billion tile requests per day ≈ 116,000 per second average, about 350,000 at peak. A compressed vector tile averages, say, 20 KB: 116,000 × 20 KB = 2.3 GB/s ≈ 19 Gbit/s average, ~56 Gbit/s at peak. That is a CDN-sized number. With a 95% hit ratio the origin sees 5% of requests: about 5,800 per second and 1 Gbit/s — manageable. Every point of hit ratio lost adds about 1,160 origin requests per second, which is why tiles are designed to be cacheable.

Tile count. Zoom level z has 4z tiles. The whole pyramid from zoom 0 to 18 is 1 + 4 + 16 + … + 418 = (419 − 1) ÷ 3 ≈ 91.6 billion tiles. At 20 KB each that would be about 1.8 PB — but about 71% of Earth is ocean and most land is empty at street scale, so most of those tiles are identical "blank" tiles. Vector tiles stop at zoom 14: (415 − 1) ÷ 3 ≈ 358 million tiles, at most about 7 TB, much of it deduplicated empties. The deep dive explains why stopping at 14 works.

Routing QPS. 100M × 1 = 100 million routes per day ≈ 1,160 per second, about 3,500 at peak. Navigation adds re-routing checks; assume they double it to 7,000 per second at peak.

Routing graph size. The Western Europe road network used in the contraction-hierarchies paper had about 18 million nodes (junctions and shape points that matter for routing) and 42 million directed edges. Assume the world routing graph is about 10× that: 200 million nodes, 500 million edges. At 16 bytes per node (coordinates plus an offset into the edge array) and 12 bytes per edge (target, weight, flags): 200M × 16 + 500M × 12 = 3.2 GB + 6 GB ≈ 9-10 GB, and CH shortcuts add roughly the same order again — so a world graph fits in a 64 GB machine. Again: replicate rather than shard, though in practice you partition by continent for blast radius and rebuild speed.

Routing compute. The CH paper measured about 0.16-0.22 ms per query on one core. Call it 0.5 ms with path unpacking and overheads: one core does about 2,000 routes per second, so 7,000 per second at peak is 4 cores. Plain Dijkstra on the same graph settles millions of nodes per long query and takes on the order of seconds; at 7,000 queries per second that would be thousands of cores. Preprocessing is worth a factor of thousands.

Traffic probes. Suppose 10 million phones are navigating at peak, each sending a location every 5 seconds: 2 million location reports per second, each about 50 bytes — 100 MB/s into a stream pipeline. This is the heaviest write load in the whole system, and it is where cd5's location-ingestion lessons apply.

Coordinates are stored as integers (degrees × 107) to avoid floating-point surprises and halve storage. Places and the road graph are separate datasets with different update rates: a place edit is a row update plus an index patch; a road edit triggers a graph rebuild and tile regeneration for the affected area. Live speeds and historical profiles are keyed by the same edge id that the routing graph uses, so applying traffic is an array update.

5 · High-level design and the critical path

Walk through a "coffee near me, then route to it" request. (1) The app is already showing the map: it computed which tiles cover the screen at the current zoom and fetched them from the CDN, most served from the edge's cache. (2) The search request goes through a load balancer to a search service in the nearest region. It computes the cells covering a 1 km circle around the user, reads candidates from the in-memory geo index, filters by category and opening hours, computes true distances, ranks, and fetches display details for the top 20 from a cache in front of the places database. (3) The user taps a café; the app sends a route request. The route service snaps both points to the nearest road edges, runs a contraction-hierarchies query on the in-memory graph whose weights include current traffic, unpacks shortcuts into a full path, computes the ETA, and returns a compressed polyline. (4) During navigation the app reports progress every few seconds; the server checks whether the user left the route or whether a much faster route has appeared.

Behind the request path sit three offline or streaming pipelines: the tile generator (map data → vector tiles per zoom → object store, by area when data changes); the graph builder (road data → routing graph → node ordering and shortcuts, daily or on edits); and the traffic pipeline (probes → map matching → per-segment speeds every minute → route service weights).

6 · Deep dive A: proximity search, from geohash precision to a quadtree

Why a plain index fails. A table with lat and lng columns and a composite index on (lat, lng) can answer WHERE lat BETWEEN a AND b AND lng BETWEEN c AND d, but the B+ tree (db6) sorts by latitude first: it can jump to the latitude band, but then must scan every row in that band around the whole planet to check longitude. A 1 km band of latitude through Europe contains places from Lisbon to Vladivostok. The fix, as cd5 taught, is to turn two dimensions into one key — a cell — so that nearby points mostly share a key and one index lookup returns them.

Geohash precision versus cell size, by hand. A geohash interleaves longitude and latitude bits (longitude first) and writes them five bits per base-32 character. Precision n gives 5n bits; longitude gets the extra bit when 5n is odd. So precision 6 is 30 bits: 15 for longitude and 15 for latitude. Longitude spans 360°, so a cell is 360 ÷ 215 = 360 ÷ 32,768 = 0.01099° wide; at the equator one degree of longitude is about 111.3 km, so the cell is 1.22 km wide. Latitude spans 180°: 180 ÷ 32,768 = 0.00549°, × 110.6 km ≈ 0.61 km tall. Precision 5 is 25 bits (13 longitude, 12 latitude): 360 ÷ 8,192 × 111.3 ≈ 4.89 km by 180 ÷ 4,096 × 110.6 ≈ 4.86 km. Precision 7 is 35 bits (18 and 17): about 153 m by 152 m.

The last column matters: lines of longitude converge toward the poles, so a cell's east-west width in kilometres is the equator width times the cosine of the latitude. At 60° north (Oslo, Helsinki) cos 60° = 0.5, and a precision-6 cell is only about 0.61 km wide.

Choosing precision for a radius. The rule: pick the longest precision whose cell is at least the search radius in both dimensions, then search the user's cell and its 8 neighbours. Why this works: the user is somewhere inside the centre cell; a circle of radius r around them extends at most r in every direction, so if each cell is at least r across, the circle cannot reach beyond the 3×3 block. For r = 500 m at the equator, precision 6 (1.22 × 0.61 km) qualifies; precision 7 (153 m) is too small. The 3×3 block covers 3.66 km × 1.83 km ≈ 6.7 km², while the circle is π × 0.5² ≈ 0.79 km², so we read about 8.5 times more area than we need, and filter by true distance. For r = 2 km, precision 6 fails (0.61 km tall), so use precision 5: the 3×3 block is 14.7 × 14.6 km ≈ 214 km² for a 12.6 km² circle — 17 times the area. That waste is the cost of fixed cells and is why some systems cover the circle with a mix of cell sizes (S2's region coverer does exactly this) instead of one 3×3 block.

The density problem. Now the numbers that break fixed cells. Central Manhattan has thousands of restaurants per square kilometre; a 3×3 block of precision-6 cells (6.7 km²) might hold 20,000 places, every one of which must be distance-checked and ranked to return the top 20. Rural Mongolia might have zero places in a 3×3 block of precision-5 cells. A fixed grid gives the wrong cell size almost everywhere. We want cells that are small where places are dense and large where they are sparse.

The quadtree. A quadtree starts with one square covering the whole area. Each node has a capacity, say 100 points. Insert points into the node; when it would exceed capacity, split it into four equal child squares (north-west, north-east, south-west, south-east), move each point into the child that contains it, and from then on the node is internal and only its children hold points. Children split in turn when they overflow. The result is a tree whose depth follows density: in Manhattan, leaves are a few tens of metres across; over the Pacific, the root's children are leaves holding nothing.

A split traced by hand. Take an 8 km × 8 km area with coordinates from (0, 0) to (8, 8) and a capacity of 4. Insert five cafés at (1, 1), (2, 6), (5, 5), (6, 7) and (7, 6). The first four fit in the root. The fifth overflows it, so the root splits at its centre (4, 4) into four 4 km squares: SW (0-4, 0-4) gets (1, 1); NW (0-4, 4-8) gets (2, 6); NE (4-8, 4-8) gets (5, 5), (6, 7) and (7, 6); SE (4-8, 0-4) is empty. Insert two more at (5.5, 6.5) and (6.5, 5.5): NE now has five, so it splits at its centre (6, 6) into 2 km squares. Two points sit exactly on the split lines, so we need a boundary rule: "x < mid goes west, otherwise east; y < mid goes south, otherwise north". Then the new SW (4-6, 4-6) gets (5, 5); NW (4-6, 6-8) gets (5.5, 6.5); NE (6-8, 6-8) gets (6, 7) and (7, 6); SE (6-8, 4-6) gets (6.5, 5.5). Boundary rules are a real source of bugs: decide them once and use the same rule for insert and search.

Radius search, step by step. To find everything within r of a query point q: start at the root. For each node, compute the minimum distance from q to the node's box: clamp q's x into [box.xmin, box.xmax] and q's y into [box.ymin, box.ymax], and measure the distance from q to that clamped point (zero if q is inside). If that minimum distance is greater than r, nothing inside can qualify — prune the whole subtree. Otherwise, if the node is internal, recurse into its children; if it is a leaf, test each point's true distance. With the query at (5.7, 5.7) and r = 1 km in the example above: the root contains q (distance 0) → visit children. SW box (0-4, 0-4): clamp gives (4, 4), distance √(1.7² + 1.7²) = 2.4 km > 1 → prune. NW (0-4, 4-8): clamp (4, 5.7), distance 1.7 > 1 → prune. SE (4-8, 0-4): clamp (5.7, 4), distance 1.7 → prune. NE contains q → recurse. Its four 2 km children all lie within 1 km of (5.7, 5.7) because q is 0.3 km from the split point (6, 6), so all four are visited and their five points checked. We read 5 of 7 points; on real data with millions of points the same pruning reads a few hundred.

The cost. Building is O(n log n) in practice. A radius query costs the depth of the tree (about log₄ of n ÷ capacity — for 200 million places at capacity 100, log₄ 2,000,000 ≈ 10.5 levels in a balanced tree; real trees are deeper in cities) plus the leaves touched times capacity. Memory is the points (6.4 GB at 32 bytes each) plus about 2 million leaves and 0.7 million internal nodes — tens of megabytes. Updates are cheap (insert into a leaf, split if needed), but deletes can leave many near-empty leaves, so production systems rebuild the tree periodically (say nightly, which takes minutes) and apply the day's edits as a small overlay.

Ranking. Distance alone is a poor ranking: a 4.8-star café 600 m away usually beats a 3.1-star one at 400 m. A typical first version: score = w₁ × relevance to the query + w₂ × rating (shrunk toward the average when there are few reviews) + w₃ × popularity − w₄ × distance, with open-now as a filter. Later versions learn the weights from what users tap, which is a learning-to-rank problem; the index's job is to deliver a few hundred good candidates fast.

Real distance. The final filter needs true distance on a sphere. The haversine formula gives it: with latitudes φ₁, φ₂, longitudes λ₁, λ₂ in radians and Earth radius R ≈ 6,371 km, d = 2R · asin(√(sin²(Δφ/2) + cos φ₁ · cos φ₂ · sin²(Δλ/2))). For short distances an approximation is fine and cheaper: treat a degree of latitude as 111.2 km and a degree of longitude as 111.2 × cos(latitude) km, then use Pythagoras. In the example above, internally convert to metres around the query point first, then all box tests are plain 2-D arithmetic.

Quadtree versus geohash versus S2. A geohash is a fixed-grid quadtree written as a string — prefix length is depth — and it lives happily in any sorted key-value store; its weakness is uniform cell size. A point quadtree adapts to density but is a custom in-memory structure. Google's S2 combines both: it projects the sphere onto the six faces of a cube, subdivides each face as a quadtree to 30 levels, and numbers cells along a Hilbert curve into 64-bit ids. S2 documents the sizes: a level-13 cell averages 1.27 km², a level-16 cell about 19,800 m², a level-30 leaf under 1 cm². Because the ids are integers in Hilbert order, a region (a circle, a polygon) is covered by a list of id ranges of mixed levels, each one a fast range scan in an ordered index — adaptive coverage without a custom tree.

7 · Deep dive B: the tile pyramid

Projection. A map is flat; the Earth is not. Web maps use Web Mercator, which maps longitude linearly to x and stretches latitude so that shapes look right locally. The stretch grows without bound toward the poles, so the map is cut at about ±85.05° latitude, the value that makes the world an exact square. That square is the zoom-0 tile.

The pyramid. At zoom 0 the whole world is one 256 × 256-pixel tile. At zoom 1 it is 2 × 2 tiles, at zoom 2 4 × 4, and at zoom z it is 2z × 2z = 4z tiles. Each tile is named by (z, x, y), with x counting from the west edge (longitude −180°) and y from the north edge. Each zoom level halves the ground distance a pixel covers: at the equator zoom 0 is about 156,543 m per pixel (Earth's circumference, 40,075 km, divided by 256), zoom 10 about 153 m, zoom 14 about 9.6 m and zoom 18 about 0.6 m — enough to see individual parked cars. Away from the equator, multiply by the cosine of the latitude.

From coordinates to a tile, by hand. Which tile contains London (51.5074° N, 0.1278° W) at zoom 12? With n = 212 = 4,096: x = (lng + 180) ÷ 360 × n = (179.8722 ÷ 360) × 4,096 = 2,046.5, so x = 2,046. For y, Web Mercator uses y = (1 − ln(tan φ + sec φ) ÷ π) ÷ 2 × n; with φ = 51.5074°, tan φ = 1.2575 and sec φ = 1.6066, so ln(2.8641) = 1.0523; 1 − 1.0523 ÷ π = 0.6651; ÷ 2 × 4,096 = 1,362. The tile is 12/2046/1362, and at that latitude each pixel covers 38.2 × cos 51.5° ≈ 23.8 m. A phone screen of about 1,080 × 2,000 pixels at 256-pixel tiles shows about 5 × 8 tiles; the app adds a ring of neighbours for smooth panning, which is where "about 100 tiles per user per day" comes from.

Raster versus vector tiles. A raster tile is a picture (PNG or JPEG): simple, but every style, language, and screen density (a high-DPI phone wants 512-pixel images) needs its own copy, and zoom 18 needs its own set of 68.7 billion. A vector tile stores the geometry (road lines, building polygons, label points) and attributes in a compact binary format (the Mapbox Vector Tile spec uses protocol buffers and tile-local integer coordinates). The device draws it with the GPU. One tile set serves every style and language; rotation and tilt are free; and because geometry scales, a zoom-14 tile can be drawn at zoom 16 or 18 by overzooming — enlarging the same geometry — as long as zoom 14 stored enough detail. That single trick cuts the pyramid from 91.6 billion tiles to 358 million.

Generating and updating tiles. Tile generation is a batch job partitioned by area: for each tile, clip the source data to its bounds (plus a small buffer so lines crossing the edge join seamlessly), simplify geometry to the zoom's pixel size (no point storing a coastline wiggle smaller than a pixel), drop features too small to show, encode, and write to object storage under a versioned key. When a road is edited, regenerate only the tiles that intersect the edit at each zoom — at zoom 14 that is usually one or two tiles; at zoom 0 it is the single world tile (whose content may not change at all). Many empty tiles (open ocean) are byte-identical; store one copy and point to it, or have the server return a shared "empty" response.

Serving. Tiles are the ideal CDN object: immutable under a versioned URL, small, and popular in a heavy-tailed way (city centres at low zooms are requested constantly; a forest at zoom 14 rarely). With a 95% edge hit ratio our origin handles about 5,800 requests per second; most misses are long-tail, high-zoom tiles. Egress is the real cost: 10 billion tiles a day at 20 KB is 200 TB per day. Cutting the average tile size by 20% through better simplification saves 40 TB per day — tile size is a cost metric someone owns.

8 · Deep dive C: routing and ETA

The graph. Model the road network as a directed graph: nodes are junctions (and dead ends), edges are road segments between them, one per direction of travel (a one-way street has one edge). Each edge has a weight — the cost to traverse it. For "fastest route" the weight is travel time: length ÷ expected speed. A 600 m street at 30 km/h is 600 ÷ 8.33 m/s = 72 seconds. Turn restrictions (no left turn) and turn costs are modelled by expanding junctions into small subgraphs or by putting costs on edge pairs; we ignore them here and return to them in the deep section.

Dijkstra's algorithm. Keep a tentative distance for each node, starting at 0 for the source and infinity elsewhere, and a priority queue ordered by tentative distance. Repeatedly pop the node with the smallest distance — it is now settled, its distance final — and relax each outgoing edge: if distance(u) + weight(u, v) is less than v's tentative distance, lower it and push v. Stop when the target is settled. Trace on a tiny graph: S→A 4 min, S→B 2, B→A 1, A→T 3, B→T 7. Pop S (0): A = 4, B = 2. Pop B (2): A = min(4, 2 + 1) = 3, T = 9. Pop A (3): T = min(9, 3 + 3) = 6. Pop T (6): done, path S→B→A→T, 6 minutes. Correctness comes from non-negative weights: when a node is popped, no unexplored path can be shorter, because every such path would already cost at least as much as the popped distance.

Why it is too slow at scale. Dijkstra settles nodes in rings of increasing distance from the source. For a 300 km trip it settles every node within 300 km of travel time — in Europe, millions of nodes — and spends most of its time in villages behind the source and in the opposite direction. On a continent-sized graph that is on the order of seconds per query with a binary heap.

A* search. A* changes only the priority: instead of ordering by distance-so-far g(v), order by g(v) + h(v), where h(v) is an estimate of the remaining cost from v to the target. If h never overestimates (it is admissible), A* still returns the shortest path, and it explores far fewer nodes in the wrong direction. For travel time, the natural admissible h is straight-line distance to the target divided by the highest speed anywhere in the graph (say 130 km/h). The weakness: in a city, where actual speeds are 25 km/h, that h underestimates by a factor of five, so the search is only weakly guided. Better admissible bounds exist (landmarks: precompute distances to a few dozen landmark nodes and use the triangle inequality), but the large speed-ups come from preprocessing.

Bidirectional search. Run Dijkstra forward from the source and backward from the target (on reversed edges), alternating, and stop once the smallest tentative distances in the two queues sum to at least the best meeting path found. Two balls of radius d/2 contain roughly half the nodes of one ball of radius d on a road network, so it is about a 2× win alone — but it is the frame that contraction hierarchies build on.

Contraction hierarchies. The insight is the taxi dispatcher's: long routes go through a small set of important roads. CH makes that exact. Preprocessing: give every node an importance order. Then contract nodes from least to most important. To contract node v, remove it; for each pair of neighbours u (with an edge into v) and w (with an edge out of v) that remain, check whether the path u→v→w is the only shortest path from u to w. If it is, add a shortcut edge u→w whose weight is weight(u, v) + weight(v, w), remembering v as its middle node. If some other path from u to w avoiding v is no longer (a witness), no shortcut is needed. The check is a small local Dijkstra from u, limited in hops — the witness search.

A worked contraction: node B is a minor junction with a road from A (4 min) and a road to C (3 min). There is also a back road A→X→C costing 9 min. Contract B: the path A→B→C costs 7; the witness search from A to C without B finds only 9 > 7, so add shortcut A→C = 7 (via B). If the back road had cost 6, it would be a witness and no shortcut would be added. The paper's ordering heuristic is driven by the edge difference: shortcuts added minus edges removed when contracting v. Contracting a dead-end street removes edges and adds none, so it goes early; contracting a motorway junction would add many shortcuts, so it goes late. Remarkably, for road networks with travel-time weights the graph stays sparse throughout.

Querying: split the contracted graph's edges into "upward" edges (to a more important node) and "downward" edges. Run a bidirectional Dijkstra where the forward search from the source uses only upward edges and the backward search from the target uses only upward edges of the reversed graph. The paper proves the two searches meet at the most important node on the shortest path: any shortest path, viewed through its shortcuts, first climbs and then descends in importance. Each search climbs a short cone of local streets onto arterials and motorways, then stops. The result is a path that contains shortcuts; to produce turn-by-turn directions, recursively replace each shortcut by its two halves (via the stored middle node) until only original edges remain.

The numbers from the paper. On the Western Europe graph (18,029,721 nodes, 42,199,587 directed edges), the "economical" variant needed about 545 s of node ordering plus 57 s of hierarchy construction, and answered random queries in 223 µs settling 459 nodes; the "aggressive" variant spent about 1,627 s ordering and 170 s constructing to get 159 µs and 368 settled nodes. The extra space was negative or near zero — the search structure needs less memory than the input graph, because each edge is stored only at its less important end. Unpacking a full path took about 0.3 ms. Compared with Dijkstra's millions of settled nodes, that is the factor of thousands the estimates promised.

Live traffic and ETA. Traffic starts as probe data: anonymised location reports from phones in navigation. A GPS fix is typically accurate to several metres in the open and much worse between tall buildings, so the first step is map matching: given a sequence of noisy points, find the sequence of road segments the vehicle most likely travelled (a hidden Markov model is the classic method — candidate segments near each point are the hidden states, and transitions favour routes whose road distance matches the straight-line distance between fixes). From matched traces, each segment gets observed traversal times. A stream job (sc18) aggregates per segment over one-minute windows, discards outliers (a delivery van stopping, a bus at a bus stop), and requires several distinct vehicles before publishing a live speed. Segments without enough recent data fall back to their historical profile: the typical speed for that hour of the week, built from months of data.

The ETA is the sum over route segments of the predicted time to traverse each one at the time you reach it. A worked example for a 4-segment trip starting at 17:30:

SegmentLengthReached atSpeed usedTime
1 · local street2 km+0 minlive 40 km/h3.0 min
2 · motorway10 km+3 minlive 100 km/h6.0 min
3 · ring road (jammed now)5 km+9 minblend: live 30, profile 60 → 40 km/h7.5 min
4 · city centre1.5 km+16.5 minprofile for 17:45, 25 km/h3.6 min
Total20.1 min

Segment 3 is the interesting one. Right now it is jammed at 30 km/h, but we will reach it in 9 minutes, and jams evolve. The blend weights live data heavily for segments reached soon and shifts toward the profile for segments reached later; at 9 minutes out, a weight of 2/3 on the live 30 km/h and 1/3 on the profile's 60 km/h gives 2/3 × 30 + 1/3 × 60 = 40 km/h. Using pure live speed would say 10 minutes for that segment; pure history would say 5. Getting this blend right is where machine learning earns its place: Google's production ETA model is a graph neural network over "supersegments" — groups of adjacent road segments that share significant traffic volume — trained on past trips to predict travel time from live and historical features. Google reported it reduced negative ETA outcomes by over 40% in cities like Sydney and improved real-time ETA accuracy by up to 50% in several cities compared with the previous production system.

Combining traffic with CH. CH preprocessing assumed fixed weights, and traffic changes weights every minute. The paper's key observation is that node ordering barely depends on weights — motorways are important for a sports car and a truck alike — so you keep the order and rerun only the hierarchy construction with new weights, which took 57-88 s for Western Europe in its experiments. Newer techniques separate structure even more cleanly: multi-level overlay methods (customisable route planning, and OSRM's multi-level Dijkstra) partition the graph into cells and recompute only the boundary-to-boundary distances inside cells whose weights changed, in seconds. OSRM recommends its multi-level pipeline by default for exactly this reason, keeping CH for cases like very large distance matrices. A pragmatic alternative: compute the route with periodically refreshed weights, then evaluate the ETA along it with the freshest per-segment speeds, and re-route during the trip if a materially faster path appears.

9 · Bottlenecks, failure modes and trade-offs

Hot cells. Cell-based partitioning puts a whole neighbourhood on one shard; a stadium or a festival makes that shard hot. Replicate the index rather than shard it (it fits in RAM), cache popular cell queries for tens of seconds, and pre-scale for known events.

Freshness versus cost. Places: a business that closed still appears until the next index patch — tolerable for minutes, not months. Tiles: a new road appears only after regeneration; a versioned rollout takes hours. Traffic: a crash shows up after map matching and aggregation, typically a minute or two; until then, routes send people into it. Each has its own freshness target, and the interview answer is to name them separately.

Cross-region routes. If each region has its own graph, a route from Paris to Berlin crosses partitions. Precompute an overlay of boundary nodes so long routes stitch through it, or keep whole continents on one machine, which the memory estimates allow.

Bad data. A missing one-way flag sends drivers the wrong way; a wrongly tagged closed road creates detours. Map data errors are the most common user-visible routing bugs, and the fix is process (validation rules, edit review, probe-based anomaly detection: if nobody ever drives a segment the graph says is open, flag it).

Graceful degradation. If the traffic pipeline fails, route on historical profiles and say ETAs may be less accurate; if the route service is overloaded, shed alternative-route computation first. If the places database is slow, serve from the index's denormalised fields without details.

Trade-offs to state at the close. Fixed cells (simple, storable in any ordered key-value store) versus quadtrees (adaptive, custom memory structure) versus S2 (adaptive covering with integer ids). Raster tiles (simple clients) versus vector tiles (one data set, client rendering, overzoom). CH (fastest queries, slower to absorb new weights) versus multi-level overlays (slightly slower queries, fast customisation). Live speeds (reactive, noisy) versus profiles (stable, blind to incidents), blended by time-to-reach.

k-nearest neighbours on a quadtree

"The 10 nearest cafés" has no radius, and guessing one is fragile: too small returns fewer than 10, too large reads too much. The exact method is a best-first search. Put the root in a priority queue keyed by its minimum distance to the query point. Repeatedly pop the closest entry: if it is a node, push its children (or, for a leaf, its points) with their distances; if it is a point, emit it. Stop after emitting k points. Because a node's key is a lower bound on any point inside it, a point is only emitted when nothing unexplored could be closer, so the result is exact and the search touches only the nodes near the answer.

Why S2 uses a Hilbert curve

Any 2-D-to-1-D mapping must break some neighbourhoods: points close on the map sometimes get far-apart ids. Geohash's Z-order curve jumps between distant parts of the square at every power-of-two boundary, so a small search region can map to many separate id ranges. The Hilbert curve is continuous — consecutive ids are always adjacent cells — so a compact region maps to fewer, longer ranges, which means fewer index seeks. Projecting onto a cube first keeps cells roughly equal in area (S2 level-13 cells range from 0.76 to 1.59 km²), whereas latitude/longitude rectangles shrink toward the poles.

Points versus shapes: when you need an R-tree

Quadtrees and cell indexes are natural for points. Roads, parks, and delivery zones are lines and polygons, and the question becomes "which shapes intersect this box" or "which zone contains this point". An R-tree groups shapes by their bounding boxes into a balanced tree of nested rectangles that may overlap; PostGIS indexes geometries this way (via GiST). Cell schemes can index shapes too, by covering each shape with a set of cells and storing it under every cell it touches, which is what S2-based systems do. The choice is about query type: point-in-polygon lookups at very high QPS (which delivery zone is this order in?) favour precomputed cell coverings.

Turn costs and restrictions

A route that makes an illegal left turn is wrong however fast it is. The clean model is an edge-based graph: make each road segment a node and each allowed turn an edge, with the turn's cost (a left turn across traffic might add 20 seconds) as its weight. This roughly triples the graph size, which is why production systems often keep the node-based graph and handle turns specially at junctions. Turn costs also break a naive CH, because the cost of passing through a junction depends on where you came from — contraction must operate on the edge-based model or carry turn tables.

Time-dependent routing

Weights that depend on the time you reach the edge (rush hour starts while you drive) make routing harder: a path that is shortest now may not be shortest when its later edges are evaluated at their arrival times. As long as the network is FIFO (first-in, first-out: leaving later never makes you arrive earlier), a time-dependent Dijkstra that evaluates each edge at its reach time stays correct. Preprocessing with time-dependent shortcuts is possible but storing a travel-time function per shortcut is expensive, so many systems use static preprocessing for the route and time-dependent evaluation for the ETA.

Alternative routes

Users want two or three sensible choices, not the shortest path plus near-copies of it. A common approach (the "via-node" method) considers paths s→v→t through candidate nodes v found in both CH search spaces, and accepts one only if it shares little with the best path (say under 80% overlap), is not much longer (say within 25%), and has no silly local detours. The CH search spaces from the main query provide the candidates almost for free.

Map matching is a decoding problem

With GPS error of tens of metres downtown, a point can lie closer to a parallel street than to the one actually driven. The hidden Markov model approach scores each candidate segment by its distance from the fix (emission probability) and each transition by how well the road distance between consecutive candidates matches the straight-line distance between fixes (transition probability), then picks the most likely sequence with the Viterbi algorithm. It is the same dynamic-programming idea used in speech recognition, and it is what turns noisy dots into segment speeds.

Location privacy is a design requirement

Probe data reveals where people live and work. Strip user identifiers at ingestion, rotate session ids frequently, drop the first and last few hundred metres of each trip (which point to home), aggregate before storing, and publish a segment speed only when several distinct vehicles contributed. These are not legal niceties: a speed computed from one car is also that car's location.

The real case: contraction hierarchies and Google's ETA model

The 2008 paper by Geisberger, Sanders, Schultes and Delling at Karlsruhe is short and readable, and its abstract states the whole idea: order nodes by importance, contract the least important node by replacing shortest paths through it with shortcuts, then answer queries with a bidirectional search where the forward search uses only edges to more important nodes and the backward search only edges from more important nodes. On a Western Europe network from the company PTV with 18,029,721 nodes and 42,199,587 directed edges (travel times derived from 13 road categories), its tuned variants answered random queries in 159-223 µs, five times faster than the best previous hierarchical technique, with negative space overhead. It also measured what matters for traffic: with the node order fixed, rebuilding the hierarchy for a different speed profile took 57-88 s. That experiment is the seed of the "structure versus weights" split every production router now uses.

Open-source routers show the lineage in practice. OSRM (the Open Source Routing Machine) implements both contraction hierarchies and multi-level Dijkstra, and its documentation recommends the multi-level pipeline by default — because it can absorb new weights quickly — while keeping CH for very large distance matrices, where CH's small search spaces win.

On the ETA side, Google's 2021 paper "ETA Prediction with Graph Neural Networks in Google Maps" describes a graph neural network deployed in production, operating on supersegments of adjacent road segments with shared traffic. The team describes what it took to make training stable, including MetaGradients to adapt the learning rate, and reports negative ETA outcomes reduced by over 40% in cities like Sydney compared with the previous production baseline. The accompanying DeepMind post adds that predictive ETAs were already accurate for over 97% of trips, and that the model improved real-time ETA accuracy by up to 50% in places like Berlin, Jakarta, São Paulo, Sydney, Tokyo and Washington D.C. The lesson for an interview: the routing algorithm and the ETA model are separate components, and the ETA is an ML system with its own data pipeline and evaluation.

Failure modes you will see

Ghost jams and phantom free-flow. A handful of probes on a quiet road, one of them a stopped delivery van, produce a fake jam; a real jam on a road with no probes stays invisible. Minimum-vehicle thresholds, outlier rejection and fallbacks to profiles are the defences; the symptom is a cluster of complaints about strange detours.

Rerouting herds. When everyone is told about the same jam, everyone takes the same side street, which then jams. Systems mitigate by spreading alternatives across users and by predicting the effect of their own advice — a feedback loop with no parallel in ordinary web services.

Stale tiles after edits. If tiles are cached under unversioned URLs with long TTLs, a corrected road keeps its old drawing for days. Versioned URLs make the rollout explicit and instant once clients switch.

Index rebuild failures. A nightly quadtree or cell-index rebuild that fails leaves yesterday's data in place — fine — but one that "succeeds" with a corrupt input (say, coordinates swapped latitude for longitude) puts every café in the ocean. Validate rebuilt indexes against a sample of known queries before swapping them in.

What to monitor

Search: p50/p99 latency, candidates scanned per query (a jump means cells are too large or an index regression), zero-result rate. Tiles: edge hit ratio, origin request rate, average tile bytes, egress per day. Routing: query latency and settled-node counts, customisation lag (age of weights in use), share of segments running on live versus profile speeds. ETA: error distribution on completed trips, especially the rate of large underestimates, broken down by city and time of day. Traffic pipeline: probe ingest rate, map-matching failure rate, end-to-end lag from probe to published speed.

Goal: build a point quadtree, run radius searches, and confirm against brute force that it returns exactly the same places while reading far fewer. Python 3.12, standard library only, a few seconds.

"""Point quadtree with split-on-overflow and radius search, checked against brute force."""
import math, random, time

class Node:
    __slots__ = ("x0", "y0", "x1", "y1", "pts", "kids")
    def __init__(self, x0, y0, x1, y1):
        self.x0, self.y0, self.x1, self.y1 = x0, y0, x1, y1
        self.pts, self.kids = [], None

CAP = 32

def insert(n, p):
    while n.kids:                                   # descend to the leaf containing p
        mx, my = (n.x0 + n.x1) / 2, (n.y0 + n.y1) / 2
        n = n.kids[(p[0] >= mx) + 2 * (p[1] >= my)]  # 0=SW 1=SE 2=NW 3=NE
    n.pts.append(p)
    if len(n.pts) > CAP and n.x1 - n.x0 > 1e-6:     # split on overflow
        mx, my = (n.x0 + n.x1) / 2, (n.y0 + n.y1) / 2
        n.kids = [Node(n.x0, n.y0, mx, my), Node(mx, n.y0, n.x1, my),
                  Node(n.x0, my, mx, n.y1), Node(mx, my, n.x1, n.y1)]
        pts, n.pts = n.pts, []
        for q in pts: insert(n, q)

def box_dist(n, x, y):                              # min distance from (x, y) to the box
    dx = max(n.x0 - x, 0, x - n.x1); dy = max(n.y0 - y, 0, y - n.y1)
    return math.hypot(dx, dy)

def radius(n, x, y, r, out, stats):
    stats["nodes"] += 1
    if box_dist(n, x, y) > r: return                # prune the whole subtree
    if n.kids:
        for k in n.kids: radius(k, x, y, r, out, stats)
    else:
        stats["points"] += len(n.pts)
        out.extend(p for p in n.pts if math.hypot(p[0] - x, p[1] - y) <= r)

random.seed(1)
W = 100.0                                           # a 100 km x 100 km region
city = [(random.gauss(30, 2), random.gauss(70, 2)) for _ in range(150_000)]   # dense centre
rural = [(random.uniform(0, W), random.uniform(0, W)) for _ in range(50_000)]
places = [(min(max(x, 0), W - 1e-9), min(max(y, 0), W - 1e-9)) for x, y in city + rural]
root = Node(0, 0, W, W)
t = time.perf_counter()
for p in places: insert(root, p)
print(f"built {len(places):,} points in {time.perf_counter() - t:.2f}s")

for (x, y, r) in [(30, 70, 0.5), (30, 70, 2.0), (80, 20, 5.0)]:
    out, stats = [], {"nodes": 0, "points": 0}
    radius(root, x, y, r, out, stats)
    brute = [p for p in places if math.hypot(p[0] - x, p[1] - y) <= r]
    print(f"q=({x},{y}) r={r} km: {len(out)} found, brute {len(brute)}, "
          f"same={sorted(out) == sorted(brute)}, nodes visited {stats['nodes']}, "
          f"points checked {stats['points']:,} of {len(places):,}")
  1. Run it. Every query should print same=True: pruning by box distance never loses a valid answer, because a box's minimum distance is a lower bound for every point inside it.
  2. Compare "points checked" with 200,000. In the dense centre with r = 0.5 km, the tree reads a few thousand points to find a few hundred; in the rural query with r = 5 km it reads a few hundred. Brute force reads all 200,000 every time.
  3. Change CAP to 4 and to 512. Small capacity means a deeper tree (more nodes visited, fewer wasted point checks); large capacity means a shallow tree that checks many more points. The best value balances node overhead against per-point checks.
  4. Replace the quadtree with a fixed grid of 1 km cells (a dictionary keyed by (int(x), int(y))) and compare points checked for the dense query and the rural query: the grid wastes work in the city or in the countryside depending on the cell size you choose — the density problem from the lesson.
Work it out

You are designing "nearby restaurants" for a city app with 2 million places in one metro area of 50 km × 50 km. Two thirds of the places are in a 5 km × 5 km downtown. Queries use a 500 m radius and return the top 20. (a) What geohash precision would you choose, and how many candidates does a downtown query read? (b) What about a suburban one? (c) What do you change?

Answer

(a) For 500 m, precision 6 (about 1.2 km × 0.6 km at the equator; narrower east-west at higher latitudes) is the longest precision whose cell is at least the radius, so search the 3×3 block: about 3.7 × 1.8 km ≈ 6.7 km². Downtown density is 2,000,000 × 2/3 ÷ 25 km² ≈ 53,000 places per km², so the block holds about 6.7 × 53,000 ≈ 355,000 candidates — to return 20. Even at 50 ns per distance check that is about 18 ms of CPU per query before ranking, and it is a lot of memory traffic.

(b) The rest of the area holds 667,000 places over 2,475 km² ≈ 270 per km², so a suburban 3×3 block holds about 1,800 candidates — fine.

(c) The fixed grid is right for the suburbs and wrong downtown by two orders of magnitude. Options: (1) use a quadtree with a capacity of about 100, so downtown leaves become tens of metres across and a 500 m search touches a few hundred leaves of up to 100 points each — the circle's area holds about 0.79 × 53,000 ≈ 42,000 places, so you read a little more than that; (2) cover the circle with S2 cells of mixed levels to cut the 8.5× area waste of the 3×3 block; (3) most importantly, question the product: 42,000 places genuinely lie within 500 m downtown, so reading them all to rank 20 is wasteful. Shrink the radius adaptively (expand from 100 m until you have, say, 500 candidates), or pre-rank each leaf's places by quality so you scan only the top few per leaf. The general lesson: in dense areas, the cost is set by how many places are genuinely inside the radius, and the fix is adaptive radius plus per-cell pre-ranking, not a cleverer index alone.

Estimate it

Your vector tiles average 25 KB, users load 120 tiles per day, and you have 50 million DAU. The CDN charges $0.02 per GB of egress and your origin charges $0.09 per GB. The edge hit ratio is 92%. What is the daily egress bill, and what is a 1-point hit-ratio improvement worth?

Answer

Tiles per day: 50M × 120 = 6 billion. Bytes: 6 × 109 × 25 KB = 150 × 1012 B = 150 TB/day = 150,000 GB. All of it leaves the CDN to users: 150,000 × $0.02 = $3,000/day. Misses (8%) also cross from origin to CDN: 12,000 GB × $0.09 = $1,080/day. Total about $4,080/day, roughly $1.5M a year.

Each hit-ratio point moves 1% of traffic, 1,500 GB/day, off the origin: 1,500 × $0.09 = $135/day, about $49,000 a year, plus 60 million fewer origin requests per day (6 billion × 1%), about 2.5 million per hour. Cutting average tile size by 20% would save 20% of both lines, about $816/day — usually the bigger lever. The point to make out loud: for a tile service, bytes per tile and hit ratio are cost metrics with owners, not just performance metrics.
