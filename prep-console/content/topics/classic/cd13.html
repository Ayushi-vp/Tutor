A woman at a concert takes a photo on her phone, picks a filter, types a caption and taps Share. The venue's mobile network is overloaded; the upload stalls at 60%, drops, and resumes when she walks outside. Within a second of the upload finishing, the photo is in her followers' feeds as a crisp image on a large phone, a small thumbnail on her profile grid, and a medium image in a friend's browser in another continent. A year later, someone scrolls back to it and it still loads instantly. Meanwhile, the band she was watching posts their own photo of the crowd, and 40 million people see it within the hour.

The feed side of this — deciding which posts each person sees and pushing post ids into timelines — is the news-feed problem from cd3. This lesson is about the other half, which is where most of the bytes, the cost and the engineering live: getting a multi-megabyte file off an unreliable phone connection; turning it into the handful of sizes and formats that phones actually display; storing hundreds of terabytes of new images every day for years without paying for disk seeks you do not need; and delivering hundreds of thousands of images per second to phones all over the world, where the bill is measured in petabytes of egress.

Each of those breaks a naive design. Uploading through the application servers ties them up streaming bytes. Resizing on demand repeats the same CPU work millions of times. Storing each photo as a file in a normal file system costs several disk operations per read, which Facebook found to be the bottleneck of its first photo store. And a CDN, which seems to solve reads, still leaves a long tail of older photos that miss the cache and a stampede problem for the hottest ones.

This lesson follows the interview flow: requirements; estimates done by hand (upload rate and bandwidth, storage per photo and per year, image fetches per second, egress and its cost, cache size); API and data model; the high-level design with the upload and read paths; then three deep dives — uploads (pre-signed URLs, resumable and multipart uploads), the processing pipeline and the storage maths per photo, and delivery (CDN hit ratio, egress cost, and the hot celebrity post). The real case is Facebook's Haystack paper, which describes a photo store built for exactly this workload, and its successor f4 for photos that have gone cold. The lab is a calculator for storage and egress that you can point at your own assumptions.

The mental model: a photo lab with a mail-order catalogue

Think of an old-fashioned photo lab that serves a whole country by mail. Customers do not hand film to the shop owner at the counter; they drop it in a dedicated post box that goes straight to the lab's loading dock (the owner just gives them a numbered envelope). In the darkroom, every roll is developed once and printed at a fixed set of sizes — wallet, postcard, poster — because printing on demand for every request would keep the darkroom busy forever. Prints go into a warehouse organised for fast retrieval, not into a filing cabinet where every request means opening three drawers. And copies of popular prints are stocked in local shops in every town, so most customers never touch the warehouse at all.

The numbered envelope is a pre-signed URL: permission to deliver exactly one item to exactly one place, valid for a short time. The post box straight to the dock is the direct upload to object storage, bypassing the API servers. The darkroom is the asynchronous processing pipeline that generates derivatives — fixed sizes and formats. The warehouse is blob storage designed for one disk read per photo. The local shops are the CDN. And the shop owner at the counter is the metadata service: small records about who posted what, when, with which caption and which stored sizes.

The analogy breaks in instructive ways. A real lab ships each print once; a popular photo here is fetched millions of times, and each local shop has to fetch its first copy from the warehouse — if a thousand shops all ask at the same second, the warehouse is swamped. And mail is slow, so nobody minds waiting; here the user expects to see their own photo posted instantly, which is achieved by showing it from the phone's local copy while the lab catches up.

Two paths that share almost nothing

The most useful single observation in this design is that metadata and media have opposite shapes. Metadata — users, posts, follows, likes, comments, and the list of stored variants for each photo — is small (hundreds of bytes per record), changes constantly, needs transactions and indexes, and lives in sharded databases with caches in front. Media — the image bytes — is large (kilobytes to megabytes per object), never changes once written (an edit creates a new object), needs no queries beyond "get this key", and lives in object storage behind CDNs. Treat them separately and each becomes a well-understood problem; mix them (bytes in the database, or metadata queries against blob storage) and both suffer.

The hour, in order

In the interview, state the split in the first five minutes so the interviewer knows you will not spend the hour on feed ranking. Size the media path in the estimates — that is where the impressive numbers are. Then spend the deep dives on upload, processing and storage, and delivery, and say explicitly that fan-out follows cd3's hybrid design.

1 · Requirements

Functional. (1) Upload a photo (or a few in a carousel) with a caption; the upload survives flaky networks. (2) View a home feed of posts from accounts you follow, with images at the right size for the screen. (3) View a profile grid of thumbnails. (4) Like and comment. (5) Delete a post, after which it disappears for everyone. Out of scope, said aloud: video and stories (cd4 covers video delivery), direct messages (cd2), search, ads, and feed ranking (cd3).

Non-functional. Uploads are durable once acknowledged — never lose a photo. A post appears to its author immediately and to followers within seconds. Feed images load fast worldwide: the first image visible in well under a second on a mobile network, which means they come from a CDN edge near the user. High availability for reads; uploads may queue during incidents but must not be lost. Storage and delivery cost matter at this scale and are an explicit requirement. Location metadata in photos must not leak.

Clarifying questions. How many daily users and uploads? Do we keep originals at full resolution? Which screen sizes and formats must we serve? Is there a regional or legal requirement on where photos are stored? How fast must a deletion take effect everywhere?

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Assumptions, stated: 500 million daily active users (DAU), 100 million photo uploads per day, each user views about 100 images a day while scrolling, an original averages 3 MB (a modern phone JPEG or HEIC), and peak traffic is 3× average. A day is 86,400 seconds.

Upload rate. 100,000,000 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 1,160 uploads per second on average, about 3,500 per second at peak. For comparison, Facebook's Haystack paper reported one billion new photos a week in 2010 — about 1,650 per second on average — so these numbers are realistic for a large service.

Upload bandwidth. 3,500 per second × 3 MB = 10.5 GB/s ≈ 84 Gbit/s of ingest at peak. That is why the bytes must not pass through API servers: it would take a fleet of servers doing nothing but relaying bytes.

Derivative sizes. Choose a ladder of four widths, each a JPEG at a sensible quality: a 150 × 150 grid thumbnail (~10 KB), 320 px (~30 KB), 640 px (~80 KB) and 1,080 px (~200 KB). That is 320 KB per photo. Add WebP copies for clients that accept them; Google reports lossy WebP is 25-34% smaller than comparable JPEG, so call it 70% of the JPEG size: 224 KB. Total derivatives ≈ 544 KB per photo.

Storage per day and year. Derivatives: 100M × 544 KB = 54 TB per day, about 20 PB per year; with three full copies, 60 PB. Originals: 100M × 3 MB = 300 TB per day, about 110 PB per year — over five times the derivatives. With three copies, 330 PB a year; with erasure coding at 1.4× (explained in the deep dive), 153 PB. That single number forces a product question: do we keep originals at all?

Image fetches. 500M × 100 = 50 billion image fetches per day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 580,000 per second average, about 1.7 million per second at peak. (Haystack's 2010 paper reported over one million images per second at peak — the same order.)

Egress. Most fetches are the feed sizes; assume an average of 150 KB per fetch. 580,000 × 150 KB ≈ 87 GB/s ≈ 700 Gbit/s average, about 2 Tbit/s at peak; per day, 50 billion × 150 KB = 7.5 PB. At an assumed blended CDN price of $0.01 per GB, that is 7.5 million GB × $0.01 = $75,000 per day, about $27 million a year. Every 10% cut in bytes per image is worth about $2.7 million a year.

Origin load. With a 90% CDN hit ratio, origin storage serves 10%: about 58,000 requests per second and 750 TB per day. At 95%, half that. The Haystack paper reports that roughly 10% of photo requests from CDNs reached its storage layer, consistent with this assumption.

Processing compute. Decoding a 12-megapixel image and producing four resized JPEGs takes on the order of 100-200 ms of CPU; call it 0.2 core-seconds. At 3,500 uploads per second: about 700 cores at peak, before WebP and before any ML moderation. AVIF encoding is several times slower again, which is why it is usually done later and selectively.

Hot cache size. Most views are of recent posts. Two days of uploads at about 320 KB of JPEG derivatives each is 200M × 320 KB ≈ 64 TB — the working set the CDN as a whole must hold, split across edge locations by what each region views.

3 · The API

POST /v1/uploads           {content_type: "image/jpeg", size: 3145728, sha256: "…"}   Idempotency-Key
     → {upload_id, method: "PUT", url: "https://uploads.example.com/u/7f3a…?X-Signature=…&Expires=…",
        max_bytes, expires_in: 900}              (multipart: {part_urls[], part_size})
POST /v1/uploads/{upload_id}/complete   {parts: [{n, etag}]}        → {status: "processing"}
POST /v1/posts             {upload_ids: [...], caption}   Idempotency-Key  → {post_id, status: "processing"}
GET  /v1/posts/{post_id}   → {status, caption, images: [{w: 1080, url_jpeg, url_webp}, {w: 640, …}, …]}
GET  /v1/feed?cursor=…     → {posts: [... same image objects ...], next_cursor}
DELETE /v1/posts/{post_id}

The API never sees image bytes. Image URLs in responses point at the CDN and contain the immutable object key, for example https://img.example.net/p/8a1c…e2/1080.webp, so they can be cached for a year. The upload creation request carries the file's size and SHA-256 hash, which lets the server sign the URL for exactly that size and later verify that what arrived is what the client meant to send.

      created_at)                                        -- sharded by owner_id

blob store:  uploads/7f3a…        (original, private, lifecycle-deleted if never completed)
             p/8a1c…e2/1080.jpg   (derivatives, immutable, served via CDN)

The post id is time-ordered (a 64-bit id combining a timestamp, a shard number and a sequence, as cd3 discusses), so posts sort by time without a separate index and feeds can merge them cheaply. Storage keys are random and unguessable, and never reused: a re-crop or filter change creates a new media id. Deleting a post changes its status first (instant for every reader), and the blobs are removed afterwards.

5 · High-level design and the critical paths

Upload path. (1) The app compresses the photo on the device if it is larger than the maximum stored resolution — an easy 30-70% saving on upload bytes — and computes its hash. Many apps start this step as soon as the user picks a photo, overlapping the upload with the time the user spends writing a caption. (2) It calls POST /v1/uploads and receives a pre-signed URL. (3) It uploads directly to the uploads bucket, resuming if the connection drops. (4) It calls complete, then creates the post. The post is stored as processing, and a job is put on a durable queue. (5) The app shows the post to its author at once from the local copy (an optimistic UI). (6) Workers process the job (next deep dive) and set the post to ready, which triggers fan-out to followers' feeds (cd3).

Read path. (1) The app requests the feed from the API and gets post metadata with image URLs for each available size and format. (2) It picks the variant whose width is closest to the display width times the screen's pixel density (a 360-point-wide phone at 3× density wants 1,080 pixels). (3) It fetches from the CDN; an edge hit returns in tens of milliseconds. (4) An edge miss goes to a regional shield cache, and a shield miss to the media store. (5) The app prefetches the next few images while the user scrolls.

6 · Deep dive A: uploads — pre-signed URLs, multipart and resumable

Pre-signed URLs. A pre-signed URL is an ordinary URL to one object key with extra query parameters: the expiry time, the allowed HTTP method, and a signature computed with a secret key held by the server. The object store recomputes the signature when the request arrives and allows the operation only if it matches and has not expired. The phone never holds storage credentials; it holds permission to do one thing to one key for a short time. On Amazon S3, a URL signed with long-lived user credentials (Signature Version 4) can last up to 7 days, and one signed with temporary credentials dies when they do; for uploads, minutes are plenty. Tighten it further: a POST policy can restrict the content type and a content-length range, so the URL cannot be used to store a 5 GB file, and the key should be one the server chose, never one the client names.

Why not upload through the API? At 84 Gbit/s of peak ingest, relaying bytes through API servers would need a fleet just for that, would hold connections open for the seconds or minutes an upload takes on a slow network, and would double the bytes moved inside the data centre. Direct upload removes all three costs and lets the storage service, built for this, absorb the traffic.

Multipart uploads. A large object can be uploaded as numbered parts that the store assembles at the end. On S3, parts are 5 MiB to 5 GiB (the last part can be smaller), with up to 10,000 parts per upload. The flow is: initiate (get an upload id), upload parts in any order and in parallel (each returns an ETag, a checksum-like identifier), then complete with the list of part numbers and ETags. A failed part is retried alone. For a 3 MB photo, one PUT is simpler and multipart is unnecessary; for video or 50 MB RAW files, parts give parallelism and retry granularity. An incomplete multipart upload keeps its parts — and their storage cost — until aborted, so set a lifecycle rule that aborts uploads older than a day or so.

Resumable uploads. A phone on a train loses connectivity every few minutes. The tus protocol shows the general design: the client creates an upload (declaring Upload-Length), sends bytes with PATCH requests carrying Upload-Offset, and after a failure sends HEAD to learn the server's current offset and continues from there. A worked case: a 24 MB video upload reaches 15.7 MB when the connection drops. HEAD returns Upload-Offset: 15728640; the client sends PATCH with the remaining 9,437,184 bytes from that offset. Without resume, it would restart from zero — and on a network that drops every three minutes, a file that takes four minutes to upload would never finish.

Idempotency and deduplication. The app may retry "create post" after a timeout without knowing whether the first attempt succeeded. An idempotency key (cd9) on post creation makes the retry return the same post instead of creating a duplicate. The content hash enables deduplication: if the same bytes were uploaded before (a re-share, a retry after the upload actually completed), the server can skip the upload and reuse the stored object.

Validation after upload. Nothing the client sends is trusted until checked. The worker confirms the object's size and hash match what was declared, decodes it with a hardened image library (malformed images are a classic attack vector against decoders, so this runs in an isolated sandbox), rejects anything that is not an image, and enforces maximum dimensions.

7 · Deep dive B: the processing pipeline and storage maths per photo

The pipeline. Each upload becomes one job on a durable queue (sc10). A worker: (1) fetches the original; (2) validates and decodes it; (3) applies the orientation tag and converts colour space; (4) strips EXIF metadata, especially GPS coordinates, which otherwise reveal where photos were taken — often someone's home; (5) runs moderation and safety checks; (6) generates the ladder of sizes, each resized from the full image with a good filter and encoded at a tuned quality; (7) writes each derivative to the media store under a deterministic key derived from the media id, width and format; (8) records the variants and sets the post to ready. Because keys are deterministic, a retried job overwrites identical objects and is idempotent.

Storage maths per photo, worked. For one photo: JPEG derivatives 10 + 30 + 80 + 200 = 320 KB; WebP at 70% = 224 KB; derivatives total 544 KB; the original 3,000 KB. With originals kept, 3.54 MB per photo; without, 0.54 MB — 6.5 times less. Now multiply by redundancy. Three full copies (the classic choice for hot data) triple it. Erasure coding splits each object (or each large block of objects) into k data pieces and computes m parity pieces such that any k of the k + m pieces can rebuild the data. A 10 + 4 code stores 14 pieces for 10 pieces of data — 1.4× overhead — and survives the loss of any 4 pieces. The trade: reading a piece that lives on a failed disk requires reading k other pieces and decoding, so it suits data that is read rarely. A natural policy: derivatives of recent photos on replicated hot storage; originals and old derivatives on erasure-coded warm storage.

Applied to a year of 100 million photos a day: derivatives at 3× = 20 PB × 3 = 60 PB; originals at 1.4× = 110 PB × 1.4 = 153 PB. If originals were kept at 3×, 330 PB. The choice of redundancy scheme for originals is worth about 177 PB a year — a far bigger lever than any cache tweak.

Haystack: one disk read per photo. How the bytes are laid out on disk matters as much as how many copies exist. Facebook's first photo store kept each photo as a file on network-attached storage (NAS) appliances mounted over NFS, the Network File System; even after reducing directories to hundreds of files, reading a photo cost about three disk operations — one for directory metadata, one for the inode (the file system's per-file record), one for the data — and with thousands of files per directory it was often more than ten. Reads of the long tail of older photos, which miss the CDN, were throttled by these metadata seeks. Haystack fixed it by storing millions of photos inside one large file per volume (100 GB), each photo a needle: a header with the photo's 64-bit key, a 32-bit alternate key for the size variant, a random cookie, flags and size, then the data and a checksum. Each storage machine keeps, for each volume, an in-memory map from (key, alternate key) to offset and size. Facebook reported about 10 bytes of memory per photo on average (40 bytes for the four sizes of one image), against 536 bytes for one Linux XFS inode structure. A read is a memory lookup and a single disk read of the whole needle. Writes are appends; deletes set a flag; a background compaction copies live needles into a new file to reclaim space. The paper reports each usable terabyte cost 28% less and served 4× more reads per second than the NAS appliance it replaced.

Cold photos cost less. Most photos are viewed heavily for days and rarely afterwards. Facebook's f4 paper made this concrete: Haystack's scheme of three copies, each on RAID-6 disk arrays (disks grouped so data survives two disk failures, at about 20% parity overhead), gives an effective replication factor of 3 × 1.2 = 3.6. f4 moved warm data onto erasure-coded cells (Reed-Solomon codes within a data centre, and a second copy in another data centre) at 2.8, and then, using an XOR code across three data centres, to 2.1 — roughly 40% less raw storage for the bulk of the corpus, with lower throughput that warm data does not need.

8 · Deep dive C: delivery — hit ratio, egress cost and the hot celebrity post

Why the CDN works here. Derivatives are immutable files under unique URLs, served many times each, and popularity is heavily skewed toward recent photos and popular accounts. All three properties make CDN caching nearly ideal: set Cache-Control: public, max-age=31536000, immutable (one year, never revalidate), and an edge can serve each cached image until it is evicted for something more popular.

What the hit ratio buys. At 580,000 fetches per second, each percentage point of hit ratio is 5,800 origin requests per second and 75 TB a day of origin reads. Ways to raise it: (1) keep the number of distinct URLs per photo small — a fixed ladder of four widths and two formats, not arbitrary sizes requested by clients; (2) never put per-user parameters in image URLs; (3) add a tiered cache: hundreds of edge locations miss to a few regional origin shields, so the origin sees one miss per shield instead of one per edge; (4) normalise requests (format negotiation done in a consistent way so the cache key does not fragment).

What egress costs, and how to cut it. From the estimates: 7.5 PB a day at an assumed $0.01 per GB is $75,000 a day. The levers are bytes per image and the format mix. Serving WebP to the clients that accept it, at about 70% of JPEG's size for the same quality, cuts that line by up to 30% — about $22,000 a day if every client took it. Serving the right width matters as much: a 1,080-pixel image (200 KB) shown in a 320-pixel slot wastes 170 KB per view. Quality tuning per image (simple images compress well at lower quality settings without visible loss) adds a few more percent. Each is a cost project with a measurable payoff.

The hot celebrity read, worked. An account with 300 million followers posts. Suppose 30 million people view it in the first hour, each fetching one feed-size image: 30,000,000 ÷ 3,600 ≈ 8,300 requests per second for the same object, peaking far higher in the first minutes. The CDN as a whole handles this easily — once the object is cached at each edge. The danger is the first second. Suppose the CDN has 300 locations, each with 20 cache servers that do not share contents: without help, up to 6,000 servers miss at nearly the same moment and each requests the object from the origin. Three mechanisms remove the stampede. Request coalescing (sometimes called collapsed forwarding): a cache server that is already fetching an object holds other requests for it and answers them all from the one response. A shield tier: edges miss to a handful of regional shields, so the origin sees a few requests instead of thousands. Pre-warming: when an account above a follower threshold posts, push the new derivatives into the shields (or request them through each region) before fan-out makes the post visible. Facebook's Haystack paper describes the same instinct — it planned to push recently uploaded photos proactively into its internal cache, because recent photos are read soonest and most.

The metadata side of the same post. Like and comment counts for that post become hot keys in the metadata store; shard the counter (N sub-counters summed on read, as in-memory counters in in9) and cache the summed value for a second or two. The feed fan-out for 300 million followers is handled by cd3's hybrid: celebrity posts are pulled at read time rather than pushed into 300 million timelines.

9 · Bottlenecks, failure modes and trade-offs

Processing backlog. A surge of uploads (New Year's Eve) or a slow moderation model grows the queue; posts stay "processing" for minutes. Mitigate with autoscaled workers keyed on queue age, priority for small jobs, and degrading gracefully — publish once the JPEG ladder exists and add WebP or AVIF later.

Partial failures. A post marked ready before all variants are written produces broken images. Write all required variants first, then flip status, and make the client fall back to a neighbouring size if one fails to load.

Deletion everywhere. Metadata deletion is instant; cached bytes are not. Unguessable keys mean a deleted photo cannot be found by URL, CDN purge requests remove cached copies within minutes, and storage deletes follow. In an append-only store, the needle is flagged deleted at once and space is reclaimed later by compaction.

Region and residency. Some countries require user data to stay in-region. Store originals in the user's home region; derivatives may be cached globally only if policy allows.

Abuse. Uploads are an attack surface: malformed files against decoders (sandbox decoding), illegal content (hash matching and classifiers before public visibility), storage abuse (quotas per account, size limits in the signed policy).

Trade-offs to close on. Keep originals (future re-encoding, zoom) versus not (5-6× less storage). Pre-generate sizes (fast, predictable, more storage) versus resize on demand (less storage, CPU per miss, slower first view). Replication (fast reads, 3×) versus erasure coding (1.4×, slower degraded reads) by temperature. More formats (smaller bytes) versus cache fragmentation and encoding cost. Pre-warming (no stampede) versus pushing bytes that might not be viewed.

Choosing the size ladder from data

Four widths is not a law; it is the result of measuring what clients display. Log the rendered width and pixel density for each image view, compute the distribution, and choose ladder steps so that the bytes wasted (serving a larger image than displayed) plus the cost of each extra variant (storage, encoding, cache fragmentation) is minimised. Steps of roughly 2× in width (4× in pixels) are a reasonable start; adding a 1,440-pixel step for tablets is worthwhile only if enough views are on them.

Format negotiation without wrecking the cache

Browsers announce which formats they accept in the Accept header. If the CDN varies its cache by the full Accept header, every browser version's slightly different string becomes a separate cache entry and the hit ratio collapses. Instead, normalise at the edge: map the Accept header to one of a few classes ("avif", "webp", "jpeg") and use the class as the cache key, or put the format in the URL and let the app choose. Mobile apps know their own capabilities, so explicit format URLs are simplest there.

Why Haystack's URLs carry a cookie

If photo ids were sequential, anyone could enumerate them and download private or deleted photos straight from storage. Haystack stored a random cookie in each needle and required the same cookie in the URL; a request with the right photo id but wrong cookie fails. The general principle: blob URLs are bearer capabilities, so they must be unguessable (random 128-bit components) or signed with an expiry for private content.

Compaction and the cost of deletes in append-only stores

Append-only volumes make writes sequential and reads simple, but a delete only flags a needle; the bytes stay until compaction copies live needles into a new file and swaps it in. Compaction reads and writes whole volumes, so it competes with serving traffic and is scheduled when load is low. A burst of deletes (a large account removed) leaves volumes mostly garbage until compacted; capacity planning must include that slack.

Read-after-write for the author

The author expects to see their post instantly, but processing takes seconds and feed caches lag. The app shows the local image immediately; the API returns the author's own processing posts in their own profile and feed by reading from the primary rather than a lagging replica for a short window after they post. Followers see the post only once it is ready.

Deduplication by content hash, carefully

Identical bytes uploaded twice can share one stored object, with reference counting so deleting one post does not delete the other's image. But deduplicating across users leaks information: if uploading a file is instant because it already exists, an attacker can test whether someone else uploaded a specific file. Many systems therefore deduplicate only within one account, or always transfer the bytes and deduplicate silently on the server.

Perceptual hashes and safety

Cryptographic hashes change completely if one pixel changes, so they cannot find re-encoded copies of known abusive images. Perceptual hashes produce similar values for visually similar images, letting the pipeline match uploads against databases of known illegal content even after resizing or recompression. This step runs before a post becomes public, and its false-positive rate decides how many uploads need human review.

Private accounts and signed delivery URLs

Unguessable keys are enough for public posts, but a private account's photos should not be viewable by someone who was once sent a URL. The usual design is signed CDN URLs (or signed cookies) with a short expiry, issued by the API only to viewers allowed to see the post, and verified at the edge. The catch is caching: if the signature is part of the cache key, every viewer's URL is a different cache entry and the hit ratio collapses. Configure the CDN to verify the signature and then strip it from the cache key, so all authorised viewers share one cached object. Revoking access (the owner blocks a follower) then takes effect when that follower's signed URLs expire — minutes, not instantly — which is the trade-off to state.

Video changes the shape

Adding video multiplies everything: originals are hundreds of megabytes, processing means transcoding into a ladder of bitrates (cd4), uploads need multipart and resume for real, and delivery is segment-based streaming. The architecture — direct upload, async pipeline, immutable renditions, CDN — stays the same, which is the argument for building it cleanly for photos first.

The real case: Haystack and f4 at Facebook

The Haystack paper (Beaver, Kumar, Li, Sobel and Vajgel, OSDI 2010) describes Facebook's photo storage at a moment of rapid growth: over 260 billion images and 20 PB of data, users uploading one billion new photos (60 TB) each week, and over one million images served per second at peak. Each uploaded photo was stored in four sizes, and each size in three places, so every upload became twelve stored images. CDNs served the popular photos, but the paper's central observation was that the long tail of less popular photos still generated a large share of traffic that missed the CDN, and that the NFS-based store spent its disk operations on file-system metadata — directories and inodes — rather than on photo data.

Haystack's design had three parts. The Store held 100 GB volume files of needles on each machine, grouped into logical volumes replicated on three machines, with an in-memory index so a read is one disk operation. The Directory mapped logical volumes to physical ones, balanced writes and reads, and decided whether a photo URL should point at the CDN or at Facebook's own Cache. The Cache, a distributed hash table keyed by photo id, deliberately stored only photos requested directly by users (not via the CDN, where a miss was unlikely to hit again) and only from write-enabled machines, because recently uploaded photos are read most; it reported about an 80% hit rate for those. The results: about 10 bytes of memory per photo for the index, 4× more reads per second per terabyte and 28% lower cost per terabyte than the previous NAS design, and roughly 10% of all photo requests from CDNs reaching Haystack.

Four years later, the f4 paper (OSDI 2014) reported the next problem: Haystack was optimised for reads per second, not storage efficiency, and most BLOBs had become warm — rarely read. Haystack's three copies on RAID-6 meant an effective replication factor of 3.6. f4 used Reed-Solomon erasure coding within a data centre and a second copy elsewhere (2.8), then XOR coding across three data centres (2.1), and stored over 65 PB of logical data that way. The lesson for an interview: plan storage by temperature from day one, and expect to move data between tiers as it ages.

Failure modes you will see

Stuck uploads. A client starts an upload, the app is killed, and the multipart parts sit in the bucket forever. Lifecycle rules that abort incomplete uploads after a day, and metrics on incomplete uploads by age, catch it.

Broken images after a deploy. A new encoder setting produces images some old Android versions cannot decode. Roll out encoder changes gradually, keep JPEG as the fallback, and monitor client-side image decode errors by app version.

Origin overload after a CDN incident. When a CDN provider has an outage and traffic shifts to another, the new CDN starts cold and the origin sees a huge miss rate. Keep the shield tier sized for a cold start, or maintain warm standby with a second CDN for a share of traffic.

Location leaks. An EXIF-stripping step skipped for one format (say HEIC) exposes GPS coordinates in public images. Test the pipeline per format and scan stored derivatives for location tags.

What to monitor

Upload success rate and time-to-ready (p50/p99) by network type; queue age; worker errors by format; CDN hit ratio by size and region; origin requests per second and bytes; egress bytes per day and per image view; client image load time and decode errors; storage growth by tier; and moderation latency and backlog.

ORIGINAL_KB = 3000

    return jpeg + (jpeg * WEBP_RATIO if webp else 0) + (ORIGINAL_KB if keep_original else 0)

print("== storage per year (PB) ==")
for keep in (False, True):

views_s = DAU * VIEWS_PER_USER / 86400

for hit in (0.85, 0.90, 0.95, 0.98):

Estimate it

A photo app has 50 million uploads a day. Product wants to keep full-resolution originals (4 MB average) forever for a future "print" feature, and keep four JPEG derivatives totalling 300 KB. Storage costs $0.02 per GB-month on replicated hot storage (3 copies included in the price) and $0.006 per GB-month on erasure-coded cold storage. What does the first year's uploads cost per month at the end of the year under (a) everything hot, (b) originals cold, (c) no originals?

Answer

Per day: originals 50M × 4 MB = 200 TB; derivatives 50M × 300 KB = 15 TB. Per year: originals 73 PB = 73,000,000 GB; derivatives 5.5 PB = 5,475,000 GB.

(a) Everything hot: (73,000,000 + 5,475,000) × $0.02 = $1.57 million per month at the end of year one, growing each year.

(b) Originals cold: 73,000,000 × $0.006 = $438,000, plus derivatives hot 5,475,000 × $0.02 = $109,500: about $548,000 per month — a third of (a).

(c) No originals: about $110,000 per month.

The answer to give: the original-retention decision is worth over $1 million a month after one year and grows linearly every year after. If the print feature is speculative, keep originals cold (cost b), or keep a high-quality 2,048-pixel version instead of the full original (perhaps 600 KB, a sixth of the size). Present it as a product trade-off with numbers, not a technical preference.

Diagnose it

After launching a "share to story" feature that renders images at 720 pixels wide, the CDN hit ratio for images drops from 94% to 81%, origin CPU spikes, and storage grows faster than uploads. What happened?

Answer

The new feature requested a size not in the ladder, and the image service was configured to resize on demand for any requested width. Every story view of an older photo produced a new URL (the 720 width), which missed the CDN, triggered a resize at the origin (CPU spike), and stored the new variant (storage growth). Because each 720-pixel variant is new, the hit ratio for the whole image domain fell. Fixes: snap requested widths to the existing ladder (serve 1,080 or 640 and let the client scale), or add 720 to the ladder deliberately and backfill it in a background job rather than on the read path. Also cap on-demand resizing to an allow-list of widths, so a client bug cannot create unbounded variants — an easy denial-of-service vector.
