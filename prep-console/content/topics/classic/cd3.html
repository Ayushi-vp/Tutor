A user follows 400 accounts: friends, a few news sites, a football club, a pop star with 30 million followers. They open the app on the bus and expect, in under a second, the newest posts from all 400, already in order, with images loading. They will do this ten times today. Meanwhile, somewhere, the pop star posts a photo, and within seconds 30 million phones should be able to see it — while 30 million other people post about their lunch, each to a few hundred followers.

The obvious implementation — when someone opens the app, look up the 400 accounts they follow, fetch each one's recent posts, sort, and return the top 50 — is correct and works for a small product. At hundreds of millions of users it means every app open runs a 400-way query across the database, tens of thousands of times a second. Twitter called this "a massive select statement over all of Twitter" and reported that it was tried and died.

The opposite approach — when someone posts, immediately copy the post into a ready-made feed for each of their followers, so opening the app is one lookup — makes reads trivial and turns one celebrity post into 30 million writes. Both extremes fail, for opposite reasons, and the interesting part of this problem is designing the hybrid between them, then adding ranking without losing the latency budget.

This lesson follows the interview flow: requirements; estimates worked by hand (reads and posts per second, fan-out writes, timeline memory, storage and bandwidth); the API and data model; the high-level design; deep dives on fan-out on write versus read and the celebrity problem, on timeline storage and hydration, and on ranking; then failure modes and trade-offs. The real case is Twitter's own description of its timeline architecture, and the lab builds a skewed follow graph to measure how a few large accounts dominate fan-out cost.

The mental model: a newspaper delivered versus a newspaper collected

Imagine every account is a small newspaper and every user subscribes to some of them. There are two ways to get the news to readers. In the delivery model, every time a newspaper prints a story, it puts a copy through the letterbox of every subscriber. Reading is instant — your doormat already has everything, in order — but a newspaper with 30 million subscribers needs 30 million deliveries per story. In the collection model, each newspaper keeps its stories at its own office, and when you want to read, you visit every office you subscribe to and collect the latest stories. Printing is cheap, but every reading session means visiting 400 offices.

Fan-out on write is delivery; fan-out on read is collection. The hybrid is what common sense would do: small newspapers deliver, and for the handful of enormous ones you subscribe to, you pop into their office on the way — a few extra stops, not 400.

The analogy breaks in one useful way: what gets delivered is not the newspaper, it is a slip of paper with the story's number. When you read, you look up the full stories by number in a central library (the post store), which is how a story can be corrected or withdrawn after the slips went out — the library simply returns the new version or nothing.

The vocabulary

A user timeline is one author's own posts, newest first. A home timeline is the merge of the user timelines of everyone you follow. Fan-out is distributing one post to many followers. Hydration is turning a list of post ids into full posts (text, author name and avatar, media links, counts) by fetching them, usually in one batched multi-get from a cache. The social graph is the set of follow relationships, stored both ways: who I follow, and who follows me.

Three jobs, three different budgets

It helps to separate the system into three jobs from the start, because each has its own latency budget and failure tolerance. Publishing must be fast for the author and durable — once the author sees the post as sent, it must exist — but can hand everything else to the background. Distribution (fan-out) can take seconds and may fall behind under load without anything being lost, because the post is already stored. Reading must be fast and almost always available, even if some content is slightly stale or ranking is switched off. A design that keeps these three jobs decoupled degrades gracefully: a slow fan-out means late posts, not failed posts, and a slow ranker means a chronological feed, not an error page.

Why reads win the argument

Every design decision in a feed follows from the read-to-write ratio. People read feeds far more than they post: in Twitter's 2013 numbers, about 300,000 timeline read requests per second against about 6,000 write requests per second. When reads outnumber writes fifty to one, doing work once at write time to save it on every read is the right trade — until the write-time work for one post becomes millions of operations. That single sentence is the shape of the answer, and the rest of the hour is the numbers and mechanics behind it.

One more framing to carry through: the precomputed timeline is a cache, not data. The source of truth is the post store and the social graph. If a timeline is lost, stale or missing for an inactive user, it can be rebuilt from those two. That is what lets the timeline layer be in memory, capped, eventually consistent and aggressively trimmed.

1 · Requirements

Functional. Users can publish a post (text, optionally images or video); follow and unfollow other users; view a home timeline of posts from people they follow, newest first; view any user's profile timeline; like and reply, with counts shown. Out of scope, said aloud: direct messages, search, trends, ads, notifications and the internals of content moderation.

Non-functional. Timeline loads fast — p99 under about 200 ms for the API call. A new post becomes visible to followers within a few seconds (eventual consistency is acceptable; nobody can tell if their friend saw it two seconds earlier). Your own post must appear in your own timeline immediately (read-your-writes). Very high availability for reading: if ranking or counts are degraded, the feed should still load. Posts are durable forever.

The clarifying question: chronological or ranked? A chronological feed is a merge of sorted lists; a ranked feed adds candidate generation, features and a model. Design chronological first, then extend to ranking — the sequencing itself shows judgement.

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Assume 300 million daily active users, each opening the feed 10 times a day and posting 0.5 times a day, following on average 300 accounts (so each account has, on average, 300 followers — every follow edge has one follower and one followee).

Reads. 300M × 10 = 3 billion timeline requests a day ÷ 86,400 s ≈ 35,000 per second; with a 3× evening peak, about 100,000 per second.

Posts. 300M × 0.5 = 150 million posts a day ÷ 86,400 ≈ 1,700 per second, about 5,000 at peak. Read-to-post ratio: 35,000 ÷ 1,700 ≈ 20:1.

Fan-out writes under pure push. 1,700 posts/s × 300 followers = 510,000 timeline inserts per second on average. Large but tractable for an in-memory store with batching. The average hides the problem: an account with 30 million followers posting once creates 30 million inserts in one burst.

Timeline memory. Keep each home timeline as a capped list of the newest 800 entries (Twitter's figure). Each entry: post id (8 bytes), author id (8 bytes) and a few flag bytes (retweet, reply) — call it 20 bytes. For 300M active users: 300M × 800 × 20 B = 4.8 TB; replicated 3 times, about 15 TB of RAM. That is why timelines hold ids only, are capped, and exist only for recently active users.

Post storage. A text post with metadata is about 1 KB. 150M × 1 KB = 150 GB per day, about 55 TB per year before replication. Media dominates: if 10% of posts carry a 500 KB image, that is 15M × 500 KB = 7.5 TB a day, about 2.7 PB a year, stored in blob storage behind a CDN.

Read bandwidth. A hydrated page of 50 posts without media is about 50 × 1 KB = 50 KB. 35,000/s × 50 KB = 1.75 GB/s, about 14 Gbit/s on average and 40 Gbit/s at peak — spread over many API servers and well within a modern data centre, but large enough that responses are compressed and images are fetched separately from the CDN.

Post cache. Hydration reads 50 posts per request: 35,000 × 50 = 1.75 million post lookups per second, nearly all for recent posts. Caching the last few days of posts (150M × 1 KB × 3 days ≈ 450 GB) in a memcached or Redis tier serves almost all of them; Twitter's post cache held about the last month and a half.

3 · The API

POST /v1/posts                       {text, media_ids?, reply_to?}  → {post_id}
POST /v1/users/{id}/follow           (DELETE to unfollow)
GET  /v1/timeline/home?cursor=<post_id>&limit=50
     → {posts: [...hydrated...], next_cursor: "<smallest post_id returned>"}
GET  /v1/users/{id}/posts?cursor=...&limit=50
POST /v1/posts/{id}/like

Pagination uses a cursor, not page numbers: the client sends the smallest post id it has, and the server returns posts with smaller ids. If new posts arrive while the user scrolls, an offset-based page two would repeat items from page one; a cursor is stable. Media is uploaded separately (a pre-signed upload URL to blob storage), and the post carries media ids.

4 · The data model

posts(post_id PK, author_id, text, media_ids, reply_to, created_at, status)
user_posts(author_id, post_id)              -- author's own timeline, newest first
follows(follower_id, followee_id, since)    -- stored both directions:
followers(followee_id, follower_id)         --   "who do I follow" and "who follows X"
home_tl:{user_id}  → in-memory list of (post_id, author_id, flags), capped at 800
counts(post_id, likes, replies, reposts)    -- approximate, updated asynchronously

Post ids are time-ordered. Use a Snowflake-style id (a 64-bit number whose top bits are a millisecond timestamp, then a machine id and a sequence). Sorting by id is then sorting by time, merging timelines is merging sorted lists of integers, and the cursor is simply an id. This small decision simplifies everything downstream.

The social graph is stored in both directions because both are hot: "who does this reader follow" (for pull and reconstruction) and "who follows this author" (for fan-out). A popular account's follower list can have tens of millions of entries, so fan-out workers read it in pages from a sharded graph service, never in one query.

Counts are approximate and asynchronous. A viral post can receive thousands of likes per second; a synchronous counter update on one row would be the hottest lock in the system. Record like events, aggregate them in the background, and show counts that may lag by seconds.

5 · High-level design and the critical paths

Post path. The post API validates the post, assigns a post id, writes it to the post store and the author's user timeline, puts it in the post cache, and enqueues a fan-out job — then returns to the author. The author's client inserts the post into its own displayed feed immediately, which gives read-your-writes without waiting for fan-out. A fan-out worker takes the job, checks whether the author is a pull account; if not, it reads the author's followers page by page, keeps only active users, and appends the post id to each follower's timeline, pipelining thousands of inserts per round trip to the in-memory store.

Read path. Walk it with numbers using the sequence diagram. The timeline service finds the reader's home timeline (one in-memory lookup, about a millisecond), reads the list of pull accounts the reader follows (a small cached set, typically zero to a handful), reads each one's recent posts from the user-timeline cache, k-way merges everything by post id, takes the newest 50, and hydrates them with a multi-get from the post cache and the user cache (author names and avatars). Filters run during hydration: deleted posts, blocked or muted authors, content not visible in the reader's country. Twitter reported a median of about 5 ms to get timeline data out of the timeline service, and about 400 ms for a complete API response at the time.

6 · Deep dive A: fan-out on write, on read, and the hybrid

Pure fan-out on read costs, per timeline request, one lookup per followed account plus a merge: at 300 followees and 35,000 requests per second, about 10 million user-timeline reads per second, every second, even when nothing new was posted. It wastes work recomputing the same merge for people who refresh repeatedly, and its latency grows with how many accounts you follow.

Pure fan-out on write costs, per post, one insert per (active) follower. On average that is 510,000 inserts a second — fine — but the distribution of followers is extremely skewed, and cost follows the skew. Twitter in 2013 had accounts with 31, 28, 28 and 23 million followers. Each post from one of them was tens of millions of inserts; Twitter reported fan-out to a million followers taking about 3.5 seconds at the median, and up to five minutes at p99. Worse, celebrities reply to each other, so a reply's fan-out could overtake the original's, and some followers saw the reply before the post it answered.

The hybrid. Mark accounts above a threshold as pull. Their posts go only into their own user timeline (one write) and into a small hot cache. Everyone else's posts are pushed. At read time, the service merges the reader's pushed timeline with the recent posts of the pull accounts that reader follows. Because a typical reader follows only a few such accounts, the read cost grows by a few cache lookups, while the write cost loses its worst bursts entirely. Twitter's stated direction in 2013 was exactly this: stop fanning out the highest-follower accounts and merge them at read time, which they said saved tens of percent of compute.

Choosing the threshold. Compare costs per post. Pushing costs roughly active followers inserts. Pulling costs, for every timeline request by any of those followers, one extra lookup — so it scales with active followers × how often they open the app ÷ how often the author posts. An author who posts rarely to followers who read often is cheap to push (one insert serves many reads); an author who posts constantly to millions is cheap to pull. In practice: start with a follower threshold in the tens or hundreds of thousands, measure fan-out queue time and read latency, and adjust; lower it under load so the queue drains. The drill computes this for a concrete skewed platform.

Only fan out to active users. Most accounts have not opened the app in weeks. Twitter only kept timelines in memory for users who had logged in within 30 days (adjustable to cache capacity); for everyone else the post simply did not go into a cached timeline. When an inactive user returns, the timeline is reconstructed: look up whom they follow, fetch each account's recent posts, merge, store. It costs a slow first load (Twitter quoted about 2 seconds from a cold cache) for a large saving on every post.

New follows and unfollows. When you follow someone, backfill their last few posts into your timeline asynchronously, or accept that their posts appear only from now on. On unfollow, filter their posts at read time and remove them lazily. Say which you choose.

7 · Deep dive B: timeline storage and hydration

Each home timeline is an in-memory list keyed by user id, newest first, trimmed to 800 entries after each insert. Twitter used Redis lists for this, with each timeline replicated on three machines so a failed machine does not force reconstructing every timeline it held; the timeline service effectively ran three hash rings and read from whichever replica answered fastest. Fan-out workers pipeline inserts — Twitter sent about 4,000 destinations at a time — because one network round trip per insert would cap throughput at a few thousand inserts per worker per second.

Entries are ids, not posts. Reading returns ids; hydration turns ids into posts with one multi-get to the post cache and one to the user cache. Twitter's tweet service cached about the last six weeks of tweets in memcached, and its user service cached the entire user base. Hydration is also where read-time filtering happens: a deleted post is simply missing from the post cache (or marked deleted), a blocked author is dropped, and country-specific legal restrictions are applied. This is the main reason to store ids: correctness for edits, deletes and blocks is enforced at one point instead of in millions of copies.

The timeline store is a cache. If it is lost, timelines are rebuilt from the post store and the social graph — slower, but correct. Never put anything in it that cannot be recomputed, and size it for active users only.

8 · Deep dive C: from chronological to ranked

A ranked feed ("For You") replaces "newest first" with "most likely to interest you", and the architecture becomes a recommender funnel. X open-sourced the code for its version in 2023; the home-mixer service describes the stages. Candidate generation gathers posts from several sources: in-network posts from people you follow (from the search index), out-of-network posts found by traversing a graph of who engaged with what, and posts from suggested accounts. Feature hydration fetches the signals a model needs for each candidate — X's README says about 6,000 features — such as the author's relationship to you, the post's engagement so far, and embeddings of you and the post. Scoring runs a neural network that predicts engagement probabilities. Filters and heuristics enforce author diversity, the balance of in-network and out-of-network content, removal of already-seen posts and visibility rules. Mixing adds ads and modules.

The infrastructure consequences: you cannot precompute the final order (it depends on the moment and the model), so the chronological timeline becomes one candidate source among several; feature fetching and model inference must fit a budget of a few hundred milliseconds; pagination must pin the ranked list for a session; and every component needs a fallback — if the ranker times out, serve the chronological merge.

9 · Bottlenecks, failure modes and trade-offs

Fan-out lag during spikes: posts appear late; mitigate with prioritised queues, the dynamic threshold, and never blocking the post API on fan-out. Timeline store loss: rebuild on demand, which turns into a reconstruction storm if many timelines vanish together — replicate timelines and rate-limit reconstruction. Hot celebrity post: every follower's read touches the same user-timeline cache entry; replicate it across cache nodes and allow a second of staleness. Counts: approximate and asynchronous by design. Ordering anomalies: a reply before its parent during slow fan-out; hide replies whose parent has not been hydrated, or fetch the parent on demand.

The trade-offs to close on: push for ordinary accounts and pull for large ones; ids rather than content in timelines; timelines as rebuildable cache for active users only; eventual consistency for others' posts with read-your-writes for your own; and ranking as an optional layer with a chronological fallback.

The threshold is a cost function, not a number

Fan-out cost per post is the number of followers who will actually read the post from their stored timeline — active followers, not total followers. Pull cost is paid on reads: every timeline request by a follower of a pull account does an extra lookup, whether or not that account posted. So the crossover depends on the ratio of an author's posting rate to their followers' reading rate. A news account posting every minute to 2 million readers who each refresh twice a day generates far more inserts than reads saved; a celebrity posting twice a week to the same audience is the reverse. A strong answer frames the threshold as this cost comparison, says it would be measured per account class, and makes it dynamic: under load, lowering it shifts work from the backlogged write path to the read path, which has more slack.

Consistency you deliberately decline

Two users refreshing at the same moment can see different orders; a post can reach one follower minutes before another; unfollowing does not retroactively purge your stored timeline. None of this is observable in a way users care about, and providing it would require coordinating millions of writes. The guarantees worth keeping are narrower: your own post appears to you immediately (the client inserts it locally, or the read path merges the user's own recent posts), deleted and blocked content never renders (enforced at hydration), and a reply is not shown before its parent. Naming which guarantees you decline and why they are unobservable is a stronger answer than claiming the feed is consistent.

The social graph is its own hard system

"Who follows this author?" for 30 million followers is not one query; it is a paged scan of a sharded adjacency list, read by many fan-out workers in parallel. "Whom does this reader follow?" is read on every reconstruction and every pull merge. Twitter ran a dedicated social graph service (FlockDB) for this. Facebook built TAO, a read-optimised, eventually consistent graph cache in front of sharded MySQL, because ordinary key-value caching does not handle adjacency lists with millions of entries and very high read rates. In an interview, it is enough to say the graph is a separate sharded service with both directions materialised and cached, read in pages by fan-out.

Merging is cheap if ids are time-ordered

A k-way merge of k sorted lists keeps a heap of the k list heads and repeatedly pops the largest id. Producing the top 50 from 1 pushed timeline and 3 pull lists costs about 50 × log₂(4) = 100 comparisons — microseconds. This is only true because post ids sort by time; with random ids you would need to fetch timestamps for every candidate first. It is a concrete example of an earlier decision (Snowflake-style ids) paying off in a later component.

Ranking changes the infrastructure, not just the order

Once the order depends on a model, you need a feature store that serves thousands of features per candidate at low latency, consistent with what the model was trained on (the training/serving gap from ml14); a candidate retrieval layer that may use embeddings and approximate nearest-neighbour search; model serving with batching to use accelerators efficiently; and logging of what was shown and what was engaged with, to train the next model. A light ranker that cuts thousands of candidates to hundreds before the heavy model is standard. The timeline cache from the chronological design survives as one candidate source, and as the fallback.

Fan-out lag is the incident you will actually have

During a major event, both the posting rate and the share of posts from high-follower accounts rise at once. The fan-out queue grows, propagation slips from seconds to minutes, and users report that "the app is broken" while every service is technically up. The defences are layered: separate queues by priority (fan-out to recently active users first, dormant users last or never), shed fan-out for accounts near the threshold by temporarily treating them as pull, scale workers on queue age rather than CPU, and alert on end-to-end propagation time (post created to visible in a sampled follower's timeline) rather than on queue length alone.

Reposts, replies and duplicates

A repost (retweet) is not a new copy of the post; Twitter stored a pointer to the original. That raises a feed-quality problem: if five accounts you follow all repost the same viral post, a naive timeline shows it five times. Store the original post id alongside the repost entry (the 4 bytes of flags in Twitter's entries marked retweets and replies), and deduplicate at read time by original id, showing it once with "reposted by A and 4 others". Replies have the opposite issue: a reply from someone you follow to someone you do not is usually noise, so Twitter's business rules stripped @replies to people you do not follow. Both are read-time rules applied during merge and hydration, which is another argument for keeping timelines as thin lists of ids and flags.

Multiple regions

A global feed is served from several regions. Posts are written in the author's home region and replicated to the others asynchronously (sc17); fan-out runs in each region against that region's timeline store, so a reader in Europe reads a European timeline. The consequences are small and acceptable: a post reaches readers in another region after replication lag plus fan-out time, and a region that falls behind shows older timelines rather than failing. What must not happen is a cross-region call on the read path: every hop in the read sequence should be local, with the post and user caches populated by replication.

The client is a cache too

Mobile apps keep the last timeline they received and show it instantly on launch, then fetch "newer than my newest id" in the background and show a "new posts" pill rather than reflowing the screen. This hides read latency entirely for most opens, cuts payload sizes (only the delta is sent), and makes the cursor-based API pay off a second time. It also means that the server's p99 is not the user's experience; the user experiences the client's cache plus a delta.

When pull is the right answer

For a new product with a few hundred thousand users, fan-out on read with an index on (author, post id) is correct, simple and fast enough, and precomputation is premature. The crossover comes when read volume × merge cost exceeds fan-out cost — for typical social graphs, somewhere in the low millions of active users. Being able to argue against your own design, and say when you would not build it, is what interviewers mean by judgement.

The real case: Twitter's timeline architecture

In a 2013 QCon talk, "Timelines at Scale", Twitter's VP of Engineering Raffi Krikorian described the home timeline in detail; High Scalability published a careful written gloss with the numbers. Twitter then had 150 million active users, handled about 300,000 queries per second to generate timelines, and about 6,000 requests per second on writes. Tweet input averaged about 5,000 per second (400 million a day), peaked around 7,000 daily and exceeded 12,000 during large events. Timeline delivery was about 30 billion deliveries a day (about 300,000 per second), and fan-out to a million followers took about 3.5 seconds at the median — but up to five minutes at p99.

The design matches this lesson almost line by line. Naive read-time materialisation — "a massive select statement over all of Twitter" — had been tried and abandoned. Instead, a fan-out service looked up followers in the social graph service and inserted each tweet's id into the followers' home timelines in a Redis cluster with a couple of terabytes of RAM, pipelining 4,000 destinations at a time. Each timeline entry was the tweet id, the author's user id and 4 bytes of flags; each home timeline held at most 800 entries; each was replicated on three machines. Only users active in the last 30 days had a cached timeline; others went through reconstruction on return. Reads found the timeline on one of the three replicas, then hydrated tweets with a multi-get from the tweet service (caching about six weeks of tweets) and the user service (caching every user). Read-time filtering removed content not allowed in particular countries.

The talk was candid about the problem. Accounts with tens of millions of followers — Lady Gaga had 31 million — made fan-out slow, and because celebrities reply to each other, replies sometimes arrived before the tweets they answered, confusing users. Someone who had followed recently could see a tweet minutes before a long-time follower. The plan was to stop fanning out the highest-value accounts and merge their tweets at read time, which Twitter estimated would save tens of percent of compute: the hybrid.

Ten years later, X's open-sourced recommendation code shows the next stage. The For You timeline in home-mixer gathers candidates from the search index (about half of posts, per the README), graph-based recommenders and follow recommendations, fetches about 6,000 features, scores with a neural "heavy ranker", applies filters for diversity, balance, already-seen posts and visibility, and mixes in ads and modules. The chronological "Following" timeline still exists alongside it.

Failure modes and what to monitor

Propagation time (post to visible in followers' timelines), at p50 and p99, split by author follower bucket — the single most important feed metric. Fan-out queue age, not just depth. Timeline read latency and the share of reads that needed reconstruction (a spike means the timeline store lost data or the active-user window is too small). Hydration cache hit ratio for posts and users. Ranker timeouts and fallback rate for ranked feeds. Ordering anomalies, such as replies served without their parent. Twitter monitored request paths with distributed tracing (Zipkin, based on Google's Dapper), reporting a median of 5 ms for timeline service reads, 100 ms at p99, and a couple of hundred milliseconds at p99.9 when reads hit disk.

Goal: build a skewed follow graph and measure how much fan-out work a tiny set of large accounts causes, then check that the hybrid returns the same feed as pure pull. Python 3.12, standard library, about 15 seconds.

"""Fan-out on write vs read vs hybrid on a skewed follow graph. Python 3.12 stdlib."""
import heapq, random
from collections import defaultdict

random.seed(1)
USERS, FOLLOWS_EACH, CAP = 20_000, 50, 800     # CAP = timeline length, as at Twitter
# Preferential follows: account i is chosen with weight 1/(i+1) (a Zipf-like popularity curve).
weights = [1 / (i + 1) for i in range(USERS)]
followers = defaultdict(set)
following = defaultdict(set)
for u in range(USERS):
    for f in set(random.choices(range(USERS), weights, k=FOLLOWS_EACH)) - {u}:
        followers[f].add(u); following[u].add(f)

counts = sorted((len(v) for v in followers.values()), reverse=True)
print("top accounts' followers:", counts[:5], " median:", counts[len(counts) // 2])

# One post per account; post id doubles as timestamp (Snowflake-style, time-ordered).
posts = {a: [pid] for pid, a in enumerate(random.sample(range(USERS), USERS))}

def push_cost(threshold):
    """Timeline inserts if accounts below the threshold fan out on write."""
    return sum(len(followers[a]) for a in posts if len(followers[a]) < threshold)

def read_cost(user, threshold):
    """Extra author timelines merged at read time = followees at/above the threshold."""
    return sum(1 for f in following[user] if len(followers[f]) >= threshold)

timelines = defaultdict(list)                 # materialised (pushed) timelines
def fan_out(threshold):
    timelines.clear()
    for a, pids in posts.items():
        if len(followers[a]) < threshold:
            for u in followers[a]:
                timelines[u].extend(pids)
    for u in timelines:                       # keep only the newest CAP ids
        timelines[u] = sorted(timelines[u], reverse=True)[:CAP]

def home(user, threshold):
    pushed = timelines[user]
    pulled = [sorted(posts[f], reverse=True) for f in following[user]
              if len(followers[f]) >= threshold]
    return list(heapq.merge(pushed, *pulled, reverse=True))[:50]

reader, results = 123, {}
for T in (float("inf"), 10_000, 3_000, 0):
    fan_out(T)
    label = "pure push" if T == float("inf") else "pure pull" if T == 0 else f"hybrid T={T:,}"
    pull_accounts = sum(1 for a in posts if len(followers[a]) >= T)
    results[label] = home(reader, T)
    print(f"{label:>15}: {pull_accounts:>6,} pull accounts | {push_cost(T):>9,} timeline writes"
          f" | reader merges {read_cost(reader, T):>2} author lists")
print("same top-50 feed under every strategy:", len({tuple(v) for v in results.values()}) == 1)
  1. Run it. The first line shows the skew: the top account has about 20,000 followers in a 20,000-user network while the median account has about 10.
  2. Compare the four strategies. Pure push costs about 827,000 timeline writes for one post per account. Marking just the 6 accounts above 10,000 followers as pull removes about 90,000 writes (11%) and costs this reader 4 extra lists to merge.
  3. Check the last line: True means every strategy produced the identical top-50 feed. Push, pull and hybrid are performance choices, not correctness choices.
  4. Change the weights to 1 / (i + 1) ** 1.5 to make the graph more skewed and rerun: the top accounts grab a larger share of follows, and the same small number of pull accounts now removes a much larger fraction of writes.
  5. Pick a reader who follows many large accounts (try a few values of reader) and watch the merge count grow under pull. This is why the threshold must consider readers' cost, not only authors'.
Work it out

Your platform has 200M users. The follower distribution is heavily skewed: 99.9% of accounts have fewer than 10,000 followers (median 150), while 2,000 accounts have over 1M followers, averaging 12M each. Post rate is 2,000/s overall, and the 2,000 large accounts post 50 times/day each.

(a) What is the fan-out cost under pure push? (b) How much of it comes from the large accounts? (c) Where should the threshold go?

Answer

(a) Ordinary accounts. Their post rate is essentially the full 2,000/s (the large accounts contribute 2,000 × 50 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 1.16 posts/s, which is negligible). At a median 150 followers, fan-out is 2,000 × 150 = 300,000 timeline writes/s. Entirely manageable with a queue and a few hundred workers.

(b) Large accounts. 1.16 posts/s × 12M followers ≈ 13.9M timeline writes/s. That is about 46× the entire rest of the platform combined, generated by 0.001% of accounts making 0.06% of the posts.

And the average understates the problem, because it is bursty rather than steady. A single account with 100M followers posting once creates 100M writes that must land in seconds — an instantaneous spike no steady-state capacity plan covers. Under pure push, the platform's write capacity is effectively set by its largest account.

(c) Where the threshold goes. Compute the crossover rather than guessing. Pushing costs followers writes per post. Pulling costs one extra cache read per follower who opens the app, but only for the celebrities they follow — and because a typical user follows very few large accounts, that merge is 2–5 extra reads regardless.

Set the threshold where fan-out stops being cheap relative to its bursts — around 50,000–100,000 followers. Check what that captures: it marks perhaps 20,000–50,000 accounts as pull, removing essentially all 13.9M writes/s while adding a handful of reads to each timeline request. Fan-out drops from about 14.2M/s to about 300,000/s, a 98% reduction, at a read cost of a few milliseconds.

Two refinements worth volunteering. First, make the threshold dynamic: under load, lower it so more accounts become pull and the queue drains. Second, weight by active followers — an account with 200k followers of whom 5k opened the app this month is cheap to push, while one with 60k highly engaged followers is not. The follower count is a proxy; the cost is the thing.

Estimate it

Twitter in 2013 had about 150 million active users, 800-entry home timelines, entries of a tweet id (8 bytes), a user id (8 bytes) and 4 bytes of flags, and three replicas of every timeline. How much RAM do full timelines need, and what does the answer tell you about the 30-day active-user rule?

Answer

Per timeline: 800 × 20 B = 16,000 B = 16 KB. For 150M users: 150M × 16 KB = 2.4 TB. Three replicas: 7.2 TB, before Redis per-key and list overhead (which can add tens of percent). Twitter described its timeline cluster as "a couple of terabytes of RAM", which only fits if far fewer than all users have a full timeline at once: many active users have timelines shorter than 800, and anyone not seen for 30 days has none. The rule is a memory budget expressed as a time window — Twitter said the window "can change depending on cache capacity" — and reconstruction is the price paid by returning users.
