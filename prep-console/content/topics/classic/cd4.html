It is 9 pm on a Friday and a new season of a hit series has just been released. Twenty million people press play within the hour, on phones on trains, on smart TVs on home Wi-Fi, on laptops on hotel networks. Each expects the picture to appear within a couple of seconds and never to freeze. Some of them have a 100 Mbps fibre connection; some have a mobile signal that swings between 20 Mbps and almost nothing every few seconds.

Most system design questions are about requests. This one is about bytes. A single high-definition stream is several megabits per second; twenty million of them is a hundred terabits per second — more than any single data centre on earth can send to the internet, and more than many countries' international links carry. If every viewer pulled video from a central origin, the links between that origin and the viewers' internet providers would melt long before the servers did.

So the design question flips from "how do I serve video?" to "how do I avoid serving it from the centre at all?" The answer has three parts. First, prepare the video in advance: one uploaded master becomes dozens of versions at different qualities, chopped into short, independent, cacheable files. Second, put copies of those files physically close to viewers — Netflix goes as far as installing its own servers inside internet providers and filling them overnight with what it predicts people will watch tomorrow. Third, let each viewer's player decide, every few seconds, which quality its network can sustain right now, so a train entering a tunnel means a softer picture rather than a frozen one.

This lesson follows the interview flow: requirements, estimates (concurrent streams, egress, storage per hour of content, transcoding work, playback-position writes), API and data model, the high-level design of both the pipeline and playback, then deep dives on adaptive bitrate, on CDN placement and hit ratio, and on per-title encoding, followed by failure modes and trade-offs. The real case is Netflix's Open Connect and its published encoding and adaptation research; the lab simulates two adaptive-bitrate strategies on a bumpy network and computes CDN hit ratios under a Zipf popularity curve.

The mental model: a bakery chain, not a single bakery

Imagine a famous bakery with millions of customers. If everyone queued at the one shop, the street would jam no matter how fast the bakers worked. So the bakery bakes in a central kitchen overnight, in several sizes of each loaf, and trucks them to small shops in every neighbourhood before opening time — stocking each shop with what that neighbourhood usually buys. Customers walk to their local shop. If the shop has run out of a large loaf, the customer happily takes a medium one rather than leave empty-handed. Only rarely does a shop need to call the central kitchen for something unusual.

Map it across. The central kitchen is the transcoding pipeline: it turns each uploaded film into a bitrate ladder of versions (renditions) at different resolutions and bitrates. The trucks are fill: copying files to edge servers ahead of demand, during off-peak hours. The neighbourhood shops are edge caches — inside internet providers or at internet exchange points. Taking the medium loaf is adaptive bitrate: the player stepping down a rung instead of stalling. And calling the central kitchen is a cache miss going back to the origin.

Where the analogy breaks: a loaf is bought once, but a film is fetched as a stream of small pieces, and the customer can change size between pieces. That is the core trick of modern streaming — video is cut into segments of a few seconds, each an ordinary file, and the player picks the quality of every segment independently.

Two systems that share almost nothing

The control plane is an ordinary web application: browsing the catalogue, search, accounts and billing, recommendations, and the playback request that checks you may watch this title and tells your player where to fetch it. It handles thousands to tens of thousands of requests per second, like any large web service.

The data plane moves the video itself: terabits per second of segment files from edge caches to players. It is not "a service" in the usual sense; it is a fleet of cache servers that do nothing but serve files over HTTP, steered by the control plane. Keeping these apart is the most important framing in the interview: the hard numbers live in the data plane, and the control plane never touches a video byte.

Three words to know before the numbers

A codec is the compression method (H.264 plays everywhere; newer codecs such as HEVC, VP9 and AV1 squeeze the same picture into fewer bits but need newer devices). Bitrate is how many bits per second a stream uses — the main driver of both quality and cost. A manifest is the small index file a player downloads first, listing every rendition and how to fetch its segments; HLS calls it a playlist (.m3u8), MPEG-DASH an MPD (.mpd). With those three, the rest of the system is a story about producing segments once and delivering them millions of times.

1 · Requirements

Functional. Ingest a title (or a user upload) and make it playable; browse and search the catalogue; play with automatically adapting quality on any device; resume where you left off on another device; multiple audio tracks and subtitles. Out of scope, said aloud: recommendations (a separate system that consumes viewing events), billing, and live streaming — which is a genuinely different design with no preparation window, covered briefly in the deep section.

Non-functional. Playback starts within about 2 seconds. Rebuffering — playback freezing because the buffer ran empty — under a fraction of a percent of viewing time. The catalogue stays playable even if the ingest pipeline is backed up. Video is protected by DRM (digital rights management). And cost: bandwidth is the dominant bill, so efficiency per hour watched is a first-class requirement.

The clarifying question: a professionally produced catalogue (Netflix: tens of thousands of titles, known release dates) or user uploads (YouTube: an enormous, mostly unwatched long tail)? A curated catalogue can be pushed to edges in advance; a user-generated long tail cannot, and must be cached on demand. The same building blocks apply, but the caching strategy and encoding budget differ completely.

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Assume 200 million subscribers, 10% watching at the evening peak, and an average delivered bitrate of 5 Mbps (a mix of phones at 1-2 Mbps, HD TVs at 5 Mbps and 4K at 15 Mbps).

Concurrent streams and egress. 200M × 0.10 = 20 million concurrent streams. 20M × 5 Mbps = 100,000,000 Mbps = 100 Tbps at peak. Compare: a large cloud region might have a few terabits per second of internet capacity. You are one to two orders of magnitude short. This single comparison justifies everything that follows.

Per viewer. 5 Mbps for a 2-hour film is 5 × 7,200 = 36,000 megabits = 4.5 GB. A subscriber watching 2 hours a day uses about 135 GB a month.

Edge servers. Netflix's current storage appliance is listed at about 200 Gbps of throughput. 100 Tbps ÷ 200 Gbps = 500 appliances running flat out; with headroom, redundancy and the need to be in thousands of ISP locations, the real fleet is in the thousands — which is what Netflix describes.

Storage per hour of content. Netflix's 2015 fixed ladder had ten rungs: 235, 375, 560, 750, 1,050, 1,750, 2,350, 3,000, 4,300 and 5,800 kbps, summing to 20,170 kbps. One hour of every rung: 20.17 Mbps × 3,600 s = 72,612 megabits ≈ 9.1 GB per codec family. With two or three codec families, audio in several languages and 4K/HDR rungs, call it about 30 GB per hour. A catalogue of 100,000 hours (films plus every episode of every series) is about 3 PB at origin. An appliance holds up to 120 TB, so a full catalogue copy needs dozens of appliances; most sites hold the popular subset.

Transcoding work. If encoding one rung of one hour of 1080p video takes about 2 CPU-core-hours, a 10-rung ladder in 3 codecs is about 60 core-hours per content hour. A 2-hour film is 120 core-hours: five days on one core, but split into a thousand chunks across a thousand cores it finishes in minutes. The work is embarrassingly parallel — independent pieces with no communication — which is the word to use.

Playback position writes. Each player reports its position every 30 seconds or so (for resume and for analytics): 20M ÷ 30 = about 670,000 writes per second at peak. Small and low-value individually, so batch them and write to a store built for high write rates, never on the playback path.

Control plane. Plays per second at peak: if a session lasts about an hour, 20M concurrent streams start at about 20M ÷ 3,600 ≈ 5,600 plays per second, plus browsing. A large but ordinary web workload.

3 · The API

POST /v1/uploads                       → {upload_id, part_urls[]}     (pre-signed, resumable)
POST /v1/uploads/{id}/complete         → starts the pipeline
GET  /v1/titles/{id}                   → metadata, artwork, episodes
POST /v1/playback/{title_id}           {device caps, network hints}
     → {manifest_url, edge_urls[], license_url, session_id}
GET  <edge>/{asset}/{rendition}/seg_{n}.m4s          (plain HTTP, cacheable)
POST /v1/license                       {session_id, challenge} → key   (DRM)
POST /v1/sessions/{id}/progress        {position_s, bitrate, stalls}   (batched)

Note what is not there: no endpoint streams video from your application servers. The playback call returns URLs pointing at edge caches chosen for this client, and from then on the player talks HTTP to those caches directly.

It helps to see a manifest once. A simplified HLS master playlist for one title looks like this:

#EXTM3U
#EXT-X-STREAM-INF:BANDWIDTH=750000,RESOLUTION=512x384,CODECS="avc1.4d401e"
v750/index.m3u8
#EXT-X-STREAM-INF:BANDWIDTH=1750000,RESOLUTION=720x480,CODECS="avc1.4d401f"
v1750/index.m3u8
#EXT-X-STREAM-INF:BANDWIDTH=4300000,RESOLUTION=1920x1080,CODECS="avc1.640028"
v4300/index.m3u8

Each line pair is one rendition: its peak bandwidth in bits per second, resolution and codec, then the URL of a media playlist. Each media playlist lists that rendition's segments in order (seg_1.m4s, seg_2.m4s, …) with their durations. The player reads the master, filters out renditions its hardware cannot decode, and from then on chooses a rendition per segment. Because every URL is relative, the same manifest works on any edge that holds the files — steering only has to change the host name.

4 · The data model

titles(title_id, type, metadata, release_at, regions)
assets(asset_id, title_id, codec, resolution, bitrate_kbps, segment_template, drm_key_id)
manifests(title_id, device_class, manifest_blob)        -- generated per device family
viewing(user_id, title_id, position_s, updated_at)      -- high write rate, wide-column store
edge_inventory(appliance_id, file_id, stored_at)        -- reported by appliances

Segments and manifests live in object storage at origin and on edge disks; they are immutable files, so they cache forever and need no invalidation. The viewing table is the high-volume one: partition by user so "where was I in everything?" is one partition read. The edge inventory is what lets the control plane steer each client to an appliance that actually has the files.

5 · High-level design: the pipeline and the playback path

Ingest. The master file is uploaded in parts directly to object storage with pre-signed URLs, so a failed upload resumes rather than restarts. The pipeline splits the master into chunks at keyframe boundaries — a keyframe is a frame that decodes on its own, and the frames after it are stored as differences from it, so a chunk must start on one. Each chunk is transcoded into every rung and codec in parallel; an orchestrator tracks the task graph and retries failed chunks individually. Outputs are packaged as segments of a few seconds with manifests for HLS and DASH, checked automatically for defects, encrypted, written to origin, and scheduled for fill to edges.

Playback. Walk the critical path with the sequence diagram. The player calls the playback API with its device capabilities. The control plane checks the subscription and licensing for the region, decides which renditions this device can play, asks the steering service which edge appliances have those files and are healthy and close in network terms, and returns the manifest and a list of edge URLs. The player fetches the manifest, requests a DRM license, then requests the first segments at a conservative bitrate so the picture appears quickly. From then on it runs the adaptive loop: after each segment, pick the next segment's rung.

6 · Deep dive A: adaptive bitrate — how the player chooses

The player holds a buffer: seconds of video downloaded but not yet played. Playback drains it at one second per second; downloading a 4-second segment adds 4 seconds. If a 4-second segment at 4,300 kbps takes 2 seconds to download, the buffer grew by 2 seconds; if it takes 6 seconds, the buffer shrank by 2. If the buffer reaches zero, playback freezes: rebuffering. The ABR algorithm's job is to choose each next segment's bitrate to maximise quality while keeping the buffer away from zero and avoiding constant switching, which viewers find annoying.

Throughput-based rules estimate bandwidth from recent downloads and choose the highest rung below, say, 80% of it. They react quickly but believe noisy estimates: on a phone the measured rate swings widely from segment to segment, so the player oscillates, and a sudden drop catches it mid-download of a large segment. Buffer-based rules choose the rung from buffer occupancy: the lowest rung while the buffer is below a reservoir (enough seconds to absorb one slow download), then progressively higher rungs as the buffer fills through a cushion, reaching the top rung when it is nearly full. Because the buffer changes slowly, so do decisions.

The Stanford and Netflix paper behind this ran A/B tests with over half a million real users per group in 2013. The simple buffer-based version cut the rebuffer rate by 10-30% against Netflix's then-default throughput-led algorithm, but delivered a lower bitrate at start-up because it sat in the reservoir at the lowest rung. Adding a simple throughput estimate during start-up, when the buffer is empty and carries no information, fixed that: the final design kept a 10-20% rebuffer reduction with essentially the same average bitrate and fewer switches. The lesson generalises — the buffer is the stable control signal; throughput is only useful when the buffer has nothing to say.

Start-up is its own problem: time to first frame dominates perceived quality, so players begin with a low or medium rung (a few hundred kilobits per second downloads a 4-second segment almost instantly) and step up as the buffer grows. That is why the first seconds of a stream often look soft.

7 · Deep dive B: where the bytes live, and the hit ratio

There are three tiers. Appliances embedded inside ISPs serve viewers without the traffic ever leaving the ISP's network: lowest latency for the viewer, no transit cost for anyone. Appliances at internet exchange points (IXPs) serve ISPs too small to host hardware, one network hop away. Origin storage in the cloud holds everything and should serve only a small share of bytes.

The economics depend on the byte hit ratio — the share of bytes served from the edge. Popularity is long-tailed; a useful model is Zipf: the title ranked k gets traffic proportional to 1/k. Then the share of requests going to the top k of N titles is H(k) ÷ H(N), where H(n) = 1 + 1/2 + … + 1/n ≈ ln n + 0.577. Work it by hand for N = 20,000: H(20,000) ≈ 9.90 + 0.58 = 10.48. An edge with room for the top 2,000 titles: H(2,000) ≈ 7.60 + 0.58 = 8.18, so 8.18 ÷ 10.48 ≈ 78%. Top 5,000: (8.52 + 0.58) ÷ 10.48 ≈ 87%. Top 200: (5.30 + 0.58) ÷ 10.48 ≈ 56%. The hit ratio grows with the logarithm of cache size, so each doubling adds only about ln 2 ÷ 10.5 ≈ 7 points.

Two consequences. First, the last percentage points are expensive, so large sites hold a full catalogue copy and small ones hold the head. Second, which titles are cached matters more than how many: popularity varies by country, by time of day and by release schedule. Netflix's answer is proactive fill: because it can predict what members will watch and when, its control plane pushes the predicted files to appliances during configurable off-peak windows, and appliances can fill from each other rather than from the backbone. A traditional CDN fills on demand (the first viewer's request is a miss); proactive fill makes even the first viewer's request a hit and moves the fill traffic to hours when links are idle.

Steering ties it together: appliances report their health, the network routes they learn from the ISP (via BGP, the internet's routing protocol), and which files they hold; the control plane uses this to give each client URLs for the best appliances that have the needed files. If an appliance fails or is overloaded, the player falls back to the next URL.

8 · Deep dive C: the ladder and per-title encoding

A fixed ladder applies the same bitrate-resolution pairs to every title. Netflix's 2015 post explains why that wastes bits: across 100 sampled titles encoded at 1080p, some reached very high quality (45 dB PSNR, where coding artefacts are invisible) at 2,500 kbps or less, while others needed 8,000 kbps or more to reach an acceptable 38 dB. Per-title encoding runs trial encodes of each title at several resolutions and quality settings, plots quality against bitrate for each resolution, and picks rungs close to the upper envelope of those curves — the convex hull, where no other choice gives better quality for the same bits — spaced roughly one just-noticeable difference apart. Results: an episode of the animated BoJack Horseman streamed at 1080p for 1,540 kbps, where the fixed ladder gave only 480p at 1,750 kbps; Orange Is the New Black's top rung dropped from 5,800 to 4,640 kbps, a 20% saving at the same quality; and complex action titles got a higher top rung than 5,800 kbps to remove artefacts.

The trade-off is economic. Encoding is a one-time cost per title; bandwidth is paid on every stream. For a title watched millions of times, a 20% bitrate cut pays for many trial encodes on the first evening. For a user upload watched twice, it never pays back. So a platform with a long tail encodes uploads with a cheap fixed ladder first and re-encodes with the expensive method once a video becomes popular.

9 · Bottlenecks, failure modes and trade-offs

Release-night spikes: pre-position scheduled releases in the previous fill window; an unfilled episode turns into origin traffic over peering links exactly at peak. Edge failure: the player retries the next URL from the steering list; quality may dip, playback should not stop. Transcoding backlog: delays new titles but never touches playback of existing ones, because the pipeline and the data plane share nothing; prioritise by release date. DRM license outage: no new plays can start even though bytes are cached, so the license service needs the availability of the playback API itself, with caching of licenses per session. Telemetry overload: 670,000 progress writes per second must be batched and must never block playback.

Trade-offs to close on: storage and encoding compute spent to save bandwidth (more rungs, more codecs, per-title ladders); proactive fill versus on-demand caching (prediction quality versus simplicity); short segments (faster adaptation and start-up) versus long ones (fewer requests, better compression); and the player owning quality decisions, which makes the server simple and cacheable at the price of trusting client logic you must test across thousands of device types.

Live streaming is a different system

With video on demand, the pipeline can take hours and the files exist before anyone asks. With live, every segment must be encoded, packaged and on the edge within seconds of being filmed. Segments are short — 1-2 seconds, or delivered in smaller parts with chunked transfer so the edge can forward a segment while it is still being written — because the player's latency behind live is roughly a few segment lengths. Nothing can be pre-positioned, so the edge sees a synchronised thundering herd: a million viewers all requesting the newest segment within the same second. Request coalescing at the edge (one upstream fetch per segment, every other request waits for it) and a tiered cache (edges fetch from a mid-tier, not directly from origin) are what keep origin alive. Encoding has a hard deadline per segment, so live ladders are usually fixed rather than per-title. Treating live as "the same but faster" is a classic interview trap.

Codecs: an economic decision with a compatibility constraint

Newer codecs (HEVC, VP9, AV1) need substantially fewer bits than H.264 for the same perceived quality, which on a bandwidth-dominated bill is worth a great deal. But older devices cannot decode them in hardware, and software decoding drains batteries. So services encode several codec families and choose per device in the playback call: every additional family multiplies encoding compute and storage to save bandwidth only on devices that can use it. The senior answer quantifies it: storage and encoding are one-time per title, bandwidth is per stream, so a new codec pays off first on the most-watched titles and the most-capable devices, and the long tail may never get it.

DRM and caching: separating keys from bytes

Segments are encrypted once, with a content key per title or rendition, using common encryption so the same ciphertext works with several DRM systems (Widevine, FairPlay, PlayReady). Every viewer downloads identical encrypted files, which cache perfectly. Each playback session asks a license service for the key, wrapped so only that device's secure decoder can use it, and the license can carry rules (expiry, maximum resolution on insecure devices). The design insight generalises far beyond video: put the per-user part (keys, authorisation) in a small, separate, highly available service, and keep the bulk data identical for everyone so it can be cached.

Why segments are files and not a stream protocol

Older streaming used dedicated protocols with a stateful server per viewer. HTTP adaptive streaming won because segments are plain files: every HTTP cache, CDN and load balancer already knows how to serve them, they pass through firewalls, they can be fetched over HTTP/2 or HTTP/3 (net8), and a failed request is simply retried, perhaps from a different server. The cost is per-request overhead and a latency floor of a few segment lengths for live, which low-latency variants reduce with partial segments.

Segment length is a trade-off you can compute

Shorter segments let the player react sooner and start faster: with 2-second segments the first frame needs only 2 seconds of video downloaded, and a bandwidth drop is answered within 2 seconds. But every segment is an HTTP request, and every segment must begin with a keyframe, which costs far more bits than the frames that follow it. Work the request rate: at 20 million concurrent streams, 2-second segments mean 10 million segment requests per second across the edge fleet; 6-second segments mean about 3.3 million. Per appliance at 200 Gbps serving 5 Mbps streams (40,000 streams), that is 20,000 versus 6,700 requests per second — manageable either way, but the difference shows up in CPU, logs and TLS work. Video on demand therefore tends toward segments of 4-6 seconds, while live, where latency matters, uses short segments or partial segments and pays the overhead. Keyframe spacing must match: if keyframes are every 2 seconds, segments can be any multiple of 2.

The metric that matters is measured on the client

Server logs show bytes sent; they cannot see a frozen picture on a phone. Quality of experience is a composite measured in the player — time to first frame, rebuffer ratio (stall time over play time), average delivered bitrate, switches per hour, and play failures — and every session reports it. That telemetry is itself a large stream-processing pipeline (sc18) with tens of thousands of events per second per million viewers, and it is what drives decisions such as which ABR change to ship, which ISP needs more appliances, and which title failed to fill.

Per-shot and beyond

Per-title encoding chooses one ladder per title, but difficulty varies within a title: a dialogue scene needs fewer bits than an explosion. Encoding each shot with its own settings (Netflix later described a "dynamic optimizer" that does this) pushes the same idea further: spend bits where viewers would notice. The pattern is general — measure perceived quality with a metric that tracks human judgement (Netflix built VMAF for this), and optimise bits against it rather than against a fixed recipe.

User-generated content changes the economics

For a platform where anyone can upload, most videos are watched a handful of times. Fill-ahead is impossible, cache space is spent on a head that changes hourly, and encoding budgets must be tiny for the tail. Typical answers: encode a minimal ladder on upload (perhaps two or three rungs in one codec), cache on demand at the edge with a tiered hierarchy, and promote videos that cross a view threshold to a full ladder with better codecs. Popularity prediction from early view velocity decides what gets the expensive treatment.

The real case: Netflix Open Connect

Netflix started Open Connect in 2011 when its traffic became a significant share of consumer ISP traffic. Its own overview gives two reasons: to work directly with ISPs, and to build "a proactive, directed caching solution that is much more efficient than the standard demand-driven CDN solution", reducing upstream demand "by several orders of magnitude". The building blocks are purpose-built cache servers called Open Connect Appliances (OCAs), deployed in two ways: at internet exchange points, connected to ISPs by settlement-free peering, and free of charge inside qualifying ISPs' networks, where Netflix supplies the hardware and the ISP supplies power, space and connectivity. The current storage appliance is a 2U server listed at about 200 Gbps of throughput and up to 120 TB of raw storage, drawing about 400 W at peak; a lower-cost "global" appliance for smaller partners does about 80 Gbps with up to 60 TB.

The division of labour matches this lesson's control and data planes exactly. OCAs store no member data — no viewing history, DRM information or account data. They do two things: report their health, the BGP routes they have learned and which files they hold to control-plane services in AWS; and serve files over HTTP and HTTPS. When a member presses play, playback services in AWS check authorisation and licensing, work out which files the device needs given its characteristics and network conditions, ask the steering service to pick suitable OCAs, and hand the client URLs; the OCA then serves the files. Fill happens during off-peak windows "because we can predict with high accuracy what our members will watch and what time of day they will watch it", and OCAs can fill from each other to spare backbone capacity.

Two Netflix publications show the encoding and playback halves. The December 2015 per-title encoding post documented the fixed ten-rung ladder (235 to 5,800 kbps) used since late 2010, why it was wrong for both simple and complex content, and the convex-hull method that replaced it, with the BoJack Horseman (1080p at 1,540 kbps instead of 480p at 1,750) and Orange Is the New Black (top rung 4,640 instead of 5,800 kbps, 20% less) examples. The 2014 SIGCOMM paper with Stanford tested buffer-based adaptation on over half a million real users per arm and cut rebuffering 10-20% at the same average bitrate.

It is worth doing the capacity arithmetic from the ISP's side, because that is how these deployments are negotiated. An ISP whose subscribers pull 400 Gbps from the service at peak could host two storage appliances at about 200 Gbps each, but would want at least three for redundancy, so losing one does not push a third of the traffic onto its peering links. If those appliances hold the most-watched part of the catalogue for that country and are filled nightly, the great majority of bytes never leave the ISP's network; the remainder flows from appliances at a nearby exchange point. Both sides gain: viewers get lower latency and fewer stalls, and the ISP carries less traffic over links it pays for. That alignment of incentives, rather than any clever algorithm, is why embedding caches in ISPs works at all.

Failure modes and what to monitor

Fill misses: a title not on the appliances where it is wanted shows up as rising origin and peering egress in one region and higher start-up time for that title; monitor fill completeness for scheduled releases before their release time. Hot appliances: steering must spread load; watch per-appliance throughput against capacity and the share of sessions falling back to a second URL. Player regressions: a new app version with a buggy ABR shows up as a rise in rebuffer ratio or switches for one device class; roll out player changes as A/B tests with stall-rate guardrails. License service errors: play failures with bytes still flowing for existing sessions. Encoding defects: automated quality checks on every rendition, because a corrupt rung may only be fetched by some devices on some networks and is otherwise invisible until complaints arrive.

Goal: watch two adaptive-bitrate strategies handle the same bumpy network, then compute CDN hit ratios under Zipf popularity. Python 3.12, standard library, runs in under a second.

"""Adaptive bitrate: throughput-based vs buffer-based (BBA-style) on a bumpy network,
then the CDN hit ratio of caching the top-k titles under Zipf popularity. Python 3.12 stdlib."""
import math, random

LADDER = [235, 375, 560, 750, 1050, 1750, 2350, 3000, 4300, 5800]   # kbps, Netflix 2015 fixed ladder
SEG = 4.0                                                          # seconds of video per segment

random.seed(3)
def network(t):                       # kbps available at time t: good, a 60 s dip, then noisy
    base = 6000 if t < 120 or t > 180 else 900
    return max(200, random.gauss(base, base * 0.35))

def throughput_rule(buffer, last_kbps):
    """Pick the highest rung below 80% of the last measured throughput."""
    safe = 0.8 * last_kbps
    return max([r for r in LADDER if r <= safe], default=LADDER[0])

def buffer_rule(buffer, last_kbps, reservoir=10, cushion=40):
    """Map buffer occupancy to a rung: lowest below the reservoir, highest above it + cushion."""
    if buffer <= reservoir:
        return LADDER[0]
    frac = min(1.0, (buffer - reservoir) / cushion)
    return LADDER[int(frac * (len(LADDER) - 1))]

def play(rule, minutes=6):
    t, buffer, last, stall, switches, prev, bits = 0.0, 0.0, 3000.0, 0.0, 0, None, []
    while t < minutes * 60:
        rate = rule(buffer, last)
        switches += prev is not None and rate != prev
        prev = rate
        bw = network(t)
        dl = rate * SEG / bw                        # seconds to download one segment
        if dl > buffer:                             # buffer drains to zero: playback stalls
            stall += dl - buffer
        buffer = max(0.0, buffer - dl) + SEG
        buffer = min(buffer, 60.0)                  # player stops fetching at a 60 s buffer
        t += dl
        last = bw
        bits.append(rate)
    return sum(bits) / len(bits), stall, switches

for name, rule in [("throughput-based", throughput_rule), ("buffer-based", buffer_rule)]:
    avg, stall, sw = play(rule)
    print(f"{name:>17}: avg {avg:5.0f} kbps | rebuffering {stall:5.1f} s | switches {sw}")

# CDN hit ratio: N titles with Zipf(s=1) popularity; an edge caches the k most popular.
N = 20_000
H = lambda n: sum(1 / i for i in range(1, n + 1))
for k in (200, 2_000, 5_000):
    approx = (math.log(k) + 0.5772) / (math.log(N) + 0.5772)
    print(f"cache top {k:>5} of {N}: hit ratio {H(k) / H(N):.3f} (ln approximation {approx:.3f})")
  1. Run it. With seed 3 the throughput rule averages about 3,150 kbps with about 9 seconds of rebuffering and 64 switches; the buffer rule averages about 3,640 kbps with almost no rebuffering and 43 switches. The network has a 60-second dip to about 900 kbps starting at 2 minutes.
  2. Read play() to see the buffer arithmetic: each 4-second segment takes rate × 4 ÷ bandwidth seconds to download, drains the buffer by that much, then adds 4 seconds. A stall happens when the download takes longer than the buffer holds.
  3. Change the seed to 1, 2, 4 and 5. Buffer-based usually wins on stalls and switches, but not always (seed 2 hits a long dip that drains even its buffer). ABR is a policy under uncertainty, which is why Netflix tested it with A/B experiments rather than by argument.
  4. Shrink reservoir to 2 seconds: the buffer rule becomes greedy and starts stalling during the dip. Grow cushion to 50: fewer switches, slower climb.
  5. Read the hit-ratio lines: caching 10% of titles serves 78% of requests, 25% serves 87%, and the logarithmic approximation matches the exact sum — the by-hand formula from deep dive B is safe to use in an interview.
Work it out

A service has 150M subscribers. At peak, 12% are watching. The bitrate mix is 30% mobile at 1.5 Mbps, 45% HD at 5 Mbps, 25% 4K at 15 Mbps. Edge appliances serve 92% of bytes; the rest comes from regional origin. Transit bandwidth costs £0.008 per GB.

(a) What is peak egress? (b) What does the origin traffic cost per month? (c) Per-title encoding could cut average bitrate 20%. What is that worth, and what does it cost you?

Answer

(a) Peak egress. Concurrent viewers: 150M × 0.12 = 18M. Average bitrate: (0.30 × 1.5) + (0.45 × 5) + (0.25 × 15) = 0.45 + 2.25 + 3.75 = 6.45 Mbps. Total: 18M × 6.45 Mbps = 116 Tbps.

Worth saying out loud: 4K is 25% of viewers and 58% of the bits. Any conversation about bandwidth cost is really a conversation about 4K.

(b) Origin cost. 8% of 116 Tbps = 9.3 Tbps at peak. Peak is not constant — assume the daily average is about 40% of peak, so about 3.7 Tbps sustained. Convert to bytes: 3.7 Tbps ÷ 8 = 462 GB/s. Over a month: 462 GB/s × 2.6M s ≈ 1.2 billion GB (1.2 exabytes). At £0.008/GB that is about £9.6M a month — for the 8% that misses the edge. Sanity check the unit conversions out loud; an off-by-8 between bits and bytes is the most common error here.

That number reframes everything: pushing the edge hit rate from 92% to 96% halves it, saving nearly £5M a month. Edge hit rate is the single most valuable metric in this system, and it is worth stating that you would instrument it per region and per title.

(c) Per-title encoding. A 20% bitrate reduction cuts both edge and origin bytes by 20%. On the origin bill alone that is ~£1.9M/month, plus proportional savings on edge appliance capacity and ISP peering. Across a year it is comfortably eight figures.

What it costs you: per-title analysis means transcoding each title multiple times to find its optimal ladder, so encoding compute rises substantially — plausibly 2–5× — and the pipeline gets slower and more complex. But encoding is a one-time cost per title while bandwidth is paid on every stream, so for anything with meaningful viewership the payback is immediate. The nuance to volunteer: this logic inverts for user-generated content, where most uploads are watched a handful of times and expensive per-title optimisation would never pay back. Encode the head carefully, the tail cheaply — and that asymmetry is the real design principle.

Estimate it

An ISP's embedded appliances have room for 4,000 of your 40,000 titles (assume equal sizes and Zipf popularity with exponent 1). Its subscribers pull 400 Gbps from you at peak. How much crosses the ISP's peering links, and what happens if you double the cache?

Answer

H(4,000) ≈ ln 4,000 + 0.577 = 8.29 + 0.58 = 8.87. H(40,000) ≈ 10.60 + 0.58 = 11.18. Hit ratio ≈ 8.87 ÷ 11.18 ≈ 79%, so about 21% of 400 Gbps = 84 Gbps crosses peering at peak. Doubling to 8,000 titles: H(8,000) ≈ 8.99 + 0.58 = 9.57, ratio ≈ 86%, so about 58 Gbps — a third less upstream traffic for twice the disks. Then say what beats doubling: fill by this ISP's predicted demand rather than global rank, and pre-position scheduled releases, because real popularity is more concentrated and more predictable than a static Zipf curve.
