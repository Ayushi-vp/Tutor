Asha opens a ride app outside a train station at 6 pm. Within a second she sees little car icons gliding around the map; within five seconds of tapping "request" she is told a driver will arrive in four minutes, and she can watch the car approach. Meanwhile, the app on that driver's phone has been sending its position every few seconds for hours, as have a million other drivers' phones across hundreds of cities.

Behind that small experience sits a query every interviewer loves: "find the available drivers nearest to this point". It looks like a database query, and it is the hardest part of the design for one reason — the data moves. Each driver is a point that changes every four seconds, so the system must absorb hundreds of thousands of position updates per second while answering proximity searches in milliseconds. And then it must turn a search result into a commitment: one driver, one rider, no double booking, even when dozens of riders at a stadium are all trying to get the same few cars.

Those two halves pull in opposite directions. Driver positions are numerous, fast-changing and individually worthless after a few seconds — perfect for memory, approximate and eventually consistent. Trips are few, slow-changing and each one is money, safety and a legal record — they need strict, transactional correctness. A good design keeps them apart and joins them with a matching step that is careful exactly where it must be.

This lesson follows the interview flow: requirements; estimates (location writes per second, matching load, memory, history storage, bandwidth); API and data model; high-level design with the critical path of a ride request; deep dives on the geospatial index (geohash, S2 and H3, worked by hand), on matching and contention, and on ETA and surge pricing; then failure modes and trade-offs. The real case is Uber's own description of H3, its hexagonal grid, and the lab builds geohash encoding, neighbour search and a nearest-driver query you can check against brute force.

The mental model: a radio dispatcher with a gridded map

Picture an old taxi company. On the wall is a city map divided into numbered squares. Each driver radios in every few minutes — "car 17, square C4" — and the dispatcher moves a pin. When a customer calls from square C4, the dispatcher does not scan the whole map: they look at the pins in C4 and the squares touching it, pick the car that can get there fastest (knowing the river and the one-way streets), and radio that driver: "pickup at the station, can you take it?" If the driver says yes, the dispatcher writes the job in the ledger — and crosses that car off as busy so nobody else is sent the same car.

Every piece maps across. The gridded map is a geospatial index: points bucketed into cells so "what is near here?" means "what is in these few cells?". The radio check-ins are location updates. Looking at neighbouring squares is neighbour search. Knowing the river is ETA on the road graph. "Can you take it?" is an offer. The ledger is the trip state machine, and crossing a car off is an atomic claim.

Where the analogy breaks: the dispatcher has one map and one ledger. We have a million drivers updating every four seconds, hundreds of requests per second, and many matching servers working at once — so the map must be sharded across machines and the "cross off" must be atomic, or two servers will send the same car to two people.

Two dimensions into one

Databases are excellent at one-dimensional lookups: "rows with key between X and Y" or "row with key K". A location has two dimensions, and an index on latitude cannot also narrow by longitude. The trick every geospatial scheme uses is to turn a 2-D position into a 1-D cell id such that nearby points usually get the same or related ids. Then an ordinary hash map or sorted index answers proximity queries. Three schemes are worth knowing. Geohash interleaves bits of longitude and latitude and writes them as a short string, giving rectangles. S2 (Google) projects the Earth onto the six faces of a cube and orders square-ish cells along a Hilbert curve, a space-filling path that keeps neighbours close. H3 (Uber) tiles the Earth with hexagons at 16 resolutions.

Why the obvious designs fail

Two tempting first answers both break on the numbers. The first is "store each driver's latest position in a table and query by latitude and longitude ranges". At 250,000 updates per second, every update rewrites an indexed row on disk, and the proximity query still scans a stripe of the planet because a B-tree can only narrow one coordinate at a time. The second is "keep all drivers in one server's memory and loop over them". A million records fit easily, but a linear scan per request at hundreds of requests per second is a quarter of a billion distance computations per second, and one server is one failure away from a city with no rides. Bucketing by cell fixes both: an update touches one small in-memory record, a search touches a handful of cells, and cells spread naturally across machines by geography.

A reader who stops here has the right picture: bucket drivers into cells in memory, search a rider's cell and its neighbours, rank a short list by arrival time, offer the trip to one driver at a time, and record the result in a small, strict state machine.

1 · Requirements

Functional. Drivers go online and their app reports location continuously. A rider requests a ride from a pickup to a destination and sees a price and pickup ETA. The system matches the request to a driver, who can accept or decline. Rider and driver track each other live; the trip moves through pickup, in progress and completion, and is priced at the end. Out of scope, said aloud: payments (a separate design; mention pre-authorisation and capture), driver onboarding, food delivery and shared rides (pooling is a much harder optimisation, worth naming and setting aside).

Non-functional. A match within about 5 seconds of a request. Driver positions visible to riders within 2-3 seconds. A trip is never assigned to two drivers and a driver never has two trips. A completed trip is never lost (it is money and a legal record). A failure in one region does not affect others. Location data is sensitive personal data: access is controlled and audited.

The clarifying question: city-scale or global? It matters because rides are almost entirely local — nobody is matched across continents — so the system shards naturally by geography, which is a gift.

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Assume 5 million drivers, 20% online at peak, each reporting every 4 seconds; 15 million rides a day.

Location writes. 5M × 0.2 = 1 million online drivers. 1M ÷ 4 s = 250,000 location updates per second. This is the dominant load, and it is all writes.

Ride requests. 15M ÷ 86,400 ≈ 174 per second on average; with a 3× rush-hour peak, about 520 per second. Say the contrast out loud: location writes outnumber ride requests about 500 to 1, which tells the interviewer where the next forty minutes go.

Busy drivers at peak. If pickup plus trip averages 25 minutes (1,500 s), peak trips in progress ≈ 520 × 1,500 ≈ 780,000 — most of the million online drivers, which is what a healthy marketplace at rush hour looks like.

Live tracking pushes. Each rider on a trip receives the driver's position every 4 seconds: 780,000 ÷ 4 ≈ 195,000 pushes per second, carried over long-lived connections.

Matching work. Each request scans a few cells and might find a few hundred candidates (the lab scans 271); computing straight-line distance for each is cheap: 520 × 300 ≈ 156,000 distance computations per second. ETA queries on the road graph are expensive, so only the best 10 or so get one: 520 × 10 ≈ 5,200 routing queries per second.

Memory for current positions. One record per driver — id, latitude, longitude, cell id, heading, speed, status, timestamp — is about 100 bytes. 1M × 100 B = 100 MB. The entire live state of the world's drivers fits in the memory of one laptop; it is the write rate, not the size, that needs a fleet.

Bandwidth. An update with protocol overhead is about 200-300 bytes: 250,000 × 300 B = 75 MB/s, about 600 Mbit/s inbound across all regions. Modest.

History. 250,000 × 86,400 = 21.6 billion points a day. Compressed to about 50 bytes each, that is about 1.1 TB per day and roughly 400 TB a year — streamed asynchronously to cheap object storage for analytics, disputes and model training, never written synchronously.

3 · The API

Driver app, over a persistent connection:
  → LOCATION {lat, lng, heading, speed, ts}            every 4 s
  ← OFFER    {trip_id, pickup, eta, expires_in: 15}
  → ACCEPT / DECLINE {trip_id}
  → TRIP_EVENT {trip_id, event: arrived|started|completed, idempotency_key}

Rider app:
  POST /v1/quotes   {pickup, dropoff}                     → {quote_id, price, eta, expires_at}
  POST /v1/trips    {quote_id}  Idempotency-Key: <uuid>   → {trip_id, state: "requested"}
  GET  /v1/trips/{id}           (and a push channel for state and driver position)
  POST /v1/trips/{id}/cancel

The idempotency key on trip creation is not decoration: a rider tapping "request" on a flaky connection, losing the response and tapping again must not create two trips with two drivers driving towards them. The server stores the key with the trip and returns the existing trip on a retry.

4 · The data model

driver_state (in memory, sharded by cell)
  driver_id → {lat, lng, cell, heading, status: available|offered|on_trip, offer_expires, ts}
cell_index  (in memory)
  cell_id → set of available driver_ids
trips (transactional database, sharded by city)
  trip_id PK, rider_id, driver_id, state, pickup, dropoff, quote, created_at, idempotency_key UNIQUE
trip_events (append-only)
  trip_id, seq, event, actor, at
location_history (object storage, partitioned by day and city)

The trip state is a strict state machine: requested → matching → driver_assigned → driver_arrived → in_progress → completed, with cancelled reachable from several states and nothing reachable from completed. Each transition is a conditional update ("set state to driver_arrived where state is driver_assigned") plus an appended event, in one transaction. This is where correctness lives: a trip is a fare, an insurance record and possibly evidence, not a row to be updated freely.

5 · High-level design and the critical path

Walk a request through the sequence diagram. Asha's app has a quote with a price and an ETA. She taps request; the trip service creates the trip in state matching (idempotently) and hands it to the matching service for her city. Matching computes her cell, reads the drivers in that cell and its neighbours from the location index (a few in-memory lookups, around a millisecond), filters to available drivers heading in a sensible direction, ranks them by straight-line distance, and asks the ETA service for road-network ETAs for the best ten (tens of milliseconds). It picks the best by ETA and tries to claim that driver: an atomic compare-and-set on the driver's state from available to offered with a 15-second expiry. If the claim fails — another matching worker got there first — it tries the next candidate. If it succeeds, it pushes an offer to the driver's phone.

The driver has 15 seconds. On accept, the trip service runs one transaction: trip matching → driver_assigned with the driver id, driver offered → on_trip, and an event appended. Asha's app receives the assignment and starts showing the driver's live position. On decline or timeout, the soft lock expires or is released and matching offers the next candidate. Under good conditions the whole sequence takes a few seconds, most of it the driver's reaction time.

6 · Deep dive A: the geospatial index, worked by hand

Geohash. Start with longitude range −180 to 180 and latitude range −90 to 90. Take the rider at latitude 12.9716, longitude 77.5946 (central Bengaluru). First bit, longitude: the midpoint is 0; 77.59 ≥ 0, so write 1 and keep 0 to 180. Second bit, latitude: midpoint 0; 12.97 ≥ 0, write 1, keep 0 to 90. Third, longitude: midpoint 90; 77.59 < 90, write 0, keep 0 to 90. Fourth, latitude: midpoint 45; 12.97 < 45, write 0, keep 0 to 45. Fifth, longitude: midpoint 45; 77.59 ≥ 45, write 1, keep 45 to 90. The first five bits are 11001 = 25, and the 26th character of the base32 alphabet 0123456789bcdefghjkmnpqrstuvwxyz is t. Continue and the six-character geohash is tdr1v9. Each character adds 5 bits, alternating which coordinate gets 3 of them, so a 6-character cell is 215 slices of longitude by 215 of latitude: 360° ÷ 32,768 ≈ 0.011° of longitude and 180° ÷ 32,768 ≈ 0.0055° of latitude — about 1.19 km wide and 0.61 km tall near the equator. A 5-character cell is about 4.9 km square; a 7-character cell about 150 m square.

The index is a map from cell to the set of available drivers in it. Because a geohash is a prefix code, cells also nest: everything in tdr1v9 is in tdr1v. A sorted index (Redis's geospatial commands store a 52-bit geohash as a sorted-set score) can answer "everything with this prefix" as a range scan.

The boundary trap. Geohash orders cells along a Z-shaped curve (bit interleaving is Z-order or Morton order). Cells adjacent on the map are usually close in the order but not always: at a high-level boundary, two points metres apart can differ from the first character. In the lab, points 22 m apart across the longitude 78.75° line hash to tdrczf and tf21b4. So a search always takes the rider's cell and its eight neighbours, computed by stepping one cell width or height from the centre and re-encoding.

When is the 3×3 block enough? The rider is somewhere inside the centre cell, so the block extends at least one full cell (0.61 km in the short direction) from the rider in every direction. If the k-th nearest driver found inside the block is closer than that, no driver outside can beat it, and the answer is exact. In the lab the fifth-nearest is 0.24 km away, so the 271 drivers scanned give exactly the same five as a brute-force scan of all 20,000. If the k-th nearest is farther, widen to the next ring of cells.

H3. Uber's grid starts from an icosahedron (a 20-sided solid) wrapped around the Earth, lays 122 base cells over it — 110 hexagons and 12 pentagons, because a sphere cannot be tiled with hexagons alone; the pentagons sit at the icosahedron's vertices, positioned in the oceans — and subdivides each cell about seven ways per level, across 16 resolutions (0 to 15). Each finer resolution has about one seventh the area: resolution 7 averages 5.16 km², resolution 8 0.74 km², resolution 9 0.105 km². The number of cells at resolution r is 2 + 120 × 7r, so resolution 8 has 2 + 120 × 5,764,801 ≈ 692 million cells worldwide. A cell id is a 64-bit integer from which the parent at any coarser resolution can be read by truncation. Neighbour search uses a k-ring: all cells within grid distance k, which is 1 cell for k = 0, 7 for k = 1 and 19 for k = 2 (in general 3k² + 3k + 1).

Sizing. A city of about 740 km² at resolution 8 is about 1,000 cells. With 40,000 online drivers that is about 40 per cell on average — far more downtown, a handful in the suburbs. A k = 1 search (7 cells) downtown returns a few hundred candidates; in the suburbs it may return none, so the search widens ring by ring until it has enough candidates or hits a maximum radius.

Updates. A driver moving at 36 km/h covers 40 m in 4 seconds, while a resolution-8 cell is about 1 km across, so the great majority of updates leave the driver in the same cell. The common case is overwriting a record in place; only a cell change touches two sets (remove from old, add to new). That is why 250,000 updates per second is affordable on a modest fleet. The index is sharded by cell (or by city, then cell), so a shard's work is proportional to the drivers in its cells, and very dense downtown cells are spread across shards deliberately.

S2 deserves one sentence: it projects the sphere onto a cube, subdivides each face into a quadtree of cells, and numbers them along a Hilbert curve, which keeps neighbours closer in the 1-D order than geohash's Z-order. Google uses it widely; it is a fine answer if you can explain it.

7 · Deep dive B: matching, contention and offers

Ranking. Straight-line distance is only a filter. The rider waits for arrival time, which depends on the road network, one-way streets, turns, traffic and which side of a motorway the driver is on. So matching computes road-network ETAs for a short list. Routing over a city's road graph in tens of milliseconds requires preprocessing (techniques such as contraction hierarchies precompute shortcuts so a query explores a tiny part of the graph), and the time estimate is corrected by a learned model trained on real trips.

Contention. Several matching workers can pick the same best driver at the same moment, especially in a busy area. The claim must be atomic: a compare-and-set on the driver's state that succeeds only if it is still available, setting it to offered with an expiry. The loser moves to its next candidate. This is the same race as two people booking one seat, and naming the pattern shows you recognise it.

Offers, not assignments. Drivers can decline or not respond, so a trip is offered to one driver at a time with a timeout (commonly 10-15 seconds). The soft lock with an expiry means a driver who drives into a tunnel during the offer becomes available again automatically. Each decline costs the rider seconds, so a good ranking that includes acceptance likelihood matters.

Greedy versus batched. Greedy matching assigns each request the moment it arrives. It is simple and fast, but it can give the only nearby driver to a rider who had other good options, leaving the next rider stranded. Batched matching collects requests in an area for a short window (a couple of seconds), builds a cost matrix of ETAs between waiting riders and available drivers, and solves an assignment problem to minimise total wait. Riders wait a moment longer to be matched and the marketplace as a whole does better, particularly during spikes.

8 · Deep dive C: ETA, surge and the location stream

Uber's H3 post explains why hexagons were chosen in the first place: Uber calculates surge pricing by measuring supply and demand in hexagons in each city, and hexagons minimise the quantisation error as people move through a city and make radius approximations easy. Surge is computed from the location and request streams: a stream processor counts available drivers and open requests per cell over a sliding window (say, the last two minutes), computes a ratio, maps it to a multiplier, and then smooths it — across neighbouring cells, because demand does not stop at a hexagon edge, and across time, because surge is a feedback loop. Higher prices reduce demand and draw drivers in, which lowers the ratio, which lowers the price; a controller without smoothing and limits on how fast prices move will oscillate.

The same location stream feeds ETA models (recent speeds on each road segment), driver positioning suggestions, fraud checks (GPS spoofing shows up as impossible jumps) and the history archive. The location index itself does not need to be durable: if a shard restarts, it is fully rebuilt within one 4-second reporting interval.

9 · Bottlenecks, failure modes and trade-offs

Location shard loss: a few seconds of degraded matching in those cells, then full recovery from fresh updates; do not add synchronous replication for data with a 4-second life — say this is deliberate. Network loss on the driver's phone mid-trip: the app buffers locations and events and uploads on reconnect; trip transitions are explicit idempotent events, so gaps in location do not corrupt the trip. Hot cells: stadium exits and airports concentrate requests and drivers; split them across shards, switch to batched matching and cap search radius. Region failure: rides are local, so regions fail independently; trips in progress must be recoverable from the trip database replica in another zone. Stuck trips: a trip that sits in a state too long (a driver's phone died at pickup) needs timeouts and a reconciliation job.

The trade-offs to close on: in-memory, unreplicated location data for speed versus a few seconds of blindness after a crash; hexagons (uniform neighbours, good for statistics) versus geohash (simple, sortable strings) versus S2; greedy versus batched matching (latency per rider versus marketplace efficiency); and strict transactions only at the claim and the assignment. Close with the split you opened with: a high-write, eventually consistent location plane where staleness is harmless, and a low-volume, strongly consistent trip plane where a race is a disaster.

Why hexagons matter, beyond looks

Uber's post makes two concrete arguments. First, distance: a hexagon's centre is the same distance from all six neighbours' centres, while a square has two neighbour distances (edge and corner) and a triangle three. A k-ring of hexagons therefore approximates a circle, and "expand by one ring" adds the same distance in every direction. Second, analysis: per-cell statistics — supply, demand, surge — and smoothing between neighbours are cleaner when every neighbour is equivalent; with squares you need separate weights for edge and corner neighbours. The costs are real too: children only approximately nest inside parents (a hexagon cannot be split exactly into seven hexagons), so truncating an id to a parent introduces small shape distortion; and the 12 pentagons per resolution have five neighbours, which code must handle even though they sit in oceans.

Resolution is a tuning decision with a failure mode at each end

Cells too large and every search scans thousands of irrelevant drivers and every cell is a hot shard; too small and a search must union many cells and still finds nobody in sparse areas. The density difference within one city can be a hundredfold. Mature systems choose resolution by local density, or index at a fine resolution and search upward through parents (cheap with H3 because parent ids come from truncation), stopping as soon as enough candidates are found. A useful rule is to aim for tens of drivers per searched cell at the busiest times.

ETA is a machine-learning system on top of a graph algorithm

A shortest-path search over the road graph gives a route and a naive time from speed limits. The real time depends on live traffic, time of day, weather, turn penalties, traffic lights and how long pickup takes at a particular building (airports and malls are notorious). Production ETA combines a fast routing engine — preprocessed so each query explores only a small part of the graph — with a model that corrects the routing estimate using features from recent trips. ETA error directly affects matching quality, rider trust (quoted versus actual) and pricing, so it is monitored per city and time of day.

The marketplace is a control system

Surge raises prices, which suppresses demand and attracts supply, which lowers surge — a feedback loop. Tuned badly, prices flap up and down every minute, which riders and regulators both hate. The engineering tools are the classic ones from control: smoothing (an exponentially weighted average of the ratio), hysteresis (require a bigger change to drop the price than to raise it), rate limits on how fast the multiplier moves, spatial smoothing across neighbouring cells, and caps. Framing surge this way, rather than as "multiply price by demand over supply", is a genuinely senior answer.

Idempotency on every state-changing mobile call

Mobile networks drop responses constantly. A rider's "request" may reach the server while the reply is lost; a driver's "completed" tap may be sent twice. Every state-changing call carries a client-generated idempotency key stored with the result: a retry returns the original result instead of creating a second trip or charging twice. Combined with conditional state transitions ("only move to completed from in_progress"), this makes the trip state machine safe to drive from unreliable clients.

Location privacy and audit

Location history is precise movement data about real people. It is needed for disputes, safety investigations, insurance and model training, so it cannot simply be discarded; and it must not be freely queryable either. Store it with access controls, audit every access, minimise retention where law and purpose allow, and keep rider and driver identities separable from movement traces for analytics. Raising this unprompted distinguishes a senior answer, because misuse of internal location tools has caused real harm and regulatory action in this industry.

Batched assignment, concretely

Suppose three riders and three drivers are waiting in an area, with ETAs (minutes) rider A: 2, 3, 9; rider B: 3, 8, 9; rider C: 4, 9, 4 to drivers 1, 2, 3. Greedy in arrival order gives A → driver 1 (2), B → driver 2 (8), C → driver 3 (4): total 14 minutes. Solving the batch gives A → driver 2 (3), B → driver 1 (3), C → driver 3 (4): total 10 minutes, with nobody waiting 8. The Hungarian algorithm solves such a matrix exactly in polynomial time; at city scale, batches are small per area, so this is cheap. The cost is a short collection window before anyone is matched.

The real case: Uber's H3

Uber published H3 in 2018 as "our grid system for efficiently optimizing ride pricing and dispatch, for visualizing and exploring spatial data". Its post is explicit about the problem: analysing marketplace events at the exact point where each happens is "very difficult and expensive", while analysis over areas is practical, so Uber buckets events into cells. Its example is the one in this lesson: "we calculate surge pricing by measuring supply and demand in hexagons in each city that we serve."

The design choices follow from that use. Hexagons were chosen because "people in a city are often in motion, and hexagons minimize the quantization error introduced when users move through a city", and because a hexagon has one centre-to-neighbour distance where squares have two and triangles three. A plain Mercator projection with square cells was rejected because Mercator distorts areas badly and squares "require multiple sets of coefficients" for analysis. H3 instead projects the Earth onto an icosahedron using gnomonic projections centred on its faces, lays 122 base cells over it (ten per face, some shared), and introduces twelve pentagons at the vertices — oriented, following Buckminster Fuller's icosahedron, so that all vertices fall in water. It supports sixteen resolutions, each about one seventh the area of the previous; cell ids are 64-bit integers that can be truncated to find ancestors; and a kRing function returns cells within grid distance k, approximating circles. The library is open source, and its documentation publishes the per-resolution statistics used in this lesson (resolution 8 averages 0.737 km², resolution 9 0.105 km²).

The lesson for an interview: Uber's own description puts the grid at the centre of pricing and marketplace analysis, with neighbour rings as the primitive for "near here". You can use the same primitive for candidate search, but be careful not to claim more about Uber's internal dispatch than it has published.

The other public lesson: location data is a liability

In August 2017 the US Federal Trade Commission announced a settlement with Uber over its privacy and security claims. According to the FTC, after news reports in 2014 that employees were improperly accessing rider and driver data, Uber said access was strictly limited and closely monitored; it built an automated monitoring system in December 2014 but stopped using it less than a year later, and for more than nine months afterwards rarely monitored internal access. The complaint also said Uber let engineers use a single access key with full administrative access to cloud data, without multi-factor authentication, and stored sensitive information including geolocation in plain readable text in database backups; an intruder accessed more than 100,000 drivers' names and licence numbers in May 2014. The settlement required a comprehensive privacy programme with independent audits every two years for 20 years. For the design, that translates into concrete requirements: per-engineer credentials, encrypted backups, access logging and review for location history, and least-privilege tooling — not optional extras.

Failure modes and what to monitor

Time to match and match rate per city and per cell type (downtown, suburb, airport): the core product metric. Offer acceptance rate and decline reasons: falling acceptance means ranking is sending drivers trips they do not want. Claim conflict rate (failed compare-and-sets): rising contention signals a spike that batched matching should handle. Location ingestion lag and per-shard update rate: skewed shards show up here first. ETA error distribution, quoted versus actual, by city and hour. Stuck trips: trips in a state longer than expected. Surge stability: how often a cell's multiplier changes direction, to catch oscillation. Access to location history: who queried which person's movements, and why.

Goal: implement geohash encoding and neighbours, build a cell index over 20,000 simulated drivers, and check that searching 9 cells finds the same nearest drivers as a brute-force scan. Python 3.12, standard library, under a second.

"""Geohash encode, neighbours, and a nearest-driver search vs brute force. Python 3.12 stdlib."""
import math, random
from collections import defaultdict

B32 = "0123456789bcdefghjkmnpqrstuvwxyz"

def encode(lat, lng, precision=6):
    lat_r, lng_r, bits, even, out = [-90.0, 90.0], [-180.0, 180.0], [], True, []
    while len(out) < precision:
        rng, val = (lng_r, lng) if even else (lat_r, lat)   # bits alternate: lng, lat, lng, ...
        mid = (rng[0] + rng[1]) / 2
        bits.append(int(val >= mid))
        rng[0 if val >= mid else 1] = mid
        even = not even
        if len(bits) == 5:                                  # 5 bits -> one base32 character
            out.append(B32[int("".join(map(str, bits)), 2)]); bits = []
    return "".join(out)

def bbox(gh):
    lat_r, lng_r, even = [-90.0, 90.0], [-180.0, 180.0], True
    for ch in gh:
        for b in f"{B32.index(ch):05b}":
            rng = lng_r if even else lat_r
            rng[0 if b == "1" else 1] = (rng[0] + rng[1]) / 2
            even = not even
    return lat_r, lng_r

def neighbours(gh):
    """The 8 surrounding cells: step one cell height/width from the centre and re-encode."""
    (la0, la1), (lo0, lo1) = bbox(gh)
    clat, clng, dlat, dlng = (la0 + la1) / 2, (lo0 + lo1) / 2, la1 - la0, lo1 - lo0
    return [encode(clat + i * dlat, clng + j * dlng, len(gh))
            for i in (-1, 0, 1) for j in (-1, 0, 1) if (i, j) != (0, 0)]

def km(a, b):                                               # haversine distance
    p1, p2 = math.radians(a[0]), math.radians(b[0])
    h = math.sin((p2 - p1) / 2) ** 2 + math.cos(p1) * math.cos(p2) * math.sin(math.radians(b[1] - a[1]) / 2) ** 2
    return 2 * 6371 * math.asin(math.sqrt(h))

if __name__ == "__main__":
    rider = (12.9716, 77.5946)                              # central Bengaluru
    cell = encode(*rider)
    (la0, la1), (lo0, lo1) = bbox(cell)
    print("rider cell", cell, f"≈ {km((la0, lo0), (la1, lo0)):.2f} km tall × {km((la0, lo0), (la0, lo1)):.2f} km wide")
    print("neighbours", neighbours(cell))

    random.seed(0)
    drivers = {d: (rider[0] + random.uniform(-0.1, 0.1), rider[1] + random.uniform(-0.1, 0.1))
               for d in range(20_000)}
    index = defaultdict(set)                                # cell -> driver ids (the geo index)
    for d, pos in drivers.items():
        index[encode(*pos)].add(d)

    cand = set().union(*(index[c] for c in [cell, *neighbours(cell)]))
    best = sorted(cand, key=lambda d: km(rider, drivers[d]))[:5]

Work it out

A city has 40,000 online drivers at peak, reporting every 4 seconds, spread over 2,000 H3 cells — but unevenly: the busiest 50 cells hold 30% of drivers. A rider request searches its cell plus 2 rings (19 cells total) and needs at least 20 candidates.

(a) What is the write rate and per-shard load if you have 16 shards? (b) What happens to matching in the busiest cells versus the quietest? (c) A concert ends and 3,000 riders request within 60 seconds from 3 adjacent cells. What breaks?

Answer

(a) Writes: 40,000 ÷ 4 = 10,000 location updates/s for the city. Across 16 shards that is 625/s each if evenly distributed — but it is not. The busiest 50 cells hold 12,000 drivers producing 3,000 writes/s, and if those cells hash unevenly, one shard could take several times its share. The fix is to shard on cell id with enough cells per shard that the law of large numbers helps, and to monitor per-shard write rate rather than assuming uniformity.

(b) Density asymmetry. In a busy cell, one cell alone holds ~240 drivers, so the 19-cell search returns hundreds of candidates — you scan and rank far more than the 20 you need, wasting work and ranking latency. In a quiet cell there may be 2 drivers across all 19 cells, so you fail to reach 20 candidates and must expand further, adding rings until you either find drivers or give up.

So the same search has opposite problems at the two extremes. The fix is adaptive: choose cell resolution by local density (finer cells downtown, coarser in the suburbs) and stop expanding as soon as you have enough candidates rather than always searching 19 cells. Volunteering that one fixed resolution cannot serve both is the insight here.

(c) The concert. 3,000 requests in 60 seconds = 50/s concentrated in 3 cells, against a citywide normal of maybe 5/s. Three things break, in order:

  • Supply exhaustion, not compute. Those cells plus their rings might hold 400 drivers. The first few hundred riders are matched; the rest have no candidates at any radius. The system is not overloaded — there are genuinely not enough cars. Recognising that this is a marketplace failure rather than an engineering one is the most important part of the answer.
  • Contention on every remaining driver. Dozens of riders race for the same few drivers, so the atomic claim rejects most attempts and each rider cycles through candidates, multiplying matching work at exactly the wrong moment. Batched assignment over a short window fixes this properly: solve the allocation once across all waiting riders instead of letting them fight.
  • Search amplification. As radius expands to find anyone, each request scans more cells, so per-request cost rises just as request rate rises — a superlinear load increase. Cap the radius and fail fast with an honest wait estimate rather than scanning the whole city.

What you would actually build: detect the demand spike per cell, switch those cells to batched matching, surge to pull drivers in from surrounding areas, and — the part that matters most for user experience — tell riders a realistic wait rather than spinning. Some of the answer here is not technical, and saying so is a strength rather than a dodge.

By hand: what are the first two characters of the geohash for latitude 12.9716, longitude 77.5946, and how wide is a 2-character cell?

Answer

Ten bits, alternating longitude first. Longitude 77.59: ≥ 0 → 1 (0..180); < 90 → 0 (0..90); ≥ 45 → 1 (45..90); ≥ 67.5 → 1 (67.5..90); ≥ 78.75? no → 0 (67.5..78.75). Latitude 12.97: ≥ 0 → 1 (0..90); < 45 → 0 (0..45); < 22.5 → 0 (0..22.5); ≥ 11.25 → 1 (11.25..22.5); < 16.875 → 0. Interleaved (lng, lat, lng, lat, …): 1 1 0 0 1 | 0 1 1 0 0 = 11001, 01100 = 25, 12 → t, d. So the hash starts td, matching the lab's tdr1v9. A 2-character cell has 5 longitude bits (360° ÷ 32 = 11.25°) and 5 latitude bits (180° ÷ 32 = 5.625°): roughly 1,250 km by 625 km near the equator. Notice the cell's eastern edge is 78.75° — exactly the boundary the lab uses to show two points 22 m apart with different first-level cells.
