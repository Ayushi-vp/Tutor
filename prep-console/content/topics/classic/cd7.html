At 10:00 on a Tuesday, tickets for the year's biggest stadium tour go on sale. There are 60,000 seats per show. Eight hundred thousand people have been sitting on the page since 9:45, and at 10:00:00 every one of them clicks. Somewhere among them are bot farms running thousands of scripted browsers, built to grab the best seats in milliseconds and resell them for ten times the price. Within a few minutes, every seat must end up with exactly one buyer who actually paid — not zero, not two — and the hundreds of thousands who get nothing should at least be told so honestly and quickly.

Most of the year, a ticketing system is easy: Ticketmaster says it processes over 600 million tickets a year, which averages under 20 per second. The data is small: a stadium is tens of thousands of seat rows. What makes the design hard is the shape of demand. On-sales concentrate a year's worth of desire for one artist into a few minutes, on a handful of rows, and every request is trying to change the same rows at the same instant. A design that would be perfectly fine for ordinary e-commerce — check stock, then reserve — sells the same seat several times, or melts down under its own retries.

The core of the answer is small and precise: a seat can be claimed only by a single atomic database operation that succeeds only if the seat is still available, and the claim is a temporary hold with an expiry so abandoned checkouts free their seats. Around that core sit the parts that make it survivable: a waiting room that turns a stampede into a steady flow, seat maps served slightly stale from caches, payment calls made safe to retry, and defences against bots, which are not an afterthought here but the main adversary.

This lesson follows the interview flow: requirements; estimates (on-sale arrival rate, seat-map reads, hold attempts, sell-out time, the size of the data); API and data model; high-level design and the critical path of a purchase; deep dives on the atomic hold and double-booking, on payment within a hold, and on the waiting room and admission rate; then failure modes and trade-offs. The real case is Ticketmaster's own explanation of the 2022 Taylor Swift Eras Tour on-sale, and the lab races 200 threads for 50 seats in SQLite to show check-then-act double-booking next to an atomic conditional update.

The mental model: a cloakroom of numbered hangers and a doorman

Picture a theatre box office with a board of hooks, one per seat. Taking a seat's tag off its hook and putting it in your hand is a hold: nobody else can take that tag while you have it. You walk to the cashier and pay; the cashier stamps the tag and it is sold. If you wander off without paying, a clerk returns the tag to its hook after ten minutes, and it is available again. The key property is physical: there is one tag per seat, so two people cannot both hold it. And at the door stands a doorman who lets people into the lobby in batches, so the crowd at the board never becomes a crush.

Each part maps to the design. The board is the seats table. Taking a tag is an atomic conditional update: change this seat from available to held, only if it is still available. The clerk returning tags is hold expiry (plus a sweeper). The cashier is the payment step, which must work even if the card terminal times out. The doorman is the virtual waiting room.

The analogy breaks where software is weakest. Software has no physical tags: "look at the hook, then take the tag" is two separate steps in a program, and between them another request can take it. That gap — the check-then-act race — is the bug this whole design exists to close. And a real doorman sees faces; ours must tell real fans from bots that look exactly like browsers.

Reads and writes have opposite needs

There are two kinds of traffic and they deserve opposite treatment. Reads — browsing events, looking at the seat map — are enormous in volume and can tolerate being a few seconds stale, so they are served from caches and CDNs. Writes — claiming seats, confirming purchases — are much fewer, must be exactly correct, and go to the authoritative database, one small, atomic statement at a time. Mixing them up (a live database query per seat-map view, or a cached answer to "is this seat mine?") is how designs fail.

An on-sale, minute by minute

It helps to picture the timeline. At 9:30 the event page is already served from the CDN and the waiting room is live; people arriving early see a countdown, not the booking system. At 10:00:00 the waiting room randomises everyone who arrived before the opening (so nobody gains from refreshing at exactly the right millisecond) and starts admitting them at a controlled rate, while later arrivals join the back of the line. Admitted users see a seat map that is a couple of seconds old, pick seats, and either get a hold within a few hundred milliseconds or an immediate "those just went, here are the nearest alternatives". Within ten or fifteen minutes most seats are sold or held; then the interesting work is at the margins — expired holds returning seats, the waiting room telling the remaining hundreds of thousands that the show is sold out, and payment reconciliation catching any charge that did not become an order. Each phase has its own component, and none of them is the database being hammered by a million people.

A reader who stops here has a correct picture: a doorman meters the crowd, people look at a slightly stale map, and the only way to get a seat is a single atomic claim that expires if you do not pay in time.

1 · Requirements

Functional. Browse events and venues; view a seat map with availability; select specific seats (reserved seating) or a quantity (general admission); hold them while checking out; pay; receive tickets; release seats on abandonment; cancel and refund. Out of scope, said aloud: the resale marketplace, dynamic pricing internals, venue onboarding, and the ticket scanning at the gate.

Non-functional. Never sell a seat twice — the one absolute rule. Fairness: roughly first-come, first-served, not fastest-connection or best-bot. Survive on-sale spikes of hundreds of thousands of users within minutes, with honest feedback (queue position, sold out) instead of errors. Payment must never charge without a ticket, or issue a ticket without payment. Seat maps may be a few seconds stale.

The clarifying question: reserved seating or general admission? Reserved seating means users pick specific seats and contention is on individual rows; general admission is a counter per section (decrement if positive), which is simpler but has an even hotter single row. Also ask whether there is a pre-registration or presale step — it changes the bot story completely.

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Assume a 60,000-seat stadium, 800,000 users arriving in the first 90 seconds, orders averaging 2 seats, and checkout taking about 4 minutes.

Steady state is tiny. Ticketmaster processes more than 600 million tickets a year: 600M ÷ 31.5M seconds ≈ 19 tickets per second on average. If capacity were planned for the average, every big on-sale would fail.

Arrival rate. 800,000 ÷ 90 s ≈ 8,900 arrivals per second, and each frustrated user refreshes or retries, so offered load can easily exceed 20,000 requests per second for the booking flow alone, plus seat-map views.

Seat-map reads. If 800,000 people each refresh the map every 5 seconds, that is 800,000 ÷ 5 = 160,000 map reads per second. Served from a CDN with a 2-second TTL, the origin sees one read per map region per 2 seconds per edge location — a few hundred per second.

Orders and holds. 60,000 seats ÷ 2 = 30,000 successful orders. If the event sells out in about 10 minutes, that is 30,000 ÷ 600 s = 50 confirmed orders per second, and several times that in hold attempts (some fail on contention, some are abandoned). A single well-indexed database partition handles hundreds to thousands of small conditional updates per second, so the write volume is genuinely modest — once admission is controlled.

Concurrent holds. With a 4-minute checkout, the number of holds outstanding at once is about (holds started per second) × 240 s. At 125 hold starts per second that is 30,000 outstanding holds — half the stadium invisible to everyone else at any moment. Hold length is therefore a first-order design parameter.

Data size. A seat row (event id, seat id, section, row, number, price tier, state, hold id, expiry, version) is about 100 bytes: 60,000 × 100 B = 6 MB per event. Tens of thousands of events a year are a few hundred gigabytes. Storage is not the problem; contention and fairness are.

Payment calls. About 50-100 per second at peak, each taking hundreds of milliseconds to seconds at the provider — modest in rate, but each is a call to an external system that can time out with an unknown result.

3 · The API

GET  /v1/events/{id}                          → event, sections, prices         (cached)
GET  /v1/events/{id}/seatmap                  → availability by section/seat   (cached 2-5 s)
POST /v1/events/{id}/holds   {seats[] | {section, qty}}   Idempotency-Key, queue token
     → 201 {hold_id, seats, expires_at}  |  409 {unavailable: [...], alternatives: [...]}
POST /v1/holds/{hold_id}/checkout  {payment_method}       Idempotency-Key
     → 202 {order_id, status: "pending_payment"}
GET  /v1/orders/{order_id}                    → pending | confirmed | failed | refunded
DELETE /v1/holds/{hold_id}                    → releases seats immediately

Every state-changing call carries an idempotency key, so a buyer whose phone retries a request after a timeout gets the same hold or order, not a second one. The hold call also carries the waiting-room token, so only admitted users can claim seats.

4 · The data model

events(event_id PK, venue_id, on_sale_at, status)
seats(event_id, seat_id, section, row, number, price_tier,
      state ENUM('available','held','sold'), hold_id, hold_expires_at, version,
      PRIMARY KEY (event_id, seat_id))              -- partitioned by event_id
holds(hold_id PK, event_id, user_id, seat_ids[], expires_at, status, idempotency_key UNIQUE)
orders(order_id PK, hold_id UNIQUE, user_id, amount, status, payment_ref, idempotency_key UNIQUE)

Partition by event_id: every hold touches seats of one event, so it is a single-partition transaction, and one hot event cannot spread its contention across unrelated events if very popular events are placed on their own shards. hold_id UNIQUE on orders means one hold can become at most one order. For general admission, replace per-seat rows with a counter per section — UPDATE sections SET remaining = remaining - :n WHERE event_id=:e AND section=:s AND remaining >= :n — plus hold rows recording what each buyer has claimed.

5 · High-level design and the critical path

Walk one purchase through the sequence diagram. A user who passed the waiting room picks seats A-12 and A-13. The booking service runs the hold: one conditional update on the event's partition claiming both seats if both are available (or their holds have expired), with an 8-minute expiry; the row count says 2, so the hold commits. The user sees a timer. At checkout the service creates an order in pending_payment, extends the hold while payment is in flight, and calls the payment provider with an idempotency key derived from the order id. The provider authorises the card. The service then confirms: in one transaction, it changes both seats from held (with this hold id) to sold, marks the hold converted and the order confirmed. If that conditional update affects fewer rows than expected — the hold was somehow lost — the payment is voided or refunded automatically.

6 · Deep dive A: the atomic hold and why check-then-act fails

The tempting implementation reads the seat, checks it is available, then updates it. Two buyers, Ana and Ben, both want A-12. Ana's request reads "available"; a millisecond later Ben's request reads "available"; Ana writes "sold"; Ben writes "sold". Both are told they have the seat. Nothing crashed; the bug is the gap between the read and the write. In the lab, 200 threads racing for 50 seats this way produce around 134 double sales.

The fix is to make the check part of the write:

UPDATE seats
   SET state = 'held', hold_id = :hid,
       hold_expires_at = now() + interval '8 minutes',
       version = version + 1
 WHERE event_id = :eid
   AND seat_id IN (:seats)
   AND (state = 'available'
        OR (state = 'held' AND hold_expires_at < now()))
-- then: if rows_affected != len(seats) → ROLLBACK, report which seats went

Three properties to point out. It is a single statement: the database evaluates the condition and changes the row under the row's lock, so there is no window for another writer. It reclaims expired holds in the same predicate, so correctness never depends on a background sweeper having run. And the row-count check makes the whole party's request all-or-nothing — a group of four does not want three seats together and one behind a pillar.

Alternatives. Pessimistic locking (SELECT … FOR UPDATE, then update) is also correct, but holds row locks across a round trip to the application, which serialises contenders and can deadlock when two parties lock overlapping seats in different orders (always lock in seat-id order). Optimistic concurrency (read with a version, update where version matches, retry on failure) is correct and avoids locks, but under heavy contention most attempts fail and retry. For "find me any 2 seats in section A", SELECT … FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED LIMIT 2 lets many buyers each grab different free seats without waiting for each other. SERIALIZABLE isolation turns the race into transaction aborts, which is correct but expensive at on-sale contention.

Why not a distributed lock? A lock service (Redis, ZooKeeper) would have to be held across the user's checkout and an external payment call — minutes. If the holder pauses (a long garbage-collection pause, a network partition), the lock can expire while the holder still believes it owns the seat, and without fencing tokens checked by the database the protection is illusory. Keeping the hold in the same row, in the same database that will record the sale, makes the hold itself the lock, and makes it survive crashes and expire on its own.

Hold expiry needs belt and braces: the predicate treats expired holds as available, and a sweeper periodically resets expired holds so the seat map shows those seats again. The sweeper can be lazy for correctness and eager for user experience.

7 · Deep dive B: payment inside a hold

Payment is the dangerous boundary because it crosses into a system you do not control and that can fail ambiguously: a request can time out after the card was actually charged. Rules that make it safe:

Idempotency keys. Every charge request carries a key derived from the order id. If the booking service retries after a timeout, the provider recognises the key and returns the original result rather than charging twice.

Never release a hold while payment is in flight. Extend the hold when checkout starts. If the hold expires mid-charge and someone else takes the seat, you now have a paid buyer with no seat.

Resolve unknown outcomes, do not guess. On a timeout, query the provider by idempotency key or wait for its webhook (a callback the provider sends when a payment completes). Only then mark the order failed or confirmed.

Design the unhappy path. If payment succeeds but the confirm update affects zero rows — the hold was lost to an operator action or a bug — refund automatically and tell the user. A daily reconciliation job matches every provider charge to an order and flags orphans. This is a small saga: hold (compensate: release), charge (compensate: refund), confirm.

8 · Deep dive C: the waiting room and the admission rate

Without admission control, 8,900 arrivals per second plus retries hit a booking service that can perhaps process a few thousand hold attempts per second. It saturates; latency climbs; requests time out; users refresh, which raises the load further. Meanwhile most hold attempts target the same few best seats and fail on contention, so useful throughput collapses below even the nominal capacity. This is congestion collapse: more offered load produces less useful work.

A virtual waiting room fixes the shape of the traffic. Arriving users get a signed queue token and a position, usually from an edge service that can absorb millions of connections because it serves only a static page and a small API. A controller admits users at a set rate; admitted users get a token the booking service checks. Admission is fair (by arrival time or a randomised lottery at the opening instant, not by whose connection is fastest), honest (position and estimated wait), and protective (the booking service sees only what it can handle). It must run on separate, independently scalable infrastructure, or it becomes the thing that falls over.

Choosing the rate. Work backwards from inventory, not from service capacity. There are 30,000 orders to sell. If roughly 60% of admitted users complete a purchase within their 4-minute window, admitting about 125 users per second sells out in about 30,000 ÷ (125 × 0.6) ≈ 400 seconds, about 7 minutes, with a few hundred users per second of hold attempts — comfortably inside capacity. Admitting at 3,000 users per second because the service could take it would let 180,000 people in within a minute to fight over 60,000 seats, recreating the contention the queue exists to remove. The controller should adjust the rate live from measured hold and conversion rates, and stop admitting once the remaining inventory is covered by people already inside, telling the rest promptly.

Bots. Bots are the main adversary, so the waiting room is also the bot filter: pre-registration and verification before the on-sale, access codes tied to accounts and checked again at checkout, limits per account, card and device, behavioural signals (how a session moves and types), and challenges for suspicious sessions. Ticketmaster says only verified buyers could enter the Eras Tour queue and that this kept resale to under 5% of tickets, against 20-30% typical for on-sales without verification.

9 · Bottlenecks, failure modes and trade-offs

Hot partition: one event is one partition taking 100% of the spike; the write volume is modest once admission is controlled, but isolate popular events on their own shards so they cannot degrade others. Pre-scale on the known schedule: on_sale_at is known in advance, so warm caches, scale out and pre-generate seat maps beforehand; reactive autoscaling cannot respond to a 20× step in seconds. Seat-map staleness: deliberate; design "that seat just went — here are the nearest alternatives" as the common path. Abandoned holds: shorten holds to what real buyers need, release on explicit cancel, limit concurrent holds per account. Database failover mid-on-sale: holds are committed rows, so only in-flight uncommitted work is lost and users retry; the conditional update still prevents double sales. Payment provider slowdown: holds pile up in pending payment; extend them, slow admission, and surface the delay rather than releasing seats.

Trade-offs to close on: slightly stale reads for cheap scale versus exact reads; hold length (conversion) versus effective inventory; admission rate (wait time) versus contention; strict bot defences (fewer scalpers) versus friction for real fans; and a single conditional statement per event partition rather than distributed locks. The honest summary: you chose to show users slightly stale availability and occasionally tell them a seat has gone, because the alternative is a system that is either slow for everyone or occasionally sells the same seat twice.

Why the hold must live next to the seat

Martin Kleppmann's well-known critique of lock services explains the danger: a client acquires a lock with a timeout, then pauses — a long garbage-collection pause, a stalled virtual machine, a network delay — for longer than the timeout. The lock expires, another client acquires it, and the first client wakes up still believing it owns the resource and writes anyway. The only robust fix is a fencing token: a number that increases with each lock grant, which the storage system checks and rejects if it is older than the last one it saw. A seat hold stored in the seat row is that design by construction: the hold id and expiry live in the row the confirm statement checks, so a stale holder's confirm simply matches zero rows. The general lesson: put the lock where the data is, or make the data check the lock.

General admission: a single hot counter

General admission sections are sold by quantity: "2 tickets in the standing area". That is a counter — remaining — decremented conditionally (WHERE remaining >= :n). It is even hotter than per-seat rows because every buyer touches the same row. Three techniques help. Batching: collect hold requests for a few milliseconds and apply them in one transaction. Sharding the counter: split 20,000 tickets into 20 sub-counters of 1,000 and pick one at random, falling back to others when one is empty; this multiplies write throughput at the cost of a slightly more complex "sold out" check. Pre-allocating to the queue: give each admitted batch of users a reservation of inventory, so most decisions are local.

Fairness is a product decision with engineering consequences

"First come, first served" sounds fair but rewards whoever has the lowest network latency to the servers, or a bot that opens thousands of sessions. Common designs randomise order among everyone who arrived before the opening moment (a lottery for the early crowd), then serve later arrivals in arrival order. Others allocate access by pre-registration and invite groups in waves, as Verified Fan does. Each choice changes the waiting room's data structures: a lottery needs a shuffle of the pre-opening queue; waves need code issuance and checks at login and checkout; pure arrival order needs a reliable, tamper-proof timestamp per user.

The seat map is a read model, not the source of truth

The seat map is derived from the seats table and published as a cached document per section, refreshed every couple of seconds. Treat it as a read model: a separate representation built for reading, which may lag. Pushing live updates to every viewer on every hold (via WebSockets) is possible for small venues, but for a million viewers it turns each hold into a broadcast storm, exactly when the system is busiest. A compromise is to push coarse updates (section-level availability) and let precise seat availability be confirmed only at hold time.

Pre-scaling and the cost of being wrong

Because on-sales are scheduled, capacity is planned, not reactive. Teams estimate demand from registrations (Ticketmaster had 3.5 million registrations and historically sees about 40% of invited fans show up, buying about 3 tickets each), scale the waiting room, booking service and database ahead of time, and run load tests at several times the expected peak. Even so, the Eras Tour showed that the unknown — bot traffic and people without codes — can dominate: total requests reached four times Ticketmaster's previous peak. The defensive design is graceful degradation: when overloaded, slow admission and tell users, rather than letting the booking path fall over.

"Best available" seat selection

Many buyers do not pick seats; they ask for "the best 4 seats together in price tier B". That turns the hold into a search: find a run of 4 adjacent available seats in the same row, best rows first, and claim them atomically. Doing the search on the seat map cache and then claiming specific seat ids works most of the time, but during an on-sale the cache is often wrong and the claim fails. Better is to run the search inside the database against current state with FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED, so concurrent searchers each skip rows someone else is in the middle of claiming, or to keep a per-section in-memory allocator (one owner per section, fed from the database) that hands out contiguous blocks without contention. Either way, the final write is still the same conditional update, so a bug in the search can produce a poor seat choice but never a double sale.

Multiple regions

Buyers are global but a seat can only have one authoritative owner, so an event's seats live in one primary region; buyers far away pay a round trip or two of extra latency on the hold and confirm calls, which is acceptable for a few calls per purchase. What is replicated everywhere is the read side: event pages, seat maps and the waiting room run at the edge or in every region. Trying to accept holds in several regions at once for the same seats would need cross-region consensus on every claim, which adds hundreds of milliseconds and failure modes for no benefit — this is a case where a single writer per event is the right answer.

Idempotency across the whole flow

Mobile and web clients retry. A buyer double-taps "buy"; a phone loses signal after sending; a load balancer retries an idempotent-looking request. Each state-changing call — create hold, checkout, cancel — carries a client-generated key stored with a unique constraint, and a repeat returns the stored result. The payment provider call uses a key derived from the order id. This turns every unclear outcome into a safe retry, which is what lets the rest of the system treat failures simply.

The real case: Ticketmaster and the Eras Tour on-sale

On 15 November 2022, Ticketmaster ran the Verified Fan presale for Taylor Swift's Eras Tour, and its own post afterwards is a rare, public, numbers-first account of a ticketing on-sale under extreme load. Over 3.5 million people pre-registered — the largest registration in its history. Based on history (about 40% of invited fans show up and buy, averaging 3 tickets), Ticketmaster sent codes to about 1.5 million people for 52 show dates, and put the remaining 2 million on a waiting list. Verification was tied to accounts at login, so only verified buyers could enter the queue, and buyers also had to enter their code to complete a purchase.

Then the traffic arrived. "The staggering number of bot attacks as well as fans who didn't have codes drove unprecedented traffic on our site, resulting in 3.5 billion total system requests – 4x our previous peak." Ticketmaster says it usually sells through a stadium show in about an hour, but slowed some sales and pushed back others to stabilise the systems, at the cost of longer queue waits. It estimated that about 15% of interactions across the site had issues, "including passcode validation errors that caused fans to lose tickets they had carted". Even so, over 2 million tickets sold that day, the most for an artist in a single day, and less than 5% of the tour's tickets had appeared on the secondary market, against 20-30% for on-sales without Verified Fan. The post also makes the marketplace point bluntly: based on the traffic, Taylor Swift would have needed to play over 900 stadium shows to satisfy demand.

Three lessons map onto this design. First, admission control and verification work — the queue was restricted to verified buyers and the resale rate was low — but the infrastructure in front of the queue must withstand traffic from people who are not admitted, including bots. Second, under overload the right move is to slow the flow deliberately (Ticketmaster paused and staggered sales) rather than let the booking path collapse. Third, the cruellest failure is a correctness failure on the happy path: buyers losing carted tickets because of validation errors. Holds and their surrounding checks must be at least as reliable as the payment path.

Reading the incident as an engineer

Two details in Ticketmaster's account are worth dwelling on. First, verification happened at login and again at checkout through the access code — two dependencies on the purchase path. When validation errors occurred under load, fans with seats already in their cart lost them, which means a supporting service failed in a way that released holds rather than preserving them. A design lesson follows: when a dependency on the checkout path fails, prefer to keep the hold and retry or extend, rather than discarding work the customer has already done. Second, the traffic that caused the trouble came largely from people who could not buy at all — bots and fans without codes. Capacity planning based on expected buyers (1.5 million invitations, roughly 40% turnout) was not the binding constraint; the binding constraint was the cost of rejecting everyone else. Cheap, early rejection at the edge — before requests reach anything stateful — is therefore as important as the booking path itself.

Failure modes and what to monitor

Double sales (must be zero): a nightly check that every sold seat has exactly one confirmed order. Hold success rate and contention rate (holds failing because seats were taken), per event. Hold-to-sale conversion and abandonment, which drive hold length. Waiting-room depth, admission rate and wait estimates versus actuals. Payment latency, unknown-outcome rate and reconciliation mismatches. Bot signals: blocked sessions, challenge rates, purchases per card and device. Seat-map cache age, to keep staleness within the promised few seconds.

Goal: reproduce double-booking with check-then-act and prevent it with one conditional update plus expiring holds. Python 3.12, standard library (sqlite3, threading), about 15 seconds.

"""Double-booking: check-then-act vs one conditional UPDATE, with expiring holds.
Python 3.12 stdlib (sqlite3 + threads). 200 buyers race for 50 seats."""
import os, random, sqlite3, tempfile, threading, time

DB = os.path.join(tempfile.mkdtemp(), "tickets.db")

def setup():
    con = sqlite3.connect(DB)
    con.executescript("""
      DROP TABLE IF EXISTS seats; DROP TABLE IF EXISTS sales;
      CREATE TABLE seats(seat INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, state TEXT, hold_id TEXT, expires REAL);
      CREATE TABLE sales(seat INTEGER, buyer TEXT);""")
    con.executemany("INSERT INTO seats VALUES (?, 'available', NULL, NULL)", [(s,) for s in range(50)])
    con.commit(); con.close()

def naive(buyer):
    """SELECT to check, then UPDATE in a separate step: the classic race."""
    con = sqlite3.connect(DB, timeout=30)
    seat = random.randrange(50)
    free = con.execute("SELECT state FROM seats WHERE seat=?", (seat,)).fetchone()[0] == "available"
    time.sleep(0.002)                               # the gap: network hop, app logic, GC pause
    if free:
        con.execute("UPDATE seats SET state='sold' WHERE seat=?", (seat,))
        con.execute("INSERT INTO sales VALUES (?, ?)", (seat, buyer)); con.commit()
    con.close()

def atomic(buyer):
    """One statement claims the seat only if it is still free (or its hold has expired)."""
    con = sqlite3.connect(DB, timeout=30)
    seat = random.randrange(50)
    cur = con.execute("""UPDATE seats SET state='held', hold_id=?, expires=?
                         WHERE seat=? AND (state='available' OR (state='held' AND expires < ?))""",
                      (buyer, time.time() + 1.0, seat, time.time()))
    con.commit()
    if cur.rowcount == 1:                           # we own the hold; "pay", then confirm
        time.sleep(random.choice([0.01, 0.01, 0.01, 1.5]))     # 1 in 4 buyers is too slow
        cur = con.execute("""UPDATE seats SET state='sold' WHERE seat=? AND hold_id=?
                             AND state='held' AND expires >= ?""", (seat, buyer, time.time()))
        if cur.rowcount == 1:
            con.execute("INSERT INTO sales VALUES (?, ?)", (seat, buyer))
        con.commit()
    con.close()

def wave(fn, n, tag):
    threads = [threading.Thread(target=fn, args=(f"{tag}{i}",)) for i in range(n)]
    for t in threads: t.start()
    for t in threads: t.join()

def report(fn, label):
    con = sqlite3.connect(DB)
    sold = con.execute("SELECT COUNT(*), COUNT(DISTINCT seat) FROM sales").fetchone()
    held = con.execute("SELECT COUNT(*) FROM seats WHERE state='held'").fetchone()[0]
    con.close()
    print(f"{fn.__name__:>6} {label}: {sold[0]} sales for {sold[1]} distinct seats "
          f"-> {sold[0] - sold[1]} double-sold; {held} seats in (expired) holds")

def run(fn):
    setup(); random.seed(7)
    wave(fn, 200, "a"); report(fn, "wave 1")
    if fn is atomic:                                # later buyers reclaim expired holds
        time.sleep(1.1); wave(fn, 200, "b"); report(fn, "wave 2")

run(naive)
run(atomic)
  1. Run it. The naive version reports around 185 sales for 50 distinct seats — about 135 double sales — because each thread checks, pauses 2 ms (standing in for network and application time) and then writes.
  2. The atomic version reports zero double sales in every wave: a buyer's UPDATE … WHERE state='available' OR expired either changes the row (rowcount 1, they own the hold) or matches nothing (rowcount 0, someone else has it).
  3. Look at the held seats after wave 1: slow buyers (1 in 4 takes 1.5 s against a 1 s hold) lose their holds, and their confirm statements match zero rows — no seat is sold to a buyer whose hold expired. Wave 2 reclaims those expired holds through the same predicate, with no sweeper.
  4. Delete the OR (state='held' AND expires < ?) part of the claim and rerun: wave 2 sells nothing new and the same 26 or so seats stay stuck in expired holds. Without reclaiming expiry in the predicate, availability depends entirely on a sweeper having run.
  5. Try shortening the hold to 0.3 s: more slow buyers lose seats (lower conversion) but expired seats return sooner (more effective inventory) — the hold-length trade-off from the drill.
Work it out

A 60,000-seat stadium goes on sale at 10:00. 800,000 users arrive within the first 90 seconds. Your booking service can process 3,000 hold attempts/s. The average user takes 4 minutes to complete payment, and 25% abandon.

(a) Without a waiting room, what happens? (b) With a waiting room, at what rate should you admit? (c) How many seats are locked up in abandoned holds at any moment, and what does that do?

Answer

(a) Without a waiting room. 800,000 users over 90 seconds is ~8,900 arrivals/s, and each will retry when it fails, so offered load is well above 20,000 attempts/s against a capacity of 3,000. The service saturates, latency climbs, requests time out, users refresh — which increases load. Meanwhile almost every attempt targets a seat someone else is also claiming, so the conditional updates conflict and the useful throughput is a fraction of even the 3,000. The observable outcome is a site that is down for everyone for several minutes, after which some seats have sold by luck. This is congestion collapse: retries and timeouts make useful work fall as offered load rises.

(b) Admission rate. Work backwards from what the inventory can absorb, not from what the service can process. 60,000 seats at an average 2 seats per order = 30,000 successful orders. 75% of admitted users complete, so you need about 40,000 admissions in total. To sell out over roughly 10 minutes, admit about 40,000 ÷ 600 s ≈ 70 users/s; to sell out in 5 minutes, about 135/s. Either is far below the 3,000/s capacity, which leaves headroom for retries and seat-map traffic. The 800,000-person queue is not "drained"; once the remaining inventory is covered by people already inside, stop admitting and tell everyone else.

The key insight to state: the admission rate should be derived from inventory depletion, not from service capacity. Running at 3,000/s just to use the capacity would admit 180,000 people in a minute to compete for 60,000 seats, recreating the contention you built the queue to remove.

(c) Abandoned holds. At 135 admissions per second with a 4-minute checkout, about 135 × 240 ≈ 32,000 users are mid-checkout at once, each holding 2 seats — more than the stadium, so in practice holds are limited by remaining inventory. Of whatever is held, 25% belongs to buyers who will abandon: if 60,000 seats are held at the peak, about 15,000 seats are sitting in holds that will never convert, invisible to everyone else until they expire.

The consequences are real: the seat map shows sold out while 15,000 seats are unsold, users leave, and the seats only return in a wave when holds expire — producing a confusing second rush. Mitigations, in order: shorten the hold window (if most real buyers finish in 2-3 minutes, a 3-minute hold halves the locked inventory compared with 6), release immediately on explicit abandonment rather than waiting for the timer, sweep aggressively so returns are continuous rather than bursty, and show "seats may become available" instead of a flat sold-out, with a refresh that surfaces returns as they happen.

The deeper point worth making: hold duration is a direct trade-off between conversion (longer holds let slow users finish) and effective inventory (longer holds mean more seats invisible). It is a tunable business parameter, and the right value is measured against real abandonment data, not chosen by intuition.

Diagnose it

After an on-sale, 37 customers complain they were charged but have no tickets. The logs show their payment calls timed out after 10 seconds and the orders were marked failed; their holds were released and the seats resold. What went wrong, and what do you change?

Answer

A timeout is an unknown outcome, not a failure. The provider actually completed those charges after the booking service gave up, so money moved with no seat behind it. Changes: (1) never release a hold while a payment is in flight — extend it and keep the order in pending_payment; (2) on timeout, look up the charge by idempotency key or wait for the provider's webhook before deciding; (3) if a charge succeeds and the seats cannot be confirmed, refund automatically and notify; (4) run reconciliation that matches every provider charge to a confirmed order and alerts on mismatches. Then refund the 37 customers now and apologise — the fix for the past is operational, the fix for the future is in the state machine.
