Three people are editing a project proposal at the same time: Anna is fixing a typo in the first paragraph, Ben is deleting a sentence in the second, and Chloe is typing a new section at the end. Each sees their own keystrokes appear instantly, and within a fraction of a second sees the others' changes flow into the page around them. Nobody ever sees a dialog saying "this document was changed by someone else". At the end of the meeting, all three have exactly the same document on their screens — down to the last comma.

Making that work is surprisingly hard, and the reason is a single word: instantly. If every keystroke had to travel to a server and back before appearing, typing would feel like wading through mud, so each editor applies its user's edit immediately and tells the others afterwards. That means the same edits arrive at different machines in different orders. And an edit like "insert the letter H at position 1" only makes sense relative to a particular version of the text: if someone else deleted a word earlier in the line a moment before, position 1 now points somewhere else. Apply edits naively and the three screens quietly drift apart, with no error anywhere.

There are two great families of solutions. Operational transformation keeps position-based edits but rewrites each incoming edit to account for the edits that happened concurrently; it powers Google Docs and powered Google Wave. Conflict-free replicated data types redesign the document so that edits name stable identities instead of positions and therefore commute. And there are pragmatic middle paths: Figma, whose documents are trees of design objects rather than long strings, built a simpler server-authoritative scheme inspired by both.

This lesson follows the interview flow: requirements; estimates (concurrent editors, operations per second, fan-out, session servers, operation-log and snapshot storage, latency budget); API and data model; the high-level design around a per-document session server; deep dives on OT, on CRDTs, and on Figma's property-based model with persistence, presence and undo; then failure modes and trade-offs. The real case is Figma's own description of its multiplayer system, and the lab implements a tiny OT transform and fuzz-tests it for convergence against naive application.

The mental model: annotating a shared paper by phone

Imagine three editors each holding a printed copy of the same page, talking on a conference call. Anna says "add 'very' before the fourth word of line 2". Ben, at the same moment, says "delete the second word of line 2". If each person simply applies instructions in the order they hear them, Anna applied hers first and then Ben's, while Ben applied his first and then Anna's — and "the fourth word" meant a different word on each copy because Ben's deletion shifted everything. Their papers no longer match, and nobody noticed.

There are two fixes a sensible group would invent. The first: whenever you receive an instruction, adjust it for anything that happened concurrently — "Ben deleted a word before the fourth, so Anna's 'fourth word' is now the third". That is operational transformation. The second: before the meeting, give every word on the page a permanent sticker number, and phrase instructions as "add 'very' before word #17" — word #17 is the same word on every copy, no matter what was deleted around it. That is the idea behind CRDTs. In both cases a chairperson who repeats every instruction in one agreed order — a central server — makes life much easier.

Where the analogy breaks: editors type several characters per second each, edits travel over networks with variable delay, and some editors work offline for hours and reconnect with a pile of edits. The fixes must be automatic, fast, and correct for every possible interleaving, not just the ones a person would think of.

The pieces of the system

Each user's editor holds a full copy (a replica) of the document and applies local edits instantly. A WebSocket connects it to a session server that owns the live document: it holds the current state in memory, puts every operation into one order, broadcasts it to everyone else, and persists it. Storage holds periodic snapshots plus an operation log of everything since. Separately, a lightweight presence channel carries cursors and selections, which are allowed to be lossy.

Why the simple answers fail

Two simple designs come to mind first, and both fail on contact with users. Locking — only one person may edit a paragraph, or the whole document, at a time — is correct, but every keystroke would wait for a lock round trip, people would constantly be blocked by a colleague who left their cursor somewhere, and offline editing would be impossible. Last-writer-wins on the whole document — whoever saves last overwrites — is what emailing attachments does, and it silently discards everyone else's work. What users want is that everyone types freely, everyone's work survives, and everyone ends up with the same text. That combination forces the design to accept concurrent edits and then reconcile them, which is what the algorithms in this lesson do.

It also helps to separate two guarantees early. Convergence means every replica ends with the same document once all edits have arrived; it is precise and testable. Intention preservation means the merged document reflects what each person meant; it is fuzzy and cannot be fully guaranteed. Every algorithm guarantees the first and approximates the second.

A reader who stops here has the right picture: local echo for speed, a single owner per document for order, an algorithm (transformation, CRDT ids, or per-property last-writer-wins) that guarantees every replica converges, and snapshots plus a log for durability.

1 · Requirements

Functional. Several users edit one document at the same time; each sees their own edits instantly and others' within a fraction of a second; live cursors and selections; edits made offline reconcile on reconnect; version history and restore; comments; view-only and edit permissions. Out of scope, said aloud: page layout and rendering, spreadsheet formulas, search.

Non-functional. Local edits appear with no network wait. Remote edits appear within about 200 ms on a reasonable network. All replicas converge: once everyone has every edit, everyone sees the same document. No acknowledged edit is ever lost. The document loads quickly even after years of history.

The clarifying questions: How many simultaneous editors — three people on a doc, or 80 on a design canvas? Must offline editing for long periods work? Is the content mostly text (a long sequence) or structured objects (a tree of shapes with properties)? The answers choose the algorithm.

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Assume 5 million users editing at the evening peak, in about 2 million live documents (most documents have one or two editors, some have dozens), with 20% of editors typing at any instant.

Operation rate. A fast typist produces 5-8 characters per second; editors batch keystrokes into one operation every 50-100 ms, so an active typist sends about 3 operations per second after batching. 5M × 0.2 × 3 = 3 million operations per second at peak across the fleet.

Fan-out. If a document has on average 2.5 connected editors and viewers, each operation goes to about 1.5 other people: about 4.5 million deliveries per second. Presence (cursor moves) can easily be several times that if not throttled; at 5 cursor updates per second per active user broadcast to 1.5 others, it is another 7.5 million messages per second — which is why presence is throttled, batched and droppable.

Session servers. Each live document is owned by one process. If one machine can host 10,000 live documents and handle 30,000 operations per second, then 2M documents need 200 machines by document count and 3M ops/s needs 100 by throughput; plan for about 300 with headroom and failover. Connections: 5M WebSockets, spread over gateway machines like any real-time system.

Operation log. An operation with metadata (document id, user, version, position or id, a few characters) is about 50 bytes. Average load over a day is perhaps a third of peak, 1M ops/s: 1M × 50 B × 86,400 ≈ 4.3 TB per day of log. Keeping fine-grained history for 30 days is about 130 TB; older history is compacted into periodic snapshots.

Snapshots. A billion stored documents averaging 50 KB is 50 TB of current state. Snapshot a live document every few hundred operations or every minute, so that loading needs at most a short replay.

Latency budget. Local echo: 0 ms (just render). Remote: client batching (50-100 ms) + network to the session server (20-100 ms) + server processing (under 1 ms) + network to the other client (20-100 ms) ≈ 100-300 ms. Users perceive a collaborator's text appearing within a few hundred milliseconds as "live".

3 · The core problem, worked by hand

Spend real time here, because it is what the question tests. The document is CAT. Anna inserts H at index 1 (intending CHAT). At the same moment Ben deletes index 2, the T (intending CA). Each applies their own edit instantly, then receives the other's.

On Anna's machine: CAT → her insert → CHAT → Ben's "delete index 2" → deletes the A → CHT. On Ben's machine: CAT → his delete → CA → Anna's "insert H at 1" → CHA. Anna sees CHT, Ben sees CHA, and nothing signals that anything went wrong. Worse, Anna's document lost the A that nobody deleted. This is the defining failure: positions are relative to a document state, and concurrent edits change the state.

State the requirement precisely: any two replicas that have applied the same set of operations must show identical content, regardless of the order in which the operations arrived. Two ways to achieve it: transform operations so that order no longer matters (OT), or design operations that already commute (CRDTs). A third, pragmatic option for structured data is to make the unit of conflict coarse — a whole property of an object — and let the server's order decide (Figma).

Opening a document routes the WebSocket to the session server that owns that document id (a coordination store or consistent hashing maps document to owner). If no owner exists, one is chosen and loads the latest snapshot plus the operations after it. Figma describes exactly this shape: clients are web pages talking to servers over WebSockets, and the servers run a separate process for each multiplayer document, which everyone editing that document connects to.

The critical path for one keystroke, shown in the sequence diagram: Anna's editor applies insert H at 1 locally, sends OP {client_seq 41, base_version 900}, and keeps typing. The session server receives it; since version 900 the server has applied Ben's delete as version 901, so it transforms Anna's operation against 901, applies it as version 902, appends it to the log, sends ACK {41, 902} to Anna and REMOTE {902, op} to Ben and Chloe. Ben's editor, which has its own unacknowledged edits in flight, transforms the incoming remote operation against those before applying it. Everyone converges on version 902.

5 · Deep dive A: operational transformation

OT keeps position-based operations and adds a function transform(a, b) that rewrites operation a so it has the same intended effect when applied after a concurrent operation b. For single-character inserts and deletes the rules are short:

ins(p) vs ins(q):  if p < q (or p == q and my site id is lower): unchanged, else p+1
ins(p) vs del(q):  if p <= q: unchanged, else p-1
del(p) vs ins(q):  if p < q:  unchanged, else p+1
del(p) vs del(q):  if p < q: unchanged; if p > q: p-1; if equal: no-op (already deleted)

Check it on the example. Ben's del(2) transformed against Anna's ins(1): 2 is not less than 1, so it becomes del(3). On Anna's CHAT, index 3 is the T, so she gets CHA. Anna's ins(1) against Ben's del(2): 1 ≤ 2, unchanged, so Ben's CA becomes CHA. Converged. The lab fuzz-tests these four rules on 20,000 random concurrent pairs: naive application diverges 13,919 times, the transform zero times.

The property checked there is called TP1: applying a then transform(b, a) equals applying b then transform(a, b). It is enough for two sites. With three or more sites exchanging operations directly, transforms must also satisfy a stronger property (TP2) when an operation is transformed along different paths, and several published OT algorithms were later found to break it. Practical systems sidestep this with a central server that defines one order. Google Wave's OT, based on the 1995 Jupiter system, went further: a client sends one operation (or batch) and waits for the server's acknowledgement before sending more, buffering local edits meanwhile. The server then only ever transforms a client's operation against the operations it has ordered since that client's base version, and each client only transforms incoming operations against its own small set of unacknowledged edits. That makes OT simple and fast — Joseph Gentle, who worked on Wave, calls it "a glorified for() loop with some helper functions to update character offsets", with tens to hundreds of thousands of operations per second even in unoptimised JavaScript.

The cost is the dependency on the server: offline editing for days means transforming a long list of local operations against a long list of remote ones on reconnect, and a single document's session server is a throughput ceiling. Gentle suggests this is why a Google Doc shared widely can show "This document is overloaded so editing is disabled".

6 · Deep dive B: sequence CRDTs

A CRDT removes the premise that broke naive editing: positions. Every character gets a unique, immutable identifier when it is inserted — for example (site id, counter) — and the document is an ordered list of identified characters. Operations name identifiers: "insert H with id b1 after a1" and "delete a3". Because identifiers never change, these operations mean the same thing on every replica in any order. Deletion does not remove the element; it marks it as a tombstone, because a late-arriving operation may still say "insert after a3". Concurrent inserts after the same element are ordered by a deterministic rule on their ids, so every replica places them the same way.

Rewrite the example: CAT is [C:a1][A:a2][T:a3]. Anna: insert H (id b1) after a1. Ben: delete a3. Apply in either order and the visible text is C H A with T tombstoned: CHA. No transform function anywhere. This is what makes CRDTs attractive for offline and peer-to-peer editing: there is no central orderer to wait for, and any two replicas can merge their histories later.

The costs are real. Every character carries an identifier (several bytes against one byte of text), and tombstones accumulate. Gentle reports that an early Automerge represented a 100 KB text document in 83 MB; columnar encodings that store runs of adjacent characters with consecutive ids together, as Martin Kleppmann proposed and Yjs implemented, bring overhead to roughly 1.5-2× of the content. Tombstones can be discarded once every replica is known to have seen the delete, which in a system with a server is straightforward and in a fully peer-to-peer one is not. Use a mature library (Yjs, Automerge) rather than writing one.

7 · Deep dive C: Figma's model, persistence, presence and undo

A design file is not a long string; it is a tree of objects (frames, shapes, text layers), each with properties (position, colour, text, parent). Figma describes its document as Map<ObjectID, Map<Property, Value>> and chose neither OT nor true CRDTs. Its servers are the central authority and keep the latest value any client sent for each property of each object. Two clients changing different properties of the same object never conflict; two changing the same property resolve to whichever value reached the server last — a last-writer-wins register per property. Clients apply their own changes immediately and, to avoid flicker, ignore incoming server values for properties where they have an unacknowledged change of their own. A consequence Figma accepts openly: if one person changes a text value B to AB while another changes it to BC, the result is AB or BC, never ABC — fine for a design tool.

Trees add one more problem: reparenting. Figma stores each object's parent as a property on the child (preserving object identity across moves, so concurrent edits to a moved object are not lost). That allows cycles — one client moves A under B while another moves B under A — so the server rejects parent updates that would create a cycle, and a client that is briefly in a cyclic state temporarily removes those objects until the server's answer arrives. Child order uses fractional indexing: each child's position is a fraction between 0 and 1, and inserting between two children uses a value between theirs, so no other child is renumbered. Parent and position are stored as one property so they change atomically.

Persistence. The session server appends each ordered operation to a log and writes snapshots periodically; loading reads the latest snapshot and replays the tail. Figma lets clients go offline for an arbitrary time: on reconnect, the client downloads a fresh copy of the document, reapplies its offline edits on top, and resumes syncing — making reconnection the same code path as opening a file.

Presence travels on a separate, throttled channel: a few cursor updates per second per user, batched, dropped rather than queued under load, and culled by viewport on large canvases. A stale cursor is invisible; a lost edit is a bug.

Undo is per user and expressed as a new operation: revert my last change, transformed against everything since, without reverting others' later work. Figma's guiding principle after much trouble: if you undo a lot, copy something, and redo back to the present, the document must not change — so an undo modifies the redo history at the time of the undo.

8 · Bottlenecks, failure modes and trade-offs

Session server crash: clients reconnect, a new owner loads snapshot plus log, and each client resends unacknowledged operations, which the server deduplicates by (client id, client sequence). Two owners for one document (split brain during failover) would accept conflicting orders; the log append must be conditional on the version (or fenced with an ownership token) so only one owner can write version 903. Hot documents: one process per document is a throughput ceiling; throttle presence, batch operations, fan out to viewers through read-only replicas, and cap editors. Long offline edits: with OT, transforming hours of local edits against hours of remote ones is slow and conflict-prone; CRDTs or Figma-style property merges handle it more gracefully. Metadata growth in CRDT documents: monitor state size per document and compact. Malicious clients: validate operations and permissions at the session server; convergence does not guarantee good behaviour.

Trade-offs to close on: OT (compact, simple with a server, struggles offline) versus CRDTs (no orderer needed, offline-friendly, metadata cost) versus per-property last-writer-wins (simplest, right for structured objects, wrong for shared text); one owner per document (simple ordering, a per-document ceiling) versus coordination-free replication; and strong convergence guarantees versus the unprovable goal of preserving what each person meant.

Convergence is not correctness

Every algorithm here guarantees that replicas end up identical. None guarantees the result is what anyone wanted. Two people typing different words at the same position can converge on characters interleaved into nonsense if the algorithm orders concurrent inserts character by character; good sequence CRDTs keep same-author runs contiguous to avoid exactly this. A last-writer-wins property discards one person's value completely. "Intention preservation" is the name for the goal, and it has no formal definition, so products pick rules users find acceptable and surface the rest — Figma accepts that concurrent edits to the same text value pick one, because designers rarely co-type the same label.

Rich text and structure are much harder than plain text

Formatting spans overlap characters, so "bold characters 10-20" and "delete characters 15-25" interact in ways a plain sequence algorithm does not cover; formatting is better modelled as marks anchored to character ids at both ends, with explicit rules for what happens when the anchors are deleted. Structured documents add trees (paragraphs, lists, tables), and concurrent moves can create cycles, which Figma resolves at the server and which general-purpose tree-move CRDTs remain an active research topic. When an interviewer escalates to rich text, naming these problems precisely is better than claiming a library solves them.

Why a central server makes OT tractable

With a server, there is one canonical history. Each client's operation is transformed only against operations in that history since the client's base version, and each client transforms incoming operations only against its own unacknowledged ones. Wave's rule of one outstanding operation per client keeps that second set tiny. Without a server — clients exchanging operations directly — each operation can be transformed along many different paths, and transforms must satisfy stronger conditions that are notoriously hard to get right. This is why OT and centralised products fit naturally, and why "OT needs a server" is close enough to true for an interview.

CRDT identifiers and ordering, concretely

A common sequence CRDT (RGA, replicated growable array) gives each inserted element an id of (counter, site), where the counter is one more than the largest counter the site has seen. An insert names the element it goes after. When two elements are inserted after the same element concurrently, the one with the larger id goes first; since ids are totally ordered, every replica sorts them the same way. Deletions mark tombstones. Because each operation references only ids that already existed when it was created, applying operations in any causal order yields the same list. Libraries add run-length encoding of consecutive ids and efficient search structures (B-trees, skip lists) so that applying an operation costs about log n rather than a scan.

Ordering and authority for objects

Figma's object ids must be unique without asking the server, because creating objects must work offline; each client has a unique client id that forms part of every object id it creates. Deleting an object removes all its data from the server; the data needed to undo the deletion lives in the deleting client's undo buffer. These are small, deliberate choices that show the general pattern: decide which actor is authoritative for which fact, and design ids and undo around that.

Presence at scale

Cursor traffic is quadratic: with n people in a document each broadcasting to the other n−1, cursor messages grow with n². At 80 people moving cursors 20 times a second that is 80 × 79 × 20 ≈ 126,000 messages per second for one document. Throttling to 4-5 updates per second, batching all presence into one periodic frame per client, interpolating movement on the receiving side, and sending only cursors inside each viewer's viewport bring this down by orders of magnitude without anyone noticing.

Testing is the hard part

Collaborative algorithms fail only under rare interleavings, which ordinary unit tests never produce. The standard defences are the ones in the lab, scaled up: randomised (property-based) tests that generate thousands of concurrent operation sequences and check that every replica converges; tests that deliberately reorder, delay, duplicate and drop messages; and, for the core transform or merge rules, model checking or formal proofs. Figma built a separate prototype playground that simulated three clients and a server with controllable offline periods and bandwidth limits before writing production code. In production, periodic checksums of each client's document compared with the server's turn silent divergence into an alert.

A server does not rule out a CRDT

CRDTs are often presented as peer-to-peer technology, but many products run a sequence CRDT behind an ordinary server: clients send CRDT operations to a session server, which relays them, persists them and garbage-collects tombstones once every connected client has acknowledged a deletion. The server is then an efficiency and durability point rather than a correctness requirement, which makes failover simpler (operations from any replica merge in any order) and offline work natural. The trade-off against OT is mostly about memory and document size versus the simplicity of reasoning about merges.

History, versions and blame

Because the log records every operation with its author, version history and "who wrote this" come almost for free: replay to any version, or attribute each character (by CRDT id or by tracking OT operations) to an author. Named versions are snapshots pinned forever. Restoring an old version is not rewriting history; it is a new set of operations that turns the current document into the old one, so collaborators currently editing simply see the change arrive.

The real case: Figma's multiplayer system

Figma launched multiplayer editing in 2016 and published a detailed account in October 2019, which remains one of the few public descriptions of a production collaborative editor. Its architecture is client-server: Figma clients are web pages that talk to a cluster of servers over WebSockets, and the servers run a separate process for each multiplayer document that everyone editing it connects to. Opening a document downloads a copy; from then on, updates flow both ways over the WebSocket. Clients can go offline indefinitely; on reconnect they download a fresh copy, reapply offline edits on top and resume. Only document contents use this system — comments, users, teams and projects live in Postgres and sync through a separate mechanism with different trade-offs.

Figma explains why it chose neither of the textbook answers. It did not want OT, "the standard multiplayer algorithm popularized by apps like Google Docs", because OT was "unnecessarily complex for our problem space" and produces "a combinatorial explosion of possible states". It drew on CRDTs but is "not using true CRDTs": CRDTs are designed for decentralised systems with no central authority, and since Figma's server is the authority, it could drop that overhead. The document is a tree of objects, conceptually Map<ObjectID, Map<Property, Value>>; the server keeps the latest value any client sent for each property; conflicts only arise on the same property of the same object and resolve to the last value the server received. Simultaneous editing of the same text value therefore yields one user's value, never a merge, which Figma accepts because it is a design tool, not a text editor.

The hard parts it describes are the ones in this lesson's deep dive: suppressing flicker by ignoring server values that conflict with unacknowledged local changes; unique object ids generated offline by embedding a client id; reparenting as a parent property with server-side cycle rejection and a temporary client-side workaround; fractional indexing for child order, stored together with the parent link so moves are atomic; and an undo/redo rule that took several attempts to get right. It also describes building a prototype "playground" that simulated three clients and a server, to explore offline and bandwidth scenarios before touching the real codebase — a good practice for any system whose correctness depends on rare interleavings.

A contrasting data point comes from the other side of the OT world. Joseph Gentle, who worked on Google Wave and later built the open-source ShareJS on the same OT model, points out that OT's dependence on a central server shows up in user-visible ways: he suggests the "This document is overloaded so editing is disabled" message Google Docs shows when a document is shared widely exists because each document's edits are serialised through one place. That is the same per-document ceiling as Figma's process per document, and it is the price of the simplest correct ordering. Products accept it because almost all documents have a handful of editors, and they add read-only fan-out for the rare document with thousands of viewers.

Failure modes and what to monitor

Divergence is the failure that matters most and the hardest to see: periodically have clients send a checksum of their document at a version and compare with the server's; any mismatch is a bug. Operation latency from local apply to remote visible, at p50 and p99. Acknowledgement lag and the size of each client's unacknowledged buffer. Per-document load: operations per second, connections and broadcast queue depth for the hottest documents. Snapshot age and replay length on load. Session failovers and duplicate operations dropped on resend. Rejected operations (permission, validation, cycles), which reveal client bugs.

Goal: implement the four OT rules for single-character insert and delete, replay the CAT example, and fuzz-test convergence. Python 3.12, standard library, runs instantly.

"""A tiny operational transform for single-character insert/delete, fuzz-tested for convergence.
Python 3.12 stdlib."""
import random

# op = ("ins", pos, char, site) or ("del", pos, site) or ("noop",)
def apply(doc, op):
    if op[0] == "ins":
        return doc[:op[1]] + op[2] + doc[op[1]:]
    if op[0] == "del":
        return doc[:op[1]] + doc[op[1] + 1:]
    return doc

def transform(a, b):
    """Rewrite op a so it has the same intent when applied after concurrent op b."""
    if a[0] == "noop" or b[0] == "noop":
        return a
    if a[0] == "ins" and b[0] == "ins":
        # same position: lower site id goes first (a deterministic tie-break both sides agree on)
        if a[1] < b[1] or (a[1] == b[1] and a[3] < b[3]):
            return a
        return ("ins", a[1] + 1, a[2], a[3])
    if a[0] == "ins" and b[0] == "del":
        return a if a[1] <= b[1] else ("ins", a[1] - 1, a[2], a[3])
    if a[0] == "del" and b[0] == "ins":
        return a if a[1] < b[1] else ("del", a[1] + 1, a[2])
    # del vs del
    if a[1] < b[1]:
        return a
    if a[1] > b[1]:
        return ("del", a[1] - 1, a[2])
    return ("noop",)                                    # both deleted the same character

def random_op(doc, site):
    if doc and random.random() < 0.5:
        return ("del", random.randrange(len(doc)), site)
    return ("ins", random.randrange(len(doc) + 1), random.choice("xyz"), site)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    # The worked example: "CAT"; Anna inserts H at 1, Ben deletes T (index 2), concurrently.
    doc, anna, ben = "CAT", ("ins", 1, "H", 1), ("del", 2, 2)
    naive_a, naive_b = apply(apply(doc, anna), ben), apply(apply(doc, ben), anna)
    ot_a = apply(apply(doc, anna), transform(ben, anna))
    ot_b = apply(apply(doc, ben), transform(anna, ben))
    print(f"naive: Anna sees {naive_a!r}, Ben sees {naive_b!r}")
    print(f"OT:    Anna sees {ot_a!r},  Ben sees {ot_b!r}")

    random.seed(0)
    diverged_naive = diverged_ot = 0
    for _ in range(20_000):
        doc = "".join(random.choice("abcd") for _ in range(random.randint(0, 6)))
        a, b = random_op(doc, 1), random_op(doc, 2)
        if apply(apply(doc, a), b) != apply(apply(doc, b), a):
            diverged_naive += 1
        if apply(apply(doc, a), transform(b, a)) != apply(apply(doc, b), transform(a, b)):
            diverged_ot += 1
    print(f"\n20,000 random concurrent pairs: naive diverged {diverged_naive}, OT diverged {diverged_ot}")
  1. Run it. Naive application gives Anna 'CHT' and Ben 'CHA' — the silent divergence from section 3, including the wrongly deleted A. With transformation both see 'CHA'.
  2. Read the fuzz result: of 20,000 random concurrent pairs on short random documents, naive application diverges about 13,900 times; the transform diverges zero times. That is the TP1 property holding for every case the generator produced.
  3. Break the tie-break: replace (a[1] == b[1] and a[3] < b[3]) with just a[1] == b[1], so both sites believe their insert goes first at an equal position. The fuzz now reports about 2,700 divergences, all from two inserts at the same position.
  4. Replace return ("noop",) with return a, so two concurrent deletes of the same character both delete. The fuzz still reports 0 divergences — both replicas delete an innocent neighbour in the same way — yet the result is wrong. Convergence is not correctness; add a check that the final length matches the intended edits to catch it.
  5. Extension: write transform_list(op, ops) that transforms one operation against a list, and test three concurrent operations through a single server order — the shape Wave and Jupiter use.
Work it out

A document contains CAT. Three clients are connected. Anna inserts S at position 3 (making CATS). Simultaneously, Ben deletes position 1 (the A, making CT). The operations reach the third client, Chloe, in the order Ben-then-Anna, and reach Anna as Ben's arriving after her own.

(a) What goes wrong with naive index-based operations? (b) How does OT fix it? (c) How does a CRDT avoid the problem entirely?

Answer

(a) Naive application diverges.

ReplicaOrder appliedResult
Annainsert S at 3, then delete at 1CATS → delete index 1 → CTS
Chloedelete at 1, then insert S at 3CT → insert at index 3 → out of range

Chloe's document is only 2 characters long, so "insert at 3" is undefined — it either errors, or clamps to the end giving CTS by luck. Rely on luck and a slightly different example diverges outright: if Anna inserts H at 1 while Ben deletes index 2, Anna ends with CHT and Ben with CHA (the lab reproduces this). The users now see permanently different documents with no error raised anywhere — which is the defining failure of this problem.

(b) OT transforms the incoming operation against what has already been applied. When Anna's insert(S, 3) arrives at Chloe, who has already applied delete(1), the transformation function reasons: a deletion occurred at index 1, which is before index 3, so everything after it shifted left by one. It rewrites the operation to insert(S, 2). Chloe applies it to CT and gets CTS — matching Anna. Convergence restored.

This works, and the difficulty is that you need a correct transformation function for every pair of operation types (insert against insert, insert against delete, delete against delete...), each handling ties and overlaps, and the whole set must satisfy formal convergence properties. With three or more concurrent clients you must also transform against chains of operations in the right order. Several published OT algorithms were later proven incorrect precisely here, which is why production OT relies on a central server to impose one order.

(c) A CRDT removes the premise. There are no indices. Each character has a unique immutable identifier, so the document is not CAT but something like:

[C: id=a1] [A: id=a2] [T: id=a3]

Anna's operation is not "insert at 3" but "insert S with id=b1, after a3". Ben's is not "delete index 1" but "mark a2 deleted". Now apply them in either order:

  • Ben first: a2 is tombstoned → visible text CT. Then Anna: place b1 after a3 → CTS.
  • Anna first: b1 placed after a3 → CATS. Then Ben: tombstone a2 → CTS.

Identical, with no transformation logic anywhere. The operations commute because each one names the thing it acts on rather than a position that other operations can invalidate. Ben's delete cannot break Anna's insert because Anna's insert never mentioned a number that Ben's delete could shift.

The cost, stated honestly: every character now carries an identifier (several bytes against one byte of content), and a2 must be retained as a tombstone until every replica has acknowledged it — you cannot discard it, because a late operation might still reference it. For a document edited over years this metadata can exceed the content, which is why production CRDTs compress adjacent runs into blocks with a shared identifier range and garbage-collect tombstones once every replica has seen the delete. That engineering is what separates the idea from something shippable.

Choose it

You are designing three products: (1) a shared plain-text notes app used mostly online by small teams; (2) a field-inspection app whose users edit forms offline for days in places with no signal; (3) a whiteboard of shapes and connectors used by 50 people in workshops. Which concurrency model fits each, and why?

Answer

(1) Server-ordered OT (or a server-mediated sequence CRDT). Text is a sequence, users are online, and a session server per document is natural; OT keeps operations compact and simple. A CRDT library also works well here and buys future offline support.

(2) CRDTs. Days of offline edits on several devices must merge without a central orderer and without surfacing hundreds of transform conflicts; operations that commute are exactly what is needed. Form fields can be last-writer-wins registers or multi-value registers that show both values for a human to pick.

(3) Figma-style per-property last-writer-wins with a central server. Shapes are objects with independent properties; concurrent edits to different properties should both survive, and same-property conflicts can simply take the last value. Add fractional indexing for z-order, parent links with cycle rejection for grouping, and throttled, viewport-culled presence for 50 cursors. The general lesson: choose the algorithm from the data model and the connectivity, not from fashion.
