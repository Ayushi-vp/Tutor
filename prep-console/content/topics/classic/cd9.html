Sam taps "Pay £50" on a phone in a café with weak Wi-Fi. The spinner turns for ten seconds and then the app says "Something went wrong". Sam taps again. Did Sam just pay once, twice, or not at all? Somewhere a server called a card network, which talked to Sam's bank, which may well have approved the payment — and then the response got lost on the way back. If the system guesses "failed" and lets Sam pay again, Sam is charged twice. If it guesses "succeeded", the café may hand over an order nobody paid for. Both guesses are bugs that move real money.

Payment systems are unusual in system design interviews because scale is not the hard part. Even a very large processor handles thousands of payments per second — Stripe reported processing 300 million transactions over the four days from Black Friday to Cyber Monday, an average of under a thousand per second — which a well-run database can absorb. What is hard is that the acceptable rate of lost or duplicated money is zero, while every component is distributed: the client, your API, your database, the acquirer, the card network and the bank, each connected by networks that can drop any message at any moment.

The design that survives this has a few non-negotiable pieces. Every request carries an idempotency key, so retries are safe. Every payment is a state machine that records intent before acting and never guesses outcomes. Every movement of money is written to a double-entry ledger of immutable entries that must always balance, so the books can be checked mechanically. And every day, a reconciliation process compares your records with the outside world's and treats each difference as an incident. Together they make it possible to answer the question auditors, regulators and customers will ask: where did every cent go?

This lesson follows the interview flow: requirements; estimates (payments per second, in-flight external calls, ledger growth, idempotency storage, event volume); API and data model; the high-level design and the critical path of a payment; deep dives on idempotency, on the double-entry ledger, and on unknown outcomes, reconciliation and payouts; then failure modes and trade-offs. The real case is Stripe's own writing on idempotency and on Ledger, its money-movement system, and the lab builds an idempotent charge endpoint and a double-entry ledger in SQLite, retries it concurrently and checks the books.

The mental model: a cashier with a receipt book and a carbon copy

Imagine an old-fashioned cashier. Every customer who wants to pay hands over an order slip with a unique number printed on it. Before doing anything, the cashier looks for that number in the receipt book. If it is there, the cashier simply hands back a copy of the receipt already written — the customer has asked twice, and paying twice would be wrong. If not, the cashier writes the slip number in the book first, then takes the money, then writes the receipt. And every receipt is written with carbon paper into a ledger where every line has two sides: "£50 in to the till", "£48.25 owed to the café owner", "£1.75 kept as commission". The two sides always add up. At closing time, the cashier counts the till against the ledger, and any difference is investigated before going home.

Every piece maps to the design. The order-slip number is the idempotency key. Writing it in the book before acting is claiming the key under a unique constraint. Handing back the existing receipt is returning the stored response to a retry. The two-sided ledger is double-entry bookkeeping, with entries that sum to zero. Counting the till against the ledger is reconciliation.

Where the analogy breaks: our cashier calls out to a bank over a telephone line that sometimes goes dead mid-sentence, and the bank may or may not have done what was asked. A real cashier would simply ask again; we must make sure asking again cannot make the bank act twice, and we must be honest with the customer while we find out.

Who is in the payment chain

A card payment involves several parties, and the design depends on knowing which one you are. The customer pays a merchant. The merchant uses a payment service provider (PSP) such as Stripe or Adyen, which offers one API for many payment methods. Behind the PSP sits an acquirer, the bank or processor that accepts card payments for merchants and connects to the card networks (Visa, Mastercard), which route the request to the customer's issuing bank, which approves or declines. Money moves later, in batches: settlement transfers funds from issuers to acquirers to PSPs, and the PSP pays out to the merchant's bank account, keeping its fees. A design interview usually asks you to be the PSP: you expose the API, hold the ledger, and call out to acquirers.

A payment happens in stages, over days

From the customer's point of view a card payment is instant; from the ledger's point of view it is a sequence spread over days. Authorisation happens in about a second: the issuing bank checks the card and funds and places a hold, but no money has moved. Capture tells the network to actually take the authorised amount — immediately for most online purchases, later for hotels or orders that ship in parts, and possibly for less than the authorised amount. Settlement moves funds between banks in batches, typically a day or two later, net of interchange and network fees. Payout sends the merchant's share to their bank on a schedule. And weeks or months later, a chargeback can pull money back if the cardholder disputes the payment. Each stage is a separate event, can fail on its own, and produces its own ledger transaction — which is why "the payment succeeded" is never the end of the story.

A reader who stops here has the right picture: claim the request's key, record the intent, call out with your own reference, record the outcome only when it is known, post balanced immutable entries, and check everything against the outside world every day.

1 · Requirements

Functional. Merchants create payments (charge a customer's card or other method), capture authorised payments, issue full or partial refunds, see balances and transaction history, and receive payouts to their bank accounts; the platform takes fees. Asynchronous outcomes (bank approvals, 3-D Secure challenges, disputes) are delivered to merchants as webhooks. Out of scope, said aloud: fraud model internals (mention where it sits), currency conversion rates, and card-data storage details — beyond one sentence that card numbers are tokenised in a separate secure vault so the rest of the system never sees them, which keeps most services outside the strictest PCI DSS scope.

Non-functional. Never charge a customer twice for one intent. Never lose a payment that succeeded. The ledger always balances and every entry is explainable years later (financial records are commonly retained for many years). Strong consistency for money state; eventual consistency is fine for dashboards. The customer-facing call should finish in a couple of seconds, most of which is the card network.

The clarifying question: are we the processor, or an orchestrator over one? Assume a PSP that holds the ledger and calls acquirers — the common case and the more interesting one.

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Assume 1,000 payments per second on average and 10,000 per second at peak (a seasonal sale). For scale, Stripe processed 300 million transactions with $18.6 billion of volume over Black Friday to Cyber Monday: 300M ÷ (4 × 86,400 s) ≈ 870 per second on average, about $62 per transaction.

The point of the numbers is that they are small. 10,000 writes per second fits on a well-tuned relational primary, and sharding by merchant is available if needed. Say out loud: the difficulty here is correctness, not scale.

In-flight external calls. A card authorisation takes perhaps 300 ms to 2 s at the acquirer. By Little's law (requests in flight = arrival rate × time each spends in the system), 10,000 per second × 1 s average = 10,000 calls in flight at peak. Use asynchronous I/O and bounded connection pools per acquirer, with timeouts, or threads and sockets run out long before the database does.

Ledger growth. A payment's lifecycle produces several transactions — capture, settlement, fee, payout, sometimes refund — say 6 entries of about 100 bytes: 600 B per payment. 1,000/s × 600 B × 31.5M s/year ≈ 19 TB a year of entries, perhaps 50 TB with indexes; kept for seven years, a few hundred terabytes. Append-only and mostly cold, so it partitions by time.

Idempotency keys. One per mutating request: 1,000/s × 86,400 ≈ 86 million a day. With the stored response (about 500 bytes), that is about 43 GB a day; retained for at least 24 hours, a few days' worth is under 200 GB.

Internal events. Every state change emits events consumed by ledgers, notifications, analytics and risk. Stripe's Ledger alone sees about five billion events a day — about 58,000 per second, far more than external payments, because each payment generates many internal movements.

3 · The API

POST /v1/payments          Idempotency-Key: 7c9e…   {amount: 5000, currency: "gbp",
                                                      payment_method: "pm_tok_…", capture: true}
  → 201 {id: "pay_123", status: "succeeded" | "processing" | "requires_action" | "failed"}
POST /v1/payments/{id}/capture   Idempotency-Key: …   {amount?}
POST /v1/refunds           Idempotency-Key: …         {payment: "pay_123", amount?}
GET  /v1/balance                                       → available, pending (by currency)
webhook → merchant: payment.succeeded, payment.failed, refund.succeeded, dispute.created

Every POST takes an idempotency key. Status values are a state machine, not a boolean: processing exists precisely for "we do not know yet". Stripe's PaymentIntent object is a real-world example, with states such as requires_payment_method, requires_action, processing, succeeded and canceled.

4 · The data model and the ledger

idempotency_keys(merchant_id, key, request_hash, response, created_at,
                 PRIMARY KEY (merchant_id, key))
payments(payment_id PK, merchant_id, amount_minor, currency, status, psp_reference UNIQUE,
         acquirer, created_at)
accounts(account_id PK, type, currency, owner_id)
  -- types: acquirer_clearing, merchant_balance, merchant_pending, platform_revenue,
  --        payouts_in_transit, cash, chargeback_reserve
ledger_txns(txn_id PK, kind, reference, created_at)
ledger_entries(entry_id PK, txn_id, account_id, amount_minor BIGINT, currency)
  -- INVARIANT: for every txn_id and currency, SUM(amount_minor) = 0
outbox(event_id PK, payload, published_at NULL)

Three rules to state explicitly. Integers in minor units: amount_minor is a 64-bit integer of pence or cents, never a float. Entries are immutable: a refund or correction is a new transaction; nothing is ever updated or deleted, so the history of what happened is never rewritten. Balances are derived: an account's balance is the sum of its entries; if that becomes slow, keep a cached balance together with the id of the last entry it includes, and rebuild it from entries whenever in doubt.

The sign convention used here: debits are positive, credits are negative, so every transaction sums to zero. An asset account (cash, money owed to us by the acquirer) grows with debits; a liability (money we owe merchants) or revenue account grows with credits, which is why their balances appear negative in raw sums.

5 · High-level design and the critical path

Walk a payment through the sequence diagram. The merchant's server sends POST /v1/payments with idempotency key K. The API inserts K into idempotency_keys — if the insert conflicts, this is a retry, and it returns the stored response (or "processing" if the first attempt is still running). Having claimed K, it creates the payment in state pending with a fresh reference R, and commits. Only then does it call the acquirer, passing R as the acquirer's own idempotency key. The acquirer approves. In one database transaction the API moves the payment to succeeded, posts the capture's ledger entries, writes an outbox event payment.succeeded, and stores the response against K. It returns the response. The outbox relay publishes the event; the webhook service tells the merchant.

6 · Deep dive A: idempotency done properly

Networks fail in ambiguous ways. Stripe's post lists the cases: the connection never established (safe to retry), the server failed midway, or the server succeeded and the response was lost. In the last two cases the client cannot know what happened, so it must retry, and the server must make retries safe. Stripe's mechanism is the Idempotency-Key header on every mutating request: "Stripe's idempotency works by saving the resulting status code and body of the first request made for any given idempotency key, regardless of whether it succeeds or fails. Subsequent requests with the same key return the same result, including 500 errors."

The implementation has four details that interviewers probe. Claim first. Insert the key under a unique constraint before doing any work. "Check whether it exists, then process" lets two concurrent retries both see nothing and both charge; with a unique insert, exactly one wins and the others get a conflict. Store the result. Save the final response with the key, in the same transaction as the state change, so a retry gets the identical answer, the same payment id included. Bind the key to the request. Store a hash of the parameters and reject a reused key with different parameters — Stripe's idempotency layer "compares incoming parameters to those of the original request and errors if they're not the same". Scope and expire. Keys are scoped to the API account (two merchants may pick the same string) and kept long enough to cover realistic retries; Stripe says keys can be removed once they are at least 24 hours old, after which a reused key starts a new request.

Two subtleties. If a request fails validation, or conflicts with a concurrent request with the same key that is still running, Stripe does not save a result, because no endpoint execution started — the client can simply retry. And idempotency must be carried through every hop: your reference R goes to the acquirer as its idempotency key, the outbox gives each event an id that consumers deduplicate on, and webhooks you receive carry event ids you record. On the client side, retries use exponential backoff with jitter — waiting proportionally to 2n after the n-th failure, plus randomness — so that a recovering server is not hammered by synchronised retries.

7 · Deep dive B: the double-entry ledger

Double-entry bookkeeping records every movement of money as equal and opposite entries in at least two accounts. Use the café payment: £50 (5,000 pence), fee 2.9% + 30p. Fee = 5,000 × 29 ÷ 1,000 + 30 = 145 + 30 = 175 pence; the merchant is owed 5,000 − 175 = 4,825.

Three properties make this model valuable. The invariant is mechanical: every transaction sums to zero per currency, so a query can prove the books balance (the lab runs it on 1,100 transactions). History is complete: because entries are never changed, you can reconstruct any account's balance at any past moment by summing entries up to that time — Stripe says of its Ledger that "transactions previously published into Ledger cannot be deleted or modified, and we can always reconstruct past state by processing all events up to that point." Clearing accounts expose missing legs: money in transit passes through intermediate accounts that should return to zero when each flow completes. Stripe describes this as reservoirs and pipes: at steady state, terminal accounts are full and intermediate "clearing" accounts are empty, and it measures clearing — the fraction of the ledger appropriately zeroed out — as a data-quality signal.

Refunds and corrections are new transactions. A full refund where the platform keeps the 30p fixed fee: acquirer_clearing −5,000 (money going back to the card), merchant_balance +4,855 (the merchant bears 4,825 plus the 30p), platform_revenue +145 (fee returned except 30p). Sum: −5,000 + 4,855 + 145 = 0. If an entry was wrong, you post a reversing transaction and a corrected one; you never edit the original.

Where the ledger lives. For a single PSP, the payment state change and its ledger entries should be in the same database transaction, so they cannot disagree. At larger scale, the ledger often becomes its own service fed by events from many producers (payments, payouts, disputes, fees), with the outbox pattern guaranteeing that every state change produces its event exactly once in effect.

8 · Deep dive C: unknown outcomes, reconciliation and payouts

The timeout. You call the acquirer and it times out. Three possibilities: the request never arrived; it arrived and failed; it arrived and succeeded but the response was lost. The correct pattern: the intent is already durable (payment pending with reference R). Write no ledger entries. Return processing to the merchant — honest, and a valid state in the API. Then resolve it: query the acquirer by R (safe because R was also the acquirer's idempotency key), or wait for the acquirer's webhook, or, as a last resort, wait for the next settlement file. Only when the outcome is known do you post entries and move to succeeded or failed. A merchant who retries with the same key during this window gets "processing", not a second charge.

Reconciliation is the safety net under everything. Every day, acquirers and banks send settlement files and statements listing what actually moved. A reconciler matches each external line to ledger transactions by reference, amount and currency, and every unmatched item — a charge you think succeeded that the acquirer never settled, a settlement for a payment you marked failed, a fee you did not expect — becomes an exception with an owner and a workflow. Clearing account balances are reconciliation's summary metric: if acquirer_clearing has not returned to zero for last Tuesday's captures, something is missing. Stripe states that 99.99% of its dollar volume is fully ingested and verified within four days, and that Ledger and its data-quality platform give over 99.9999% explainability of money movement.

Payouts. A merchant's available balance is captured funds past a holding period, minus refunds, fees and a reserve for chargebacks (disputes where the card network pulls money back, sometimes months later). A daily payout job computes it from the ledger, posts a transaction moving it from merchant_balance to payouts_in_transit, sends a bank transfer with an idempotency key, and on the bank's confirmation moves it from in-transit to cash out. Every step is idempotent and reconciled against bank statements, because a payout sent twice is money that may never come back.

9 · Bottlenecks, failure modes and trade-offs

Acquirer outage or slowness: in-flight calls pile up; use per-acquirer timeouts, circuit breakers and bounded concurrency, route to a secondary acquirer for new payments where contracts allow, and never retry a payment on a different acquirer while the first attempt's outcome is unknown. Database primary failover: committed state survives; in-flight requests fail and are retried by clients with the same keys. Hot merchant: one giant merchant's balance account takes every write; append entries without locking a balance row, and compute balances asynchronously or shard the merchant's pending account. Duplicate webhooks or events: normal at-least-once behaviour; consumers deduplicate by event id. Clock and currency mistakes: every entry carries its currency; cross-currency transactions post in each currency separately through FX accounts so each side still sums to zero.

The trade-offs to close on: honest "processing" states and slower certainty versus optimistic answers that occasionally lose money; a single transactional store for state and ledger (simple, strongly consistent) versus a separate ledger service fed by events (scales, needs outbox and reconciliation); key retention (longer protects late retries, costs storage); and synchronous fraud checks (fewer losses, more latency) versus asynchronous review. The principle underneath all of them: the system never guesses about money. It records intent durably, converges on the truth through idempotent retries and reconciliation, and shows customers an honest intermediate state in the meantime.

Exactly-once is a property of effects, not delivery

The Two Generals problem shows that two parties communicating over a lossy channel can never be certain the other received their last message. For payments, it means "exactly once delivery" is impossible to promise. What is achievable is: deliver at least once (retry until acknowledged), and make processing idempotent at every hop (deduplicate by key or event id). The observable effect — one charge, one ledger transaction, one webhook acted upon — happens exactly once. Saying precisely this, and naming where each deduplication happens (API keys, acquirer references, outbox event ids, webhook event ids), is the senior answer.

The transactional outbox and why not two-phase commit

After a payment succeeds, the database must be updated and an event must be published for webhooks, analytics and the ledger service. Doing them separately risks one without the other: crash after the database commit and the event is lost; publish first and crash, and the event describes something that never committed. Two-phase commit across a database and a message broker is slow and fragile. The outbox pattern writes the event into an outbox table in the same local transaction as the state change; a relay reads unpublished rows, publishes them, and marks them published, retrying until it succeeds. Duplicates are possible (publish then crash before marking), so consumers deduplicate by event id. The result is that state and events can never disagree for long, using only local transactions.

Sagas for multi-step money movement

Some operations span systems that cannot share a transaction: authorise a card, reserve inventory, capture, create a payout. A saga runs them as a sequence of local steps, each with a compensating action — release an authorisation, refund a capture, cancel a payout before it is sent. If step 3 fails, compensations run in reverse. Compensations in payments are not free (a refund is visible to the customer and may cost fees), so the order of steps is chosen to make the likely failures cheap: authorise before capturing, capture only when the goods are certain.

Ledger performance without losing correctness

Summing all entries for a busy merchant on every balance query does not scale. Keep a cached balance per account with the id of the last entry it includes, updated asynchronously; any reader can verify or rebuild it from entries. Avoid locking a hot balance row on every payment: append entries freely and let a single writer per account (or a periodic job) roll them up. Specialised ledger databases go further — TigerBeetle, for example, is built around double-entry transfers and enforces balance limits inside the database — but the core idea is the same: the immutable entries are the truth, balances are a derived, rebuildable view.

Fees, rounding and multi-currency

Percentage fees produce fractions of a minor unit, so define the rounding rule (round half up, round down, banker's rounding) and apply it in one place; across millions of transactions the choice is visible in revenue. Currencies differ in minor units: pounds and dollars have 2 decimal places, Japanese yen has none, some currencies have 3, so the currency's exponent must be part of the money type. Cross-currency payments post in each currency separately through foreign-exchange accounts, with the rate recorded, so that every transaction still sums to zero per currency.

Data quality as a product

At Stripe's scale, correctness is monitored like availability. Its Ledger post describes explicit cross-system checks for completeness, automated anomaly detection, and quality targets measured continuously: 99.99% of dollar volume ingested and verified within four days, over 99.9999% explainability of money movement, while data volume grew tenfold. The lesson for a design interview: name the correctness metrics (unmatched reconciliation items, uncleared clearing balances, unbalanced transactions — which must be zero) and say who is paged when they move.

Holding periods, reserves and chargebacks

Money captured today is not yet safe to pay out. Customers can dispute a card payment long after purchase, and when they do, the card network debits the acquirer, which debits the PSP, which must recover it from the merchant. If the merchant has already been paid and has since disappeared, the platform absorbs the loss. So captured funds sit in a pending merchant account for a holding period before becoming available, and riskier merchants have a reserve: a percentage of each payment, or a fixed amount, held back for longer. Each of these is just more accounts and more balanced transactions — pending to available when the hold ends, available to reserve and back — which is why a ledger with clear account types makes risk policy implementable rather than a tangle of flags.

Testing money code

Unit tests with hand-picked amounts miss the bugs that matter. Two techniques catch more. Property-based tests generate thousands of random sequences of operations — charges, partial captures, refunds, chargebacks, payouts, in random orders with random retries and timeouts injected — and assert invariants after every step: every transaction sums to zero, no account goes negative where it must not, every charge has at most one capture, and reconciling against a simulated acquirer finds nothing. Replay tests take a day of production events (scrubbed of personal data) and run them through a new version of the posting logic, comparing the resulting balances with the old version's; any difference must be explained before deploying. The lab's zero-sum assertion and reconciliation check are miniature versions of both.

Security boundaries

Card numbers live only in a tokenisation vault with its own network segment, access controls and audit; everything else handles tokens. This shrinks the systems subject to the strictest PCI DSS requirements and limits the damage of a breach elsewhere. Internal service calls that move money use mutual TLS and per-service credentials, and administrative actions that can create ledger entries (manual adjustments) require two-person approval and are logged immutably.

The real case: Stripe's idempotency keys and Ledger

Stripe has written about both halves of this design. Its idempotency post starts from the fact that networks fail in exotic ways and that many failures leave the client uncertain whether its request was processed. Its answer is a contract any client can rely on: send an Idempotency-Key header on mutating requests, and if a request fails because of a network error, "you can safely retry it with the same idempotency key, and the customer is charged only once." The API reference makes the semantics precise: the status code and body of the first request with a key are saved and returned to every later request with that key, including 500 errors; keys may be up to 255 characters, and Stripe suggests V4 UUIDs; keys can be pruned once they are at least 24 hours old; and parameters are compared with the original request, with a mismatch treated as an error. The post adds the client half: retry with exponential backoff and random jitter so that many clients failing at once do not hammer a recovering server, which Stripe's own client libraries do automatically.

The ledger half is described in Stripe's February 2024 post on Ledger, its system for tracking and validating money movement. Ledger is "based on double-entry bookkeeping, a standard method for guaranteeing that all money in a system is fully accounted for by balancing credits and debits." It is immutable: published transactions cannot be deleted or modified, and past state can be reconstructed by replaying events. Stripe models its systems as fund flows moving money between accounts — for example, a charge.creation event sets up a balance in an undisbursed account, and a later charge.release moves the funds to the business balance. Steady state means terminal accounts are full and intermediate clearing accounts are empty, and Stripe measures clearing — the fraction of the ledger appropriately zeroed out — as a quality signal. The scale: each day Ledger sees about five billion events, and 99.99% of dollar volume is fully ingested and verified within four days; together with Stripe's data-quality platform it reports over 99.9999% explainability of money movement. For context, the same post notes that over Black Friday to Cyber Monday Stripe processed 300 million transactions with $18.6 billion of volume while the API maintained over 99.999% availability.

The design lesson is how ordinary the core ideas are. Neither idempotency keys nor double-entry bookkeeping is new; what makes them work at Stripe's scale is applying them everywhere, without exception, and measuring correctness continuously.

It is worth reading Stripe's quality numbers the way an engineer would. "99.99% of our dollar volume is fully ingested and verified within four days" sets two targets at once: a completeness target (almost all money accounted for) and a latency target (within four days, which allows for banks' settlement cycles). It also implies a residue — 0.01% of dollar volume — that is not fully verified in that window and must be worked through by exception queues and people. At $18.6 billion over one long weekend, 0.01% is about $1.9 million, which shows why reconciliation tooling and staffing are part of the system, not an afterthought. In an interview, framing correctness as measurable targets with an explicit residue and an owner for it is a strong close.

Failure modes and what to monitor

Unbalanced transactions (must be zero; page immediately). Uncleared clearing balances by age and acquirer. Reconciliation exceptions: count, value and age of unmatched items. Payments stuck in processing beyond a threshold, by acquirer. Idempotency conflicts and parameter-mismatch errors, which reveal client bugs. Acquirer latency, timeout and decline rates, with circuit-breaker state. Outbox lag: age of the oldest unpublished event. Webhook delivery failures to merchants. Duplicate charge reports from customers, investigated individually because each is a potential correctness bug.

Goal: build an idempotent charge endpoint and a double-entry ledger, hammer it with concurrent retries, and prove the books balance. Python 3.12, standard library (sqlite3, threading), runs in a second.

"""Idempotency keys + a double-entry ledger in SQLite. Python 3.12 stdlib.
Amounts are integers in pence. Positive = debit, negative = credit; every transaction sums to 0."""
import json, random, sqlite3, threading, uuid

con = sqlite3.connect(":memory:", check_same_thread=False)
lock = threading.Lock()                              # one connection, serialised like a DB primary
con.executescript("""
CREATE TABLE idem(key TEXT PRIMARY KEY, request_hash TEXT, response TEXT);
CREATE TABLE txns(txn_id TEXT PRIMARY KEY, kind TEXT, state TEXT);
CREATE TABLE entries(txn_id TEXT, account TEXT, amount INTEGER);
""")
acquirer_charges = []                                # what the card network actually did

def post(txn_id, kind, legs):
    assert sum(a for _, a in legs) == 0, "unbalanced transaction"
    con.execute("INSERT INTO txns VALUES (?, ?, 'posted')", (txn_id, kind))
    con.executemany("INSERT INTO entries VALUES (?, ?, ?)", [(txn_id, acct, a) for acct, a in legs])

def charge(key, amount, merchant):
    req = json.dumps([amount, merchant])
    with lock:
        try:                                         # claim the key FIRST, under a unique constraint
            con.execute("INSERT INTO idem VALUES (?, ?, NULL)", (key, req)); con.commit()
        except sqlite3.IntegrityError:
            h, resp = con.execute("SELECT request_hash, response FROM idem WHERE key=?", (key,)).fetchone()
            if h != req:
                return {"error": "idempotency key reused with different parameters"}
            return json.loads(resp) if resp else {"status": "processing"}
    txn = str(uuid.uuid4())
    acquirer_charges.append((txn, amount))           # the external call (could time out here)
    fee = amount * 29 // 1000 + 30                   # 2.9% + 30p
    with lock:
        post(txn, "capture", [("acquirer_clearing", amount),
                              (f"merchant:{merchant}", -(amount - fee)),
                              ("platform_revenue", -fee)])
        resp = {"status": "succeeded", "txn": txn, "fee": fee, "merchant": merchant}
        con.execute("UPDATE idem SET response=? WHERE key=?", (json.dumps(resp), key)); con.commit()
    return resp

def refund(txn_id, amount, fee, merchant, keep_fixed_fee=True):
    back = fee - 30 if keep_fixed_fee else fee       # policy: platform keeps the 30p fixed fee
    with lock:
        post(str(uuid.uuid4()), f"refund:{txn_id}", [("acquirer_clearing", -amount),
                                                     (f"merchant:{merchant}", amount - back),
                                                     ("platform_revenue", back)])
        con.commit()

if __name__ == "__main__":
    # 1) A flaky client retries the same £50 charge 5 times, concurrently.
    key = str(uuid.uuid4())
    results = []
    threads = [threading.Thread(target=lambda: results.append(charge(key, 5000, "M1"))) for _ in range(5)]

Work it out

A customer pays £50 for an order. Your platform takes a 2.9% + 30p fee. Write the ledger entries for (a) the successful capture, and (b) a later full refund. Then (c): the acquirer call times out and you never learn the outcome. Walk through exactly what happens.

Answer

(a) The capture. Fee = 5000 × 0.029 + 30 = 145 + 30 = 175p. Merchant receives 5000 − 175 = 4825p. All amounts in pence, all entries in one transaction, summing to zero (debits positive, credits negative):

AccountAmountMeaning
acquirer_clearing+5000money arriving from the card network
merchant:M1 (pending)−4825owed to the merchant
platform_revenue−175our fee

Sum: 5000 − 4825 − 175 = 0. The invariant holds, and note that nothing was updated — three rows were appended. Note also that the merchant balance is pending, not available: chargeback risk means it is not paid out yet.

(b) The refund. A new transaction, linked to the first, with entries in the opposite direction. The interesting question is the fee, and it is a business policy you should ask about rather than assume. With a full fee refund:

AccountAmountMeaning
acquirer_clearing−5000money returned to the cardholder
merchant:M1+4825clawed back from the merchant
platform_revenue+175fee returned

If the platform keeps the 30p fixed fee, then platform_revenue is +145 and the merchant is debited 4855 instead, so the merchant bears the fixed fee (−5000 + 4855 + 145 = 0). Either is defensible; what is not defensible is leaving it undefined, because then the books will not balance. The original entries are never touched — the refund is new rows, and the payment's history shows both events, which is exactly what an auditor needs.

(c) The timeout. Walk it precisely, because this is the question:

  1. Before calling the acquirer you already wrote a payment row in state pending, with the idempotency key and your own reference. So the intent is durable regardless of what happens next.
  2. The call times out. You write no ledger entries — you do not know whether money moved, and guessing in either direction is a money-losing bug. The payment stays pending.
  3. The customer sees processing, not success and not failure. This is honest and it is the correct product behaviour.
  4. If the customer retries with the same idempotency key, the unique index returns the existing pending payment rather than starting a second charge. This is the moment the idempotency key earns its place.
  5. A reconciliation worker picks up payments pending past a threshold and queries the acquirer using your reference. Because you passed your own reference as their idempotency key, they can tell you authoritatively what happened.
  6. On "succeeded", write the ledger entries and move to succeeded. On "never received", mark failed — and only now is a fresh attempt safe. If the acquirer also cannot say, escalate to a human with the daily settlement file as the tiebreaker.

The principle to state: the system never guesses about money. It records intent durably, converges on the truth through reconciliation, and shows the customer an honest intermediate state in the meantime. Every alternative — optimistically marking success, or retrying blind — trades a small amount of user-facing latency for a small probability of losing or duplicating real money, and that trade is never worth making.

Diagnose it

Finance reports that the acquirer_clearing account for one acquirer has grown by about £12,000 a day for a week instead of returning to zero. Payments look healthy and no customers have complained. What is probably happening and how do you find it?

Answer

Captures are debiting clearing (money expected from the acquirer) but matching settlement credits are not being posted, so the expected money has not been matched to money received. Candidates: the settlement-file ingestion for that acquirer is failing or changed format; settlements are arriving net of a new fee the reconciler does not recognise, so amounts do not match and lines fall into an exception queue; or the acquirer is genuinely withholding funds (a reserve or a dispute hold). Check the reconciliation exception queue for that acquirer first, then compare the settlement files' totals with the week's captures, then call the acquirer. Fix the cause, post the missing settlement and fee transactions (never edit old ones), and add an alert on clearing age so a week does not pass next time. The general lesson: a clearing account that does not clear is the ledger telling you a leg of a flow is missing, before any customer notices.
