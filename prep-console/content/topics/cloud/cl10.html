A fintech company's architecture review proudly lists "multi-AZ everything" and "daily backups". Then three incidents happen within a year. A migration script with a bad WHERE clause deletes a month of transactions; the multi-AZ database replicates the deletion to its standby in milliseconds, and the "daily backup" turns out to be a snapshot schedule that silently stopped when someone changed the database's name. A ransomware group that compromises a CI credential deletes every snapshot in the account before encrypting the data. And during a major regional event, the team's failover runbook — "launch the stack in us-west-2 with Terraform, update the DNS record" — fails at both steps: the deploy tooling and the DNS zone's control plane both live in the impaired region, and the DR region's account quota allows a quarter of the instances they need.

Each failure maps onto a gap between a reliability idea and its cloud implementation. sc17 laid out the ideas: failure domains, RTO and RPO, the disaster-recovery ladder, multi-region data patterns, static stability. This lesson is about making those ideas real with cloud services and configuration, and checking them with numbers: what exactly a zone protects against; how backups must be placed, locked and tested to survive the incidents you fear; how health checks and DNS failover behave second by second; how to move traffic away from a sick zone; how quotas become availability limits; and what backup-and-restore, pilot light and warm standby actually deliver in minutes and dollars for a concrete system.

It closes with two real events: AWS's December 2021 us-east-1 network event, which impaired control planes, monitoring and AWS's own support tooling while running workloads kept going, and Google Cloud's April 2023 Paris incident, in which a fire in one zone degraded the whole region through shared services.

Think of cloud reliability as insurance for a shop, bought in layers, where each layer covers a different kind of disaster and costs a different premium.

The first layer covers a broken till or a burst pipe in one room: you keep spare equipment in another room of the same building — that's running in multiple availability zones. Cheap, and it handles the common accidents. The second layer covers a mistake by staff — someone shreds the wrong ledger: you keep copies of the books as they were each day for the last month — that's backups with point-in-time recovery. The third covers theft by someone with keys: copies kept in a bank vault the shop's keys don't open, under a time lock — that's backups in a separate account behind a vault lock. The fourth covers the whole street being closed: a second shop across town, either an empty unit you could fit out in a day (backup and restore), a unit with the stock already delivered but no staff (pilot light), a small open branch that can scale up (warm standby), or two full branches serving customers all the time (active-active).

The two numbers that decide how much insurance to buy come from the business, not from engineering: the recovery time objective (RTO) — how long the service may be down — and the recovery point objective (RPO) — how much recent data may be lost. An internal reporting tool might accept a day of downtime and a day of lost data; a payments ledger might accept fifteen minutes and zero. Engineering's job is to meet those numbers at the lowest cost and prove it.

A useful habit is to write the objectives down per system rather than for "the platform". A company typically has a handful of systems where minutes matter (checkout, payments, the login path), a larger group where an hour or two is tolerable (internal APIs, search indexing), and a long tail where a day is fine (reporting, back-office tools). Each group gets the cheapest layer that meets its numbers, and the expensive protections — a standby region, frequent restore drills — are spent where downtime actually costs money. Writing the numbers down also exposes hidden coupling: a "tolerate a day" system that the checkout path calls synchronously quietly inherits checkout's fifteen-minute target.

Where the analogy breaks: insurance pays out after the fact; it doesn't make the shop reopen faster. Cloud reliability layers actually do the recovery, but only if they're configured, have capacity, and don't depend on whatever just failed. A second region whose failover requires the first region's DNS control plane is insurance with the claim form locked in the burning building.

1. Zones: the baseline, and its limits

Running in multiple availability zones is the baseline for production because zone-level events are the most common large failures and the cheapest to survive (cl1). The configuration checklist: compute spread across at least three zones with enough capacity in any two to carry peak (about 67% maximum utilisation per zone); databases with a synchronous standby or replicas in other zones (cl7); zone-local copies of zonal dependencies such as NAT gateways (cl3); load balancers enabled in every zone; and caches replicated across zones.

What zones don't cover, from the incidents in this track: regional software failures (S3 in 2017, Kinesis in 2020, DynamoDB's DNS in 2025), logical errors that replicate everywhere, and physical events that spread through shared regional services. Gray failures — a zone that's slow or dropping a fraction of packets rather than down — are the most common zone problem in practice, and the hardest for health checks to see.

Zonal shift is the tool for those. With Amazon Application Recovery Controller (ARC), you can shift a supported resource's traffic away from one zone — for load balancers, the zone's nodes stop receiving traffic — for an expiry you set between one minute and three days, extendable, and cancel it at any time. AWS's documentation is explicit that you must pre-scale so the remaining zones can take the load. Zonal autoshift lets AWS start such a shift when its own telemetry detects a zone impairment, and runs regular practice shifts to prove your application tolerates it.

2. Backups that survive the incidents you fear

Each incident class needs a property of the backup:

IncidentBackup property neededAWS mechanism
Bad migration, dropped table, corrupted writesrestore to a moment before the error; retention longer than time to noticeRDS/Aurora PITR (up to 35 days), DynamoDB PITR, S3 versioning
Account compromise, ransomware, malicious insidera copy the compromised credentials can't delete or decryptAWS Backup cross-account copy, vault access policies, Vault Lock (compliance mode), KMS key in the backup account
Region-wide failurea copy and the means to restore it in another regioncross-region copies, Aurora Global Database, DynamoDB global tables, S3 replication
Provider- or tool-level deletion (cl9's UniSuper case)a copy outside the provisioning system's reachseparate account; for critical data, another provider or a third-party backup system

AWS Backup centralises this: backup plans (schedules, retention, lifecycle to cold storage, copy actions) apply to resources selected by tag; recovery points land in vaults; copy rules send them to vaults in other regions and accounts in the same organisation. Vault Lock enforces retention: in governance mode, privileged users can still remove the lock; in compliance mode, once a grace period ends, nobody — including the root user — can delete recovery points before their retention expires or change the lock. AWS Backup also offers logically air-gapped vaults that are isolated by design and shareable for recovery.

Three practical traps. Encryption keys: a snapshot encrypted with a KMS key in the production account can't be restored elsewhere unless the key is shared — and if the attacker schedules that key for deletion, the copies become unreadable. Copies for compromise protection should be re-encrypted with a key owned by the backup account. Consistency: a crash-consistent volume snapshot of a running database is like pulling the power; database-native backups (RDS snapshots, PITR) are transactionally consistent. Restore time: restoring creates new resources — a new database endpoint, volumes that load blocks lazily from S3 (cl6) — and large restores take hours. AWS Backup's restore testing feature can run scheduled restores and validation to measure it.

3. Health checks and DNS failover, traced

Route 53 health checks are the most common way to detect a failed regional endpoint and steer traffic away from it. From the Route 53 documentation (October 2026): health checkers in locations around the world send requests every 30 seconds, or every 10 with the fast interval option; for HTTP(S) checks, a check fails if a TCP connection isn't established within 4 seconds or the endpoint doesn't return a 2xx or 3xx status within 2 seconds of connecting (HTTPS checks don't validate certificates); each checker marks the endpoint unhealthy after the configured failure threshold (three by default); and Route 53 considers the endpoint healthy if more than 18% of checkers report it healthy, unhealthy otherwise — a threshold chosen so that network problems isolating a few checker locations don't cause failovers. Health checks for AWS endpoints cost $0.50 a month each, plus $1.00 per optional feature such as HTTPS, string matching or the fast interval; the first 50 for AWS endpoints are free.

Three refinements matter. Calculated health checks combine children ("healthy if 2 of 3 zones' endpoints are healthy") so one zone's failure doesn't trigger a regional failover. CloudWatch-alarm health checks fail over on metrics such as error rate, catching gray failures that simple probes miss. And ARC routing controls act as manual on/off switches implemented as health checks, operated through a cluster data plane spread across five regions, so a human decision to fail over doesn't depend on editing DNS records through Route 53's control plane in us-east-1 — the anti-pattern AWS's fault-isolation guidance calls out (cl1).

4. Quotas and limits as availability risks

Quotas cap what you can do, and failure recovery is when you try to do the most. A list worth checking per region you rely on:

  • Compute: on-demand and spot vCPU quotas per instance family group; GPU families often start at zero; capacity for specific instance types in specific zones (cl4).
  • Serverless: Lambda account concurrency (1,000 by default) and per-function scale rate (cl5); Fargate task quotas.
  • Networking: free IP addresses in subnets, Elastic IPs (5 per region by default), NAT gateways per zone, security group rules (cl3).
  • API rates: EC2 RunInstances and Describe calls, STS AssumeRole (600 per second per account per region by default for those calls), KMS requests, Route 53 change rates — all hit by automation during recovery.
  • Service throughput defaults: DynamoDB table throughput (40,000 units per table by default), SNS publish rates (30,000 per second in us-east-1 but 1,500 in Frankfurt in October 2026), SQS FIFO limits by region.

DR regions are the classic trap: quotas start at defaults and grow with use, so a region that runs nothing day to day may allow a fraction of production's scale. Service Quotas publishes utilisation for many quotas as CloudWatch metrics; alarm at 70–80%, raise DR-region quotas to production levels in advance, and include quota checks in drills.

5. Three DR strategies, worked with time budgets and cost

Take a concrete system: an e-commerce API on 20 m7g.large instances behind an Application Load Balancer, an Aurora PostgreSQL cluster (writer plus one reader, db.r7g.large) with 2 TB of data writing about 50 GB of changes a day, a Valkey cache, and 50 TB of product images in S3. Primary region us-east-1, DR region us-west-2 (whose prices were the same as us-east-1's for these services in October 2026). The business asks what each strategy delivers and costs.

Backup and restore. AWS Backup copies the daily Aurora snapshot and configuration to a vault in the DR region (and a separate account); images are replicated by S3 replication; all infrastructure is in Terraform, applied only on disaster.

Pilot light. Data replicated continuously; infrastructure defined and partly provisioned; compute off.

  • Aurora Global Database with a secondary cluster in us-west-2: replication "typically under a second" per the documentation, so RPO ≈ 1 second. Cost: one db.r7g.large ($0.276 × 730 ≈ $201), 2 TB of storage ($0.10 × 2,048 ≈ $205), replicated log transfer (~1.5 TB a month × $0.02 ≈ $30).
  • VPC, load balancer and security groups pre-provisioned (the ALB costs $0.0225 an hour, ≈ $16 a month), autoscaling group at zero instances, DNS failover records already in place.
  • Failover: decide (15 min), fail over the global database (minutes — measure), scale the autoscaling group to 20 (5–10 min, if quota and capacity allow), warm up, DNS shifts automatically: RTO ≈ 30–45 minutes.
  • Monthly ≈ $450 plus image replication.

Warm standby. Pilot light plus a running, scaled-down stack: 5 m7g.large instances (5 × $0.0816 × 730 ≈ $298), a small cache, the load balancer serving health checks continuously. Failover: the global database fails over, the autoscaling group scales from 5 to 20 under load, DNS already points traffic as the health checks fail: RTO ≈ 10–20 minutes, RPO ≈ 1 second. Monthly ≈ $750–800 plus images. Because the stack is always running, its configuration is continuously proven — the biggest practical advantage over pilot light.

For comparison, production's own compute and database cost about $1,191 (instances) + $403 (two Aurora instances) + $205 (storage) ≈ $1,800 a month before the cache, load balancer and transfer. Warm standby adds roughly 40%; pilot light 25%; backup-and-restore a few percent. Active-active (sc17) roughly doubles the bill and moves the hard problems into data consistency. The business decision is whether hours of downtime cost more than a few hundred dollars a month — usually yes for revenue-generating systems.

6. Failover that depends only on data planes

The best DR plans are the ones that need the fewest actions in the failing moment, and none of them on a control plane. Concretely: infrastructure in the DR region is created ahead of time by the same IaC pipeline (not during the event); DNS records and health checks exist already, so failover is Route 53 answering differently, not someone editing a record; credentials for the DR region's workloads are issued there, with STS regional endpoints; the CI/CD system, artifact registry, secrets and observability stack used during recovery are available outside the primary region; and humans can sign in through a break-glass path that doesn't depend on the primary region's identity provider. AWS's fault-isolation whitepaper recommends exactly this posture for global services: rely on their data planes, not their control planes, in recovery paths.

Detection is usually the longest part of RTO

Post-incident timelines often show that systems failed in seconds, recovery mechanics took minutes, and deciding to fail over took the rest. Partial failures are ambiguous — error rates at 8%, a region that might recover in ten minutes — and failing over has costs and risks of its own (data loss up to RPO, failback work, failover bugs). Teams shorten detection by defining failover criteria in advance ("if checkout error rate exceeds 5% for 10 minutes and the provider's health dashboard confirms a regional event, fail over"), automating the evidence gathering, and pre-authorising the on-call engineer to act. Automatic regional failover is rarer than automatic zonal failover precisely because the decision is high-stakes and false positives are expensive.

Failback is a second failover

After failing over, the old primary region recovers, but its data is now stale and the secondary has taken writes. Returning is a planned operation: replicate back (for Aurora Global Database, the old primary rejoins as a secondary and resynchronises; for self-managed replication, a rebuild), verify, then switch over with no data loss during a quiet period. Reconciling the RPO window — writes that reached the old primary but not the secondary before the failure — is a business process as much as a technical one: they may need to be replayed, compensated or acknowledged as lost.

Health checks can cause outages

Aggressive health checking turns transient problems into failovers and failovers into flapping. A dependency check embedded in a health endpoint ("healthy only if the database responds") can mark every instance or every region unhealthy at once when that shared dependency is slow, removing all capacity — the classic cascading health-check failure. Good practice separates liveness (the process can serve) from deep readiness, uses calculated checks with thresholds across zones, fails open when every target looks unhealthy (load balancers do this by routing to all targets when all fail), and adds hysteresis so recovery requires sustained health.

Recovery capacity is a shared resource

In a large regional event, thousands of customers fail over to the same few neighbouring regions at once, all launching instances and creating resources. Capacity and API rate limits in those regions are suddenly contended. This is why AWS's guidance emphasises static stability and pre-provisioned capacity, why capacity reservations exist for DR regions, and why warm standby is more reliable in practice than pilot light even though their designed RTOs differ by only tens of minutes.

Data-corruption recovery needs different tooling from infrastructure recovery

Infrastructure failures are loud; corruption is quiet. A bug writing wrong values for six days is discovered on day seven. Replicas, multi-region databases and even hourly snapshots all faithfully preserve the corruption. Recovery means restoring a point-in-time copy from before the bug into a separate environment and reconciling — repairing affected rows from the clean copy while keeping valid writes made since. PITR retention, immutable logs of changes (event streams, CDC), and tools to diff datasets are what make that possible, and they're absent from many DR plans that only consider losing a region.

Cells limit how much one failure can reach

Zones and regions are failure domains the provider defines; cells are ones you define. A cell is a complete, independent copy of a service — its own compute, data partition and dependencies — serving a slice of customers, with a thin routing layer that maps each customer to a cell. A bad deploy, a poison request or a noisy tenant then takes down one cell's customers rather than everyone, and deployments roll out cell by cell. AWS uses cells internally (the Kinesis remediation in cl8 is an example) and recommends them in the Well-Architected guidance; the costs are duplicated fixed overhead per cell and the routing layer, which must itself be extremely simple and highly available. Cells complement zones and regions: a regional failure still takes out every cell in the region.

SLAs, error budgets and what reliability costs

Each extra nine costs disproportionately more (sc17), and providers' SLAs don't compose into yours: a request touching five services each at 99.95% has, at best, about 99.75% availability if their failures are independent — and they often aren't. Senior reliability work is choosing where the money goes: the highest-traffic, highest-value paths get zone resilience, tested restores and a standby region; internal tools get backups and a runbook. Writing those decisions down per system, with measured RTO and RPO from the last drill, is what turns "highly available" from a slogan into a property.

Failure modes and their symptoms

Backups that weren't. A backup job silently stopped (renamed resource, tag removed, permissions changed); discovered during an incident. Monitor backup job success per resource, alarm on resources without a recent recovery point, and run restore tests.

Restores slower than RTO. A multi-terabyte restore takes hours; lazily loaded volumes make the restored database slow for hours more. Measure restore time in drills and choose strategies accordingly.

Keys and permissions missing in the recovery location. Encrypted snapshots can't be restored because the KMS key isn't available or shared; IAM roles for the restored application don't exist in the DR account. Include keys and roles in DR IaC and test with the real recovery account.

DNS failover that doesn't fail over. Health checks probing an endpoint that stays up while the application behind it fails (checking the load balancer's static page rather than a real path), TTLs of hours, clients caching forever. Probe a meaningful path, keep TTLs at 60 seconds or so for failover records, and make clients reconnect on errors.

Hidden single-region dependencies. Identity provider, CI/CD, container registry, secrets, observability, status page or a third-party API hosted in the primary region. Inventory them and test with the primary region blocked.

Quotas in the DR region. Scale-up fails with vCPU limit errors. Pre-raise quotas.

Gray zone failures not detected. Elevated latency in one zone; health checks pass. Monitor metrics per zone and use zonal shift on evidence.

What to measure

Per critical system: last successful backup and restore test, measured restore time, replication lag to the DR region, DR-region quota utilisation at production scale, health-check status history, results of the latest game day (measured RTO and RPO against targets), and time since the last real switchover.

Case one: AWS us-east-1, 7 December 2021

AWS's summary explains that it runs an internal network, separate from the main AWS network, hosting foundational services such as monitoring, internal DNS and authorisation services and parts of the EC2 control plane. At 7:30 AM PST, an automated activity to scale capacity of an AWS service hosted in the main network triggered an unexpected behaviour from a large number of clients inside the internal network: a surge of connection activity that overwhelmed the networking devices between the two networks, causing congestion, latency and errors, which in turn caused more retries. Internal monitoring was impaired, which made it harder for operators to see what was happening; they worked through the morning to identify the cause, move traffic and add capacity, and the network devices fully recovered at 2:22 PM PST.

The impact had a clear shape. Running EC2 instances, existing load balancers and direct access to S3 and DynamoDB kept working — data planes in the main network were largely fine. Control planes and services depending on the internal network were impaired: EC2 API calls had elevated errors and latency from 7:33 AM; Route 53 APIs were impaired from 7:30 AM to about 2:30 PM (DNS resolution itself kept working); console sign-in failed; STS had elevated latency until 4:28 PM; API Gateway recovered by 4:37 PM; ECS, EKS and Fargate APIs had errors, Fargate normalising by 5:00 PM; EventBridge event delivery was paused from 12:35 to 2:35 PM; Amazon Connect recovered by 4:41 PM; CloudWatch monitoring was delayed. AWS's own Support Contact Center and the Service Health Dashboard were affected, and AWS committed to a new dashboard and a support system architecture spanning regions.

The lesson matches this lesson's main theme. Customers whose systems were statically stable — capacity already running, no need to launch instances, change DNS records, or mint new credentials during the event — saw little impact. Customers whose recovery depended on control planes, on autoscaling to add capacity, or on deploying fixes through tools in the region, struggled for hours. And a monitoring system in the impaired path slowed even AWS.

Case two: Google Cloud europe-west9 (Paris), 25–26 April 2023

Google's incident report describes a cooling-system water pipe leak in a non-Google part of a multi-tenant data-centre facility in Paris. Water entered an associated uninterruptible power supply (UPS) room and led to a fire. In zone europe-west9-a, about 58% of Compute Engine instances and 55% of persistent-disk volumes went offline. Zones europe-west9-b and europe-west9-c weren't physically damaged but were impaired through shared regional systems; the report highlights regional Spanner, whose loss of quorum amplified the outage's scope, and IAM, and the other zones regained service once those regional systems recovered on 26 April. Google's remediations included a detailed risk analysis of water intrusion across its data centres, evaluating drip trays in multi-story facilities, and an audit of regional Spanner allocation across all regions.

It's the mirror image of the AWS case: the trigger was physical and confined to one zone, but regional software shared by all zones spread it. Both say the same thing about design. Zones are excellent protection against most failures and should be the baseline; systems whose RTO can't tolerate a regional degradation need a second region, prepared in advance, with a failover path that doesn't depend on the region in trouble.

Goal: simulate Route 53-style health checking and DNS failover, then build a DR strategy calculator — Python 3.12 standard library only. About 20 minutes; no cloud account needed.

  1. Checkers. Model 16 health-checker locations, each probing every 30 seconds with a random offset. A probe fails if the endpoint is down, or randomly with probability 2% to mimic network noise. Each checker is unhealthy after threshold consecutive failures and healthy again after one success.
  2. Aggregate. At each second, the endpoint is healthy if more than 18% of checkers say healthy. Fail the endpoint at t = 300 s and record when the aggregate flips. Repeat 200 times and report the distribution of detection times for threshold 3 at 30 s and at 10 s intervals.
  3. Flapping. With the endpoint never down, raise noise to 30% and count false failovers per hour with threshold 1 and threshold 3.
  4. Clients. Give 10,000 clients cached answers with random remaining TTLs uniform in [0, 60] s, plus 2% that never refresh. Report the share of clients on the secondary 30, 60 and 120 s after the flip.
  5. Strategies. Encode the three strategies from section 5 as lists of (step, minutes low, minutes high) and monthly costs; compute RTO ranges and print a table. Add the downtime cost per hour as an input and find, for each strategy, the expected annual cost (monthly cost × 12 + probability of a regional event per year × RTO × downtime cost).

What to look for: detection around 60–100 s at 30-second intervals and threshold 3, around 20–40 s at 10 seconds; threshold 1 producing frequent false failovers under noise; about half the clients moved 30 s after the flip and nearly all except the never-refreshers by 60 s. Review: "our DR plan is solid"

A B2B SaaS (RTO target 1 hour, RPO 15 minutes) presents its plan. Production in us-east-1: EKS across three zones, RDS PostgreSQL Multi-AZ (1.5 TB), S3 documents (20 TB, versioned). DR: RDS automated snapshots copied to us-west-2 every 6 hours; S3 cross-region replication to us-west-2 in the same account; Terraform for us-west-2 in the same repository, never applied; DNS in Route 53 with a 1-hour TTL, failover done by "updating the record"; EKS images in an ECR repository in us-east-1; CI runners in us-east-1; SSO through an identity provider hosted in us-east-1. Identify every way this plan misses its targets and propose fixes in priority order.

Answer

RPO miss. Snapshot copies every 6 hours give RPO up to 6 hours, not 15 minutes. Use a cross-region read replica (or Aurora Global Database after migrating) for seconds of RPO, keep PITR in-region for logical errors.

RTO miss. Restoring 1.5 TB plus applying never-tested Terraform for a full EKS stack won't happen in an hour. Move to at least pilot light: the replica running, VPC, EKS cluster and node groups pre-provisioned at minimal size, applied continuously by the same pipeline so it's proven; consider warm standby for the critical services.

Control-plane dependencies. "Updating the record" is a Route 53 control-plane call (us-east-1) — and the 1-hour TTL makes it slow anyway. Pre-create failover records with health checks (or ARC routing controls), TTL 60 s.

Single-region dependencies. Images in an us-east-1 ECR repository (enable cross-region replication), CI runners in us-east-1 (have runners or a deploy path in us-west-2), SSO via a us-east-1-hosted IdP (break-glass access and regional SAML endpoints). Any one of these blocks recovery.

Compromise. S3 replication and snapshots in the same account can be deleted by the same credentials. Add AWS Backup copies to a separate account with Vault Lock; replicate critical buckets to another account with Object Lock.

Quotas and capacity. Raise us-west-2 quotas (vCPUs, IPs, load balancers) to production scale; consider capacity reservations for critical instance types.

Proof. Run a game day: block us-east-1 from a staging copy, fail over, measure RTO and RPO; then a planned production switchover. Until then the plan is a hypothesis.
