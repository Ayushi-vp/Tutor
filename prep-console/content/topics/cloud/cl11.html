At 03:10 an on-call engineer is paged: checkout error rate 14%. The dashboards show nothing wrong with the service's instances — CPU is normal. The database's metrics look fine. Is it a bad deploy (there was one at 02:40)? A dependency? The cloud provider? The status page is green. Forty minutes later someone notices that errors are only in one availability zone, and that the provider's account-specific health feed posted a notice about network degradation in that zone at 03:04. A zonal shift fixes it in two minutes. The outage lasted 45.

The following week a security alert says an access key was used from an unknown IP address. Which data did it read? The audit trail shows the key listed buckets and changed nothing — but object-level reads were never being recorded, so nobody can say which customer exports were downloaded. The legal team assumes the worst.

And at month end, the observability bill exceeds the compute bill, because someone added the instance ID as a dimension to every custom metric on an autoscaled fleet that cycles through hundreds of instances a day.

Running services in the cloud means operating a system partly built and run by someone else. The provider produces a great deal of telemetry — metrics for every managed service, logs, audit records of every API call, configuration history, health events — and you produce your own. Turning that into fast, correct answers during incidents, and into evidence after security events, depends on knowing how the telemetry is structured, what it doesn't cover by default, how alarms actually evaluate, and what it all costs. sc13 covered the principles of SLOs, burn-rate alerting and the three pillars. This lesson is about the cloud's concrete machinery: CloudWatch metrics, alarms and logs, tracing, CloudTrail and Config, provider health events, the cost model, and on-call practices for services that depend on a provider — with Datadog's 2023 outage as a reminder that the monitoring itself can fail.

Picture a large office building's control room. Some screens come with the building: temperature in each room, lift status, door alarms, power use — installed by the landlord, reporting automatically. Others the tenant installs: sensors inside their own equipment, cameras in their own rooms. There's a visitor log at reception recording every person who enters, which door, when, and on whose authority. And there's a noticeboard from the landlord: "Lift 3 under maintenance Tuesday", "Power fault on floor 2".

The cloud equivalents. The landlord's screens are provider metrics: every managed service publishes numbers about itself to CloudWatch for free — an instance's CPU and network, a load balancer's request count and latency, a queue's depth, a function's errors and duration. The tenant's sensors are your telemetry: application metrics, logs and traces, plus anything inside the virtual machine the provider can't see, such as memory and disk space. The visitor log is CloudTrail, the audit trail of every API call: who, what, when, from where. The noticeboard is AWS Health and the status page: provider-side events, some specific to your account.

Two properties of this system shape everything else. The first is cost follows volume and cardinality: logs are billed per gigabyte ingested, metrics per distinct time series, traces per span stored, audit data per event — so how you emit telemetry is a cost decision. The second is shared fate: the monitoring system runs somewhere, and if that somewhere is the region or the vendor having a bad day, you lose visibility exactly when you need it.

Put together, these sources answer the four questions every incident and investigation asks, in order. Is something wrong for users? — symptom metrics and synthetic checks. Where is it? — metrics broken down by zone, region, endpoint and dependency, and traces of slow or failing requests. Is it us or the provider? — your deploy history against provider health events and the managed services' own metrics. Who changed what? — the audit trail and configuration history. A well-run cloud estate can answer each within minutes because the telemetry was designed for those questions in advance: the right dimensions on metrics, the right fields in logs, trace context propagated end to end, and audit logs switched on where the data is. The rest of the lesson goes through each piece of machinery with that purpose in mind.

Where the analogy breaks: the building's visitor log records people at the doors. CloudTrail by default records only the "door" events — creating, changing and deleting things — not every use of the things inside. Object reads, function invocations and item lookups are separate "data events" that you must switch on and pay for. That gap is where many security investigations come to a halt.

1. Metrics: identity, resolution and retention

A CloudWatch metric is identified by a namespace (AWS/EC2, AWS/ApplicationELB, or your own such as Shop/Checkout), a metric name and up to 30 dimensions (name/value pairs such as LoadBalancer=app/prod-alb/…, AvailabilityZone=euc1-az1). From the CloudWatch documentation (October 2026), each unique combination of dimensions is a separate metric, and CloudWatch doesn't aggregate across dimensions for custom metrics — if you publish by service and endpoint, you can't later query "all endpoints" unless you also publish that combination or use metric math search expressions.

Data points have standard resolution (one minute) or, for custom metrics, high resolution (one second). Retention rolls up: points finer than 60 seconds are kept for 3 hours; one-minute points for 15 days; five-minute aggregates for 63 days; hourly aggregates for 455 days (15 months). So a graph of last quarter is hourly whatever you sent. AWS services publish many metrics free (EC2's basic metrics at five-minute resolution, one-minute with paid detailed monitoring). Custom metrics are billed per metric-month: $0.30 for the first 10,000 in us-east-1 in October 2026, falling to $0.10 and lower in higher tiers.

Ways to get application metrics in: the PutMetricData API (each call billed), the CloudWatch agent or an OpenTelemetry collector (which can also scrape Prometheus endpoints), and the embedded metric format (EMF) — structured JSON log lines from which CloudWatch extracts metrics automatically. EMF is popular in Lambda because writing a log line is cheap and synchronous-call-free:

{"_aws":{"Timestamp":1791374400000,"CloudWatchMetrics":[{"Namespace":"Shop/Checkout",
  "Dimensions":[["Service","Endpoint"]],"Metrics":[{"Name":"Latency","Unit":"Milliseconds"}]}]},
 "Service":"checkout","Endpoint":"POST /orders","Latency":182,"RequestId":"req-7f3a"}

Here RequestId is logged but isn't a dimension, so it doesn't create metrics — it's searchable in the log, which is exactly where high-cardinality fields belong.

Two blind spots catch people. The hypervisor-level EC2 metrics don't include memory or disk space; those need an agent. And percentiles: CloudWatch can compute p99 for metrics where it has raw points (and for many AWS service metrics), but averaging latency hides tails, so alarm on percentiles for latency.

2. Alarms: evaluation, missing data and composition

An alarm watches one metric (or a metric math expression) and has three evaluation settings: the period over which data is aggregated into one point, the evaluation periods N (how many recent points to look at) and datapoints to alarm M (how many of them must breach). For periods of a minute or more, alarms are evaluated every minute over a sliding window. States are OK, ALARM and INSUFFICIENT_DATA, and actions (SNS notification, autoscaling, EC2 actions, Systems Manager incidents) fire on transitions.

Missing data is the subtle part. For each alarm you choose whether periods without data are treated as breaching, not breaching, ignored (keep the current state) or missing (contributing to INSUFFICIENT_DATA). An error-count metric that's only emitted when errors occur should treat missing as not breaching; a request-count or heartbeat metric should treat missing as breaching, because silence means the service or its telemetry stopped. A service that crashes and stops emitting errors looks healthy to an error-rate alarm with the wrong setting.

Composite alarms combine others with AND, OR and NOT: page only when the error-rate alarm AND the latency alarm are both in ALARM, or suppress zone-level alarms while a deployment alarm is active. They're the main tool against alert noise. Anomaly detection alarms compare a metric with a learned band rather than a fixed threshold, useful for metrics with strong daily patterns. Prices in us-east-1 (October 2026): $0.10 per standard alarm metric per month, $0.30 for high-resolution, $0.50 per composite alarm.

For user-facing services, the alarms that page people should be SLO burn-rate alarms on error rate and latency (sc13), computed with metric math from request and error counts; resource alarms (CPU, disk, queue depth) usually create tickets, not pages, except where they predict imminent failure (disk 90% full, queue age rising).

3. Logs: groups, retention, filters and queries

CloudWatch Logs organises events into log streams (one per source, such as a container or function instance) within log groups (one per application or component), each with its own retention (from a day to never expire — the default is never, which quietly accumulates storage), encryption key and access policy. Prices in us-east-1 (October 2026): ingestion $0.50 per GB in the Standard class or $0.25 in Infrequent Access (fewer features, for logs you rarely query), storage $0.03 per GB-month, Logs Insights queries $0.005 per GB scanned.

From a log group you can: create metric filters that count or extract values from matching events into metrics; query interactively with Logs Insights; stream events through subscription filters to Lambda, Kinesis or Firehose for processing or export; and export to S3 for long-term storage and analysis with Athena. Structured JSON logs with consistent fields (timestamp, level, service, request ID, trace ID, tenant) make every one of these cheaper and more useful than free text.

A Logs Insights query to find the slowest endpoints in the last hour:

fields @timestamp, Endpoint, Latency
| filter Service = "checkout" and Latency > 1000
| stats count(*) as slow, pct(Latency, 99) as p99 by Endpoint
| sort slow desc
| limit 10

It scans every log event in the time range of the selected groups, so cost is proportional to data volume times time range: narrowing to the right log group and the last hour rather than the last week can make a query a hundred times cheaper.

4. Traces

A trace records one request's path as a tree of spans, each a timed operation with attributes (service, operation, status, database statement, queue name). The trace ID and parent span ID travel between services in headers — the W3C traceparent header for OpenTelemetry, or X-Ray's own header — and in message attributes across queues and event buses (cl8). OpenTelemetry is the vendor-neutral standard for instrumentation; AWS ships a distribution of its SDKs and collector, and X-Ray accepts OpenTelemetry data, as do third-party backends.

Tracing everything is expensive, so systems sample. Head-based sampling decides at the first service (keep 5% of requests); it's cheap but drops most failures. Tail-based sampling buffers spans in a collector and decides after the request completes (keep all errors and requests slower than 1 second, plus 1% of the rest), which captures the interesting traces at the cost of running and sizing collectors. Traces answer the question metrics can't: which hop made this request slow.

5. Audit: CloudTrail and AWS Config

CloudTrail records API calls as JSON events. A trimmed example of someone opening a security group:

{ "eventTime": "2026-10-06T14:02:11Z",
  "eventSource": "ec2.amazonaws.com",
  "eventName": "AuthorizeSecurityGroupIngress",
  "awsRegion": "eu-central-1",
  "sourceIPAddress": "203.0.113.24",
  "userIdentity": { "type": "AssumedRole",
    "arn": "arn:aws:sts::111122223333:assumed-role/platform-admin/alice@example.com",
    "sessionContext": { "sourceIdentity": "alice@example.com" } },
  "requestParameters": { "groupId": "sg-0abc…",
    "ipPermissions": { "items": [ { "ipProtocol": "tcp", "fromPort": 22, "toPort": 22,
      "ipRanges": { "items": [ { "cidrIp": "0.0.0.0/0" } ] } } ] } } }

Event types matter. Management events (control-plane operations) are recorded by default; every account gets a free, searchable 90-day event history, and the first copy delivered by a trail is free. Data events (S3 object reads and writes, Lambda invocations, DynamoDB item operations and more) are off by default and cost $0.10 per 100,000 events delivered (October 2026); extra copies of management events cost $2.00 per 100,000. Network activity events for VPC endpoints and Insights (anomalies in API call rates) are further options. CloudTrail Lake stores and queries events with SQL.

The standard organisation design: an organisation trail covering all accounts and regions, delivered to an S3 bucket in a dedicated log-archive account with Object Lock and a bucket policy that only CloudTrail can write to, KMS encryption with a key in that account, log file integrity validation on (digest files let you prove logs weren't altered), and data events enabled for sensitive buckets and critical functions. Security tooling (GuardDuty, a SIEM) reads from there.

AWS Config records the configuration of resources over time as configuration items and evaluates them against rules — managed ones such as "S3 buckets block public access" or custom ones — with optional automatic remediation. CloudTrail tells you who changed the security group and when; Config shows what it looked like before and after and which resources were attached to it. Config bills per configuration item recorded and per rule evaluation, so constantly changing resources are worth scoping.

6. Provider health

AWS Health publishes events about AWS services: public events (a service issue in a region) and account-specific ones (an instance scheduled for retirement on degraded hardware, an RDS maintenance window, a notice that a zone in your account is affected by an event). Account-specific events go to EventBridge, so they can page on-call or trigger automation (for example, drain an instance scheduled for retirement). They usually arrive before the public status page is updated and say which of your resources are affected, which is far more useful during an incident than a regional status colour. Google Cloud's Personalized Service Health and Azure Service Health play the same role.

8. On-call for services that run on a provider

On-call in the cloud adds one recurring question to every incident: is this us or them? A practical sequence:

  1. Scope by dimension. Break the symptom down by zone, region, endpoint, dependency and deployment version. Errors in one zone suggest a zonal provider issue or a zone-local dependency; errors starting at a deploy suggest you; errors in calls to one managed service suggest that service.
  2. Check provider signals. Account-specific Health events, the service's own metrics (throttles, 5xx from the AWS API), and whether other teams see the same.
  3. Mitigate first. Roll back the deploy, zonal-shift away from a bad zone, fail over, shed load with feature flags — whichever applies — before root cause is known.
  4. Escalate to the provider with evidence: request IDs, resource IDs, timestamps, regions and zones (with AZ IDs), and impact. Support plans determine response times; know yours before you need it.
  5. Learn. The post-mortem separates the provider's fault from your design choices: a zone failure is theirs; being unable to survive one is yours.

Cardinality is the cost model of every metrics system

Whether it's CloudWatch, Prometheus or a vendor, metrics systems store and index one time series per unique label combination, so cost and query speed scale with cardinality, not with event volume. A metric with labels of 10, 40 and 5 values has up to 2,000 series; adding a user ID with 100,000 values makes it 200 million. The discipline is to use metrics for bounded dimensions that you alarm or aggregate on, and to push unbounded identifiers into logs and traces where they're stored per event and queried on demand. Autoscaling, Kubernetes pod names and container IDs are the common accidental sources of cardinality, because they create new values continuously.

Alerts on symptoms, dashboards for causes

Paging on causes (CPU above 80%, a queue above 1,000) produces many pages that don't matter and misses failures nobody predicted; paging on symptoms (users' requests failing or slow, measured against an SLO) catches whatever breaks and ignores what doesn't hurt. In the cloud this matters more because many causes are the provider's and outside your control, while symptoms are always yours. Resource metrics still matter: they're the dashboards on-call uses to find the cause once a symptom alert fires, and the tickets that prevent tomorrow's incident.

Telemetry pipelines need backpressure and isolation

Logging and metrics agents run on the same hosts as applications. If the telemetry backend slows down, agents buffer, then use memory and disk, then — if poorly configured — block application threads writing logs or crash hosts with full disks. Good pipelines bound their buffers, drop or sample under pressure (debug logs first), and never make application requests wait on telemetry delivery. In serverless, writing logs to stdout keeps delivery out of the request path; in containers, sidecar or node-level collectors isolate it.

Audit logs are evidence, which changes how they're stored

Operational logs help you debug; audit logs may be used to prove what happened to regulators, courts or customers. That requires completeness (all accounts, all regions, data events where data matters), integrity (CloudTrail's digest files, chained by hash, show tampering; Object Lock prevents deletion), retention matched to obligations (often a year or more) and access control so the people investigated can't edit the record. It also requires being able to query years of events quickly when needed, which is why teams load trails into a lake (Athena, CloudTrail Lake, a SIEM) rather than reading JSON files from S3 during an incident.

Correlated failures hide in shared configuration

Datadog's regions were architecturally independent — separate infrastructure, separate cloud providers — yet failed within an hour of each other because they shared an operating-system default: an automatic security-update window from 06:00 to 07:00 UTC. The same pattern appears with certificates that expire at the same moment everywhere, global configuration pushes, feature flags, base images, and scheduled jobs. Finding these common modes is an audit exercise: list everything that changes all regions at once and stagger it, canary it or remove it.

Sampling decides what you can know later

Every telemetry system samples somewhere: traces at the edge or in a collector, logs by level or by rate, metrics by aggregation into one-minute or one-hour points. Each choice deletes information you might need in a future investigation, so it should be deliberate. A useful rule is to keep everything about failures and outliers (all error traces, all slow requests, all audit events for sensitive data) and sample the boring majority. Long-term retention follows the same logic: aggregated metrics for capacity trends, raw logs for weeks, audit trails for years. Writing these choices down per signal — what's kept, for how long, at what fidelity — turns "we don't have that data" from an incident-time surprise into a known, accepted trade-off.

Observability for AI workloads adds token-level telemetry

LLM-backed services need the usual metrics plus their own: tokens in and out per request, time to first token and inter-token latency, model and prompt versions, cache hit rates, tool calls, refusal and guardrail events, and cost per request. OpenTelemetry's generative-AI semantic conventions define standard attributes for these spans. The cardinality warnings apply with force — prompt text and user IDs belong in sampled traces or logs with access controls, not in metric dimensions — and the privacy rules from au12 and ae5 apply to what's logged at all.

Failure modes and their symptoms

Silent services. A component stops emitting metrics; error alarms stay green. Fix: heartbeat and request-count alarms with missing data as breaching, and synthetic checks.

Alert fatigue. Dozens of resource alarms page nightly; real incidents are missed. Fix: page on SLO burn rates and composite alarms; demote resource alarms to tickets; review every page in a weekly on-call handover.

Cardinality bill shock. Custom metrics with instance, pod or user IDs as dimensions. Fix: remove unbounded dimensions, use EMF with high-cardinality fields as properties, not dimensions.

Log cost runaway. A debug flag left on, a retry loop logging stack traces, a chatty library. Fix: per-group ingestion alarms, sampling, log levels controlled by configuration, retention policies on every group.

Missing audit data. A breach investigation can't answer what was read because data events or S3 access logs were never enabled; a region nobody uses wasn't covered by the trail. Fix: organisation trail across all regions, data events for sensitive resources, periodic checks that logging is still on (attackers turn it off — alert on StopLogging and DeleteTrail).

Monitoring that shares fate. The region's CloudWatch is degraded during a regional event, or the observability vendor is down. Fix: cross-region synthetic checks and a minimal independent alerting path.

Time and clocks. Metrics with wrong timestamps (clock skew, batch publishing) put alarms into INSUFFICIENT_DATA or delay them. Use NTP (the Amazon Time Sync Service) and publish with current timestamps.

What good looks like

Every user-facing service has SLO alerts and a dashboard broken down by zone, region and dependency; every log group has retention; every account is in the organisation trail with data events for sensitive stores; Health events reach on-call; synthetic checks run from outside the region; observability spend is tracked per team; and game days include "monitoring is down" as a scenario.

The case: Datadog, 8 March 2023

Datadog's post-mortem describes how, at 06:00 UTC on 8 March 2023, a security update to systemd was installed automatically on many of its Ubuntu hosts through a legacy unattended-upgrades channel that was still enabled in the base image. Installing the update restarted systemd-networkd, and on restart it removed network routes that had been added by Cilium, the container networking plugin Datadog uses in Kubernetes. Nodes lost connectivity and dropped out of their clusters. Because the operating system's default window for automatic installation was 06:00 to 07:00 UTC, the update hit tens of thousands of nodes in five Datadog regions (US1, EU1, US3, US4 and US5) — running on three different cloud providers — at nearly the same time.

For customers, Datadog's web application and APIs were unavailable or degraded, monitors were not evaluating or alerting, and data ingestion was disrupted. Datadog posted its first public notice at 06:31 UTC; web access was restored at 09:13 UTC; the first major service was fully operational again at 16:44 UTC; all services were operational on 9 March at 08:58 UTC; and historical data backfill completed on 10 March at 06:25 UTC. The response involved some 10 senior engineering leaders, around 70 incident commanders and a pool of 450–750 responders across four shifts. Datadog disabled the legacy update channel across its fleet, configured systemd-networkd so that restarts don't remove routes it didn't create, and audited for other common-mode risks.

Two lessons transfer directly. First, regions and clouds are only independent if nothing changes them all at once; a default time window in an OS image was a hidden global switch. Second, every customer whose alerting lived entirely in Datadog lost it for hours — a reminder that the observability system is a dependency with its own failure modes, and that the most critical alerts deserve an independent path.

Goal: implement CloudWatch-style alarm evaluation, a cardinality and cost estimator, and a tiny CloudTrail-style investigation — Python 3.12 standard library only. About 20 minutes; no cloud account needed.

  1. Alarm engine. Write evaluate(points, threshold, m, n, missing) where points is a list of floats or None; treat None per missing in {"breaching", "notBreaching", "ignore", "missing"} and return the state after each minute. Reproduce the figure (breaches at minutes 2, 6, 8, 9, 10; alarm at minute 10 for 3 of 5).
  2. Silent failure. Feed an error-count series that stops (all None) after minute 20. Compare states for "notBreaching" and "breaching", then add a request-count heartbeat alarm.
  3. Cardinality. Write series(dims) = product of distinct values; compute monthly cost at $0.30 per metric for the first 10,000 and $0.10 after for the lesson's metric with and without instance ID. Then simulate an autoscaling fleet replacing 10% of 30 instances per hour for a month and count distinct instance IDs.
  4. Logs. Write log_cost(rps, bytes_per_line, retention_days) using $0.50/GB ingestion and $0.03/GB-month storage, and reproduce the table.
  5. Investigation. Generate 10,000 fake CloudTrail-like events (JSON dicts with eventName, userIdentity.arn, sourceIPAddress, requestParameters.key) including 40 GetObject events by a leaked role from one IP. Write queries with list comprehensions: all actions by that role, distinct objects read, first and last time seen.

What to look for: step 2 shows the dead service staying OK with notBreaching — and the heartbeat catching it; step 3 shows 6,000 versus 200 metrics and, with fleet churn, thousands of distinct instance IDs in a month (2,190 replacements at 10% per hour) — the bill multiplies; step 5 shows how quickly an investigation goes when object-level events exist, and what you'd be unable to answer without them.

Needs an AWS account (optional): aws cloudtrail lookup-events --lookup-attributes AttributeKey=EventName,AttributeValue=AuthorizeSecurityGroupIngress --max-results 5 searches the free 90-day management event history.

Diagnose and design: "we didn't know until customers told us"

An API team on AWS had a 50-minute outage. Timeline: 14:00 deploy of version 412 to all three zones at once; 14:05 a configuration error makes the service return HTTP 200 with an empty body for 60% of requests; 14:20 the service's error-rate alarm (5xx count, missing data as not breaching) is still OK; the CPU alarm is OK; 14:48 a customer tweets; 14:50 rollback. Their observability: CloudWatch dashboards of CPU and 5xx, logs in Standard class with no retention set, a custom latency metric with dimensions service, endpoint and pod name, and a third-party vendor for traces. Explain why nothing alerted, and redesign their alerting and observability (including cost) so this is caught within five minutes.

Answer

Why nothing fired. The failure produced 200s, so 5xx alarms saw nothing; CPU was normal; there was no measure of correctness. Nothing checked what users actually got.

Detection. (1) Synthetic canaries every minute from another region that call the main endpoints and assert on the response body (non-empty, expected fields) — this alone would have paged by ~14:07. (2) An application-level success SLI: the service emits, via EMF, a count of "valid responses" and "invalid responses" (empty body, schema validation failures) by endpoint; a burn-rate alarm on the invalid ratio. (3) Business metrics with anomaly detection (orders per minute) as a backstop.

Deploys. Deploy zone by zone (or by cell) with automatic rollback on the canary and SLI alarms; a 14:00 deploy to all zones at once is why 60% of all traffic was affected.

Alarm hygiene. Add request-count heartbeats with missing data as breaching; use composite alarms so a deploy-in-progress flag doesn't suppress real pages.

Cost and data. Remove pod name from the latency metric's dimensions (pods churn on every deploy, creating new metrics each time); keep pod and request IDs as log fields. Set log retention (30 days hot, export to S3 for longer), and move verbose logs to the Infrequent Access class. Keep traces in the vendor, but make sure the canaries and the SLI burn-rate alarm page through a path that doesn't depend on that vendor.
