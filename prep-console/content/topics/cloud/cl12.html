A startup's AWS bill is $9,000 in January and $31,000 in June. Nobody can say why. The finance lead asks engineering; engineering points at the growth in users; the CEO notices users only doubled. When someone finally opens the detailed billing data they find: a staging environment with production-sized instances running 24/7 since February; $4,100 a month of NAT gateway processing from a data pipeline reading S3 through NAT; $2,300 of cross-zone traffic from a Kafka cluster whose consumers ignore zones; 38 TB of debug logs kept forever; an expired three-year reservation that nobody renewed, so the database fleet quietly moved to on-demand rates; and a GPU instance someone launched for an experiment in March and forgot.

None of these needed clever optimisation. They needed someone to see the bill in a form engineers understand, to know which team owned each line, and to understand how cloud prices work: what's billed per hour versus per gigabyte versus per request, which data paths cost money, and what commitments do. The cloud's pricing model is the source of its flexibility — pay only for what you use — and, without discipline, the reason bills grow faster than businesses.

The track has priced components along the way: network paths in cl3, compute options in cl4, functions in cl5, storage classes in cl6, databases in cl7, messaging in cl8, DR in cl10, observability in cl11. This lesson pulls them together into how bills are structured and managed: the purchasing models and how to size commitments, the hidden costs, tagging and allocation, the billing data and tools, the FinOps practice that makes engineers accountable, and a complete monthly estimate for a small production web app with every price taken from AWS's price files on 7 October 2026. The case is 37signals, whose move off AWS is the best-documented example of when renting stops being the cheap option.

Think about running a cloud estate like running a fleet of rental cars for a company.

Every car is billed by the hour, but there's more on the invoice: fuel by the litre, tolls on certain roads, a parking fee for every car whether it moves or not, a charge every time a car leaves the city, a fee for each extra key. Drivers choose routes and cars; finance pays the invoice a month later. If drivers never see the invoice, nobody notices the car that's been parked at an airport since February, the delivery van taking the toll road every trip when a free road runs parallel, or the luxury saloon booked for errands. And you can negotiate: book a car for a year and the hourly rate drops by a third — worth it for the car that's out every day, wasteful for the one used on Fridays.

The cloud equivalents. Hourly rates are instance-hours, database-hours, load balancer-hours. Fuel and tolls are usage charges: gigabytes stored, requests made, and above all bytes moved across billed boundaries — out to the internet, between regions, between zones, through NAT gateways. Parking fees are things billed just for existing: public IP addresses, unattached volumes, idle load balancers, snapshots. Year-long bookings are commitments: Savings Plans and reserved instances. Standby cars at a steep discount that can be recalled are spot capacity.

Two levers control every bill. Usage is decided by architecture and operations: which instance types, how many, how traffic flows, what's kept and for how long. Rate is decided by purchasing: on-demand, commitments, spot, negotiated discounts. Engineers own the first and should influence the second. The discipline that makes both happen continuously — visibility per team, ownership, unit costs, budgets and alerts — is called FinOps.

The order in which to pull the levers matters. Purchasing discounts are tempting because they need no engineering work, but committing to waste locks the waste in for a year or three. The usual sequence is: first make costs visible and owned; then remove what shouldn't exist (idle, orphaned, over-retained); then fix architecture that moves or stores more than it needs; then rightsize what remains; and only then commit to the steady baseline that's left. Each step makes the next one more accurate, and the final commitment covers a smaller, cleaner footprint.

Where the analogy breaks: a fleet manager can see every car on a map. In the cloud, resources multiply silently through automation — autoscaling, IaC, CI pipelines, experiments — across many accounts and regions, and a single line of code can create a cost that runs for years. Visibility has to be designed in, through account structure, tags and billing data, because nobody can see it by looking around.

1. How a cloud bill is built

Every line on a cloud bill is a rate times a quantity, identified by a usage type. In AWS's Cost and Usage Report you'll see lines like BoxUsage:m7g.large (instance-hours), EBS:VolumeUsage.gp3 (GB-months), DataTransfer-Out-Bytes (GB to the internet), DataTransfer-Regional-Bytes (cross-zone GB), NatGateway-Hours and NatGateway-Bytes, Requests-Tier1 (S3 PUTs), TimedStorage-ByteHrs (S3 storage). The units fall into a few families:

  • Capacity-time: instance-, node-, database- and load-balancer-hours (per second for many), provisioned IOPS-months, DynamoDB provisioned capacity-hours. Billed whether used or not.
  • Storage-time: GB-months of volumes, objects, snapshots, logs, backups. Billed for what's kept.
  • Requests: per million API calls, events, messages, function invocations; per GB-second for functions.
  • Data movement: per GB out to the internet, between regions, between zones, through NAT, Transit Gateway or endpoints.
  • Existence fees: public IPv4 addresses ($0.005 an hour each, about $3.65 a month, in October 2026), KMS keys ($1 a month each), secrets ($0.40 a month each), hosted zones.
  • Percentages: support plans billed as a percentage of spend; marketplace software.

Because the pricing model is granular, an architecture's cost is predictable if you can estimate its usage — which is what every worked example in this track has done. It's also why cost surprises are almost always usage surprises: something running, moving or storing more than anyone thought.

AWS's own maximums are "up to 66%" for Compute Savings Plans and "up to 72%" for EC2 Instance Savings Plans across all instance types and terms; the m7g.large figures show typical values. The break-even utilisation is the committed rate divided by on-demand: an EC2 Instance Savings Plan at $0.054 pays off if, without it, you'd have run that capacity at least 66% of the hours. Savings Plans are commitments of dollars per hour, not instances: a plan of $2.16 an hour covers 40 m7g.large instances at the plan rate, or a mix of eligible usage, and any usage beyond it is on demand.

The same pattern applies elsewhere. Compute Savings Plans also discount Fargate (Arm vCPU-hour $0.0255 on a one-year plan against $0.03238 on demand, 21% off) and Lambda (Arm GB-second $0.0000117 against $0.0000133334, 12% off). Databases use reserved DB instances: a db.t4g.medium PostgreSQL Multi-AZ instance was $0.129 an hour on demand and $0.093 on a one-year no-upfront reservation (28% off), or $758 paid upfront for the year (about $0.087 an hour, 33% off). GPU instances show the largest absolute stakes: a p5.48xlarge was $55.04 an hour on demand, $34.68 on a one-year EC2 Instance Savings Plan and $23.78 on three years (cl14).

Sizing commitments. Plot hourly eligible usage over the last few months. The floor — the level usage rarely drops below — is safe to commit to: every committed hour gets used. Above the floor, each extra committed unit is used only in the hours when demand reaches it; it's worth buying only if that's more than the break-even fraction of hours.

Two operating habits keep commitments healthy: laddering (buying in tranches every month or quarter so they expire gradually and track growth), and monitoring utilisation (how much of what you bought is used — below 95% means you over-committed) and coverage (how much eligible usage is covered — low coverage on steady workloads means money left on the table). An expired commitment silently reverts usage to on-demand rates; renewal is a calendar event someone must own.

4. Tagging, accounts and allocation

To manage costs you must attribute them to owners. Two mechanisms do this.

Accounts are the strongest boundary. Everything in an account is billed to it, including untaggable charges like data transfer and support. An account structure of one account per team or product per environment (cl13) makes most allocation automatic.

Tags refine it: key/value pairs on resources such as team=checkout, service=orders-api, env=prod, cost-center=4120. For tags to appear in billing data, user-defined keys must be activated as cost allocation tags in the billing console, and they apply only from activation onward — a common reason teams can't explain last quarter. Enforcement keeps them consistent: tag policies in AWS Organizations standardise keys and allowed values; IaC policy checks (cl9) reject untagged resources; Config rules find stragglers.

Shared costs need explicit rules. A shared Kubernetes cluster's nodes are allocated by each namespace's requested CPU and memory over time (AWS's split cost allocation data puts this into the CUR for EKS and ECS); NAT gateways and Transit Gateways by bytes per account or a fixed share; support plans and enterprise discounts proportionally to spend. The rule matters less than its being written down and stable, so teams can reason about their numbers.

Allocation enables two practices: showback, where teams see their costs, and chargeback, where their budgets pay for them. Showback is usually where to start; chargeback changes behaviour faster but needs trusted data.

5. Billing data and tools

The source of truth is the Cost and Usage Report (CUR), delivered through AWS Data Exports to an S3 bucket: hourly or daily line items with account, service, usage type, operation, resource ID, tags, rates, amortised and net costs, and commitment details. Data Exports can also produce the FOCUS format, the FinOps Foundation's open specification for billing data, so the same queries work across providers; the FOCUS site lists 1.4 as the current version in October 2026, with AWS, Azure and Google Cloud among providers generating FOCUS data. Teams load the CUR into Athena or a warehouse and build dashboards on it.

On top sit the native tools: Cost Explorer for interactive breakdowns and commitment recommendations; Budgets for alerts when actual or forecast spend crosses a threshold per account, tag or service, with optional actions; Cost Anomaly Detection, which learns spend patterns per service or account and alerts on unusual increases; and Compute Optimizer for rightsizing recommendations from utilisation. A typical CUR query to find the top cost growths by usage type between two months:

SELECT line_item_usage_type,
       SUM(CASE WHEN month = '2026-08' THEN line_item_unblended_cost END) AS aug,
       SUM(CASE WHEN month = '2026-09' THEN line_item_unblended_cost END) AS sep
FROM cur
GROUP BY line_item_usage_type
ORDER BY sep - aug DESC
LIMIT 15;

Read it as an engineer. Compute and database are about 59% — expected. The NAT gateways, their processing and their addresses cost $82, about 20% — more than twice the internet egress — and they exist so two instances can make outbound calls. Public IPv4 addresses alone are $14.60. Options, with their trade-offs:

  • Commitments: a one-year Compute Savings Plan on the two instances saves 2 × ($0.0816 − $0.0599) × 730 ≈ $31.68 a month; a one-year no-upfront reserved DB instance saves ($0.129 − $0.093) × 730 ≈ $26.28. Together about $58, 14% of the bill, with no architectural change, if the app will run all year.
  • NAT: a gateway endpoint for S3 removes that share of NAT processing for free; one NAT gateway instead of two saves $36.50 a month but loses outbound access if its zone fails (cl3) — acceptable for some apps, not others. The regional NAT gateway mode bills per zone it's active in, so it doesn't save the hourly charge.
  • Egress: a CDN in front of static assets reduces origin egress and often costs less per GB at volume.
  • Database: Multi-AZ doubles the instance and storage price; it's the price of minutes rather than hours of recovery after a zone failure (cl7, cl10). Keep it for production; drop it in staging.

The same app on serverless (Lambda, API Gateway, DynamoDB on-demand) might cost less than $50 a month at low traffic and more at high traffic (cl5); on a single instance with a local database, under $50, with no zone resilience. Cost estimates are most useful exactly like this: as a comparison between designs with explicit trade-offs.

7. The FinOps loop

The FinOps Foundation describes the practice as a cycle of three phases, run continuously:

  1. Inform: allocate costs to owners, publish dashboards per team with daily spend, trends and unit costs, forecast, and benchmark.
  2. Optimise: rightsize, schedule, remove idle resources, fix architectural waste (data movement, storage retention), buy and manage commitments, use spot where safe.
  3. Operate: budgets and anomaly alerts routed to owners, cost reviews in architecture and launch processes, policies that enforce tags and prevent expensive mistakes, and regular reporting of unit costs to leadership.

The organisational shape that works: a small central team owning tooling, data, commitments and negotiations, and engineering teams owning their own usage and responding to their own anomalies. The metric to watch isn't total spend — that should grow with the business — but unit cost: dollars per thousand requests, per active customer, per inference, per training run.

Why cloud prices are shaped the way they are

Prices reflect the provider's cost structure and incentives. Ingress is free because it fills network capacity that's otherwise idle and brings data (and workloads) in; egress is charged because it consumes transit and makes leaving costly. Cross-zone traffic is charged because it crosses metro fibre between buildings. Commitment discounts exist because predictable demand lets the provider plan capacity and lowers its cost of capital. Spot is cheap because unsold capacity earns nothing. Reading prices this way predicts which designs will be cheap — stay in a zone, keep data where compute is, commit to the predictable, flex on spot — and which will be expensive.

Commitments are a financial instrument, managed like a portfolio

A portfolio of Savings Plans and reservations has terms, exposure and risk. Over-commitment is a loss you can't undo (unused hours are gone; some reserved instances can be sold on a marketplace, Savings Plans can't). Under-commitment is a smaller, recoverable loss. The trade between one- and three-year terms is a bet on stability: three years nearly halves the rate for m7g but locks in architecture and region assumptions. Mature teams buy in tranches, keep a target coverage (say 70–80% of steady usage) rather than 100%, prefer flexible instruments when change is likely (Graviton migrations, containerisation, region moves), and track utilisation and coverage monthly like any other financial position.

Unit economics connect engineering to the business model

A rising bill is good news if revenue rises faster; a flat bill is bad news if usage collapsed. Unit costs make the comparison: cost per thousand API requests, per customer per month, per gigabyte processed, per model inference. They also expose architecture decisions: a feature whose unit cost is above its revenue per unit is a problem regardless of the total. For AI products, where GPU and model-API costs scale with usage (ae2, cl14), unit economics often determine pricing, rate limits and which model serves which customers.

Discounts beyond list prices

Large customers rarely pay list price for everything. Enterprise agreements trade spend commitments over years for percentage discounts across services; private pricing exists for specific high-volume services such as data transfer; credits come with migrations and startup programmes. These change the economics of build-versus-buy and of multi-cloud. They also create lock-in in the other direction: a spend commitment to one provider makes moving workloads elsewhere financially painful until it ends — as 37signals' four-year S3 commitment kept its storage in AWS long after compute had left.

The real comparison with owning hardware

On-premises or colocation costs include hardware amortised over three to six years, data-centre space, power and cooling, network transit, spares, staff time for procurement, installation, repair and refresh, and the capital tied up. The cloud's price includes all of that plus managed services, elasticity, global reach and speed. For a steady, predictable workload running all year at high utilisation, the hardware path often wins by a large factor, as 37signals' numbers show; for variable, growing or experimental workloads, or for teams that rely on managed services, the cloud usually wins once staff costs are counted. The honest analysis models both, including the transition cost and the value of options you give up.

Forecasting is a modelling problem, not an extrapolation

Next quarter's bill is driven by business drivers — users, requests, data ingested, models trained — multiplied by unit costs, plus planned changes (a new region, a migration, a commitment expiring). Straight-line extrapolation of last quarter's total misses all of them. A driver-based forecast (requests per month × cost per thousand requests, terabytes ingested × storage and processing cost per terabyte) is both more accurate and more useful, because when it's wrong it tells you which driver or unit cost moved. It also turns capacity and commitment planning into the same exercise: the forecast floor is what you commit to.

Cost is a non-functional requirement with trade-offs against the others

Many cost optimisations trade against reliability or latency: one NAT gateway instead of three, single-AZ databases, spot for serving, aggressive scale-in, cold storage tiers. Each is a legitimate choice for some systems and an outage waiting to happen for others. Senior engineers make the trade explicit — what reliability does this buy, at what price per month — and record it, rather than letting cost cutting and resilience work undo each other in alternating quarters.

Failure modes and their symptoms

Runaway resources. A loop in automation creates instances, a misconfigured autoscaler scales to its maximum, a forgotten GPU instance runs for months. Symptoms: a step change in daily cost for one usage type. Detection: Cost Anomaly Detection, daily per-account dashboards, budgets with forecast alerts.

Expired commitments. Same usage, higher bill, starting on an anniversary date. Track expiry dates and coverage; set calendar ownership.

Unowned spend. Untagged resources in shared accounts; nobody responds to anomalies. Enforce tagging in IaC, use accounts per team, and assign every account an owner.

Data movement creep. A new feature makes cross-region calls or streams data out; DataTransfer lines climb steadily. Review data flows in design reviews; alert on transfer usage types.

Storage accretion. Logs with no retention, snapshots without lifecycle, versioned buckets without noncurrent expiry. Default retention policies in IaC modules.

Optimisations that break things. A scheduler stops a "dev" database that production depended on; a cost cut removes the zone redundancy that would have prevented next month's outage. Tag criticality, notify before deleting, and review cost changes like other changes.

What to measure

Daily spend by account, team and service; unit costs; commitment utilisation and coverage; percentage of spend allocated to an owner; top idle resources and their cost; anomaly count and time to resolve; spend on data transfer by usage type; forecast versus budget.

The case: 37signals leaves the cloud

37signals, the company behind Basecamp and HEY, published its reasoning and numbers as it moved off AWS. In an October 2024 post, its co-owner and CTO David Heinemeier Hansson reported that seven cloud applications, including HEY, had been moved from AWS to the company's own hardware the previous summer. The cloud bill had fallen from a run rate of $3.2 million a year to $1.3 million. The new Dell hardware cost about $700,000 and was, he wrote, entirely recouped during 2023 as long-term cloud commitments rolled off, and the team managing everything was the same size — no additional hires were needed. The original estimate of $7 million saved over five years had risen to well over $10 million.

The remaining $1.3 million was S3. The post explains that while compute and managed database and search services had been on one-year commitments, file storage was locked into a four-year contract from 2021 that ran until summer 2025 — "still well over a million dollars" a year even after the commitment discounts — for almost 10 petabytes of data, much of it stored in duplicate across regions. The plan was to move to a dual-data-centre Pure Storage setup with 18 petabytes of capacity, costing about one year of S3 fees for the initial hardware and fitting in the company's existing racks and power. AWS's policy, announced in March 2024, of waiving data-transfer-out charges for customers leaving AWS entirely (on request, now with 90 days to complete the move) removed what would otherwise have been a large egress bill for the migration.

What makes the case useful is that it's specific. 37signals' workloads are steady and predictable, the company had operations skills and existing data-centre space, and it didn't depend heavily on managed services it would have to rebuild. Under those conditions, buying hardware once and running it for years beat renting the same capacity by a wide margin. Most teams don't share all of those conditions; the transferable lesson is the method — know your steady baseline, know what you pay per unit of it, know what your commitments lock in and when, and decide with numbers.

Estimate and decide: the ML platform's bill

An ML team's monthly AWS spend is $84,000. The breakdown: 6 × p5.48xlarge for training at about 40% average utilisation, on demand ($55.04/hour each); an inference fleet of 12 g6e.xlarge running 24×7 on demand ($1.861/hour); an EKS cluster of 30 m7i.xlarge on demand for data processing at 25% average CPU; 400 TB in S3 Standard, 300 TB of which hasn't been read in 90 days; $3,900 of NAT processing; $2,600 of cross-zone transfer. Leadership asks for 30% savings without harming the roadmap. Propose a plan with estimated savings and risks, using October 2026 us-east-1 prices from this track.

Answer

Training GPUs (biggest line). 6 × $55.04 × 730 ≈ $241,000 if always on — so at $84k total they're clearly not running all month; at 40% utilisation of the hours they're up, the fix is scheduling and packing rather than commitment. Run jobs through a queue that launches capacity only when jobs exist (or capacity blocks for planned runs), and use spot with checkpointing for fault-tolerant runs (cl14). A three-year Instance Savings Plan ($23.78/hour) only pays if the team will keep p5 capacity busy more than about 43% of hours for three years — unlikely to be justified at 40% today.

Inference. 12 × $1.861 × 730 ≈ $16,300. Steady 24×7: a one-year EC2 Instance Savings Plan at $1.172 (37% off) saves ≈ $6,000 a month if the fleet's floor is 12; Compute plan at $1.407 saves ≈ $4,000 with more flexibility. Also check utilisation and batching before committing.

Data processing cluster. 30 × $0.2016 × 730 ≈ $4,415 at 25% CPU. Rightsize (Karpenter consolidation, smaller or Graviton nodes) and run batch on spot: plausibly halve it, ≈ $2,200 saved.

S3. 300 TB untouched for 90 days: Standard ≈ 307,200 GB × $0.023 ≈ $7,066 a month; Glacier Instant Retrieval at $0.004 ≈ $1,229 — ≈ $5,800 a month saved if objects are large enough (check average size; cl6) and reads stay rare.

NAT and cross-zone. Gateway endpoints for S3 and DynamoDB (most of the $3,900 if it's dataset reads), zone-aware routing for the cross-zone traffic: ≈ $4,000–5,000 together.

Total: inference commitment ~$6,000, cluster ~$2,200, S3 ~$5,800, network ~$4,500, plus GPU scheduling and spot (likely the largest, perhaps $10,000+) — comfortably above $25,000 (30%) without slowing the roadmap. Risks: spot interruptions on training without good checkpointing, over-committing inference if traffic shifts to a new model or instance type (prefer Compute plans or shorter ladders), and archived data unexpectedly needed for retraining (retrieval fees). Track unit costs — cost per training run and per thousand inferences — to show the gains held.
