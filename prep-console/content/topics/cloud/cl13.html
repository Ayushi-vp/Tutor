A fast-growing company has one AWS account. Everyone has administrator access because it was easiest. CI deploys with an IAM user's access key stored in the CI system's settings. A data scientist creates a bucket for a partner, makes it public "for a day" to share a file, and forgets. A developer copies the CI key into a test script and pushes it to a public repository on a Friday evening.

Within minutes, automated scanners find the key. By Saturday morning there are 40 large instances mining cryptocurrency in eight regions the company never uses; the bill alert fires on Monday. Investigating, the security engineer discovers that CloudTrail was only enabled in one region, that the public bucket contained a customer export, and that the key could also read and write every bucket in the account. Nobody can say what was copied. A month later, a different set of stolen credentials is used to re-encrypt production objects with an attacker's key and schedule them for deletion in seven days.

None of this required the attacker to break anything. Every step used legitimate APIs with legitimate credentials against configurations nobody had chosen deliberately. That's the shape of most cloud security incidents: failures on the customer's side of the shared-responsibility line (cl1), multiplied by flat structures in which one credential reaches everything.

The track has covered the parts: IAM evaluation and least privilege (cl2), workload identity and secrets (au10, au11), network segmentation (cl3, net14), logging (cl11). This lesson is about governance at scale — how organisations make the safe configuration the default for hundreds of accounts. It covers the multi-account landing zone, preventive guardrails (SCPs, RCPs, Block Public Access, data perimeters) and detective controls, encryption and KMS key policies across accounts, secrets and network isolation in practice, compliance frameworks and evidence, and the breach patterns that keep recurring. The case is the 2025 Codefinger campaign, which held S3 data to ransom with nothing but leaked credentials and an encryption feature, and S3's decision to switch that feature off by default in 2026.

Think of cloud governance as city planning rather than locking individual doors.

You could secure a city by inspecting every building as it's built and hoping each owner locks up. Or you can zone the city: industrial here, residential there, a vault district with one gate; building codes that make every new building fire-resistant whatever the architect prefers; laws that apply everywhere (no building may be taller than the airport's flight path allows); CCTV on every main road recording who went where; and an emergency service that knows the map. Individual owners still lock their doors, but the city is safe even when some forget.

In the cloud: zoning is the account structure — separate accounts for production, development, security tooling and logs, grouped into organisational units. Building codes are preventive guardrails applied centrally: SCPs that cap what anyone in an account can do, RCPs that cap what can be done to resources, Block Public Access, encryption by default. CCTV is detective control: CloudTrail recording every call into an archive nobody in the workload accounts can alter, Config recording every configuration, GuardDuty watching for signs of attack. Emergency services are incident response: runbooks, quarantine, immutable backups.

This structure changes what security work looks like. Instead of reviewing every bucket, the platform team ensures no bucket in the organisation can become public. Instead of trusting every team to enable logging, the organisation trail records every account. Instead of hoping nobody uses an unapproved region, an SCP makes those regions unusable. Teams get freedom inside the boundaries; the boundaries are maintained centrally and changed through review.

The economics favour this approach. A breach investigation in a flat, unlogged account costs weeks of engineering and often ends with the answer that nobody can tell what was accessed, which regulators and customers read as everything. The same incident in a governed organisation is usually smaller (the credential reached one account), shorter (the trail shows exactly what it did) and recoverable (backups sat outside its reach). The controls in this lesson cost little to run once built; the expensive part is building them before the first incident rather than after it.

Where the analogy breaks: a city's building codes are inspected when a building is constructed. Cloud resources are created, changed and destroyed thousands of times a day, by automation as much as by people, and attackers use the same APIs as everyone else. Controls therefore have to act at API-call time (guardrails evaluated on every request) and continuously afterwards (detection on every change), not at a one-off inspection.

1. Accounts and the landing zone

An AWS organisation groups accounts under a management account, arranged in organisational units (OUs). AWS's multi-account whitepaper and Control Tower's default landing zone converge on a structure like the diagram: a Security OU with a log archive account (receives organisation-wide CloudTrail, Config and other logs, in immutable storage) and an audit or security-tooling account (delegated administrator for GuardDuty, Security Hub, Access Analyzer, Config aggregation); an Infrastructure OU for shared networking (Transit Gateway, central egress and inspection, cl3) and shared services; Workloads OUs split by environment, with an account per team or product per environment; a Sandbox OU for experiments, with budgets and no connectivity to production; and a Suspended OU for accounts being retired.

Why accounts and not just IAM policies inside one account? Because the account is the boundary for nearly everything: IAM principals and their default reach, service quotas, billing and cost allocation (cl12), resource visibility, and the scope of most mistakes. A developer with administrator rights in orders-dev can't affect orders-prod at all unless a cross-account role says so, and that role can be audited. The cost of many accounts is managing them — which is what landing-zone tooling (Control Tower, account factories in IaC) automates: new accounts arrive with the baseline network, logging, guardrails, SSO access and budgets already in place.

The management account deserves special handling: SCPs don't apply to it, and it can change everything in the organisation. Run nothing there except organisation administration and billing, protect its root user with MFA hardware, and keep access to a few people.

2. Preventive guardrails

Service control policies (SCPs) cap the permissions of every principal in the accounts they're attached to, through the evaluation logic in cl2. A baseline set, attached at the root and inherited:

{ "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [
    { "Sid": "NoLeavingOrg", "Effect": "Deny",
      "Action": "organizations:LeaveOrganization", "Resource": "*" },
    { "Sid": "ProtectAuditTrail", "Effect": "Deny",
      "Action": ["cloudtrail:StopLogging", "cloudtrail:DeleteTrail", "cloudtrail:UpdateTrail",
                 "config:StopConfigurationRecorder", "config:DeleteConfigurationRecorder",
                 "guardduty:DeleteDetector", "guardduty:DisassociateFromMasterAccount"],
      "Resource": "*",
      "Condition": { "ArnNotLike": { "aws:PrincipalArn": "arn:aws:iam::*:role/SecurityAdmin" } } },
    { "Sid": "NoIamUserKeys", "Effect": "Deny",
      "Action": ["iam:CreateUser", "iam:CreateAccessKey"], "Resource": "*",
      "Condition": { "ArnNotLike": { "aws:PrincipalArn": "arn:aws:iam::*:role/BreakGlass" } } },
    { "Sid": "RequireImdsV2", "Effect": "Deny", "Action": "ec2:RunInstances",
      "Resource": "arn:aws:ec2:*:*:instance/*",
      "Condition": { "StringNotEquals": { "ec2:MetadataHttpTokens": "required" } } }
  ] }

plus the region guardrail from cl2 and, for production OUs, denies on deleting KMS keys, disabling EBS default encryption, and using SSE-C (section 6). SCPs are tested in a staging OU first, because a mistake can lock an entire OU out of a service.

Resource control policies (RCPs) are the resource-side counterpart: attached to OUs or accounts, they cap what can be done to resources in them, whoever calls — including principals outside your organisation, which SCPs can't reach. The classic RCP denies access to the organisation's S3 buckets, KMS keys, SQS queues and Secrets Manager secrets unless the caller is in the organisation or is an AWS service acting on your behalf. That turns a misconfigured bucket policy granting access to "*" into a denied request.

S3 Block Public Access, set at the account or organisation level, overrides any policy or ACL that would make a bucket public; S3 applies the most restrictive combination of organisation, account, bucket and access-point settings, and new buckets block public access by default. Default encryption: S3 encrypts every new object (SSE-S3) by default since January 2023, and EBS encryption by default can be enabled per region so every new volume and snapshot is encrypted, with an SCP preventing anyone from turning it off.

Guardrails extend into the delivery pipeline: IaC policy checks (cl9) reject resources that would be caught later anyway, so developers learn at pull-request time rather than from a denied API call.

3. Data perimeters

A data perimeter combines these into three statements, each enforced with condition keys from cl2:

The network perimeter is the most powerful against leaked credentials and the hardest to roll out, because many legitimate paths (developers on laptops, SaaS integrations, AWS services) must be accounted for. Teams start with the identity perimeter on sensitive data stores, run the others in audit mode using Access Analyzer and CloudTrail to find legitimate exceptions, then enforce.

4. Detective controls

Prevention misses things; detection finds them. The organisation-wide set:

  • CloudTrail organisation trail to the log archive (cl11), with data events on sensitive buckets, and alerts on high-risk events: root sign-in, StopLogging, CreateAccessKey, PutBucketPolicy granting public access, KMS ScheduleKeyDeletion.
  • AWS Config with conformance packs (collections of rules mapped to frameworks), aggregated across accounts: unencrypted volumes, open security groups, buckets without Block Public Access, IAM users with old keys.
  • GuardDuty, which analyses CloudTrail, VPC flow, DNS and other telemetry for signs of compromise: credentials used from unusual locations or Tor, instance credentials used outside the instance, crypto-mining DNS lookups, anomalous S3 access.
  • IAM Access Analyzer for resources and roles accessible from outside the organisation, and unused access.
  • Macie to find personal or sensitive data in S3, which tells you which buckets deserve the strictest controls.
  • Security Hub to aggregate findings and score accounts against standards such as AWS Foundational Security Best Practices or CIS benchmarks.

Detection is only as good as response. Findings route by account owner to the team's channel with severity; high-severity findings page; some are auto-remediated (re-enable Block Public Access, quarantine an instance by swapping its security group). The test of the system is how many minutes pass between a dangerous change and someone acting on it.

5. Encryption and key management

Encryption at rest on AWS is server-side and transparent: the service encrypts data with a data key and protects the data key with a KMS key (envelope encryption, au11). The choices are which key and who controls it:

OptionWho controls the keyAuditUse
SSE-S3 / AWS-owned keysthe serviceno per-use recorddefault protection against media loss
AWS-managed KMS keysAWS, in your accountkey use in CloudTrailbasic separation, no custom policy
Customer-managed KMS keysyou: key policy, rotation, disableevery use in CloudTrailsensitive data, cross-account sharing, crypto-shredding
Imported key material / external key storesyou, partly outside AWSas aboveregulatory requirements to hold keys

Customer-managed keys matter because they add a second, separately controlled permission: reading an object needs both s3:GetObject and kms:Decrypt on its key. A key's key policy is a resource policy that must allow a principal — directly, or by delegating to the account's IAM policies — before anyone can use the key; an IAM policy alone isn't enough. Cross-account use needs both sides: the key policy in the key's account names the other account or role, and the other account's IAM policy allows the KMS actions on the key's ARN. This is also how backups are protected (cl10): copies in the backup account are re-encrypted with a key owned there, so production credentials can't decrypt or destroy them.

The key is also a kill switch. Disabling it, or deleting it after the mandatory waiting period (7 to 30 days), makes everything under it unreadable — useful for crypto-shredding data you must destroy, catastrophic if an attacker does it. Protect key deletion and policy changes with SCPs and alerts.

In transit, AWS APIs use TLS; enforce it on resources with policies (aws:SecureTransport, cl2) and between your services with TLS or a mesh (net9, au10).

6. Secrets and SSE-C

Secrets follow au11: stored in Secrets Manager or Parameter Store with KMS encryption, fetched at runtime by workload roles, rotated automatically where possible, never in code, images, environment files or IaC state. Governance adds organisation-wide checks: secret scanning on repositories and CI logs, Config rules for secrets without rotation, and SCPs that deny creating IAM user access keys at all.

SSE-C is worth singling out because of how it was abused. With server-side encryption with customer-provided keys, the caller sends a 256-bit key with each PUT and GET; S3 uses it to encrypt the object and discards it, storing only a hash to validate later requests. Few applications need it now that customer-managed KMS keys exist — AWS's own announcement says there's no practical security benefit left — but it gives anyone who can write objects the ability to make them unreadable to everyone else. In response to its abuse, S3 began blocking SSE-C by default in April 2026 for all new general-purpose buckets and for existing buckets in accounts with no SSE-C-encrypted objects; applications that need it must enable it explicitly per bucket. For older buckets, a bucket policy or SCP denying requests that carry the SSE-C algorithm header closes the path.

7. Network isolation in practice

Network controls (cl3, net14) are a second layer behind identity, not a replacement. The baseline: data stores in private subnets with no public endpoints; inbound internet traffic only through load balancers and WAF; egress through NAT or, better, inspected egress with allow-lists, and VPC endpoints with endpoint policies for AWS services so even S3 traffic is restricted to your buckets; no SSH or RDP open anywhere — administrative sessions through Systems Manager Session Manager or EC2 Instance Connect Endpoint, authorised by IAM and recorded; and for the most sensitive environments, no internet egress at all. Network isolation also makes the network perimeter in section 3 possible, because requests from your workloads arrive through known endpoints.

8. Compliance basics

Compliance frameworks express control objectives; clouds help you meet them but don't meet them for you. The provider's side is documented in independent audit reports (SOC 1, SOC 2 and SOC 3, ISO 27001 and related standards, PCI DSS for card data, FedRAMP for US government, and region- or sector-specific schemes), downloadable from AWS Artifact and equivalents; regulated data may need contracts such as a HIPAA business associate addendum, and services must be in scope for the framework. Your side is shown by the controls in this lesson and evidence that they operate continuously: Config conformance packs and Security Hub standards mapped to the framework, CloudTrail and Config history, IaC and change records, access reviews, backup and restore tests, and incident-response exercises. Data-residency rules (cl1) shape which regions and services are allowed; they're enforced with region SCPs and by checking which features replicate data elsewhere.

Why accounts beat policies as a boundary

Inside one account, isolation between teams relies on every IAM policy being right: resource ARNs scoped, wildcards avoided, PassRole restricted (cl2). One mistake — or one compromised role with broad permissions — crosses every internal boundary. Between accounts, the default is no access: a principal in one account can touch another account's resources only if both sides explicitly allow it, and SCPs can forbid even that. Mistakes inside an account stay inside it. The trade-off is operational overhead (networking between accounts, cross-account roles, shared services), which is why account vending and landing-zone automation are core platform investments rather than conveniences.

Guardrails need their own change management

An SCP is code that applies to hundreds of accounts at once. A typo can deny a service organisation-wide; an over-broad condition can lock teams out of incident response. Mature organisations treat guardrails like production code: in version control, reviewed, tested against representative principals with the policy simulator or test accounts, rolled out to a staging OU, then progressively wider, with a documented emergency path (a break-glass role exempt from most guardrails, itself monitored and rarely used). The same applies to RCPs and organisation-wide Block Public Access, which can break legitimate integrations if applied without discovery.

Detection engineering is about signal, not volume

Turning on every detector produces thousands of findings, most low-value, and teams learn to ignore them. Effective programmes choose a small set of high-signal detections tied to the threat model — root use, logging disabled, credentials used from unusual places, public access granted, keys scheduled for deletion, mass object rewrites — with clear owners and runbooks, then widen. Every finding should end in one of three outcomes: fixed, accepted with a recorded reason and expiry, or turned into a preventive control so it can't recur.

Ransomware in the cloud attacks recoverability

On-premises ransomware encrypts disks; cloud ransomware uses the cloud's own APIs: re-encrypting objects with attacker keys (SSE-C), scheduling KMS key deletion, deleting snapshots and backups, setting lifecycle expiry, or simply exfiltrating and threatening publication. The defences are therefore about making destruction impossible for the credentials an attacker is likely to get: backups and copies in a separate account with locked retention (cl10), Object Lock on critical buckets, SCPs that deny key deletion and SSE-C, no long-term keys, and alerts on bulk destructive patterns. A useful exercise is to ask, for each critical dataset: which single role, if stolen, could make this data unrecoverable? If the answer isn't "none", the design isn't finished.

Third-party access is part of the perimeter

Monitoring vendors, CI/CD platforms, cost tools and security scanners all hold roles in your accounts. Each is a path an attacker can use if the vendor is compromised (the 2022 theft of OAuth tokens issued to Heroku and Travis CI, covered in the auth track, and CircleCI in au11). Controls: roles per vendor with least privilege and external IDs (cl2), OIDC federation rather than stored keys for CI, Access Analyzer findings for every external principal, and periodic reviews that remove unused integrations. RCPs and the identity perimeter must explicitly carve out these vendors, which also documents them.

Identity is the new perimeter, but networks still matter

Zero-trust thinking (net14) says every request should be authenticated and authorised on its own merits, not trusted for coming from inside a network. Cloud IAM is built that way: every API call carries a signed identity. But identity alone doesn't stop a stolen credential used from an attacker's laptop; network conditions do. The strongest cloud designs combine both — identity on every call, plus perimeters that make credentials useless outside expected networks — and treat them as complementary layers rather than competing philosophies.

Security and cost governance share machinery

The same organisation structure, tags, Config rules and IaC policy checks that enforce security also enforce cost controls (cl12) and reliability standards (cl10). Platform teams that build one governance pipeline — policies as code, evaluated in CI and continuously in accounts, with findings routed to owners — get all three for the price of one, and avoid teams facing three different review processes.

Recurring breach patterns and their signatures

Leaked long-term keys. Signature: API calls from unfamiliar IPs or regions, GetCallerIdentity and enumeration calls, new IAM users or keys, large instances launched in unused regions. Unit 42's 2023 research saw exposed keys used within five minutes; AWS's quarantine policy arrived within about two minutes in that research but only limits some actions. Prevention: no IAM user keys, secret scanning, region SCPs.

Public storage. Signature: anonymous GetObject in S3 access logs or data events, Access Analyzer public findings. Prevention: organisation-level Block Public Access, identity-perimeter RCPs, CloudFront with origin access control for genuinely public content.

Application-to-credentials chains. SSRF to instance metadata (au10's Capital One analysis), code execution in a container with a broad role, CI pipelines with admin roles. Signature: instance or task credentials used from outside their compute, unusual API patterns from workload roles. Prevention: IMDSv2, least-privilege workload roles, network perimeter.

Destructive actions. Mass PutObject with SSE-C, lifecycle rules expiring buckets, ScheduleKeyDeletion, DeleteSnapshot sprees. Prevention: SCPs, immutable backups elsewhere, alerts on these specific events.

Disabled visibility. StopLogging, deleted GuardDuty detectors, removed Config recorders. Prevention: SCPs protecting them; detection on any attempt.

Response essentials

A rehearsed runbook per pattern: who decides, how to deactivate keys and revoke role sessions (a deny on tokens issued before now, cl2), how to isolate instances (swap security groups, snapshot for forensics), where logs are and who can query them, how to restore from immutable backups, and who notifies customers and regulators within required timeframes. Pre-provisioned forensic tooling and access in the security account, and a break-glass path that works when SSO is the thing compromised.

What to measure

Count of IAM users with active keys (target zero) and key age; accounts outside the landing zone or missing baseline controls; Security Hub score per account; open high-severity findings and their age; external-access findings; buckets with sensitive data and their controls; time from dangerous change to detection and to remediation; last tested restore of each critical dataset from the backup account.

The case: Codefinger and S3's change of default

On 13 January 2025, the security firm Halcyon published research on a group it named Codefinger running ransomware against Amazon S3. The attack needed no AWS vulnerability. Using compromised AWS credentials that had s3:GetObject and s3:PutObject permissions, the attackers rewrote victims' objects using SSE-C, supplying an AES-256 key they had generated themselves. Because S3 doesn't store SSE-C keys — CloudTrail records only an HMAC of the key — the data could not be decrypted without the attackers' key. They then applied lifecycle rules to delete the encrypted objects within seven days and left ransom notes, adding time pressure. Analyses by Arctic Wolf and others relayed AWS's guidance: restrict SSE-C through policy conditions where it isn't needed, avoid long-term credentials and rotate any exposed ones, enable logging that would show bulk encryption and lifecycle changes, and keep credentials out of code.

AWS then changed the default. Its storage blog gave advance notice, and in April 2026 S3 blocked SSE-C for new writes by default on all new general-purpose buckets and on existing buckets in accounts with no SSE-C-encrypted objects; requests specifying SSE-C on such buckets are rejected with 403, and the small number of applications that need SSE-C must enable it per bucket. AWS's stated reason is that customer-managed KMS keys provide the control SSE-C once offered, so its remaining practical use was mostly as a weapon.

The case teaches governance more than cryptography. The initial access was ordinary — leaked credentials. The damage came from permissions broader than any workload needed and from a feature no workload used. The controls that would have stopped it are the ones in this lesson: no long-term keys, least privilege on write paths, a guardrail removing an unused dangerous capability, immutable backups in another account, and alerts on destructive bulk actions. And the provider's response — making the safe configuration the default — is exactly what a platform team should do inside its own organisation whenever an incident reveals a capability nobody needs.

Goal: build a small "governance scanner" over a simulated organisation and an exposure-path checker — Python 3.12 standard library only. About 20 minutes; no cloud account needed.

  1. Model. Represent an organisation as JSON: OUs with attached guardrail flags (block_public, require_imdsv2, deny_regions, deny_sse_c), accounts in OUs, and resources per account (buckets with policy, BPA flags, encryption type, versioning, object lock; IAM users with key ages; roles with trust principals and permissions; KMS keys with policies).
  2. Rules. Write checks returning findings with severity: IAM user keys older than 90 days or present at all in prod; bucket public (policy Principal "*" without org condition, and no effective Block Public Access after combining OU, account and bucket settings — most restrictive wins); buckets with sensitive tag but no CMK; prod buckets without versioning or Object Lock; trust policies with Principal "*" and no org condition; SSE-C allowed on prod buckets.
  3. Inheritance. Implement effective guardrails as the union of flags from root to account, and show that moving an account from the Sandbox OU to the Prod OU changes its findings without touching the account.
  4. Exposure paths. Given "a leaked credential for role X", compute which buckets it could read, write or make unreadable (write + SSE-C allowed, or kms:ScheduleKeyDeletion on their key), and which backups survive (copies in another account with locked retention).
  5. Report. Print findings by account and severity, and a one-line answer per critical dataset to "which single role could make this unrecoverable?"

What to look for: step 3 shows why OU placement is the security architecture; step 4 reproduces the Codefinger chain in miniature — a role with PutObject on a prod bucket and SSE-C allowed can destroy data unless a backup lives elsewhere; step 5's question should return "none" for every critical dataset once guardrails and cross-account backups are in place.

Needs an AWS account (optional): aws s3control get-public-access-block --account-id <id> shows the account-level Block Public Access settings; aws organizations list-policies-for-target --target-id <ou-or-account> --filter SERVICE_CONTROL_POLICY lists SCPs attached at that level.

Review: a startup's first security baseline

A 60-person startup runs everything in two AWS accounts (prod and dev) created by the CTO, who uses the root user for billing. Findings from a review: 14 IAM users with access keys (oldest 3 years), including one used by CI; CloudTrail enabled only in us-east-1 in prod; no organisation; S3 buckets in prod include one with a policy allowing "Principal": "*" for s3:GetObject on exports/*; RDS and S3 encrypted with AWS-managed keys; backups are RDS automated snapshots in the same account; developers SSH into prod instances through a security group open to 0.0.0.0/0 on port 22. Prioritise the fixes for the next 30 days and sketch the target state.

Answer

Week 1 — stop the bleeding. (1) The public exports/* grant: remove it, turn on account-level Block Public Access, check access logs or data events for past anonymous reads and treat the data as exposed if none exist. (2) Port 22 open to the world: close it; move to Session Manager. (3) Root user: enable hardware MFA, remove any root access keys, stop using it for billing (create a billing role). (4) CloudTrail: a multi-region trail in both accounts now; alert on root sign-in and StopLogging.

Weeks 2–3 — remove long-term credentials. Replace CI's IAM user with OIDC federation to a deploy role per account (cl2, au10). Move humans to IAM Identity Center with MFA and permission sets; deactivate and delete the 13 other users' keys after checking CloudTrail for their use. Add secret scanning to repositories.

Weeks 3–4 — structure and recoverability. Create an AWS organisation (new management account; move prod and dev in), add log-archive and security accounts, an organisation trail to an Object Lock bucket, GuardDuty and Security Hub with delegated admin. Baseline SCPs: no leaving org, protect logging, approved regions, no IAM user keys, IMDSv2, no SSE-C in prod. Backups: AWS Backup copies of RDS and critical S3 data to a backup account with Vault Lock and a key owned there.

Target state (next quarter). Accounts per team and environment via an account factory; RCP identity perimeter on sensitive buckets; customer-managed KMS keys for sensitive data with tight key policies; Config conformance packs; restore tests; a short incident-response runbook rehearsed once. The single question to answer for each critical dataset: which stolen credential could make it unrecoverable — and the answer should be none.
