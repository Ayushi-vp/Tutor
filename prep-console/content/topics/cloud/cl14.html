An AI team gets approval to fine-tune an open model for a customer-support product. They write the training script, open the cloud console and try to launch an 8-GPU H100 instance. The request fails: their account's quota for that instance family is zero. They request an increase; it takes days. When it arrives, launches fail with "insufficient capacity" in their region's zones. They try spot instead, get capacity in another region, copy their 30 TB dataset across (paying for the transfer), and start training — which is interrupted four times in the first night, each time losing an hour and a half of work because checkpoints were every two hours. The run that was budgeted at $5,000 costs $9,000 and finishes a week late.

Then they serve the model. Each inference instance holds one GPU and runs at 15% utilisation outside office hours. Meanwhile another team ships a feature on a managed LLM API, hits the per-region tokens-per-minute quota on launch day, enables cross-region inference to get more throughput — and the compliance team discovers that requests from EU customers are now being processed in other regions within the geography the team picked, which nobody had checked against the contract.

ML and LLM workloads are where the cloud's assumptions bend. Compute is scarce and expensive rather than plentiful and cheap; capacity has to be planned and booked; utilisation matters more than hourly price; data is large enough that its location dictates where compute can run; and managed AI services come with quotas and data-routing options that are as much legal decisions as technical ones. gpu12 taught how to choose accelerators and estimate cost from first principles, ae2 how LLM API bills work, and k8s14 how GPUs are scheduled in Kubernetes. This lesson is about the cloud layer around them: the GPU instance families and prices, the ways to get capacity, managed ML platforms, managed LLM APIs, data locality and residency, quotas, and a fine-tuning run costed on-demand versus spot. The case is a Berkeley study of how to use spot capacity without missing deadlines.

Think of cloud GPUs as hiring specialist heavy machinery for a construction project, rather than renting ordinary vans.

Vans are everywhere: you call, one arrives, you pay by the hour. Cranes are different. There are few of them, everyone wants them at the same time, and the big ones must be booked weeks ahead, delivered to your site and set up next to each other. You can book a crane for a specific fortnight at a fixed price (a reservation), sign a year-long contract at a discount (a commitment), or take one on standby at a steep discount on the condition that it can be pulled away for another job with two minutes' warning (spot). You can also hire a crew that brings its own crane and operates it for you (a managed ML platform), or skip the crane entirely and buy finished components from a factory (a managed model API).

A crane that sits idle still bills by the hour, so the plan matters: have the materials on site before the crane arrives (data in the same region, staged fast), keep it busy (high utilisation), and make sure that if it's pulled away mid-lift you don't lose the whole day's work (checkpoints). And some materials can't legally leave the country they're in, so you hire cranes where the materials are (data residency).

The two numbers to keep in mind throughout are cost per useful unit of work — per training run, per million tokens served — rather than cost per hour, and utilisation, which converts one into the other. A cheaper GPU-hour that sits idle half the time is more expensive than a dearer one kept busy.

The work also splits into phases with very different needs. Experimentation is bursty, small and tolerant of interruption: a few GPUs for hours, ideal for spot or short on-demand use, with notebooks that must be shut down when idle. Training and fine-tuning are large, planned and deadline-bound: many GPUs together for days, which is where capacity reservations and careful checkpointing pay off. Serving is continuous and latency-sensitive: a steady baseline worth committing to, peaks worth autoscaling, and a choice between running models yourself and calling someone else's. Most cost and reliability problems come from buying capacity for one phase the way you'd buy it for another — reserving GPUs for experiments, or running a deadline-critical training job on hope.

Where the analogy breaks: cranes don't get faster every year. GPUs do, and the newest generation is both the scarcest and often the cheapest per unit of work. Capacity planning in the cloud is therefore also a bet on which hardware to target, and commitments longer than a hardware generation carry the risk of paying for yesterday's chips.

2. Capacity: quotas, availability and placement

Three separate things stand between you and running GPUs:

  • Quota. EC2 limits on-demand and spot usage per instance family group in vCPUs per region. A p5.48xlarge has 192 vCPUs, so running four needs a quota of 768 for that group. New accounts frequently start at zero for P instances; increases are requested through Service Quotas and may take time or require usage history. Request them weeks before you need them, in every region you might use.
  • Availability. Even with quota, a zone may have no free hosts of that type, and RunInstances fails with an insufficient-capacity error. For large GPU types this is common.
  • Placement. Distributed training needs instances close together on a fast network. AWS's UltraClusters provide non-blocking, petabit-scale networking; capacity reserved through Capacity Blocks is placed inside them automatically.

Capacity Blocks deserve detail because they were designed for exactly this problem. Per the EC2 documentation (October 2026): you can see available future capacity and reserve a block of GPU instances — up to 64 instances per block and 256 across blocks for an organisation on any date — starting as soon as 30 minutes later or up to eight weeks ahead, for days or weeks. They're available for P5, P5e, P5en, P6, P4d and Trainium types in selected regions.

3. Managed ML platforms

Each provider offers a platform that wraps capacity in ML-specific services: SageMaker AI on AWS, the Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform on Google Cloud (Vertex AI until its rename at Cloud Next 2026), and Azure Machine Learning (with Microsoft Foundry for generative AI). The common services:

  • Training jobs. Submit a container, a data location, an instance type and count; the platform provisions, runs, retries and shuts down. Managed spot training runs on spot capacity and resumes from checkpoints written to S3 after interruptions.
  • Resilient clusters for long, large jobs (SageMaker HyperPod): persistent clusters with health checks on GPUs and networking, automatic replacement of failed nodes, and job auto-resume — addressing the reality that large GPU fleets fail regularly.
  • Notebooks and development environments with managed images.
  • Model registry and pipelines for versioning, approval and promotion.
  • Inference endpoints: real-time (autoscaled instances), serverless, asynchronous (queued requests for long inferences) and batch transform.

The trade is a premium per instance-hour for these services. From the SageMaker and EC2 price lists in us-east-1 on 7 October 2026: an ml.p5.48xlarge training instance cost $63.296 an hour against $55.04 for the equivalent EC2 instance (15% more); ml.g6e.xlarge $2.61 against $1.861 (40% more); ml.g5.xlarge $1.408 against $1.006 (40% more). For teams without a platform team, the premium buys a great deal; teams already running Kubernetes with GPU scheduling, queues and serving (k8s14) often run on EC2 or EKS directly and pay the raw instance price.

4. Managed LLM APIs

The other path skips GPUs entirely. Amazon Bedrock, Google's model APIs on the Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform, and Microsoft Foundry Models (which include Azure OpenAI) offer hosted models — the providers' own and third-party ones — through APIs billed per input and output token, with options for batch processing at lower prices for supported models and provisioned throughput (dedicated model capacity bought for a term) for guaranteed performance. ae2 covers the token economics; the cloud-specific concerns are these:

  • Quotas. Per account, per region, per model limits on requests and tokens per minute. Launches need quota increases planned like GPU quotas.
  • Cross-region routing. Bedrock's cross-region inference routes requests to other regions to find capacity. Per the Bedrock documentation (October 2026), geographic inference profiles keep processing within a geography such as the US, EU or APAC, while global profiles may route to any supported commercial region, at about 10% lower cost; CloudTrail in the source region records where each request was processed; and region-restricting SCPs must allow the destination regions (or, for global profiles, a documented special value), or requests fail. Azure OpenAI offers analogous global, data-zone and regional deployment types.
  • Data handling. Providers document whether prompts and outputs are stored or used for training (for the major enterprise offerings, by default they aren't used to train the provider's models) — but this must be checked per service and model against your obligations.
  • Private connectivity and identity. Calls can go through VPC interface endpoints (cl3) and are authorised by IAM (cl2), so model access follows the same governance as other services (cl13).

5. Data locality and residency

ML workloads move more data than most: training sets of terabytes to petabytes, checkpoints of hundreds of gigabytes per save (cl6), model weights of tens to hundreds of gigabytes per instance start. Every byte that crosses a region boundary costs money ($0.02/GB from us-east-1 to us-west-2 in October 2026) and time, and every byte that leaves the cloud costs more (cl3, cl12). The rules:

  1. Put GPUs in the region where the data lives, and read it through S3 gateway endpoints so it never touches NAT.
  2. If you must chase capacity in another region, weigh the one-time copy (a 50 TB dataset is about $1,024 per copy, plus storage) against the compute saved; it pays for a large one-off run, rarely for recurring training.
  3. Serve models in the regions where users are, which may differ from training; copy the model artefact (small) rather than moving traffic (large and latency-sensitive).
  4. Apply residency requirements to every stage: training data, checkpoints, logs and traces with prompts, inference processing location (including cross-region routing), and backups.

A useful habit is to write down, for each model-dependent feature, which path it uses and why: the model, the provider or instance type, the region and routing, the quota or capacity it relies on, and the fallback if that capacity disappears. That one page answers most of the questions a security review, a cost review and an incident commander will ask.

6. A fine-tuning run, costed

A team plans a full fine-tune (continued training) of an 8-billion-parameter model on 20 billion tokens using p5.48xlarge instances (8 × H100). Following gpu3 and gpu12:

  • Compute. Training FLOPs ≈ 6 × parameters × tokens = 6 × 8×10⁹ × 20×10⁹ = 9.6×10²⁰.
  • Throughput per GPU. An H100 SXM's dense BF16 peak is about 989 TFLOPS; assume 40% model FLOPs utilisation (MFU), so about 396 TFLOPS of useful work per GPU.
  • Time. 9.6×10²⁰ ÷ 3.96×10¹⁴ ≈ 2.43 million GPU-seconds ≈ 674 GPU-hours ≈ 84.3 instance-hours of p5.48xlarge.

The spot line needs its assumptions spelled out. With checkpoints every 30 minutes, an interruption loses on average 15 minutes of work since the last checkpoint plus about 15 minutes to get capacity back and restore: half an hour. With one interruption every six hours of running, 84.3 hours sees about 14 interruptions and 7 extra hours. And the spot prices are a snapshot from AWS's published spot price data on 7 October 2026: $31.30 an hour for p5.48xlarge in us-west-2, but $57.76 in us-east-1 — above on-demand that day — which is exactly why "use spot" isn't a rule but a measurement. If the dataset lives in us-east-1, add about $20 per TB copied to us-west-2.

Three further adjustments matter more than the purchasing choice. MFU: at 25% the run needs 60% more hours; at 50% it needs 20% fewer — measure on a short run before booking capacity. Parallelism: eight instances would finish in about 10.5 hours of wall time if scaling efficiency holds, at roughly the same total cost — useful for deadlines and for fitting inside a Capacity Block window. Overheads: evaluation runs, failed starts, data preparation and checkpoint uploads typically add 10–20%.

Utilisation is the cost lever that dominates

Every GPU-hour billed but not spent on useful computation is waste: idle time between jobs, data-loading stalls, checkpoint pauses, communication waiting in distributed training, low batch sizes in serving, and notebooks left running. For a p5 fleet at $55 an hour per instance, raising effective utilisation from 40% to 60% saves a third of the bill — more than any commitment. The tools are scheduling (queues that keep GPUs fed with jobs), input pipelines that stream from S3 fast enough (cl6), asynchronous checkpointing, batching and continuous batching for inference (gpu10), and automatic shutdown of interactive environments. Measuring GPU utilisation and MFU per job and per team, alongside cost, is what makes these visible (cl11, cl12).

GPU fleets fail often, so resilience is a design input

Large training jobs span hundreds or thousands of GPUs for weeks, and the probability that some GPU, NIC, cable or host fails during that time approaches certainty. Jobs must checkpoint frequently, detect failures quickly (stragglers, NaNs, hung collectives), replace nodes and resume automatically. Managed clusters (SageMaker HyperPod) and Kubernetes operators for training both build this in. Checkpoint frequency follows the same optimisation as in cl6 — the interval near √(2 × checkpoint cost × mean time between failures) — and spot interruptions simply add another failure source with a shorter mean time between failures.

Spot for GPUs is a portfolio and a scheduling problem

Spot capacity for large GPU instances is scarce and correlated: when demand rises, whole pools are reclaimed together, and prices in busy regions can sit near or above on-demand. Using it well means diversifying across zones and regions (and instance types, if the code tolerates it), checkpointing frequently, and treating the deadline explicitly: run on spot while you're ahead of schedule, fall back to on-demand or reserved capacity when you're behind. That is precisely the policy the 'Can't Be Late' study formalised, and tools like SkyPilot implement across clouds. The data-gravity cost of chasing spot across regions has to be in the same calculation.

Model APIs shift risk from capacity to quotas and terms

With a managed LLM API, you don't plan GPU capacity, but you inherit the provider's: quotas per model per region, throttling under shared load, model deprecation schedules, price changes, and data-handling terms. Production designs treat the model API like any critical dependency (cl10): quotas raised and monitored, client-side rate limiting and backoff, a fallback model or provider for degradation, cross-region profiles chosen with residency in mind, and contractual review of retention and training-use terms. Provisioned throughput turns the quota risk back into a capacity purchase, with its own utilisation question.

Inference economics flip with scale

At low or spiky volume, per-token APIs or serverless inference are cheapest because you pay nothing idle. At steady high volume, dedicated GPUs with good batching win because cost per token falls with utilisation (ae2 computes the crossover). In the cloud the crossover also depends on capacity guarantees (reservations or commitments for the steady baseline), on model choice (a fine-tuned small open model on L40S versus a large proprietary model via API) and on the engineering cost of running a serving stack. Many mature teams end up hybrid: self-hosted for the high-volume core, APIs for long-tail features, spikes and the newest models.

Commitments meet fast-moving hardware

A three-year commitment on p5 halves the hourly rate, but GPU generations turn over in roughly two years and each new one delivers more work per dollar. Committing long to the current generation risks paying for capacity that's no longer the best choice before the term ends, while committing short or not at all risks having no capacity. Teams handle this by committing only to the steady inference baseline they're confident about, preferring flexible instruments (Compute Savings Plans that move across instance families) over family-specific ones, and buying training capacity per run with Capacity Blocks rather than by the year.

Governance applies with extra force

ML platforms concentrate sensitive data (training sets with personal data, proprietary corpora), valuable artefacts (model weights) and expensive resources (GPUs that crypto-miners love — cl13's leaked-key pattern). The landing-zone controls all apply: separate accounts for experimentation, training and serving; datasets and weights encrypted with keys whose policies name only the roles that need them; no internet egress from training jobs except through controlled paths; region guardrails compatible with cross-region inference choices; and cost allocation per team and job so idle GPUs have owners.

Failure modes and their symptoms

Capacity unavailable. Launches fail with insufficient capacity or quota errors on the day training should start. Fix: quotas raised in advance in several regions; Capacity Blocks booked for planned runs; a fallback instance type or region with data already there.

Interrupted training losing hours. Spot reclaims or node failures with infrequent checkpoints. Fix: checkpoint intervals sized to failure rates, asynchronous checkpoint writes, automatic resume, and a deadline-aware fallback to on-demand.

Capacity Block ends mid-run. Termination starts at 11:00 UTC on the block's last day. Fix: schedule the final checkpoint before then, size runs to the window, or extend the block in advance where possible.

GPUs starved by data. Low GPU utilisation with high input-pipeline wait. Fix: sharded datasets, parallel S3 reads, local NVMe caches or FSx for Lustre (cl6).

Idle GPU spend. Notebooks, endpoints and dev instances running nights and weekends. Fix: auto-shutdown, schedules, per-owner dashboards (cl12).

API throttling at launch. Tokens-per-minute quotas hit on a model API. Fix: quota planning, cross-region profiles where allowed, provisioned throughput, client-side rate limits and fallbacks.

Residency surprises. Inference or logs processed outside an allowed geography after enabling a routing feature. Fix: residency review of every routing and replication option; SCPs aligned with the chosen profiles; CloudTrail checks of the processing region.

Cost shocks. A forgotten p5 instance at $1,321 a day, or a misconfigured autoscaler. Fix: budgets and anomaly detection per GPU account, SCPs limiting GPU instance types to approved accounts.

What to measure

GPU utilisation and MFU per job; cost per training run and per million tokens served; time to capacity (from request to running); interruption rate and lost GPU-hours; checkpoint duration; model API throttles and latency per region; quota headroom; and spend on idle GPU resources.

The case: 'Can't Be Late' (NSDI 2024)

Zhanghao Wu, Wei-Lin Chiang, Ziming Mao, Zongheng Yang, Eric Friedman, Scott Shenker and Ion Stoica, from UC Berkeley and ICSI, studied a practical question: how to use cheap but interruptible spot instances for jobs that must finish by a deadline, such as a training run due before a product review. Their setting includes the realities this lesson describes: spot capacity appears and disappears unpredictably, each switch between instances costs a changeover delay, and on-demand capacity is the safe but expensive fallback. They collected three months of real spot availability traces on AWS and released them for others to use.

The obvious strategy — use spot whenever available, and switch to on-demand only when the remaining time is just enough to finish there — performs poorly in bad cases, because it waits too long and ends up paying for long on-demand stretches. The paper's Uniform Progress policy instead compares actual progress with a straight line from start to deadline: when the job is ahead of that line, it uses spot (or waits for spot); when it falls behind, it uses on-demand until it catches up. It needs no parameters and no assumptions about spot availability. In their evaluation it closed the gap between the greedy policy and the theoretical optimum by about 2× in both average and bad cases, and in real deployments saved 27–84% of cost across representative workloads and deadlines while meeting them.

The lesson generalises beyond the algorithm. Spot is not a purchasing setting but a scheduling problem with an explicit fallback; the deadline is an input to the cost; and the savings depend on how much slack you give the scheduler. The same group's open-source SkyPilot project implements this kind of policy across regions and clouds — which brings back the data-locality question every time it moves a job.

Goal: build a GPU training cost estimator and a spot-versus-on-demand deadline simulator in Python 3.12 (standard library only), and reproduce the lesson's numbers. About 25 minutes; no cloud account needed.

  1. Estimator. Write train_hours(params, tokens, peak_tflops, mfu, gpus_per_instance) returning GPU-hours and instance-hours (FLOPs = 6 × params × tokens). Check 8×10⁹ parameters, 20×10⁹ tokens, 989 TFLOPS, 40% MFU, 8 GPUs → about 674 GPU-hours and 84.3 instance-hours.
  2. Pricing. Price it with the October 2026 rates from the lesson (EC2 on-demand, SageMaker, the two spot snapshots, the 1-year Savings Plan rate). Then vary MFU from 25% to 55% and print the on-demand cost curve.
  3. Interruptions. Simulate spot with interruptions arriving as a Poisson process (mean time between interruptions 6 h), checkpoint interval C, restore time 15 min; compute expected total hours for C = 15, 30, 60 and 120 minutes, including checkpoint cost of 2 minutes each. Find the best C and compare with √(2 × checkpoint cost × MTBI).
  4. Deadline policies. Generate a spot-availability trace in 10-minute steps (a two-state Markov chain: available stays available with probability 0.95, unavailable stays unavailable with 0.9). For a job of 84 hours with a deadline of 120 hours, implement (a) greedy: spot when available, switch to on-demand when remaining slack equals the remaining work; (b) uniform progress: spot when progress is ahead of the straight line to the deadline, on-demand otherwise. Add a 15-minute changeover delay on every switch. Run 500 traces and report mean cost, worst-case cost and missed deadlines.
  5. Data gravity. Add the cost of copying a dataset of D TB at $0.02/GB when spot is used in another region, and find the dataset size at which spot in us-west-2 stops being cheaper than on-demand in us-east-1 for this run.

What to look for: step 2 shows MFU swinging the cost by roughly 2×; step 3 finds an optimum checkpoint interval of tens of minutes, near the square-root rule; step 4 should show both policies meeting deadlines on most traces, with uniform progress cheaper in the bad ones — the paper's result in miniature; step 5 shows the break-even dataset size (about 87 TB for the numbers here, since the spot saving of about $1,780 equals $20.48 per TB copied), which is why cheap compute far from data is often a false saving.

Plan: capacity and cost for a quarter of ML work

A team's plan for the next quarter: (1) two fine-tuning runs of a 70B model with LoRA, each estimated at 1,500 H100 GPU-hours, needed by fixed review dates in weeks 5 and 11; (2) about 3,000 GPU-hours of exploratory experiments on 1–8 GPUs, with no deadlines; (3) serving their current 8B model to an internal product at a steady 300 requests per second during business hours and 50 at night, which needs about 6 L40S GPUs at peak and 1 at night; (4) a new feature on a managed LLM API expected to need 2 million tokens per minute at peak, for EU users. Their account has zero P-instance quota, data is in eu-west-1, and contracts require EU processing. Propose capacity sources, an estimated compute budget using this lesson's prices (treat eu-west-1 prices as roughly us-east-1's for estimation and say so), and the risks.

Answer

(1) Deadline runs: Capacity Blocks. 1,500 GPU-hours ≈ 188 p5.48xlarge instance-hours each. Book a block of e.g. 4 instances for ~2.5 days in an EU region that offers them (check the supported-regions table — eu-west-1 isn't listed for Capacity Blocks in October 2026, so use an EU region that is, such as eu-north-1 or eu-west-2, and copy the data inside the EU), timed to finish a few days before each review. Compute at roughly on-demand-level prices: 188 × ~$55 ≈ $10,300 per run, $20,700 for both (block prices are dynamic — get quotes). Checkpoint before the block's 11:00 UTC termination.

(2) Experiments: spot plus small on-demand. 3,000 GPU-hours, mostly on g6e (L40S) and occasional p5. On g6e.xlarge at $1.861 on demand ≈ $5,600; spot where discounted could halve it. Request P quota now (it's zero) so on-demand fallbacks are possible.

(3) Serving: reserved baseline plus on-demand peak. 1 GPU always on, 5 more for ~10 business hours on 22 days. Baseline on a 1-year Savings Plan for g6e.xlarge (≈ $1.172/h) ≈ $855/month; peak 5 × 220 h × $1.861 ≈ $2,047/month on demand; about $8,700 for the quarter. Autoscale on queue depth; weights on NVMe for fast scale-out (k8s14).

(4) LLM API feature: EU-bounded routing and quota. Use an EU geographic inference profile (not global) or an EU data-zone deployment, request quota for 2M tokens per minute in the EU regions involved, align SCPs to allow exactly those regions, and price tokens with ae2's method; consider provisioned throughput if peak is predictable. Verify where requests are processed in CloudTrail.

Total compute (excluding API tokens): roughly $20,700 + $5,600 + $8,700 ≈ $35,000 for the quarter. Risks: P quota and Capacity Block availability in EU regions (book early), data copies across EU regions (cost and residency paperwork), MFU lower than assumed (measure on a short run), LLM API quota approval timing, and idle notebooks — put schedules on everything interactive.
