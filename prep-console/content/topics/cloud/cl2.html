A machine-learning team needs a training job to read a dataset bucket and write checkpoints. The quickest way to make it work is to give the job's role AdministratorAccess. It works on the first try, the deadline is met, and nobody revisits it. Eight months later a dependency in the training container is compromised. The attacker's code runs with the job's credentials, and those credentials can do anything in the account: read every bucket, including the one with customer PII; create new users with access keys for persistence; switch off the audit trail. The incident write-up says the root cause was a malicious package. The real root cause was a permission that was never needed.

The opposite failure is just as common. A careful engineer writes a tight policy; the job fails with AccessDenied; they add an Allow and it still fails, because a Deny they didn't know about in an organisation-wide guardrail blocks it, or because they put an object action on a bucket ARN. After an afternoon of guessing, someone adds a wildcard "temporarily".

Both failures come from not understanding the machine that sits in front of every cloud API: the identity and access management system. Every one of the billions of API calls made to a cloud each day — read an object, start an instance, publish a message — is checked against a set of policies, by fixed rules, before anything happens. If you understand those rules you can grant exactly what is needed, predict the answer to "why is this denied?" without guessing, and spot the configurations that turn a small compromise into a total one. Most cloud breaches are, at bottom, IAM failures: credentials that lived too long, roles that could do too much, trust that extended too far.

This lesson teaches IAM the way the evaluation engine sees it, using AWS as the concrete system (it's the most detailed and the one interviews probe most), with Google Cloud and Azure compared at the end. It covers principals, the policy language, identity-based and resource-based policies, the guardrails that cap them, the evaluation logic worked by hand for a request with an allow and an explicit deny, assumed roles and temporary credentials, how least privilege is achieved in practice, and the classic mistakes — with a real confused-deputy bug in an AWS service as the case.

Think of IAM as the security desk of a large office building that never sleeps and never makes exceptions.

Every time anyone wants to do anything — open a door, use a printer, take a file from a cabinet — they must present a badge at the desk and state exactly what they want: "I'm badge 4417, I want to open the file cabinet in room 3B, it's Tuesday at 14:00, and I came in through the main entrance." The desk has a binder of rules. Some rules are clipped to people ("badge 4417 may open cabinets on floor 3"). Some are taped to the objects themselves ("this cabinet may be opened by anyone from the finance department"). And some are building-wide notices from management ("nobody may remove files from the building after 18:00", "floor 9 is off-limits to contractors"). The desk applies them the same way every time: if any rule says no to this exact request, the answer is no. Otherwise, if management's notices permit it and some rule says yes, the answer is yes. If no rule says yes, the answer is no.

Map that to the cloud. The badge is a principal — the identity making the request, proven by a cryptographic signature on the API call. "Open" is the action (s3:GetObject). "The cabinet in room 3B" is the resource (arn:aws:s3:::acme-ml-datasets/train/part-0001.parquet). Tuesday at 14:00 and the main entrance are the request context: time, source IP, whether TLS was used, tags on the caller and the resource. Rules clipped to people are identity-based policies, rules taped to objects are resource-based policies, and management's notices are guardrails such as service control policies. The fixed procedure is policy evaluation.

Two more ideas complete the mental model. First, the safest badges are temporary. Instead of giving a program a permanent key, you create a role — a set of permissions with no key of its own — and let the program assume it, which returns credentials that expire after an hour or so. Stolen temporary credentials stop working on their own. Second, the goal is least privilege: each principal can do what its job needs and nothing else, so a compromise of one component stays small.

Where the analogy breaks: a human security desk might use judgement, notice something odd, or let the CEO through. IAM does none of that. It applies the rules exactly, including the rules you didn't mean to write, and it evaluates every one of billions of requests in milliseconds. That precision is its strength — you can reason about it like code — and the reason a single wildcard can be catastrophic. (One exception to "no VIPs": the account's root user, covered below.) Keep the desk in mind through the rest of the lesson: every question of the form "why was this denied?" is answered by replaying the desk's procedure, rule by rule, against the exact request that was made.

1. Principals: who can make a request

A principal is whatever signs an API request. AWS requests are signed with a secret key using the Signature Version 4 scheme: the client computes an HMAC (a keyed hash, au4) over the request using its secret access key, and AWS recomputes it to prove the caller holds the key. The access key ID in the request tells AWS which principal that is. The kinds of principal are:

  • The root user. Created with the account, signs in with the account's email address. It has full access that identity policies can't limit (AWS's evaluation documentation states that all requests are implicitly denied "with the exception of the AWS account root user, which has full access"). In an organisation, SCPs can restrict member accounts' root users. Use it only for the handful of tasks that require it, protect it with MFA (au7), and never create access keys for it.
  • IAM users. Long-lived identities with an optional console password and up to two long-term access keys (IDs beginning AKIA). They were the original model and are now the main source of leaked credentials; modern practice avoids them.
  • Roles and role sessions. A role has permissions but no credentials. A principal that the role's trust policy allows calls STS (the Security Token Service) to assume it and gets temporary credentials: an access key ID beginning ASIA, a secret, and a session token, all expiring together. The principal that then makes requests is the role session, with an ARN like arn:aws:sts::111122223333:assumed-role/ml-training/job-8812, where the last part is a session name chosen at assumption time.
  • Federated principals. Humans authenticated by an external identity provider (Okta, Entra ID, Google Workspace, IAM Identity Center) or workloads authenticated by an OIDC issuer (a Kubernetes cluster, GitHub Actions) exchange that proof for a role session. au10 traced this for workloads.
  • Service principals. AWS services acting on your behalf, named like lambda.amazonaws.com or ec2.amazonaws.com. When you create a Lambda function you give it an execution role whose trust policy allows lambda.amazonaws.com to assume it.

Groups exist (collections of IAM users to attach policies to) but are not principals — you can't name a group in a resource policy.

2. The policy language

A policy is a JSON document with a Version (always "2012-10-17", the current language version; omitting it falls back to an older version without policy variables) and a list of statements. Each statement has:

  • Effect: Allow or Deny.
  • Action (or NotAction): API operations as service:Operation, with * and ? wildcards — s3:GetObject, s3:Get*, ec2:Describe*.
  • Resource (or NotResource): ARNs the statement applies to, with wildcards. Some actions don't support resource-level scoping and need "*" — for example many Describe/List calls. The Service Authorization Reference lists, for every action, which resource types and condition keys it supports.
  • Principal: only in resource-based policies — who the statement is about.
  • Condition: optional tests on the request context.

Here is the identity-based policy we will evaluate later, attached to a role named ml-training:

{
  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [
    { "Sid": "ReadDatasets",
      "Effect": "Allow",
      "Action": ["s3:GetObject"],
      "Resource": "arn:aws:s3:::acme-ml-datasets/*" },
    { "Sid": "ListDatasets",
      "Effect": "Allow",
      "Action": "s3:ListBucket",
      "Resource": "arn:aws:s3:::acme-ml-datasets" },
    { "Sid": "WriteCheckpoints",
      "Effect": "Allow",
      "Action": ["s3:PutObject"],
      "Resource": "arn:aws:s3:::acme-ml-checkpoints/runs/*" },
    { "Sid": "NoPiiWithoutClearance",
      "Effect": "Deny",
      "Action": "s3:GetObject",
      "Resource": "arn:aws:s3:::acme-ml-datasets/pii/*",
      "Condition": { "StringNotEquals": { "aws:PrincipalTag/data-clearance": "pii" } } }
  ]
}

Notice the two ARN forms for S3: the bucket (arn:aws:s3:::acme-ml-datasets) for bucket-level actions like ListBucket, and objects (arn:aws:s3:::acme-ml-datasets/*) for object-level actions like GetObject. Mixing them up is the commonest reason a policy "should work but doesn't". The Sid is just a label.

Conditions use an operator, a condition key and values. Operators include StringEquals, StringLike (with wildcards), StringNotEquals, ArnLike, IpAddress, Bool, NumericLessThan, DateGreaterThan, and ...IfExists variants. Global keys available on most requests include aws:PrincipalTag/key (tags on the calling role or user), aws:ResourceTag/key, aws:RequestTag/key (tags being set in this request), aws:PrincipalOrgID (the caller's AWS organisation), aws:SourceIp, aws:SourceVpce (the VPC endpoint the request came through), aws:SecureTransport (whether TLS was used), aws:MultiFactorAuthPresent, aws:RequestedRegion, aws:SourceAccount and aws:SourceArn (for requests made by a service on behalf of a resource). Services add their own, such as s3:prefix. Multiple keys in one condition block are ANDed; multiple values for one key are ORed. A key that's absent from the request makes ordinary operators evaluate to false — except the negated ones like StringNotEquals, which evaluate to true when the key is missing, which is why the Deny above also blocks sessions with no clearance tag at all.

Policies can include variables: "Resource": "arn:aws:s3:::home-dirs/${aws:username}/*" gives each user their own prefix with one policy.

3. Where policies live: identity-based, resource-based and guardrails

Policies come in kinds, distinguished by where they are attached and whether they can grant:

KindAttached toCan grant?Typical use
Identity-based (managed or inline)user, group, roleyeswhat this workload or person may do
Resource-basedbucket, queue, KMS key, role (trust policy)…yeswho may use this resource, including other accounts
Service control policy (SCP)organisation root, OU, accountno — caps principalsorganisation-wide rules: regions, logging, leaving the org

Managed policies are standalone documents you can attach to many identities (up to 20 per role by default, raisable to 25, as of October 2026; each at most 6,144 characters, not counting whitespace). Inline policies are embedded in one identity (a role's inline policies together can be up to 10,240 characters). AWS also publishes AWS managed policies like ReadOnlyAccess — convenient, but broad, and AWS adds actions to them when services launch features.

A role has two policies with different jobs. Its permissions policies (identity-based) say what the role can do. Its trust policy (resource-based, with the role as the resource) says who can become the role:

{
  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [{
    "Effect": "Allow",
    "Principal": { "Service": "sagemaker.amazonaws.com" },
    "Action": "sts:AssumeRole",
    "Condition": { "StringEquals": { "aws:SourceAccount": "111122223333" } }
  }]
}

This one lets the SageMaker service assume the role, but only when acting on behalf of resources in account 111122223333 — the condition that blocks the cross-service confused deputy described in the deep section.

4. The evaluation logic, step by step

AWS's documentation describes evaluation as a fixed sequence. For a request within one account:

  1. Start at deny. Every request is implicitly denied unless something allows it.
  2. Explicit deny check. Look through every applicable policy — RCPs, SCPs, the resource-based policy, identity-based policies, the permissions boundary, the session policy — for a Deny statement matching this request. If there is even one, the final answer is Deny. Stop.
  3. RCPs. If the organisation uses resource control policies, they must contain an Allow for this action on this resource (AWS attaches a default RCPFullAWSAccess that allows everything, so this step mostly matters via Denies). Otherwise Deny.
  4. SCPs. The SCPs on every level from the organisation root down to the account must each allow the action (the default FullAWSAccess SCP allows everything; teams either keep it and add Deny SCPs, or replace it with allow-lists). Otherwise Deny.
  5. Resource-based policy. If it grants the action to this principal, it can be enough on its own in the same account. If it names the exact IAM user or role session, the answer is Allow even if identity policies, boundaries or session policies are silent. If it names the role's ARN (rather than the session), the session is still limited by any boundary and session policy.
  6. Identity-based policies. Otherwise, some identity-based policy must allow it. If none does: implicit Deny.
  7. Permissions boundary. If the identity has one, it must also allow the action. Otherwise Deny.
  8. Session policy. If this is a role session created with a session policy, it must also allow the action. Otherwise Deny.
  9. Allow.

Cross-account requests are evaluated twice: in the caller's account (identity-based policies plus that account's guardrails must allow) and in the resource's account (the resource-based policy plus that account's guardrails must allow). Both must say Allow. That's why sharing a bucket with another account needs a bucket policy naming the other account and an identity policy in the other account letting its role use the bucket. Two resource types are strict even within an account: a role's trust policy and a KMS key policy must explicitly allow the principal; an identity policy alone is not enough.

5. Worked example: an allow and an explicit deny

Set the scene in account 111122223333, part of an AWS organisation. The ml-training role has the identity policy from section 2 and no permissions boundary. It carries the tag data-clearance=none. The account's OU has two SCPs: the default FullAWSAccess and a region guardrail:

{ "Effect": "Deny",
  "NotAction": ["iam:*", "sts:*", "organizations:*", "support:*", "cloudfront:*", "route53:*"],
  "Resource": "*",
  "Condition": { "StringNotEquals": { "aws:RequestedRegion": ["eu-central-1", "eu-west-1"] } } }

(NotAction exempts global services, whose requests are routed to us-east-1 and would otherwise be blocked.) The bucket acme-ml-datasets has a bucket policy that denies any request not made over TLS:

{ "Effect": "Deny", "Principal": "*", "Action": "s3:*",
  "Resource": ["arn:aws:s3:::acme-ml-datasets", "arn:aws:s3:::acme-ml-datasets/*"],
  "Condition": { "Bool": { "aws:SecureTransport": "false" } } }

Now evaluate five requests made by a session of ml-training:

#RequestExplicit deny?SCP allows?Grant?Result
1GetObject acme-ml-datasets/train/part-0001.parquet, HTTPS, eu-central-1none matchyesReadDatasetsAllow
2GetObject acme-ml-datasets/pii/users.parquet, HTTPS, eu-central-1NoPiiWithoutClearance: key under pii/ and tag ≠ "pii"—(ReadDatasets would)Deny (explicit)
3GetObject train/part-0001.parquet over plain HTTPbucket policy: SecureTransport = false——Deny (explicit)
4PutObject acme-ml-datasets/train/new.parquetnoneyesno statement allows PutObject hereDeny (implicit)
5GetObject train/part-0001.parquet, endpoint in us-east-1SCP region deny: s3 not in NotAction, region not listed——Deny (explicit)

Trace request 2 slowly, because it's the one interviewers ask about. The engine collects the SCPs, the bucket policy and the role's identity policy. It scans for Denies. The SCP region Deny: the condition aws:RequestedRegion not in the list is false (eu-central-1 is in the list), so it doesn't match. The bucket's TLS Deny: aws:SecureTransport is true, so Bool false doesn't match. The NoPiiWithoutClearance Deny: action s3:GetObject matches, resource arn:aws:s3:::acme-ml-datasets/pii/users.parquet matches .../pii/*, and StringNotEquals aws:PrincipalTag/data-clearance "pii" is true because the tag is "none". All three parts match: explicit Deny, final answer, no further steps. ReadDatasets is irrelevant even though it matches too.

Request 4 shows the other kind of no: nothing denies it, but nothing allows it either. The error message is the same AccessDenied, but the fix is different — an Allow would fix request 4, while nothing short of changing the Deny or the tag fixes request 2. Many services now say which kind of policy blocked a request in the error message ("explicit deny in a service control policy", "no identity-based policy allows"), which shortens debugging a lot.

Two variations sharpen it. If an engineer is given a session of the same role with the tag data-clearance=pii (for example passed as a session tag by the identity provider), request 2 becomes an Allow: the Deny's condition is now false, so the Deny doesn't match, and ReadDatasets grants. And if the role had a permissions boundary allowing only s3:ListBucket, request 1 would become an implicit Deny at the boundary step, despite ReadDatasets.

6. Assumed roles and temporary credentials

Assuming a role is an API call to STS. With the CLI (needs an AWS account):

aws sts assume-role \
  --role-arn arn:aws:iam::111122223333:role/ml-training \
  --role-session-name job-8812 --duration-seconds 3600

The response, per the STS API reference, contains Credentials (an AccessKeyId beginning ASIA, a SecretAccessKey, a SessionToken and an Expiration time) and an AssumedRoleUser ARN of the form arn:aws:sts::111122223333:assumed-role/ml-training/job-8812. STS checks two things: that the role's trust policy allows the caller, and (for a caller in the same account that isn't named directly in the trust policy, or any caller in another account) that the caller's own identity policy allows sts:AssumeRole on that role.

The numbers worth knowing (from the STS API reference and IAM quotas page, October 2026): DurationSeconds ranges from 900 (15 minutes) to the role's maximum session duration, which can be set from 1 to 12 hours; the default is 3,600 seconds. Role chaining — using one role session to assume another role — caps the new session at one hour. A session policy passed at assumption (one inline JSON document plus up to 10 managed policy ARNs, together at most 2,048 characters) narrows the session to the intersection of the role's permissions and the session policy. Session tags (up to 50) become aws:PrincipalTag values for that session — that's how an identity provider's "department" attribute reaches policies. SourceIdentity records the original human through a chain of role assumptions, so CloudTrail logs can show who was behind assumed-role/deploy/…. The account-wide STS request quota was 600 requests per second per region for calls like AssumeRole, which matters for systems that assume roles per request instead of caching credentials until shortly before expiry.

Third-party access uses the same mechanism with one extra field. A monitoring vendor's account assumes a role in yours; your trust policy names the vendor's account and requires "sts:ExternalId": "<value the vendor gave you>". The external ID is the vendor's per-customer identifier; it prevents another of the vendor's customers from getting the vendor to assume your role by giving the vendor your role ARN (a confused deputy, explained in the deep section).

7. Least privilege in practice

Least privilege is easy to state and hard to do, because nobody knows the exact list of actions an application uses. The working method is a loop:

  1. Start scoped, not perfect. Write by resource and purpose: this service reads this bucket prefix and writes to this queue. Avoid AWS managed policies like AmazonS3FullAccess for workloads.
  2. Cap the blast radius. Guardrails make mistakes survivable: SCPs that deny unused regions and dangerous actions, permissions boundaries on roles developers create, resource policies that deny access from outside the organisation (an aws:PrincipalOrgID condition).
  3. Observe. CloudTrail (cl11) records every API call with the principal and outcome. IAM's "last accessed" data shows which services and actions a role has used and when. IAM Access Analyzer can generate a policy from a role's CloudTrail history, reports unused roles, keys and permissions, and reports resources and roles accessible from outside your organisation.
  4. Trim and repeat. Remove what wasn't used in, say, 90 days; review whenever the service changes.

Two tactics scale this beyond hand-written policies. One role per workload, never shared, so its permissions and its logs describe one thing. And ABAC (au8): instead of listing resources, compare tags — allow ec2:StopInstances when aws:ResourceTag/team equals ${aws:PrincipalTag/team}. New resources are covered automatically when tagged, at the cost that tagging becomes a security operation that must itself be restricted.

8. The classic IAM mistakes

These come up in nearly every cloud security review, and each has a mechanism behind it:

  • Long-lived access keys. IAM user keys in code, CI variables, laptops and container images are the most common initial access in cloud breaches. Replace with roles, federation (au10) and, for humans, single sign-on.
  • "Action": "*", "Resource": "*" on workloads. Turns any code execution in that workload into account takeover.
  • iam:PassRole on *. Lets a principal launch compute with any role, including admin, and read its credentials — privilege escalation without ever being granted admin. Scope it to specific roles and add iam:PassedToService.
  • Self-modifying permissions. iam:CreatePolicyVersion, iam:AttachRolePolicy, iam:PutRolePolicy or iam:UpdateAssumeRolePolicy on your own role or a more powerful one is admin with extra steps.
  • Trust policies that trust too much. "Principal": {"AWS": "*"} without conditions, an entire foreign account without an external ID, or an OIDC trust without a sub condition (au10).
  • Missing service-side conditions. Roles assumable by a service principal without aws:SourceAccount/aws:SourceArn, and resource policies granting a service principal without them.
  • NotAction with Allow. "Allow everything except IAM" also allows every action of every future service.
  • Using the root user for daily work, or leaving it without MFA.

The shapes differ. On Google Cloud you rarely write policy documents; you grant a role — a bundle of permissions such as roles/storage.objectViewer — to a principal on a resource, and the grant is inherited by everything below that resource. On Azure, identity lives in Microsoft Entra ID and authorisation is Azure RBAC: role assignments are additive and inherit down the scope hierarchy, and deny assignments override them. The habits carry over: no long-lived keys, one identity per workload, grant at the narrowest scope, guardrails at the top.

The confused deputy, and why it keeps reappearing

A confused deputy is a program with authority that can be tricked into using it for someone else. In clouds the deputy is usually a service or a vendor that acts across many customers. Suppose a log-analysis vendor assumes a role in each customer's account from its own account. If customer A's trust policy only says "the vendor's account may assume this role", then customer B can register with the vendor, type in customer A's role ARN as "their" role, and the vendor — a deputy with access to every customer's trust — will fetch A's data for B. The external ID fixes this because the vendor assigns it, per customer, and sends it on every AssumeRole for that customer; B can't make the vendor send A's ID. When the deputy is an AWS service (S3 delivering to SNS, a service assuming your role), the equivalent is conditions on aws:SourceAccount and aws:SourceArn, which the service populates with the account and resource it's acting for. The pattern reappears whenever a new service accepts a role ARN or a resource ARN from its caller.

The case in miniature: AppSync, 2022

AWS AppSync lets developers create data sources that AppSync accesses using a role ARN the developer supplies; the role's trust policy allows appsync.amazonaws.com. AppSync checked that the supplied role belonged to the caller's own account. Datadog's researchers found that the API accepted the parameter name in mixed case, and that the lowercase spelling servicerolearn skipped the check, so a data source could reference a role in another account. Any role trusting AppSync without source conditions was then usable by any AWS customer — in their demonstration, to read a DynamoDB table across accounts. It was reported on 1 September 2022, fixed by AWS on 6 September, and disclosed on 21 November; AWS said it confirmed the issue had not been used against other customers. The lessons for your own designs: trust policies for service principals should carry aws:SourceAccount/aws:SourceArn conditions, because they turn a bug in the deputy into a denied request; and validation done in one layer (the API) is fragile compared with a check the authorisation system enforces.

Why default deny plus explicit deny is the right algebra

The evaluation rules make the permission set a simple function: (union of grants) minus (union of denies), intersected with each guardrail. That has useful properties. Adding an Allow can never remove access; adding a Deny can never add access; guardrails compose by intersection, so a platform team can add one without reading every team's policies. It also makes analysis tractable. AWS uses an automated-reasoning engine (described in its Zelkova research) to answer questions like "can anyone outside this organisation read this bucket?" over all possible requests, which powers Access Analyzer's external-access findings. The cost is expressiveness: there's no "allow unless" except by pairing an Allow with a Deny.

Policy size, sprawl and the tagging trade-off

Hard limits shape designs: 6,144 characters per managed policy, 10,240 for a role's inline policies combined, 20–25 managed policies per role, 2,048 characters for a session policy, a default trust policy length of 2,048 characters (raisable to 8,192). A platform with 300 buckets can't list every ARN in one policy. Teams respond with naming conventions and wildcards (arn:aws:s3:::acme-team-search-*), which are cheap but trust the naming discipline, or with ABAC on tags, which scales but moves security into tag management. Neither is free; a senior answer names the trade-off and the control that makes it safe (prefix ownership enforced by SCPs, or tag changes restricted and tags required at creation).

Revocation of temporary credentials

Issued STS credentials can't be recalled; they're validated cryptographically until they expire. Revocation is therefore done by policy: attach a Deny for sessions whose aws:TokenIssueTime is before now (the console's "revoke active sessions" does this), or remove the role's permissions. For long-lived IAM user keys, deactivate and delete them. This is why session lifetime is a security parameter: with one-hour sessions, a stolen credential has an expected useful life of half an hour after theft unless the attacker can mint more — and preventing that minting (the trust policy, the source identity) matters more than shaving minutes off the duration.

The perimeter, expressed in IAM

"Data perimeter" policies are a modern pattern that uses three condition keys to state "only our identities, on our resources, from our networks". Resource policies (or RCPs) deny access unless aws:PrincipalOrgID is your organisation; identity policies (or SCPs) deny access to resources outside your organisation (aws:ResourceOrgID); and network conditions (aws:SourceVpce, aws:SourceIp) deny use of credentials from outside your networks. Together they turn leaked credentials or a misconfigured bucket from a breach into a denied request, at the cost of carefully exempting AWS services that act on your behalf.

Human access is a different problem from workload access

Workloads have stable, narrow needs, so tight policies and long-term stability are realistic. Humans have broad, changing needs and make mistakes under pressure. Good human access design leans on federation with MFA, broad read access, narrow standing write access, and just-in-time elevation for the rest — a time-limited grant with approval and logging. The failure mode to avoid is the opposite: tight human policies that cause so much friction that someone creates an IAM user with admin keys "for emergencies" and keeps it forever.

Failure modes you will see

AccessDenied in production after a change. Causes, in rough order of frequency: a new code path calls an action the role doesn't allow; the wrong ARN form (bucket vs object); a new SCP or RCP from the platform team; a KMS key policy that doesn't include the role (S3 objects encrypted with a customer-managed key need kms:Decrypt on the key and the key policy must allow it); a condition key that isn't present for that action. Read the error message for the policy type, then check CloudTrail for the denied event with its errorCode and request parameters.

Leaked access keys. Keys pushed to a public repository are typically found by automated scanners within minutes. AWS scans public GitHub and notifies the account and can attach a quarantine policy, but attackers often move first: crypto-mining instances in every region, or enumeration and data access. Response: deactivate the key, review CloudTrail for its activity across all regions, remove any persistence the attacker created (new users, keys, roles, trust policy edits), then fix the pipeline that leaked it (au11's playbook).

Privilege escalation inside the account. A compromised low-privilege role uses PassRole, policy-version or function-update permissions to become admin. Detection: CloudTrail alerts on IAM write events from workload roles, which should almost never happen.

Over-trusting roles. Cross-account roles that trust whole accounts without external IDs, OIDC trusts missing a subject condition, roles assumable by *. Access Analyzer reports the external ones; reviews catch the rest.

STS throttling. A service that calls AssumeRole for every request hits the per-account STS quota (600 requests per second per region for these calls, October 2026) and starts failing under load. Cache credentials until close to expiry; SDK credential providers already do.

What to measure

Number of IAM users and active access keys (target: trending to zero), age of the oldest key, roles with administrator-equivalent permissions, roles unused for 90 days, externally accessible resources and roles, IAM write events by non-admin principals, denied API calls by role (a sudden rise often means an attack or a broken deploy), and root user sign-ins (should be essentially never, and always alerted).

Knobs

Maximum session duration per role (shorter for powerful roles), session tags from the identity provider for ABAC, SCPs for regions and protected services, permissions boundaries on developer-created roles, Access Analyzer's unused-access and external-access analysers, and IAM Identity Center permission sets in place of IAM users.

The case, read as an engineer

The AppSync bug (Datadog Security Labs, November 2022) is worth retelling in an interview because it ties together several ideas. AppSync is a deputy: it acts with roles customers give it. The protection was a validation check in the API layer rather than something IAM itself enforced. A parsing quirk — accepting servicerolearn as well as serviceRoleArn but validating only one spelling — opened a cross-account path. AWS fixed it within five days of the report and found no use against customers. What you control: every role trusting a service principal should say for which account and resource the service may assume it, so that if the service is confused, IAM still says no.

Goal: build a toy policy evaluator that follows AWS's order, and use it to reproduce the five requests in section 5 — Python 3.12 standard library only (json, fnmatch). About 20 minutes.

  1. Matching. Write matches(stmt, req): actions and resources match with fnmatch.fnmatchcase (AWS action names are case-insensitive, resource ARNs case-sensitive — lowercase the actions); support NotAction; support conditions StringEquals, StringNotEquals (true when the key is missing) and Bool over a req["context"] dict.
  2. Evaluate. Write evaluate(req, scps, resource_policy, identity, boundary=None) returning ("Allow"|"Deny", reason): explicit deny in any policy → Deny; every SCP list must contain a matching Allow → else Deny; identity or resource Allow → else implicit Deny; boundary must allow if present.
  3. Load the scenario. Paste the identity policy, the SCPs (FullAWSAccess plus the region deny) and the bucket policy from the lesson as Python dicts.
  4. Run the five requests from the table, with context keys aws:SecureTransport, aws:RequestedRegion and aws:PrincipalTag/data-clearance.
  5. Variations. Set the tag to "pii" and rerun request 2; remove the tag entirely; add a boundary allowing only s3:ListBucket and rerun request 1.

What to look for: requests 2, 3 and 5 return explicit denies with different reasons and request 4 an implicit deny; request 1 is the only Allow. With pii, request 2 flips to Allow; with no tag it stays Deny (the negated operator treats a missing key as a match); with the boundary, request 1 becomes an implicit Deny. Shuffle the statement order: no result changes.

Needs an AWS account (optional): the IAM policy simulator (aws iam simulate-principal-policy --policy-source-arn <role-arn> --action-names s3:GetObject --resource-arns arn:aws:s3:::acme-ml-datasets/pii/users.parquet) returns an EvalDecision of explicitDeny, implicitDeny or allowed with the matching statements; compare it with your evaluator.

Diagnose: "I added the permission and it's still denied"

A data engineer's Glue job runs as role etl-orders in account 444455556666. It needs to read s3://partner-drop/orders/2026-10-07.csv, a bucket owned by a partner in account 777788889999, objects encrypted with the partner's customer-managed KMS key. The engineer added s3:GetObject on arn:aws:s3:::partner-drop/* to the role. The job fails with AccessDenied. The partner says "we added your account to our bucket policy". List the possible causes in the order you'd check them and how you'd confirm each.

Answer

1. The error text. Read the full AccessDenied message: S3 now often says whether a bucket policy, an identity policy, an SCP or a KMS key policy blocked it, which shortcuts everything below.

2. Cross-account needs both sides. The role's identity policy now allows GetObject (side one). Confirm the partner's bucket policy really grants s3:GetObject on arn:aws:s3:::partner-drop/* (object ARN, not the bucket ARN) to arn:aws:iam::444455556666:role/etl-orders or the account root principal.

3. KMS. With a customer-managed key, reading also needs kms:Decrypt: the partner's key policy must allow your role or account, and your role's identity policy must allow kms:Decrypt on the key's ARN. This is the most commonly missed piece; CloudTrail in your account shows a denied Decrypt event if it's the cause.

4. Guardrails on either side. An SCP in your organisation might deny access to resources outside your organisation (an aws:ResourceOrgID data-perimeter rule), or the partner's RCP or bucket policy might deny principals outside their organisation. Either is an explicit deny that no Allow fixes; it needs an exception.

5. Object ownership. If objects were uploaded by a third account and the bucket's ownership setting doesn't make the bucket owner own them, the bucket policy doesn't apply to those objects. Ask the partner to check the bucket's Object Ownership setting.

6. Network conditions. The bucket policy may require aws:SourceVpce or an IP range that your Glue job doesn't come from.

Confirm with CloudTrail in both accounts (the partner sees the data event if they log it), and the policy simulator for your side.
