A team moves a monolith to the cloud. They accept the console's defaults: one VPC with a 10.0.0.0/16 range, a couple of subnets, everything in a public subnet "for now" because it's easier to SSH in. Over two years the company grows to 30 accounts. Then four things happen.

A partner integration needs a private connection, and the partner's network is also 10.0.0.0/16 — the two can't be routed together. An acquisition brings another set of VPCs with the same overlap. The finance team asks why the AWS bill has a $14,000 line for "NAT Gateway" when the product barely talks to the internet; the answer is that terabytes of S3 reads from private subnets were going through the NAT gateway, at $0.045 a gigabyte, for no reason. And during a zone outage, all private instances lose outbound internet access, because the single NAT gateway lived in the zone that failed.

None of these was a bug. Each was a networking decision made by default, early, and expensive to reverse: address ranges are almost impossible to change once workloads use them, and routing mistakes hide in the bill. Cloud networking looks like it's taken care of — there's no hardware to rack — but the provider only gives you building blocks. You decide the address plan, which subnets can reach the internet and how, which firewall rules apply, how VPCs connect to each other and to your offices, and, through all of that, what every byte costs.

This lesson goes beyond net3 and net14, which introduced VPCs, subnets, route tables, NAT, security groups and NACLs. It plans a real three-zone address layout with numbers, follows packets through each gateway, compares security groups and NACLs at the level that interviewers probe, explains the options for connecting many VPCs (peering, Transit Gateway, PrivateLink, endpoints) and their failure and cost behaviour, prices every traffic path from the official price list, and compares AWS with Google Cloud and Azure. The case is Slack's January 2021 outage, which began in a Transit Gateway.

Picture a cloud network as a gated business park that you design on an empty plot of land.

The VPC is the plot, with its own address scheme: you choose a block of addresses, say 10.20.0.0/16 (65,536 addresses), and every building you put on the plot gets an address from it. The plot sits in one region. Inside it you lay out subnets, which are like separate fenced lots; on AWS each lot is in one availability zone (one physically separate data centre campus, cl1). You'll normally build the same three lots in each of three zones: one facing the street for things the public must reach (load balancers), one behind it for application servers, and one at the back for databases.

Route tables are the signposts at each lot's exit. Every signpost says "anything for the park's own addresses, stay inside" (the local route). The street-facing lot's signpost adds "everything else: out through the main gate" — the internet gateway, which is what makes the lot public. The application lot's signpost says "everything else: through the courier office" — a NAT gateway, which sends parcels out on the application servers' behalf and brings replies back, but never lets a stranger walk in. That is what makes the lot private. The database lot may have no exit to the outside at all.

Every building has a guard at its own door — a security group — who has a list of who may come in ("the application servers, on port 5432") and remembers whoever went out so their replies are let back in. Each lot can also have a gate guard — a network ACL — who checks every person in both directions against a numbered list and remembers nothing.

Two things make this more than a diagram. First, almost nothing about the address plan can be changed later without moving workloads, so the plan is a long-lived decision. Second, every arrow has a price. Bytes that stay in a zone are free; bytes that cross zones, pass through a NAT gateway or a transit hub, cross regions or leave for the internet are each billed per gigabyte. A cloud network is therefore designed twice: once for reachability and safety, and once for cost.

Connecting several parks is the next layer. Two parks can build a private road between them (peering), but a road only joins those two; with dozens of parks you build a central interchange that every park connects to once (a transit gateway). And when a park only needs to use one service in another park — not reach everything there — the other park can open a private service window directly into your lot (PrivateLink), with no road at all.

Where the analogy breaks: the "gates" and "courier office" are not buildings with a fixed capacity you can see; they're distributed software that scales automatically — usually. When they don't scale fast enough, or when a single "office" lives in one zone, they become hidden single points of failure. That's what Slack learned in 2021.

1. The address plan, worked

On AWS a VPC gets a primary IPv4 CIDR block between /16 (65,536 addresses) and /28 (16), and up to five blocks in total by default (raisable to 50) through secondary CIDRs. Each subnet takes a slice of a VPC block, lives in exactly one zone, and must also be between /16 and /28. In every subnet AWS reserves five addresses. In 10.20.0.0/19, they are 10.20.0.0 (network address), 10.20.0.1 (the VPC router), 10.20.0.2 (DNS — strictly, the DNS server is at the VPC's base plus two, and the base plus two of every subnet is reserved), 10.20.0.3 (reserved for future use) and 10.20.31.255 (broadcast, reserved even though VPCs don't support broadcast). So a /24 gives 251 usable addresses, not 254.

Now plan a production VPC for an ML-serving platform in three zones. Requirements: an EKS cluster whose pods get VPC addresses (the default AWS VPC CNI behaviour), up to about 6,000 pods per zone at peak; databases and caches; load balancers and NAT gateways; and room to grow.

Step 1: choose a block that won't collide. Pick 10.20.0.0/16 from a central plan (IPAM, below) so that no other VPC, office or likely partner uses it.

Step 2: size each tier per zone for its largest consumer. 6,000 pods plus nodes need more than 4,091 (a /20's usable count) and fit in 8,187 (a /19's), so application subnets are /19s. Databases and caches need tens of addresses; /22s (1,019 usable) leave plenty of headroom for read replicas and managed services that create several interfaces. Public subnets only hold load-balancer nodes and NAT gateways; /24s are ample.

Step 3: lay the blocks out in order, biggest first, so they stay aligned:

Why plan centrally. Two VPCs with overlapping ranges cannot be peered or routed through a Transit Gateway, and changing a VPC's addresses means moving every workload in it. Organisations with many accounts use an IP address manager (Amazon VPC IPAM, or a spreadsheet in small shops) that hands out non-overlapping blocks: for example a /12 per region (16 /16s) split into a /16 per account-VPC. Leave ranges aside for on-premises networks and for the Kubernetes service CIDR (k8s8), which also must not overlap anything pods talk to.

2. Route tables and the gateways they point at

Each subnet is associated with one route table; a route table can serve many subnets. A route says "destination CIDR → target", and the most specific match wins (longest-prefix match, net3). Every route table starts with a local route for each VPC CIDR, which can't be removed, so all subnets in a VPC can reach each other unless security groups or NACLs say otherwise. The targets you'll meet:

  • Internet gateway (IGW). One per VPC, horizontally scaled and redundant by AWS, with no bandwidth limit of its own. It does one-to-one translation between an instance's private IP and its public IP (an auto-assigned public IPv4 or an Elastic IP). An instance without a public IP can't use it, even in a public subnet. Since February 2024 AWS charges for every public IPv4 address, in use or idle — $0.005 per hour (about $3.65 a month) in October 2026 — which is one reason to keep instances out of public subnets.
  • NAT gateway. Covered in detail next.
  • Egress-only internet gateway. For IPv6, where every address is globally routable and there's no translation; it allows outbound-initiated IPv6 traffic and blocks inbound.
  • VPC endpoints, peering connections, Transit Gateway attachments, virtual private gateways (VPN) and network interfaces of appliances (for routing through a firewall).

Trace one packet. An application pod at 10.20.5.17 in AZ 1 calls api.partner.example, which resolves to 203.0.113.50. The pod's subnet route table has 10.20.0.0/16 → local, pl-s3 → vpce-s3 (a prefix list of S3's address ranges, added by the gateway endpoint) and 0.0.0.0/0 → nat-az1. 203.0.113.50 matches only the default route, so the VPC router delivers the packet to the NAT gateway in AZ 1's public subnet. The NAT gateway rewrites the source to its own private IP (10.20.108.9, say) and source port, tracks the mapping, and forwards it; the public subnet's route table sends it to the IGW, which translates 10.20.108.9 to the NAT gateway's Elastic IP, 198.51.100.7. The partner sees a connection from 198.51.100.7:43120. The reply follows the same path backwards. A read from S3 by the same pod matches the more specific prefix-list route and goes straight to S3 through the gateway endpoint.

3. NAT gateways: limits, placement and price

From the VPC documentation (October 2026): a NAT gateway supports TCP, UDP and ICMP; starts at 5 Gbps and scales automatically to 100 Gbps; processes 1 million packets per second, scaling to 10 million, and drops packets beyond that; and each of its IPv4 addresses supports up to 55,000 simultaneous connections to each unique destination (destination IP, port and protocol), with up to 8 addresses per gateway. It uses source ports 1024–65535. You can't attach a security group to it, and you can't route traffic to it through a peering connection.

The 55,000 figure matters more than the bandwidth. A fleet calling one external API endpoint (one IP, port 443) through one NAT address can hold at most 55,000 connections to it at once; beyond that, new connections fail with errors the NAT gateway reports as ErrorPortAllocation. Connection pooling and keep-alive in clients, or more NAT addresses, are the fixes.

Placement. A standard (zonal) NAT gateway lives in one zone. AWS's documentation states the consequence plainly: if resources in several zones share one NAT gateway and its zone goes down, resources in the other zones lose internet access. So production designs put one in each zone and give each zone's private subnets a route table pointing at the local one — which also avoids paying $0.01/GB each way for traffic crossing zones to reach a remote NAT. In November 2025 AWS added a regional availability mode: a single NAT gateway ID, no public subnet needed, that expands to each zone where you have network interfaces (taking up to 60 minutes to expand to a new zone, during which traffic is processed in an existing zone) and supports up to 32 addresses per zone. It's billed per zone it is active in, so it doesn't save the hourly charge; it saves configuration and the single-zone mistake.

Price, worked. In us-east-1 a NAT gateway costs $0.045 per hour plus $0.045 per GB processed (October 2026 price list). Three zonal gateways: 3 × 730 h × $0.045 = $98.55 a month standing cost. Suppose private workloads send and receive 2 TB a month to external APIs and package registries, and read 30 TB a month from S3:

4. Security groups in depth

net14 introduced security groups as stateful, allow-only firewalls attached to network interfaces, with rules that can reference other groups. Three further details matter in practice.

Connection tracking. "Stateful" is implemented by tracking connections: when an allowed packet starts a flow, the flow is recorded so return traffic is allowed regardless of rules. Tracking has limits per instance (an allowance that depends on instance size), and an instance can hit them under huge numbers of short connections; the ENA network driver exposes a conntrack_allowance_exceeded counter. Some flows are untracked — for example when a rule allows all traffic in both directions — which is one reason a very broad security group behaves differently under load.

Limits. By default a security group can have 60 inbound and 60 outbound rules (separately for IPv4 and IPv6), and an interface can have 5 security groups (raisable to 16, but rules per group × groups per interface can't exceed 1,000). Referencing a prefix list counts as its maximum number of entries. Large allow-lists of IP ranges therefore hit limits; referencing groups instead of addresses avoids most of them.

Changes apply immediately to new and, for most rule changes, existing tracked connections are not torn down — removing a rule doesn't necessarily cut a connection that's already established. When you need to cut traffic now, a NACL deny (stateless) does.

5. Network ACLs in depth

A network ACL applies to every packet entering or leaving a subnet. Rules are numbered; evaluation starts at the lowest number and stops at the first match; a final * rule denies anything unmatched. The default NACL in a VPC allows everything; a custom NACL starts by denying everything. Default quotas are 20 inbound and 20 outbound rules per NACL (raisable to 40 each).

Because NACLs are stateless, every flow needs rules in both directions. Work through a web server in a public subnet:

The ephemeral range is wide on purpose: Linux clients use 32768–60999 by default, Windows 49152–65535, and NAT gateways and load balancers use 1024–65535. Forgetting one of these rules is the classic NACL outage: everything works from some clients and fails from others. Most teams therefore keep NACLs simple — subnet-to-subnet separation and explicit denies — and do fine-grained control with security groups.

6. Connecting VPCs and reaching services privately

VPC peering links two VPCs (same or different account or region) so their address spaces route to each other; each side adds routes to the other's CIDR via the peering connection. It has no hourly charge; traffic within one zone over a peering connection is free, across zones is charged like any cross-zone traffic, and across regions at inter-region rates. It's not transitive — no routing through a peer to a third VPC, an on-premises network, or the peer's internet or NAT gateway — and the ranges must not overlap. For n VPCs, a full mesh needs n(n−1)/2 peerings: 45 for 10 VPCs, 1,225 for 50.

Transit Gateway (TGW) is a regional hub. Each VPC attaches once (through small subnets in each zone where you want the attachment), as do VPNs and Direct Connect links; TGW route tables decide which attachments can reach which, so you can let prod reach shared services but not dev. TGWs in different regions can peer. It costs $0.05 per attachment-hour and $0.02 per GB processed in us-east-1 (October 2026). Fifty VPC attachments cost 50 × 730 × $0.05 = $1,825 a month before data; 100 TB processed adds 102,400 × $0.02 = $2,048.

PrivateLink solves a different problem: offering one service to many networks without connecting the networks. The provider puts the service behind a Network Load Balancer and creates an endpoint service; each consumer creates an interface endpoint, which appears as network interfaces with private IPs in the consumer's own subnets. Traffic is one-directional (consumer to service), address overlap doesn't matter, and the provider approves which accounts may connect. AWS's own services are offered the same way: interface endpoints for STS, Secrets Manager, ECR, CloudWatch, Bedrock and most others let private subnets reach them without NAT. An interface endpoint costs $0.01 per hour per zone plus $0.01 per GB (us-east-1, October 2026), so one endpoint in three zones is about $21.90 a month before data.

Gateway endpoints for S3 and DynamoDB are the exception: free, implemented as route-table entries pointing at a prefix list, and regional (they reach the service in the same region only).

Endpoints also strengthen IAM (cl2). A bucket policy can deny requests unless aws:SourceVpce equals your endpoint's ID, so even valid credentials used from outside your network are refused; endpoint policies can restrict which buckets can be reached through the endpoint, so a compromised instance can't upload data to an attacker's bucket.

7. DNS inside the VPC

Every VPC has a DNS resolver, Route 53 Resolver, at the VPC's base address plus two (10.20.0.2 here) and at the link-local 169.254.169.253; instances get it by default. It answers for public names, for private hosted zones associated with the VPC (internal names like orders.internal.example), for instance hostnames, and for interface endpoints — with private DNS enabled, secretsmanager.eu-central-1.amazonaws.com resolves to the endpoint's private IPs inside the VPC. Each network interface can send at most 1,024 packets per second to the resolver, a limit that can't be raised, so busy services must cache DNS. For hybrid networks, resolver endpoints forward queries between the VPC and on-premises DNS servers.

Why address exhaustion hits Kubernetes first

With AWS's VPC CNI, every pod gets a real VPC address from its node's subnet, and nodes pre-allocate "warm" addresses so pods start quickly. A cluster can therefore consume addresses far faster than its pod count suggests, and a /24 per zone runs out at a few hundred pods. When it does, pods sit in ContainerCreating with IP-allocation errors while the nodes have spare CPU. The ways out are all disruptive: add a secondary CIDR (often from 100.64.0.0/10, the carrier-grade NAT range, which is non-routable on most corporate networks) and configure custom pod networking; enable prefix delegation, which assigns /28 blocks to nodes and changes fragmentation behaviour; or move to IPv6 for pods. The lesson for design reviews is to size application subnets from the pod count, not the node count, and to keep spare space in the VPC.

The hidden capacity of "infinitely scalable" network components

Internet gateways, NAT gateways, Transit Gateways and load balancers are described as scaling automatically, and they do — over minutes, along curves tuned for typical growth. A step change (a holiday reopening, a launch, a failover of a whole region's traffic) can arrive faster than the scaling. NAT gateways drop packets above their current packet rate; load balancers return 5xx while they add capacity (AWS offers pre-warming of load-balancer capacity units for known events); Slack's TGW dropped packets. The senior responses are to know which components sit on the critical path, to watch their drop and error metrics (NAT PacketsDropCount, TGW PacketDropCount*), to request capacity ahead of known surges, and to avoid putting monitoring on the same path.

Zone affinity is a design principle, not a tweak

Cross-zone charges ($0.01/GB each way on AWS) and cross-zone latency (a millisecond or two) both reward keeping a request inside one zone from load balancer to database replica. Doing that consistently is a property of the whole stack: load balancers with cross-zone load balancing off (at the cost of uneven distribution if zones have unequal capacity); Kubernetes topology-aware routing so Services prefer endpoints in the caller's zone; Kafka consumers fetching from the nearest replica; caches and read replicas per zone. It also improves failure isolation: a degraded zone affects only requests that started there, and evacuating it becomes a load-balancer change. The cost is complexity and the need to keep capacity balanced across zones.

Egress control is a security boundary

Most data exfiltration needs an outbound connection. A private subnet with a NAT gateway allows outbound connections to anywhere on the internet, which is convenient and is also the channel an attacker uses. Tighter designs route egress through an inspection point — AWS Network Firewall with domain allow-lists, or a proxy — so workloads can reach their package registries and partner APIs and nothing else, and use VPC endpoints with endpoint policies for AWS services so even S3 uploads are limited to your buckets. DNS is a covert channel too: Route 53 Resolver DNS Firewall can block or allow domains at the resolver. The trade-off is operational: every new dependency needs an allow-list change, so teams usually start with logging (to learn the real destinations) before enforcing.

Hub-and-spoke concentrates both control and risk

A central Transit Gateway with inspection gives a platform team one place to enforce segmentation and egress policy for hundreds of VPCs. It also makes that place a shared dependency in two senses: capacity (every byte between VPCs crosses it and pays $0.02/GB) and configuration (one wrong route-table association can connect prod to dev or blackhole a region's traffic). Mature designs keep the hub's configuration in code with review and automated reachability tests (AWS's Reachability Analyzer can check whether a path exists between two endpoints before and after a change), and route heavy, low-risk flows around it.

IPv6 changes the shape of the problem

With IPv6, addresses are plentiful (AWS gives a VPC a /56 and each subnet a /64) and globally unique, so exhaustion and overlap mostly disappear, and NAT is replaced by the egress-only gateway's stateful filtering. Public IPv4 charges ($0.005/hour per address in October 2026) give a cost reason to move public endpoints to dual-stack or IPv6. The obstacles are dependencies that remain IPv4-only — some partners, some AWS service endpoints, older libraries — which NAT64 with DNS64 (supported by NAT gateways and the resolver) bridges at the cost of NAT processing charges.

Failure modes and their symptoms

IP exhaustion. New instances, pods, Lambda functions in the VPC or load-balancer nodes fail to launch with "insufficient free addresses in subnet". Watch available-IP counts per subnet and alert at 20% remaining.

NAT port exhaustion. Outbound calls to one popular destination fail intermittently under load while bandwidth is fine. The NAT gateway's ErrorPortAllocation metric rises. Fix with connection pooling and keep-alive, more NAT addresses, or an endpoint if the destination is an AWS service.

Zone-dependent NAT. During a zone event, private subnets in healthy zones lose internet access because their default route points at the dead zone's NAT gateway. Fix with per-zone NAT or regional mode, and test with a zone evacuation.

NACL asymmetry. Some clients work and others time out after a NACL change; flow logs show REJECT on return traffic to high ports. Fix the ephemeral-port rules.

Security group drift. Someone adds 0.0.0.0/0 on port 22 "temporarily". Detect with config rules or a policy-as-code check in the infrastructure pipeline, and use Systems Manager Session Manager instead of SSH so port 22 needn't be open at all.

Surprise data-transfer bills. The bill grows faster than traffic. Break down the Cost and Usage Report by usage type (DataTransfer-Regional-Bytes, NatGateway-Bytes, TransitGateway-Bytes, DataTransfer-Out-Bytes) and join VPC flow logs with zone IDs to find the talkers.

DNS throttling. Bursts of SERVFAIL or timeouts from busy hosts because of the 1,024 packets per second per interface limit. Cache DNS locally.

Tools

VPC flow logs (accept/reject records per interface, with fields such as source and destination zone IDs in custom formats), Reachability Analyzer (can A reach B, and which rule blocks it), Network Access Analyzer (which paths exist from the internet to sensitive subnets), CloudWatch metrics for NAT, TGW and load balancers, and the Cost and Usage Report for transfer.

The case: Slack, 4 January 2021

Slack's engineering write-up describes the first working day of 2021. Over the holidays traffic is low; on the first Monday back, client caches are cold and clients download more than usual, so Slack goes overnight from its quietest time of year to one of its biggest days. Slack's VPCs were connected through AWS Transit Gateways. The TGWs are meant to scale transparently, but one did not scale fast enough and began dropping packets. Error rates crept up; at 6:57 AM PST, 99% of messages were being sent successfully against a normal rate above 99.999%. The packet loss made web servers wait longer on their backends, and the 7:00 AM traffic peak saturated the web tier: Slack became unavailable.

The recovery tools shared the failing path. Dashboards and alerting depended on the TGWs, so the team's visibility was degraded. Autoscaling tried to add 1,200 servers to the web tier between 7:01 and 7:15 AM, but the provisioning service hit its own limits (the Linux open-files limit and AWS quotas) while talking to its dependencies over the degraded network, so most never came into service. By around 9:15 AM Slack was degraded rather than down. AWS engineers manually increased TGW capacity; by 10:40 AM PST the change had rolled out across all zones and error rates and latency returned to normal. Slack's follow-ups included moving dashboard services into the same VPC as their data so they don't cross the TGW, load-testing the provisioning service regularly, and asking AWS to pre-scale TGWs ahead of holidays; AWS said it was reviewing the TGW scaling algorithms for large packet-per-second increases.

For a designer, the incident shows four things from this lesson at once: managed network components have scaling curves; a hub every VPC depends on is a shared failure point; monitoring and recovery tooling must not share fate with what they fix; and scaling up during a network problem multiplies load on the network.

Estimate and fix: the network line on the bill

An ML platform in us-east-1 runs a training cluster in private subnets across three zones with a single NAT gateway in AZ 1. Each month it reads 80 TB of training data from S3, pulls 3 TB of container images from a public registry, and writes 6 TB of checkpoints to S3. Its inference service sends 4 TB a month of responses to users over the internet through an Application Load Balancer, and replicas in different zones exchange 20 TB a month of feature-store traffic. Estimate the monthly network-related charges, then propose changes and the new estimate. (Use 1 TB = 1,024 GB; ignore load-balancer capacity units.)

Answer

Current state. NAT hours: 730 × $0.045 = $32.85. NAT processing: S3 reads and writes plus images go through NAT: (80 + 6 + 3) × 1,024 = 91,136 GB × $0.045 = $4,101.12. Cross-zone to reach the single NAT: two-thirds of the cluster is in other zones, so about 2/3 × 91,136 ≈ 60,757 GB crosses zones, billed $0.01 leaving one zone and $0.01 arriving in the other: 60,757 × $0.02 ≈ $1,215.14. Feature-store cross-zone: 20 × 1,024 = 20,480 GB × $0.02 (out and in) = $409.60. Internet egress for responses: (4,096 − 100) GB × $0.09 = $359.64.
