A team building a document-processing product needs somewhere to run four things: an HTTP API with steady traffic, a nightly job that re-indexes everything, a webhook receiver that is idle for hours and then gets ten thousand calls in a minute, and an OCR model that needs a GPU. Asked "where should this run?", the team picks what it knows. It puts everything on a handful of large virtual machines, sized for the webhook burst and the GPU model, running all month.

The bill arrives. The GPU sits at 4% average utilisation because inference only happens during business hours. The API servers idle at 15% CPU because they were sized for a peak that happens twice a year. The nightly job takes six hours because it runs on the same machines and has to share them. The webhook burst still drops requests because the burst arrives faster than anything was sized for. The team is paying for capacity that does nothing most of the time while still failing at the moments that matter.

Cloud compute isn't one product. It's a menu that runs from renting a specific machine by the second, to renting spare machines at a steep discount that can be taken back with two minutes' notice, to handing over a container image and letting the platform decide how many copies to run, to handing over a function and paying per call. Each option prices a different shape of load well and another shape badly. Choosing well is mostly about matching the option to the shape of each workload — steady, spiky, interruptible, long-running, GPU-bound — and knowing the mechanics underneath well enough to predict what happens at the peak.

This lesson builds that judgement. It explains how to read an instance type and what a vCPU really is, how burstable instances and Arm processors change the arithmetic, how spot capacity works and how to survive its interruptions, how autoscaling groups react to load and how long that takes, what container platforms (ECS, Fargate, EKS, Cloud Run) take off your hands, where functions fit (cl5 goes deep), and how to size and price a real fleet by hand. It ends with the decision table, and with a 2026 case where Honeycomb measured the gain from one instance generation to the next.

Think about how a city moves people. You can buy a car: it's yours, always available, you choose everything, and it costs money even when parked. You can lease one for three years at a discount. You can rent from a company's surplus fleet at a fraction of the price, on the condition that they can take the car back with two minutes' warning. You can take a taxi, paying only per trip but more per kilometre. Or you can ride a bus that runs on its own schedule. No one option is best; a delivery business, a commuter and a tourist each choose differently, and a sensible household combines several.

Cloud compute has the same structure. A virtual machine (an instance on AWS) is the car: you pick its exact type, run anything on it, and pay per second while it runs. Commitments (savings plans, reserved instances) are the lease. Spot capacity is the surplus fleet: the same machines at a large discount, reclaimable with short notice. Containers on a managed platform are closer to a chauffeur service: you say what to run and how much CPU and memory it needs, and the platform finds the machines. Functions are the taxi: per trip (per request), nothing when idle, more per unit of work at high volume.

Two forces decide which option fits a workload. The first is the shape of the load: steady load favours capacity you keep and fill (VMs or containers with commitments); spiky or rare load favours capacity you pay for only while used (functions, scale-to-zero containers); interruptible load favours spot. The second is what you'd rather spend: engineering time on running platforms, or money on the provider doing it for you. A team of four usually should spend money; a company running ten thousand cores usually should spend engineering time.

One number ties the whole menu together: cost per unit of work — dollars per million requests, per thousand documents, per training step. Hourly prices can't be compared across models (an instance-hour, a vCPU-hour of Fargate and a GB-second of Lambda measure different things), but every option can be turned into cost per unit once you know how much work one unit of capacity does at your target latency. The rest of this lesson is about measuring that work, predicting how capacity follows load, and choosing the option whose cost per unit is lowest for each load shape — while still surviving the failures from cl1.

Where the analogy breaks: unlike a car park, cloud capacity isn't infinite. When everyone wants the same GPU, or every customer fails over into the same zone, launches fail with "insufficient capacity" even though you're willing to pay. Owning (reserving) capacity matters more in the cloud than the "rent everything on demand" story suggests, especially for scarce instance types.

1. Reading an instance type

AWS names instance types like m7g.xlarge. The parts:

  • Family letter. m general purpose (4 GiB of memory per vCPU), c compute-optimised (2 GiB per vCPU), r memory-optimised (8 GiB per vCPU), x and u very large memory, i and d local NVMe storage, t burstable, p and g NVIDIA GPUs (p for training-class, g for graphics and inference-class), inf and trn AWS's Inferentia and Trainium chips.
  • Generation. 6, 7, 8, 9. Newer generations bring newer processors, usually better price-performance.
  • Attributes. g Graviton (Arm), i Intel, a AMD, d local NVMe disks, n enhanced networking, e extra memory or storage, flex a cheaper variant that doesn't guarantee full CPU all the time.
  • Size. medium, large (2 vCPUs), xlarge (4), 2xlarge (8)… each step doubles vCPUs, memory and usually network bandwidth; metal is the whole physical server.

So an m7g.xlarge is a 7th-generation general-purpose Graviton instance with 4 vCPUs and 16 GiB; a c7i.large is a 7th-generation compute-optimised Intel instance with 2 vCPUs and 4 GiB. Prices in us-east-1 on 7 October 2026 (Linux, on-demand, from the AWS price list):

2. What a vCPU is, and why Arm changes the arithmetic

A vCPU is what the instance sees as a CPU. On most Intel and AMD instance types it is one hardware thread: physical cores run two threads each through simultaneous multithreading (Intel calls it Hyper-Threading), and AWS's documentation states that each vCPU is a thread of an Intel Xeon or AMD EPYC core, except on T2 and Graviton types. On Graviton, each vCPU is a whole physical core. Two threads on one core share its execution units, so two x86 vCPUs do less than two cores' worth of work on CPU-bound code — the gain from the second thread is workload-dependent and often 10–30%, not 100%.

That's why per-vCPU prices mislead. An m7g.large costs 19% less per hour than an m7i.large ($0.0816 vs $0.1008) with the same 2 vCPUs and 8 GiB, but the real question is cost per unit of your work. The only reliable answer is to measure: run the same service on both under the same traffic and compare requests per second at the same latency, then divide price by throughput. Software must be built for arm64: most languages' runtimes and container base images support it, but native extensions, vendor binaries and code with x86-specific optimisations may not.

3. Burstable instances and CPU credits

Burstable instances (the t family) give a baseline share of CPU and let you bank unused time. The unit is the CPU credit: one vCPU at 100% for one minute (or one vCPU at 50% for two minutes). Each size earns credits at a fixed rate. From AWS's credit table (October 2026): a t4g.micro earns 12 credits an hour (10% baseline per vCPU), a t4g.small and t4g.medium 24 (20%), a t4g.large 36 (30%). Below baseline, credits accumulate up to a day's worth; above it, they're spent.

Work through a t4g.small (2 vCPUs, 24 credits an hour). At baseline it can use 24 vCPU-minutes per hour: 2 vCPUs × 20% × 60 minutes. A small web app idles at 5% on both vCPUs for 20 hours (spending 2 × 0.05 × 60 = 6 credits an hour, banking 18 an hour, capped at 576) and then runs at 70% for 4 hours (spending 84 an hour, earning 24, a net 60 an hour, 240 over four hours). The bank covers it: no throttling, no extra charge. Change the pattern to 50% for 24 hours (60 credits an hour spent, 24 earned) and the bank drains. In standard mode the instance falls to baseline when the bank is empty, which looks like a sudden slowdown; in unlimited mode (the default for T3 and T4g) it keeps running and, if its 24-hour average exceeds baseline, the surplus is billed at a flat rate per vCPU-hour. Burstable instances are excellent for spiky, low-average work and a trap for steady work.

4. Pricing models in brief (cl12 goes deep)

The same instance can be bought three ways. On-demand: the list price, per second for Linux with a one-minute minimum, no commitment. Commitments: a one- or three-year promise in exchange for a discount; for example the m7g.large's one-year, no-upfront reserved rate was $0.054 an hour against $0.0816 on demand (34% off) on 7 October 2026. Spot: spare capacity at a variable discount — AWS says up to 90% off on-demand — which AWS can reclaim. A typical production pattern covers the steady baseline with commitments, the daily swing with on-demand, and interruptible work with spot.

5. Spot capacity and interruptions

Spot instances are the provider's unused capacity. Prices move slowly with long-term supply and demand in each capacity pool (an instance type in a zone), and when the provider needs the capacity back it interrupts spot instances in that pool. On AWS you get an interruption notice two minutes before the instance is stopped or terminated, delivered as an EC2 Spot Instance Interruption Warning event in EventBridge and as an instance-action item in instance metadata (/latest/meta-data/spot/instance-action, which returns 404 until an interruption is scheduled; AWS recommends checking every five seconds). Notices are best effort. A rebalance recommendation can arrive earlier, signalling elevated risk.

Other providers differ in detail. Google Cloud's Spot VMs have no maximum runtime and, per the current documentation, a configurable preemption notice of 0 seconds (the default) or 120 seconds, followed by a best-effort shutdown period of up to 30 seconds; Google advertises discounts of up to 91%. Azure Spot VMs give a short eviction notice through Scheduled Events.

Designing for spot comes down to four habits:

  1. Diversify. Allow many instance types (say m7g, m7i, m6i, c7g, c7i, r7g in two or three sizes) in all zones. More pools means more available capacity and fewer simultaneous interruptions.
  2. Choose capacity over price. Use a capacity-optimised allocation strategy (on AWS, price-capacity-optimized), which launches from pools with the most spare capacity, not merely the cheapest.
  3. Handle the notice. On notice: deregister from the load balancer, stop pulling new work, checkpoint or finish in-flight work within two minutes. Kubernetes node termination handlers and Karpenter do this for pods.
  4. Keep a floor. Run enough on-demand capacity for what must not be interrupted and to absorb a spot shortfall.

6. Autoscaling groups, traced

An autoscaling group (ASG) manages a fleet of instances from a launch template (image, instance types, network, security groups, IAM role, user data). You set a minimum, maximum and desired count, and the subnets (zones) to use. The group:

  • launches or terminates instances to match the desired count, balancing across zones;
  • replaces instances that fail EC2 status checks or, if configured, load-balancer health checks (after a health check grace period);
  • changes the desired count through scaling policies: target tracking (keep average CPU at 50%, or requests per target at 1,000), step (add 4 instances when CPU exceeds 70%, 8 above 85%), scheduled (set desired to 30 at 08:00 on weekdays) and predictive (forecast from history and scale ahead);
  • runs lifecycle hooks that pause launches or terminations so you can warm caches or drain connections;
  • supports mixed instances (several types, on-demand base plus spot), warm pools (pre-initialised stopped instances that start in seconds) and instance refresh (rolling replacement to a new launch template).

Trace a spike. A target-tracking policy keeps average CPU at 50% on a group of 10 instances. At 12:00:00 traffic jumps 60%; CPU climbs to 80%. CloudWatch's alarm (created by the policy) needs its evaluation periods to breach — with one-minute metrics, typically by about 12:02. The group raises desired capacity to 16 and calls RunInstances. Instances boot by 12:03, user data installs the agent and pulls the application image by 12:04, the load balancer marks them healthy after two passed health checks at 12:04:30, and their metrics count after the warm-up period. From 12:00 to about 12:05 the original 10 instances carried 160% of the old load at 80% CPU — fine, because the target left 50% headroom. Had the target been 75%, they'd have been at 120% demand: queueing, latency and errors.

Scaling in is deliberately slower (to avoid flapping), and instances should drain: the load balancer's deregistration delay lets in-flight requests finish before termination.

7. Containers on managed platforms

Containers (k8s1–k8s3) package an application with its dependencies as an image. The cloud question is who runs the machines and the scheduler.

Amazon ECS is AWS's own orchestrator. A task definition lists containers, CPU and memory, ports, an IAM task role and log configuration; a service keeps N tasks running behind a load balancer, does rolling deployments and integrates with autoscaling. Capacity comes from capacity providers: your own EC2 autoscaling groups, Fargate, or Fargate Spot. There's no fee for ECS itself.

Fargate runs each task (or EKS pod) in its own isolated micro-VM sized to what you request, billed per second for vCPU and memory. In us-east-1 on 7 October 2026: $0.04048 per vCPU-hour and $0.004445 per GB-hour on x86, $0.03238 and $0.00356 on Arm. A 1 vCPU, 2 GB x86 task costs $0.04048 + 2 × $0.004445 = $0.04937 an hour, about $36 a month. You never patch a host or scale a cluster, and each task is isolated from others by a VM boundary rather than a shared kernel. The trade-offs: a premium over equivalent EC2 capacity, slower task start than a container on a warm node (image pulls on every start), no GPUs, no privileged containers or daemonsets.

Amazon EKS, GKE and AKS are managed Kubernetes: the provider runs the control plane (API server, etcd, scheduler) and you supply worker capacity — managed node groups, self-managed autoscaling groups, Karpenter-provisioned nodes, Fargate, or provider "auto" modes where the provider also manages the nodes for an extra per-instance fee. EKS charged $0.10 per cluster-hour (about $73 a month) in October 2026. Kubernetes is worth it for its ecosystem, portability across clouds, and fine-grained scheduling (k8s6–k8s14); it costs upgrade work every few months and add-ons to run.

Request-driven container platforms — Google Cloud Run, Azure Container Apps, and on AWS App Runner — sit closest to functions. You deploy an image; the platform runs instances in response to requests, each instance handling many concurrent requests (Cloud Run's per-instance concurrency is configurable, with a default of 80), scales out with load, and can scale to zero when idle, with a cold start when traffic resumes. Billing is per instance time, by default only while handling requests. They're excellent for HTTP services with uneven traffic and small teams.

8. Functions, briefly

Functions as a service — AWS Lambda, Google Cloud Run functions, Azure Functions — run your code once per event (an HTTP request, a queue message, a file upload, a schedule), each execution environment handling one request at a time on Lambda, scaling out by adding environments, and billing per request plus per GB-second of memory-time. In us-east-1 on 7 October 2026, Lambda cost $0.20 per million requests and $0.0000166667 per GB-second on x86. A function with 512 MB running 100 ms costs 0.5 × 0.1 × $0.0000166667 ≈ $0.00000083 per call plus $0.0000002 for the request — about $1.03 per million calls. Functions are the right default for glue and spiky event handling and the wrong one for steady heavy traffic, long jobs (Lambda's maximum is 15 minutes) and latency-critical paths sensitive to cold starts. cl5 is entirely about this execution model; here it just takes its place in the table.

9. Sizing and pricing a fleet by hand

An internal search API is load-tested on m7g instances: each request uses 12 ms of CPU. Traffic peaks at 3,000 requests per second for about four hours on weekdays, averages 1,800, and falls to 400 at night. It must survive the loss of one of three zones without needing to launch anything.

CPU demand. At peak: 3,000 × 0.012 s = 36 vCPU-seconds per second — 36 vCPUs fully busy. Average: 1,800 × 0.012 = 21.6. Night: 4.8.

Zone-loss sizing (static stability, cl1). If one zone disappears at peak, the other two must carry 36 vCPUs of work. Allowing 85% utilisation in that emergency: 36 / 0.85 ≈ 42.4 vCPUs across two zones, so at least 21.2 per zone. In m7g.xlarge units (4 vCPUs): 6 per zone (24 vCPUs), 18 instances, 72 vCPUs. In normal operation at peak they run at 36 / 72 = 50%.

That's a business decision, not an engineering one. The point of the exercise isn't the exact numbers, which change; it's that the cheapest option depends on load shape, availability requirements and what you'll commit to.

Capacity is a market with physical limits

On-demand capacity in a zone is finite per instance type. When demand for a type exceeds the free hosts — a new GPU, a regional event shifting traffic, a big customer's launch — RunInstances fails with an insufficient-capacity error regardless of your quota. Three tools manage this. Flexibility: accept several types and sizes and all zones, so the scheduler can find something. Reservations: on-demand capacity reservations hold capacity in a zone whether you use it or not (you pay for it either way), and capacity blocks reserve GPU clusters for fixed windows. Static stability: run what you'd need after a failure before the failure, because during a regional event everyone is launching at once. For GPU workloads, capacity rather than price is often the binding constraint (cl14).

Why spot interruption rates vary so much

Interruptions happen when the provider needs a pool's capacity for on-demand or reserved customers. A pool that is a popular type in a busy zone is reclaimed often; an older generation in a quiet zone rarely. Running a single type in one zone concentrates your risk in one pool, so a demand spike there interrupts your whole fleet at once — the worst case for a service. Spreading across 15–20 pools makes simultaneous interruption of a large fraction unlikely and lets capacity-aware allocation steer launches away from stressed pools. This is the same diversification argument as in finance, and it's why "spot is unreliable" usually means "spot with one instance type is unreliable".

Bin-packing and the hidden cost of orchestrators

Containers promise efficiency through packing many small workloads onto large nodes. The promise is kept only if requests are accurate and the node autoscaler can find good fits. Real clusters lose capacity to fragmentation (a node with 1.5 free vCPUs can't place a 2-vCPU pod), per-node overheads (kubelet and system reservations, daemonsets for logging, metrics and networking on every node), zone minimums and pods that block consolidation. Measured "requested over allocatable" ratios of 50–70% are common. Karpenter-style autoscalers improve this by choosing instance types to fit pending pods and actively consolidating underused nodes, at the cost of more node churn — which interacts with long-running pods and disruption budgets.

Fargate isolation is a security property, not just convenience

On a shared node, containers share a kernel; a kernel exploit from one pod can reach the others (os13, k8s1). Fargate runs every task or pod in its own micro-VM (AWS built Firecracker for Lambda and Fargate-style isolation), so the isolation boundary is a hypervisor rather than namespaces. For multi-tenant platforms running customer code — notebooks, agents executing code — this boundary is often worth the price premium by itself, or worth reproducing with sandboxed runtimes (gVisor, Kata Containers) on your own nodes.

Arm migration is mostly a build-system problem

Moving to Graviton rarely needs application changes in Go, Java, Python or Node. The work is in the pipeline: multi-architecture container images (one tag pointing at amd64 and arm64 variants, k8s5), CI runners for both architectures, native dependencies with arm64 builds, and observability that can compare architectures side by side. Honeycomb's 2021 retrospective describes the change as transparent to developers once CI produced multi-architecture artifacts. Once that's in place, every future generation — and every switch between providers' Arm and x86 offerings — becomes a scheduling decision rather than a project.

Graceful shutdown is part of the compute contract

Every elastic option removes capacity as well as adding it: scale-in, spot interruptions, deployments, instance refreshes, Fargate task replacements, node consolidation. Each sends a termination signal and then, after a grace period, kills the process. Services that ignore SIGTERM drop in-flight requests every time capacity shrinks, which on an autoscaled fleet is many times a day. The contract is the same everywhere: on SIGTERM stop accepting new work, let the load balancer deregister you (deregistration delay), finish or hand off in-flight work, flush buffers, then exit — within the platform's window (two minutes for spot, 30 seconds by default for Kubernetes pods, configurable for ECS tasks). Getting this right is what makes cheap, elastic capacity safe to use.

Right-sizing is continuous because software changes

The instance size that was right at launch drifts: new features add CPU per request, a library upgrade changes memory use, traffic mix shifts. Treat sizing as a measured loop like least privilege (cl2): collect CPU, memory and network per instance and per request, use provider recommendations (AWS Compute Optimizer, GKE's recommendations, VPA in Kubernetes) as input, and test changes as canaries. Over-sizing wastes money quietly; under-sizing shows up only at peak.

Failure modes

Insufficient capacity at launch. Scale-out or a deploy fails with InsufficientInstanceCapacity for a type in a zone. Symptoms: ASG activity history full of failed launches, pods pending in Kubernetes. Fix: more instance types and zones in the launch template or node pool, capacity reservations for critical baselines.

Quota limits. The account's vCPU quota for on-demand or spot instances in a region stops scaling. AWS quotas for EC2 are per instance family group in vCPUs; GPU families often start at zero. Monitor usage against quotas and raise them ahead of growth.

Scale-out too slow. Latency and errors during traffic ramps despite autoscaling. Typical causes: boot-time installation, large images, long health check intervals, target utilisation set too high. Fix with baked images, warm pools, predictive or scheduled scaling, lower targets.

Mass spot interruption. Many instances interrupted together because the fleet used one pool. Fix by diversifying and keeping an on-demand floor.

Burst credit exhaustion. A t-instance service slows to a crawl after a long busy period (standard mode) or the bill shows surplus credit charges (unlimited). Alert on CPUCreditBalance.

Noisy deploys. Instance refresh or a rolling deployment drops capacity below what peak needs. Set minimum healthy percentages, deploy outside peaks, and check the deployment's effect on capacity before starting.

Image and AMI drift. Instances launched months apart from different images behave differently; a scale-out brings up machines with an old kernel or agent. Rebuild images on a schedule and use instance refresh to roll fleets onto them.

What to measure

CPU, memory and network per instance and aggregated per service; cost per thousand requests (the number that tells you whether a change helped); scale-out latency (time from alarm to healthy capacity); launch failures by reason; spot interruptions per day by pool; cluster efficiency (requested/allocatable and used/requested); quota utilisation.

The case: Honeycomb moves from Graviton4 to Graviton5 (June 2026)

Honeycomb, an observability company, published per-service results of moving shared production compute from m8g.8xlarge (Graviton4) to m9g.8xlarge (Graviton5). The method is the lesson: both generations ran concurrently in the same Kubernetes namespace for 60 days, serving the same traffic, and Honeycomb compared per-pod metrics. Every measured service used between 11% and 26% less CPU on Graviton5; compute-bound services 16–22%. Ingest P99 latency dropped 28%; one metrics-ingest pipeline's P99 halved from 880 to 440 microseconds; tail-based sampling queues were 44–73% shorter at P95. The post doesn't give cost figures.

Price it yourself. On 7 October 2026 the AWS price list showed $1.56544 per hour for an m9g.8xlarge and $1.43616 for an m8g.8xlarge in us-east-1, a 9.0% premium. If a service's fleet is sized by CPU and needs 20% less, the cost ratio is 1.09 × 0.80 ≈ 0.87: about 13% cheaper for the same work, with better tail latency. For a service whose CPU fell only 11%, it's 1.09 × 0.89 ≈ 0.97, a smaller saving; for a memory-bound service, the newer instance may simply cost more. That spread is why per-service measurement on real traffic beats a single benchmark. The same company's 2021 Graviton2 retrospective shows the enabling work: multi-architecture CI and standardised service tooling made the move "push-button" for most services.

Design and estimate: four workloads, one platform

Return to the document-processing team from the start of the lesson. Their workloads: (1) an HTTP API at 150 requests per second steady during business hours, 20 at night, 30 ms of CPU per request; (2) a nightly re-index of 2 million documents taking 8 vCPU-hours of work per 100,000 documents, restartable per batch of 10,000; (3) a webhook receiver idle most of the day with bursts of 10,000 calls in a minute, each needing 50 ms and a write to a database; (4) an OCR model on one NVIDIA L4 GPU used 9 hours on weekdays. Choose compute for each, justify it, and rough out monthly cost using prices from this lesson (and g6.xlarge at $0.8048/hour on-demand for the L4).

Answer

1. API. Peak CPU: 150 × 0.03 = 4.5 vCPUs. Fargate Arm with three tasks of 2 vCPU / 4 GB across zones in business hours, scaling to two tasks at night (zone redundancy). Daytime: 3 × (2 × $0.03238 + 4 × $0.00356) = $0.2370/hour; night two tasks at $0.1580. Roughly 10 h × 22 days at day rate plus the rest at night rate: 220 × $0.2370 + 510 × $0.1580 ≈ $52 + $81 = ~$133 a month. No hosts to run; a small team's best trade.

2. Re-index. 2,000,000 / 100,000 × 8 = 160 vCPU-hours a night. Spot instances via AWS Batch with 20 batches of 100,000 documents (each further split into restartable batches of 10,000), diversified across c7g/m7g/c7i/m7i sizes and all zones. On on-demand c7g.large ($0.0725/hour for 2 vCPUs) it would be 80 instance-hours ≈ $5.80 a night, ~$174 a month; spot typically cuts that substantially. Make each batch idempotent.

3. Webhooks. Lambda behind a function URL or API Gateway, writing each event to a queue (SQS) rather than straight to the database; a consumer drains the queue at a rate the database can take (cl5, cl8). 10,000 calls/min at 50 ms means ~8 concurrent executions on average during the burst, well within limits. Cost is tiny: even 5 million calls a month at 256 MB and 50 ms is 5 × $0.20 + 5,000,000 × 0.25 × 0.05 × $0.0000166667 ≈ $1.00 + $1.04 ≈ $2.

4. OCR. One g6.xlarge on a schedule: started at 08:30 and stopped at 17:30 on weekdays, ~9 × 22 = 198 hours × $0.8048 ≈ $159 a month, against $588 running all month. If start-up time or capacity is a concern, keep it running but consider a reserved rate; if usage is very bursty, batch requests through a queue.

Total: on the order of $500 a month, against a fleet of always-on large VMs that would cost several times that. Shared practices keep four models manageable: one IaC repository, one logging and metrics pipeline, one deployment workflow.
