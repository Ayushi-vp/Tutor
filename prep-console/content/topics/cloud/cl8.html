An online shop starts with one service that does everything when a customer clicks "buy": charge the card, reserve inventory, send the confirmation email, update the analytics dashboard, tell the warehouse. Each step is a synchronous call. On Black Friday the email provider slows to five seconds per call, and checkout — which waits for it — times out. Customers retry; some are charged twice. The analytics service goes down for a deploy, and orders fail because the dashboard update threw an exception.

The team splits the work with messaging. Checkout now records the order and publishes "order placed"; everything else happens asynchronously. Then a second set of lessons arrives. The inventory consumer sometimes reserves stock twice, because a message was delivered twice. A malformed order message fails forever and blocks a FIFO queue. The warehouse integration, wired through an event router, silently drops events for a day while its endpoint is down, because nobody configured a dead-letter queue. And a well-meaning engineer orchestrates every clickstream event through a workflow service, producing a bill that dwarfs the compute it coordinates.

The cloud offers a set of managed integration services — queues, topics, event buses, streams and workflow engines — and they are what turn a tangle of synchronous calls into a system whose parts can fail, slow down and scale independently. sc10 explained the general concepts (queues versus logs, delivery semantics, idempotency, backoff). This lesson is about the concrete services: what each one actually guarantees about ordering, duplicates, retries and retention, how they're priced, how they fail, and how to combine them.

It covers SQS (standard and FIFO), SNS, EventBridge, Kinesis Data Streams and managed Kafka, and Step Functions, puts their semantics side by side, prices the same event volume on each, and works through the patterns — fan-out, buffering, routing, orchestration — that real systems use. The case is the November 2020 Kinesis outage in us-east-1, which shows what happens when the integration layer itself fails.

Think of a company's internal mail system, which has four different services.

The in-tray on a team's shared desk: anyone drops work in, whichever team member is free takes the top item, and once done they throw it away. If someone takes an item and goes home sick without finishing, it reappears in the tray after a while for someone else. That's a queue (SQS). Each item is handled by one worker; the tray holds work when everyone's busy.

The internal newsletter: one memo, photocopied to everyone on the distribution list the moment it's sent. If someone's office is locked, the mailroom keeps trying for a while and then gives up. That's a topic (SNS) — fan-out by push, with no backlog kept for latecomers.

The mailroom sorter: every envelope is read, and rules decide where it goes — "invoices over $10,000 to finance and to the CFO", "anything from the bank to treasury". New rules can be added without touching senders. That's an event bus (EventBridge) — routing by content.

The company journal: a bound ledger where every event is written in order and kept for a set time. Anyone can read it from any page, at their own pace, and different departments keep their own bookmarks. That's a stream (Kinesis, Kafka) — an ordered, replayable log with independent readers.

Add a fifth service: a project manager with a checklist who drives a multi-step process — "charge the card; if that works, reserve stock; if that fails, refund" — and remembers where every process is, even across days. That's a workflow orchestrator (Step Functions).

Two truths run under all of them. First, every one of them delivers at least once somewhere: a message can arrive twice, so whatever consumes it must be able to handle duplicates. Second, ordering is never global: queues and topics are unordered unless you use FIFO variants with a group key, and streams are ordered only within a shard or partition. Designs that assume "exactly once, in order" fail in production on the first retry.

Choosing among them starts with three questions about each flow of data. Does each item need exactly one handler, or does every interested party need its own copy? Does anyone need to read the history again later, or in strict order per customer or device? And how many items per second will flow, steadily and at peak? The answers point to a queue, a topic, a bus or a stream — often to a combination, such as a topic whose subscribers are queues, or a stream whose consumers write to queues for slower downstream work. The lesson works through each service's mechanics and then answers the third question with prices.

Where the analogy breaks: the company mail never charges per envelope. Cloud messaging does — per request, per event, per state transition, per gigabyte — and the price per message differs by more than ten times between services. At a few events per second it doesn't matter; at thousands per second the choice of transport is a cost decision as much as a design one.

1. SQS standard queues

A producer calls SendMessage (or SendMessageBatch with up to 10 messages); SQS stores the message redundantly. Consumers call ReceiveMessage, which returns up to 10 messages and starts each one's visibility timeout — 30 seconds by default, configurable from 0 to 12 hours — during which no other consumer sees it. The consumer processes the message and calls DeleteMessage with the receipt handle. If it doesn't delete in time, the message becomes visible again and its receive count goes up. Limits from the SQS documentation (October 2026): messages up to 1 MiB (larger payloads go in S3 with a pointer, which the extended client libraries automate), retention from 60 seconds to 14 days with a default of 4 days, delivery delays up to 15 minutes, long polling up to 20 seconds, and nearly unlimited throughput per standard queue.

Standard queues promise at-least-once delivery and best-effort ordering: a message can occasionally be delivered more than once even without failures, and order isn't guaranteed. That's the price of their scalability: the queue is spread across many servers, and a receive samples some of them. Long polling (WaitTimeSeconds up to 20) makes a receive wait for messages instead of returning empty, which cuts empty receives — and request charges — dramatically.

Dead-letter queues handle messages that keep failing. A redrive policy on the source queue names a DLQ and a maxReceiveCount; when a message has been received that many times without being deleted, SQS moves it to the DLQ. A redrive operation later moves messages back once the bug is fixed. Note the interaction with retention: a message moved to a DLQ keeps its original enqueue timestamp, so the DLQ's retention should be longer than the source's.

Trace a failure. A consumer with a 30-second visibility timeout receives an order message at 12:00:00, starts calling a slow payment API, and is still waiting at 12:00:30. The message becomes visible; a second consumer receives it at 12:00:31 and starts the same work. At 12:00:40 the first consumer finishes, charges the card and deletes — but its receipt handle is stale, and the second consumer charges the card again. Two fixes, both needed: a visibility timeout longer than the worst case (or extend it with ChangeMessageVisibility while working), and an idempotency key (the order ID) checked before charging.

2. SQS FIFO queues

FIFO queues (names ending .fifo) add two guarantees. Ordering: messages with the same message group ID are delivered in the order sent, and while a message from a group is in flight, no later message from that group is delivered — so one group is processed strictly sequentially, while different groups proceed in parallel. Deduplication: a send with a deduplication ID (explicit, or a SHA-256 of the body if content-based deduplication is on) that was already sent within the last five minutes is accepted but not delivered again — protecting against producer retries.

Throughput is the trade. Without high-throughput mode, a FIFO queue supports 300 transactions per second per API action per partition, 3,000 messages per second with batches of 10. High-throughput mode raises this substantially by partitioning on message group ID — in October 2026 up to 70,000 transactions per second (700,000 messages per second with batching) in us-east-1, us-west-2 and eu-west-1, and less in other regions. Parallelism is bounded by the number of active message groups: one group is one lane.

FIFO doesn't give exactly-once processing: a consumer that crashes after acting but before deleting will see the message again after the visibility timeout. It gives exactly-once enqueueing within the deduplication window and ordering per group. A poison message also blocks its group until it reaches the DLQ, which is why FIFO redrive counts are kept low.

3. SNS: fan-out by push

An SNS topic has subscriptions: SQS queues, Lambda functions, HTTP/S endpoints, email, SMS, mobile push, Firehose. A publish is pushed to every subscription. Subscriptions can have filter policies on message attributes or body fields, so a subscriber receives only, say, orders where country = "DE". Messages are up to 256 KB.

SNS doesn't keep a backlog. It retries delivery according to a delivery policy per protocol: for SQS and Lambda, the documentation lists 3 immediate retries, 2 one second apart, 10 with exponential backoff up to 20 seconds, then 100,000 at 20-second intervals — 100,015 attempts over 23 days; for email, SMS and mobile push, 50 attempts over 6 hours; for HTTP/S a configurable policy (total retry time capped at one hour) that by default makes few attempts. After the policy is exhausted, the message is discarded unless the subscription has a DLQ. Retries apply to 5xx and 429 errors; other errors are permanent failures.

That's why the most common pattern is SNS to SQS fan-out: each consuming service owns an SQS queue subscribed to the topic. SNS delivers to SQS quickly and reliably; each queue then gives its consumer a durable buffer, independent pace, retries and a DLQ. A slow analytics consumer accumulates a backlog in its own queue without affecting email or inventory. SNS FIFO topics deliver to SQS FIFO queues with ordering per message group and deduplication, for fan-out that must preserve order.

4. EventBridge: routing events by content

EventBridge receives events — JSON documents with standard fields (source, detail-type, time, detail) — on an event bus. AWS services publish to the default bus (an EC2 instance changing state, a file landing in S3, a CodePipeline stage failing); your applications publish to custom buses; SaaS partners publish to partner buses. Rules match events with event patterns and deliver them to targets (Lambda, SQS, SNS, Step Functions, Kinesis, API destinations that call HTTP endpoints, other buses, and more), with optional input transformation. A pattern like

{ "source": ["com.acme.orders"],
  "detail-type": ["OrderPlaced"],
  "detail": { "total": [ { "numeric": [ ">", 10000 ] } ], "country": ["DE", "FR"] } }

routes only large German and French orders. Producers don't know about consumers; adding a consumer is adding a rule.

Delivery is at-least-once to each target, without ordering. If a target fails with a retriable error, EventBridge retries with exponential backoff and jitter for 24 hours and up to 185 attempts by default, then drops the event unless the target has a DLQ (an SQS queue). Archives keep events for replay — useful for rebuilding a consumer's state or testing a new one against yesterday's traffic. Schema discovery infers schemas from events on a bus. EventBridge Pipes connect a single source (SQS, Kinesis, DynamoDB Streams, Kafka) to a single target with filtering and enrichment, replacing glue code. EventBridge Scheduler runs one-off or recurring invocations at scale.

EventBridge is priced per event ($1.00 per million custom events in us-east-1, October 2026) and is built for routing business and operational events, not for very high-volume telemetry.

5. Streams: Kinesis Data Streams and managed Kafka

A stream is a durable, ordered log. In Kinesis Data Streams, a stream is made of shards. A producer writes a record with a partition key; Kinesis hashes the key to choose a shard, so all records with the same key land in the same shard, in order, each with a sequence number. Records stay for the retention period — 24 hours by default, extendable to 365 days — whether or not anyone has read them. Consumers read each shard from a position they track (a checkpoint), so several consumers can read the same stream independently, start from the beginning, or rewind.

Capacity in provisioned mode is per shard (quotas page, October 2026): writes up to 1 MB/s or 1,000 records/s; reads up to 2 MB/s and five GetRecords calls per second, shared by all standard consumers; records up to 10 MiB. Enhanced fan-out gives each registered consumer (up to 20, or 50 in the newer on-demand Advantage mode) its own 2 MB/s per shard pushed over HTTP/2, removing contention between consumers. On-demand mode scales shards automatically — new streams start at 4 MB/s write and 8 MB/s read and can grow to 10 GB/s write in the largest regions — and bills per GB written and read plus a stream-hour fee.

Managed Kafka (Amazon MSK, Confluent Cloud, Google Cloud's and Azure's offerings) gives the same log model with Kafka's API and ecosystem: topics divided into partitions, consumer groups sharing partitions, retention by time or size, and Kafka-specific features like log compaction and transactions for exactly-once read-process-write. MSK provisioned clusters bill for brokers and storage; MSK Serverless bills by usage. Choose Kafka when you need its ecosystem (Kafka Connect, Streams, existing clients) or portability; Kinesis when you want a fully managed AWS-native stream with minimal operations.

6. Step Functions: orchestration with state

A Step Functions state machine, written in the Amazon States Language (JSON), describes a workflow: tasks (call a Lambda function, write to DynamoDB, run an ECS task, call almost any AWS API), choices, parallel branches, maps over items, waits, retries with backoff, and catch blocks that route errors to compensation steps. The service keeps each execution's state between steps, so a workflow can wait for a callback — a human approval, a payment provider's webhook — for days.

Two lessons. First, request-priced services charge per call, so batching and long polling matter as much as the service choice: the same SQS workload is ten times cheaper batched. Second, at high steady volume a stream is an order of magnitude cheaper per event than routers and topics, because it charges for capacity and bytes, not per event. At 20 events per second, all of these cost a few dollars a month and features decide; at 2,000 per second, the bill does.

Why at-least-once is the default everywhere

Exactly-once delivery across a network is impossible to guarantee without coordination between sender and receiver: a sender that doesn't get an acknowledgement can't tell whether the message or the acknowledgement was lost, so it must either resend (risking duplicates) or not (risking loss). Managed services choose resending because losing messages is worse than duplicating them, and because duplicates can be made harmless downstream with idempotency. "Exactly-once" features — FIFO deduplication, Kafka transactions, Pub/Sub exactly-once delivery, Standard workflows — narrow the window within specific boundaries (a five-minute dedup window, a single Kafka cluster's read-process-write, one region), but the end-to-end effect is still your design.

Ordering costs parallelism

Ordering is enforced by funnelling related messages through one lane: one FIFO message group, one shard, one Kafka partition. Throughput for that key is then bounded by one lane's capacity and one consumer's processing speed, and a slow or failing message blocks everything behind it in the lane. The design question is always "ordered with respect to what?" Per customer or per account is usually enough and parallelises across customers; global order serialises the whole system and is almost never needed. When keys are skewed — one enormous customer — that customer's lane becomes the bottleneck, and the answer is sub-keys with application-level reordering, or accepting less strict order.

Push versus pull decides who absorbs bursts

SNS and EventBridge push: they deliver as fast as events arrive (up to target throttles) and retry when targets fail, so a burst lands directly on the targets. SQS and streams are pulled: consumers fetch at their own pace and the backlog absorbs the burst. Pushing into a fragile target (an HTTP endpoint, a Lambda function writing to a database) without a buffer is the common way to turn a traffic spike into an outage. Putting a queue between a push source and the work, and capping consumer concurrency, turns spikes into backlog, which is measurable and drains.

The outbox problem: publishing and committing atomically

A service that writes to its database and then publishes an event can crash between the two: the order exists but nobody hears about it, or (if publishing first) the event announces an order that was rolled back. The transactional outbox (sc11) writes the event into an outbox table in the same transaction and has a separate relay publish it; change data capture (DynamoDB Streams, PostgreSQL logical replication via Debezium or DMS) does the same by turning committed changes into events. EventBridge Pipes can consume DynamoDB Streams directly, which makes "publish exactly the committed changes" a configuration exercise on AWS.

All-to-all designs fail suddenly

The 2020 Kinesis event came from a design where every front-end server kept threads for every other front-end server: resource use per server grew with fleet size, so a small capacity addition pushed every server over a fixed OS limit at once. The same shape appears in application systems — full-mesh service discovery, every pod connecting to every broker, gossip protocols — and fails the same way: fine until a threshold, then everywhere simultaneously. Cells (independent copies of a service, each serving a slice of customers) bound the mesh size; AWS's remediation included cellularising the Kinesis front end for exactly this reason.

Backpressure has to reach the producer eventually

Buffers absorb bursts, but a sustained imbalance — producers faster than consumers for hours — only moves the problem: the backlog grows until retention deletes messages or the stream's write limits throttle producers. Healthy systems make the imbalance visible (age of oldest message, iterator age), scale consumers within downstream limits, and, when that isn't enough, push back on producers: Kinesis throttles writes above shard capacity, SQS doesn't, so producers to SQS need their own rate limits or load shedding for non-critical events. Deciding in advance which events may be dropped or sampled under pressure — analytics yes, payments no — is part of the design, not an incident decision.

Schemas are the contract between teams

Once events flow between teams, their shape is an API with many unknown consumers. Changing a field name breaks someone's rule or parser silently. Treat event schemas like APIs: version them, make changes backward compatible (add fields, never repurpose them), publish them in a registry (EventBridge schema registry, Confluent or Glue schema registries for Kafka and Kinesis), and validate in CI. Envelope fields — event ID, type, version, time, correlation ID — make idempotency, routing and tracing possible across the whole system.

Failure modes and their symptoms

Growing backlog. ApproximateNumberOfMessagesVisible and ApproximateAgeOfOldestMessage climb on a queue; iterator age climbs on a stream. Consumers are too slow or failing. Scale consumers (within downstream limits), find hot keys or shards, and make sure retention outlasts the backlog — messages older than retention are deleted silently.

Poison messages. One message fails forever; with FIFO or streams it blocks its group or shard. Fix: low maxReceiveCount with a DLQ, partial batch responses, bisect-on-error and maximum record age for stream consumers.

Duplicate side effects. Double emails or charges after retries, redeliveries or a visibility timeout shorter than processing time. Fix: idempotency keys, longer visibility timeouts, delete only after success.

Silent drops. EventBridge or SNS gives up after its retry policy while a target is down, and nobody configured a DLQ. Fix: DLQs on every target and subscription, alarms on DLQ depth and on failed invocation metrics.

Throttled targets. A rule or subscription pushes faster than a Lambda function or API can take; retries pile up. Fix: SQS in between, concurrency caps, SNS HTTP throttle policies.

Hot shards and groups. Throughput stuck well below provisioned capacity because traffic concentrates on a few keys. Find them with per-shard metrics; re-key or split.

Regional quota cliffs. A design that works in us-east-1 fails in another region because default quotas differ: in October 2026 the SNS publish quota per account was 30,000 messages per second in us-east-1 but 1,500 in Frankfurt and 300 in many smaller regions, and SQS FIFO high-throughput limits also vary by region. Check quotas for every region you deploy to, and request increases before launch.

Cost surprises. Unbatched SQS calls, empty receives without long polling, high-volume events routed through EventBridge, per-event Standard workflows. Break the bill down by service and usage type.

What to watch

Queues: visible messages, age of oldest message, messages in flight, DLQ depth, empty receives. Topics and buses: publishes, failed deliveries, throttles, DLQ depth, invocation latency. Streams: incoming bytes and records against limits, write and read throttles, iterator age per consumer, per-shard metrics. Workflows: executions started, failed, timed out, throttled; duration per state. End to end: the age of the event when it's finally processed.

The case: Kinesis in us-east-1, 25 November 2020

AWS's summary describes Kinesis's front-end fleet: servers that authenticate requests, throttle, and route each request to the right back-end cluster using a shard map that every front-end server builds and maintains. To keep that map current, each front-end server holds operating-system threads for communicating with each of the other front-end servers. Between 2:44 and 3:47 AM PST, a relatively small amount of capacity was added to the fleet. Because every server's thread count grows with the number of servers, the addition pushed every server past the maximum number of threads allowed by an OS configuration. Servers could no longer complete their shard maps, and from about 5:15 AM PST Kinesis requests in the region began failing.

The fix — remove the added capacity, raise limits — was quick to decide and slow to apply: rebuilding shard maps loads the fleet, so servers could only be brought back at a rate of a few hundred per hour across a fleet of many thousands. Kinesis fully recovered at 10:23 PM PST, about 17 hours after errors began. Meanwhile, AWS services built on Kinesis failed in turn: Cognito (which buffers data through Kinesis) returned errors; CloudWatch couldn't process metrics and logs; EventBridge and CloudWatch Events were delayed; Lambda saw elevated errors from memory pressure caused by buffered metrics; reactive Auto Scaling policies were delayed because they depend on CloudWatch metrics; and AWS's Service Health Dashboard, which used Cognito, couldn't be updated normally. AWS's remediations: move to larger servers so the fleet needs fewer, add fine-grained alarming on thread consumption, raise the OS limit, accelerate cellularisation of the front-end fleet, and let CloudWatch keep metrics locally for hours when Kinesis is unavailable.

For a designer, it's a compact lesson in integration risk. The messaging layer is a dependency of many things you don't think of as "using Kinesis" — including monitoring and autoscaling. Fleets with all-to-all relationships hit limits suddenly. Recovery speed can be limited by the system's own need to avoid overload. Producers need local buffering and backoff, consumers need retention long enough to catch up after a day-long outage, and critical alarms need a path that doesn't share the failing dependency.

Goal: simulate a queue with visibility timeouts and a dead-letter queue, then a sharded stream, and see duplicates, poison messages and hot shards — Python 3.12 standard library only. About 20 minutes; no cloud account needed.

  1. Queue. Write a class with send(body), receive(now, visibility) returning messages not currently hidden (setting visible_at = now + visibility and incrementing receive_count), and delete(receipt). Add a redrive policy: on receive, if receive_count > max_receive, move to a DLQ list.
  2. Duplicates. Run two consumers in a time-stepped loop where processing takes a random 10–60 s and visibility is 30 s. Count messages processed more than once. Then set visibility to 120 s and recount.
  3. Idempotency. Add a processed set keyed by message ID; count side effects instead of processings. Duplicates should no longer produce duplicate effects.
  4. Poison. Make one message always fail. With max_receive=3, check it lands in the DLQ after three receives and that other messages keep flowing.
  5. Stream. Model a stream of 4 shards, each accepting 1,000 records per second; hash partition keys with hashlib.md5. Send 3,000 records per second with 1% of keys receiving 50% of traffic; count throttled writes per shard. Repeat with uniform keys.
  6. Costs. Reproduce the section 8 table as a function of events per second and find the rate below which the differences are under $10 a month.

What to look for: step 2 shows duplicates whenever processing exceeds the visibility timeout, and few or none at 120 s; step 3 shows that idempotency, not timeouts, is what makes effects exactly-once; step 4 shows the DLQ keeping the queue healthy; step 5 shows one shard throttling while the others idle — hot keys, not total capacity, limit streams; step 6 shows that at a few events per second every option costs about the same.

Design: an ML feature pipeline and an order workflow on one platform

A company needs (1) clickstream events from web and mobile at 8,000 events per second (peaks 20,000), consumed by a real-time feature store, a fraud model and a daily batch export to S3, with the ability to reprocess the last 7 days after a bug fix; and (2) an order workflow — reserve stock, charge card, create shipment, send confirmation — at 50 orders per minute, where a payment can take up to 2 days to settle and every step must be auditable. Choose services for each, explain delivery and ordering guarantees, estimate monthly messaging cost for (1), and list the alarms you'd set.

Answer

(1) Clickstream: a stream. Kinesis Data Streams (or MSK) with partition key = user ID, so each user's events are ordered and load spreads across users. Provisioned sizing: 20,000 records/s at peak with ~1 KB records needs at least 20 shards for records (1,000 records/s each); use ~24 for headroom, or on-demand mode. Retention 7 days so reprocessing is a checkpoint rewind. Three consumers read independently: the feature-store updater and the fraud model with enhanced fan-out (dedicated 2 MB/s per shard each), and Firehose (or a consumer) writing to S3 for the daily export. Delivery is at-least-once to each consumer, so the feature updater uses idempotent upserts keyed by event ID. Cost (us-east-1, October 2026): 24 shards × $0.015 × 730 ≈ $263; payload units 8,000 × 2.628 million ≈ 21 billion × $0.014/M ≈ $294; retention beyond 24 h and enhanced fan-out (2 consumers × 24 shards × $0.015 × 730 ≈ $526 plus $0.013/GB read) on top — roughly $1,200–1,500 a month. Routing the same events through EventBridge would cost about $21,000 a month.

(2) Orders: Step Functions Standard. Exactly-once execution, waits of up to a year (payment settlement via a callback task token), retries with backoff on each step, catch blocks for compensation (release stock, refund), and 90 days of auditable history. 50 orders/min ≈ 2.2 million a month × ~8 transitions ≈ 17.5 million × $0.000025 ≈ $438. The order service starts executions from an outbox so no order is lost; confirmation emails go through SNS → SQS with idempotency on order ID.

Alarms: Kinesis write throttles and iterator age per consumer; Firehose delivery failures; Step Functions executions failed, timed out and throttled; age of the oldest pending payment callback; DLQ depth on every queue and target; and an end-to-end "event age at processing" metric for the feature store.
