On a Tuesday, the mobile team ships a new app release. Among many changes, the purchase event now records amount in cents instead of dollars, because the payments SDK changed. Nothing fails. The ingestion job loads the events; the silver job deduplicates them; the gold job sums revenue; the feature job computes "average order value over 30 days" for every user. Every task in every DAG is green. The revenue dashboard shows a hundredfold spike that finance assumes is a dashboard bug. The recommendation model, retrained nightly, starts treating newly active app users as big spenders and pushing premium products at them. Click-through drops for two weeks before an analyst traces it back, by hand, through six tables and three teams, to one field in one event.

Every data platform has a version of this story, and most have several a month. The common thread is that data fails silently. Code fails loudly — an exception, a red task — but data that is late, duplicated, empty, mis-typed, mis-scaled or simply wrong flows through pipelines that report success, into dashboards that render, into models that train. By the time anyone notices, the bad data has been copied into many downstream tables and baked into decisions. ML systems are particularly exposed: a model has no notion that its inputs are wrong; it just learns from them, and its degradation looks like any other drift.

Data quality engineering is about making those failures loud and early. It has three parts. Checks — tests, expectations, freshness and volume monitors and anomaly detection — encode what correct data looks like and run where they can stop bad data before it is published. Contracts move the responsibility upstream, so the team that produces data agrees, in a machine-checkable form, what it promises consumers and is warned before it breaks that promise. Lineage connects everything, so a broken table can be traced to its cause and its blast radius listed in minutes rather than days.

This lesson covers each in turn: a taxonomy of data failures; dbt-style tests and expectations; freshness and volume checks; anomaly detection on data metrics, including the arithmetic of false alarms at platform scale; schema drift; where checks belong in a pipeline; data contracts with an Open Data Contract Standard example; lineage collected from code and at runtime with OpenLineage; and impact analysis. The cases are Uber's data-culture programme and Amazon's Deequ.

The mental model: a food supply chain

Think of how food safety works. A farm (the data producer) grows produce; a processor washes and packs it; a distributor moves it; a restaurant (the consumer: a dashboard, a model) cooks it. Contamination anywhere reaches the plate, and the diner is the last to know.

A safe supply chain has three kinds of protection. Inspections at each step: the processor checks that each crate is the right weight, at the right temperature, not past its date, and labelled correctly — those are tests and checks, run on every batch. Some inspections are rules ("never above 5°C"); others notice that something is unusual ("this farm usually sends 200 crates on a Tuesday and today sent 12") — that is anomaly detection. A crate that fails inspection is set aside, not shipped — a quarantine and a circuit breaker.

Supply agreements: the processor and the farm agree in writing what will be delivered — variety, size, packaging, delivery day, notice before changes. If the farm wants to switch varieties, it must tell the processor weeks in advance. That is a data contract: the producer's promise to consumers, with a change policy.

Traceability: every crate carries a lot number linking it to its farm, field and harvest date, and every dish to its ingredients' lots. When contamination is found, the chain can recall every affected product and identify the source in hours — that is lineage, used for impact analysis (what is affected?) and root-cause analysis (where did it start?).

The analogy holds well, with one difference: food is checked for a known list of hazards, while data can go wrong in ways nobody anticipated — which is why statistical anomaly detection matters more for data than spot inspections do for food. And data is copied at every step, so a contaminated batch does not just move through the chain; it multiplies.

One distinction runs through the whole lesson. Some properties of data are invariants — things that must always be true, such as "order IDs are unique" or "every order belongs to an existing user" — and are checked with rules that either pass or fail. Others are expectations about behaviour — "about ten million rows on a Tuesday", "a median order of around thirty dollars" — which hold only statistically and change as the business changes, so they are checked against history with tolerance. Rules catch the failures you can name in advance; statistical checks catch the ones you could not. A good quality plan has both, each with an owner who knows what to do when it fires.

The rest of the lesson moves from the inside of a single table outwards: what can go wrong in the data; the rules (tests); the statistics (freshness, volume and anomalies); structure (schema drift); where checks must sit to stop damage; the agreements with producers (contracts); and the map that connects all tables (lineage).

1. How data goes wrong

Data failures fall into a handful of families. Naming them makes it possible to design checks systematically rather than reacting to the last incident.

  • Missing or late (completeness, freshness): a partition never arrived, arrived half-full, or arrived after consumers ran. Causes: upstream outages, connector lag, a file dropped in the wrong path.
  • Duplicated (uniqueness): the same records loaded twice by a retry, a replay or a backfill; a dimension with two rows per key after a source change.
  • Malformed or mistyped (validity, schema): a field missing, renamed or of a new type; strings where numbers belong; dates in a new format; an enum with a new value.
  • Inconsistent (integrity): foreign keys with no matching dimension row; an order's total different from the sum of its lines; two tables that should agree on a count but do not.
  • Plausible but wrong (accuracy, semantics): cents instead of dollars; a time zone shift; a test account's traffic counted as real; a default value (0, "unknown") silently replacing a missing one. These pass every structural check and are the hardest to catch — distribution checks and reconciliations are the main defence.

The cost of a failure grows with the distance it travels. Caught at the producer, it is a code review comment. Caught at ingestion, it is a quarantined batch. Caught after publication, it is a backfill of every downstream table and a conversation with every consumer. Caught by a model's metrics, it is weeks of degraded decisions.

2. Tests and expectations

The simplest and most valuable checks are tests: assertions about data that should always hold. dbt popularised a form that every SQL engine can run: a test is a query that selects the rows violating an assertion, and it passes if the query returns no rows. Generic tests are declared in YAML on columns:

models:
  - name: fct_orders
    columns:
      - name: order_id
        data_tests:
          - unique
          - not_null
      - name: status
        data_tests:
          - accepted_values:
              arguments:
                values: ['placed', 'paid', 'shipped', 'delivered', 'cancelled']
      - name: user_id
        data_tests:
          - relationships:
              arguments:
                to: ref('dim_user')
                field: user_id
              config:
                severity: warn

Each test compiles to SQL. unique on order_id becomes, roughly, SELECT order_id FROM fct_orders GROUP BY order_id HAVING COUNT(*) > 1; relationships becomes a left join from orders to users selecting orders with no matching user. Severity decides whether a failure fails the run (error) or only warns (warn); thresholds such as "warn if more than 10 failing rows, error if more than 100" allow for known small imperfections. Singular tests are hand-written SQL files for business rules: orders whose total differs from the sum of their line items by more than a cent. (The YAML key is data_tests in recent dbt versions; older projects use tests. Check the syntax against your version.)

Two other kinds of tests complement data tests. Model contracts (dbt 1.5+) enforce a model's shape: with contract: enforced: true, dbt runs a preflight check that the model's query returns exactly the declared column names and data types, and fails the build otherwise — the dbt docs stress that a contract defines the shape, not the content. Unit tests (dbt 1.8+) run a model's SQL on small hand-written input rows and compare the result with expected rows, testing logic before it ever touches production data — the right tool for tricky transformations such as SCD2 builds or point-in-time joins.

Outside dbt, the same ideas appear as expectations in Great Expectations, checks in Soda, and constraints in Amazon's Deequ, which compiles declarative constraints into aggregation queries on Spark. They differ in syntax and integration; the categories of check are the same.

Which tests to write? For every table: uniqueness of the declared grain key (de10), not-null on keys and required columns, accepted values on enums, referential integrity on foreign keys. For important tables: business invariants (non-negative amounts, totals equal to sums of parts, end dates after start dates), cross-table reconciliation (the number of orders in gold equals the number in silver for each day), and, for SCD2 tables, the invariants of one current row per key and no overlapping intervals.

3. Freshness and volume

The most common data failures are "it did not arrive" and "only part of it arrived", and two cheap checks catch them.

Freshness compares the newest data with the current time. dbt's source freshness, for example, reads the maximum of a loaded_at_field and compares it with warn_after and error_after thresholds:

sources:
  - name: app
    tables:
      - name: events
        loaded_at_field: _loaded_at
        freshness:
          warn_after: {count: 2, period: hour}
          error_after: {count: 6, period: hour}

Measure freshness on the table consumers read, against the deadline consumers were promised (de7), not only on the first landing table — a fresh bronze table above a stuck silver job is still a stale feature.

Volume compares row counts with expectation. Static thresholds ("at least 1 million rows") rot as the business grows; a better baseline is the same weekday over recent weeks, because most data has strong weekly seasonality. Worked example: a table's Tuesday counts over the last six weeks were 10.2, 10.6, 10.4, 9.9, 10.8 and 10.5 million. The median is 10.45 million; the absolute deviations from it are 0.25, 0.15, 0.05, 0.55, 0.35 and 0.05, whose median — the MAD — is 0.2 million. A band of median ± 4 × 1.4826 × MAD (1.4826 scales MAD to be comparable with a standard deviation for normal data) is 10.45 ± 1.19 million, roughly 9.3 to 11.6 million. Today's 6.1 million is far outside: alert. A launch day at 12 million is also outside — which is why owners need a way to annotate expected changes.

Volume checks should be computed per partition (per day), and, for tables fed by several sources, per source: a table whose total looks normal can be hiding one source that stopped while another doubled.

4. Anomaly detection on data

Tests encode what you know must be true. Anomaly detection catches what you did not think to test: the cents-for-dollars change, a new client version that stops sending a field (its null rate jumps from 2% to 40%), a category whose share collapses. The approach, described in Amazon's Deequ paper among others, is to compute metrics on every load — per table: row count; per column: null fraction, distinct count (often approximate, with HyperLogLog), min, max, mean, standard deviation, quantiles, and for categorical columns the frequency of top values — store them as a time series, and flag new values that fall outside a band learned from history.

The methods range from simple to elaborate: static thresholds; z-scores against a rolling mean and standard deviation; robust bands with median and MAD (resistant to past anomalies contaminating the baseline); seasonal comparison (same weekday, same hour); forecasting models with prediction intervals; and distribution distance measures for whole distributions — the population stability index (PSI), which sums (pnew − pold) × ln(pnew / pold) over bins, or the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic. For ML inputs, distribution checks on features are the bridge to model monitoring: a feature drifting in the training table and in serving logs at the same time points to the data; drifting only in serving points to training/serving skew (ml14).

The cents example in numbers. amount had a daily mean of 42.10 with a standard deviation across days of about 1.5; the day of the release, the mean jumps to 1,812 (a mix of old clients in dollars and new clients in cents). The z-score is (1,812 − 42.1) ÷ 1.5 ≈ 1,180 — no threshold misses that. The more subtle signal comes from quantiles: the median moves from 29.99 to 2,999 as new clients dominate, and the 99th percentile jumps likewise. A check on the column's median against its seasonal band would have fired on the first hour of data.

False-alarm arithmetic. Anomaly detection at platform scale lives or dies on false positives. Suppose 2,000 tables are monitored with 10 metrics each, checked daily — 20,000 checks a day. If each check has a 0.3% chance of a false alarm (roughly a 3-sigma two-sided band on well-behaved data — real data is less well-behaved), that is 60 false alerts every day. Nobody will read them. The remedies: monitor fewer things (tier 1 and 2 tables, columns that consumers use — lineage tells you which), widen bands for noisy metrics, require persistence (two consecutive anomalous loads) or agreement between related metrics before alerting, route alerts by owner and severity, and track the precision of alerts (what fraction led to action) as a metric of the monitoring itself.

5. Schema drift

Schema drift is a change in the structure of incoming data: a field added, removed, renamed or retyped, a nested object reshaped, an enum gaining a value. de8 showed how a schema registry with a compatibility mode catches some changes for Kafka topics; files, APIs and JSON events often have no registry, so drift must be detected on arrival by comparing each batch's observed schema with the expected one.

The response should be a deliberate policy per layer. Bronze stores raw data as received, so it accepts drift and records it — the raw copy is what allows a later fix. Silver applies the policy: additive changes (a new optional field) can evolve automatically, with a notification to the owner; records that do not fit the expected schema go to a quarantine table with the reason, so they can be inspected and replayed rather than silently dropped; breaking changes (a required field missing, a type change) fail the load. Gold and training sets change schema only through code review. And every drift event, even an accepted one, should notify someone: a new enum value — status = 'held_for_review' — passes schema checks but may break downstream logic that assumed the old set.

One more practical point about schema drift: detecting it needs a cheap, explicit record of the expected schema per source. For files and JSON events, store the inferred schema of each batch (field names, types, nullability, and for enums the set of observed values) and diff it against the previous batch and against the contract. The diff itself is a useful artefact to show owners — "field coupon_code appeared; status gained value held_for_review; amount is now sometimes a string" — and makes it obvious whether a change was intended.

6. Putting checks in the write path

A check that runs after consumers have read the data is a post-mortem tool. To prevent damage, checks must gate publication. de7 introduced write-audit-publish: write output to a staging location or a table-format branch, run checks on it, publish atomically only if they pass. A failing error-severity check acts as a circuit breaker: the run fails, the previous good version remains published, and downstream jobs — which depend on the published asset rather than on task success — do not run on bad data. Warn-severity checks publish but notify.

Consumers can protect themselves too. A training job should verify, before starting, that each input table is fresh, that its latest checks passed, and that its volume is plausible, and refuse to train otherwise. Ten seconds of checks are cheaper than a day's compute and a degraded model.

The cost of gating is latency and occasional false stops. Use error severity for invariants that must never be violated (duplicate primary keys in a fact table, missing partitions) and warn severity for statistical checks, unless the table is critical enough that a false stop is preferable to a missed real problem.

7. Data contracts

Most data incidents start upstream of the data team, in application changes made by people who did not know anyone depended on a field. Tests downstream catch the damage; data contracts try to prevent it by making the dependency explicit and checked at the source.

A data contract is a versioned document, owned by the producing team, describing a dataset it publishes (an event stream, a CDC table, an exported table): the schema with types and meaning of each field (units, time zone, allowed values), the grain and keys, quality rules, freshness and completeness guarantees, the owner and support channel, and the change policy (how much notice before a breaking change, how long deprecated fields are kept). The Open Data Contract Standard (ODCS), maintained by the Bitol project under LF AI & Data, gives contracts a common YAML format (version 3.2.0, released September 2026). An abridged sketch for the purchase events in the introduction:

apiVersion: v3.2.0
kind: DataContract
id: app-purchase-events
version: 2.0.0
status: active
team: {name: mobile-commerce}
schema:
  - name: purchase_events
    properties:
      - name: event_id
        logicalType: string
        required: true
        unique: true
      - name: amount_minor
        logicalType: integer
        description: Purchase amount in minor units (cents), integer.
        required: true
      - name: currency
        logicalType: string
        description: ISO 4217 code.
    quality:
      - metric: nullValues
        column: amount_minor
        mustBe: 0
slaProperties:
  - property: latency
    value: 2
    unit: h

(Field names follow ODCS v3 in spirit; check the specification for exact keys before adopting.) The version bump to 2.0.0 and the renamed field amount_minor are the point: changing units is a breaking change, so it gets a new field name, a major version and a migration period during which both are sent.

A contract is only as good as its enforcement. In the producer's CI, a check compares the proposed schema with the contract and fails the build on a breaking change — the same compatibility logic as a schema registry. At ingestion, records are validated against the contract and failures quarantined. In monitoring, the freshness and quality guarantees are measured and violations go to the producer, not just the consumer. And there is a social mechanism: a breaking change requires the producer to publish a new version and notify consumers — whose names come from lineage.

Contracts do not remove the need for downstream tests; they move the first line of defence to where changes originate and give producers visibility of who depends on them.

8. Lineage

Lineage is the graph of how datasets derive from one another: job J reads tables A and B and writes table C. Column-level lineage refines it: column c.revenue is derived from a.amount and b.fx_rate. It can be collected in two ways.

Statically, by parsing code: dbt's manifest records every model's dependencies; SQL parsers can extract table and column lineage from queries. Static lineage covers everything in the codebase, including jobs that have not run recently, but misses dynamic SQL, notebooks and code paths decided at runtime.

At runtime, by having engines and orchestrators emit events describing what actually ran. OpenLineage is the open standard for this: each run emits events (start, complete, fail) naming the job, the run, the input and output datasets, and facets — extensible metadata such as the schema of each dataset, column lineage, row counts, data quality results and the source code location. Integrations exist for Airflow, Spark, dbt and Flink, among others, and the events are collected by a lineage service such as Marquez (the reference implementation), DataHub or OpenMetadata, or by a data catalog. Runtime lineage captures ad hoc and dynamically generated jobs, at the cost of covering only integrated tools.

Lineage is used in two directions. Impact analysis walks forward: before changing purchase_events.amount, list every table, dashboard, feature and model downstream of that column, and their owners. Root-cause analysis walks backward: a feature's distribution shifted on Tuesday — which upstream tables show the shift first, and which job or source changed then?

9. Ownership, tiers and defaults

Tools only work inside an organisation that knows who is responsible for what. Three practices make quality programmes function at scale. Every important dataset has a named owner (a team, not a person) who receives its alerts. Datasets are classified into tiers by how much depends on them — revenue reporting and production models at the top, ad hoc experiments at the bottom — and each tier has defaults: freshness deadline, completeness and duplicate thresholds, required tests, review for schema changes, retention. And quality is visible: dashboards of incidents, time to detect, time to resolve, and check pass rates per owner. Uber's programme (the case below) is a good public example of all three.

10. A worked quality plan for a training table

The recommendation team's daily training table (de7's drill), tier 1 because a production model retrains from it:

  • Contracts upstream: impression and click events and the user-features table have contracts with their producers; schema changes are checked in the producers' CI.
  • Ingestion: event validation with quarantine; per-source volume checks per hour.
  • Tests on staged output (error): impression ID unique; row count equals impressions in scope; every feature timestamp ≤ impression time; label window closed; join drop rate < 0.1%.
  • Statistical checks (warn, page on two consecutive days): positive-label rate within the seasonal band; null rate and median of the 20 most important features within bands; PSI of each feature versus the previous four weeks < 0.2.
  • Freshness: published by 06:00 UTC for day D−3; deadline alert to the owning team.
  • Lineage: OpenLineage from Airflow and Spark; the training job records input snapshot IDs, so any model can be traced to its data.
  • Consumer gate: the training job refuses to start if the table is stale or any error check failed.

Why data tests differ from software tests

Software tests check code against fixed inputs; data checks check changing inputs against expectations about the world. That changes the engineering. A data check can be right one day and wrong the next because the business changed (a new market triples volume), so thresholds need maintenance or must be learned. A failing data check is often not a bug to fix but a decision to make (accept the new pattern, or block it). And the data is too big to test exhaustively, so checks are aggregates — counts, rates, quantiles — computed efficiently. Deequ's insight was to express constraints declaratively and compile them into a small number of aggregation passes over the data, computing many metrics in one scan, and to store metric histories so that incremental loads can be checked against the past without rescanning it. The same design — metrics as a byproduct of every write, checks over metric histories — underlies most modern data observability tools.

Testing logic versus testing data

Two different failure sources need two different tests. Logic bugs — a wrong join condition, an off-by-one in a window — are best caught with unit tests on small, hand-written inputs, before deployment, where the expected output is known exactly. Data problems — upstream changes, outages, drift — can only be caught by checks on production data, at runtime. Teams that rely only on production checks discover logic bugs late and attribute them to data; teams that rely only on unit tests are blindsided by upstream changes. A transformation that builds point-in-time training sets should have both: unit tests for boundary cases (de10's half-open intervals, label windows) and runtime checks for invariants on real output.

Reconciliation: the check that catches what others miss

Structural and statistical checks look at one table. Reconciliation compares two representations of the same truth: the count of orders per day in the lake versus the source database; total revenue in gold versus the payments provider's settlement file; the number of rows in a CDC-fed table versus the source table per key range (de8). It catches the failures that leave each table internally consistent but wrong — missing partitions in both copies of a mirrored pipeline, a dropped source, a filter applied twice. Reconciliations are expensive to set up because they need a trusted second source, so they are reserved for tier 1 data, but they are the checks auditors and finance teams trust.

The economics of quality

Every check has a cost: compute to run it, latency if it gates publication, human attention when it fires, and maintenance as data changes. Every missed incident has a cost: wrong decisions, model degradation, backfills, lost trust. The right number of checks is where the marginal check costs more than the incidents it would prevent. In practice that means concentrating effort: most incidents come from a few sources (application event changes, a handful of fragile integrations) and affect a few critical tables. Lineage and incident history identify both, and investment there — contracts at the sources, deep checks on the critical tables — pays off far more than blanket coverage. Measuring time to detect and time to resolve per incident turns the programme from a feeling into something that can be managed.

Lineage at column level is hard

Table-level lineage is straightforward to extract from most code; column-level lineage is not. A column may be derived through expressions, aggregations, window functions, UDFs, SELECT * chains, pivots and JSON extraction; Python code transforming DataFrames is much harder to analyse statically than SQL. OpenLineage defines a column-lineage facet, and engines such as Spark can emit it for many plans, but coverage is uneven and opaque steps (UDFs, external services) break the chain. A pragmatic approach: aim for complete table-level lineage everywhere, column-level lineage for SQL-defined models and the most important Spark jobs, and treat gaps as known unknowns when doing impact analysis.

Contracts at ML boundaries

ML systems add two contract boundaries that classic data platforms lacked. The feature contract between a feature table and its consuming models: feature definitions, as-of semantics, units, null meaning and freshness, so a change to a feature is a versioned event that models opt into. And the prediction log contract between serving systems and the training pipeline: which inputs, model version and request context are logged with each prediction, since those logs become the next training set (rec9). Breaking either silently is how training/serving skew enters production; versioning both is cheap insurance.

How quality programmes fail

Alert fatigue. Thousands of noisy checks, static thresholds that no longer fit, alerts to shared channels with no owner. Symptoms: most alerts unacknowledged; real incidents found by consumers. Fixes: tiering, owners, robust seasonal baselines, persistence requirements, measuring alert precision.

Checks that do not gate. Tests run after publication or in a separate job that nobody waits for; failures are logged, consumers have already read the data. Fixes: write-audit-publish, consumers depending on checked assets, training jobs that verify inputs.

Quarantine as a black hole. Invalid records go to a quarantine table nobody looks at; volume quietly drops as a growing share of events fails validation after a client change. Fixes: monitor quarantine volume as a metric with alerts; triage and replay procedures.

Contracts on paper. Contracts written once and never enforced; producers unaware of them; no versioning. Fixes: enforcement in producers' CI and at ingestion, generated from schemas so they stay current, and lineage-based notification of consumers.

Stale lineage. Lineage drawn by hand or extracted once; notebooks and ad hoc jobs invisible. Fixes: automatic static and runtime collection; lineage as part of the platform's job execution, not documentation.

What to measure

Per platform: incidents per month by root-cause category, time to detect (incident start to first alert), time to resolve, share of incidents detected by checks versus by consumers, alert precision, coverage of tier 1 tables by owner, tests and freshness checks. Per table: check pass rate, quarantine rate, freshness against deadline.

The case: Uber's data culture programme (2021) and Amazon's Deequ (2018)

In "Uber's Journey Toward Better Data Culture From First Principles" (Uber Engineering blog, 16 March 2021, by Suresh Srinivas and Krishna Puttaswamy), Uber describes a platform processing petabytes daily for thousands of data users, with hundreds of thousands of datasets — many created through self-service tools — and a long tail of reports, ML features, metrics and dashboards on top. The problems they list are the ones in this lesson: datasets without owners, duplicates and superseded "sources of truth", no consistent expectations for freshness or completeness, and schema changes made without review.

Their response was a set of principles turned into defaults and tooling, implemented by modifying more than 20 existing systems. Data as code: schema changes go through review with mandatory reviewers, and reusing or extending existing schemas is preferred over creating new ones. Ownership: every dataset should have an owner; heuristics were used to assign owners to abandoned datasets, with the goal of full coverage. Tiering: datasets are classified by importance, with ad hoc temporary datasets defaulting to the lowest tier (tier 5) and eligible for deletion after a period if unused; inventorying datasets for tiering exposed duplicates and superseded tables and drove refactoring towards clearer grain. Quality defaults: data should be 99.9% complete, with completeness measured at "three nines", and raw tables should have 0% duplicate keys, with a small tolerance in modelled tables. Single definitions: metrics are defined once (in YAML) and compiled into queries for each engine instead of being copied as SQL. They report, for example, a rider-funnel dashboard that once took weeks being built in hours once data was standardised.

Amazon's Deequ paper — "Automating Large-Scale Data Quality Verification" by Sebastian Schelter and colleagues (PVLDB 11, 2018) — describes the technical counterpart: a system providing a declarative API that combines common quality constraints with user-defined validation code, enabling "unit tests for data"; executing the resulting checks efficiently by translating them into aggregation queries on Apache Spark; supporting incremental validation of growing datasets; and using machine learning to suggest constraints and to detect anomalies in the history of data-quality metrics. Its motivating example is the one in this lesson's introduction: log files that feed training data for recommender systems, where bugs in program code or changes in the semantics of attributes cause errors that surface downstream. Together the two cases show the full shape of the problem: organisational defaults decide what must be checked and who acts; declarative checks and metric histories make checking cheap enough to do everywhere it matters.

Goal: build a tiny data-quality runner: declarative checks compiled to SQL, a robust volume band, and the cents-for-dollars anomaly caught by a quantile check. Python 3.12 standard library (sqlite3, statistics); about 15 minutes.

  1. Create purchases(event_id, user_id, amount, day) with 42 days of synthetic data: about 10,000 rows per weekday and 7,000 per weekend day, amounts drawn from a log-normal distribution with median near 30.
  2. Write checks as dicts — {"type": "unique", "column": "event_id"}, {"type": "not_null", "column": "user_id"}, {"type": "accepted_range", "column": "amount", "min": 0, "max": 10000} — and a function that compiles each to a SQL query returning violating rows, runs it, and prints pass or fail with the count.
  3. Volume check: for the latest day, compute the median and MAD of the same weekday's counts over the previous six weeks and flag the day if outside median ± 4 × 1.4826 × MAD.
  4. Inject incidents for day 43: load 6,000 rows (a partial load), duplicate 50 event IDs, and multiply amounts by 100 for 60% of rows (the cents bug). Run all checks.
  5. Add a quantile check: compare day 43's median amount with the band of the previous six same-weekday medians.

What to look for. unique fails with 50 rows and the volume check flags 6,000 against a band around 10,000. The range check probably passes — 100 × 30 = 3,000 is under 10,000 — which is the point: the cents bug is "plausible but wrong". Only the median check fires on it, jumping from about 30 to about 3,000. That is why distribution checks complement rules.

Design: stopping the next silent incident

Recap the introduction's incident: a mobile release changed purchase.amount from dollars to cents; nothing failed; finance and a recommendation model consumed bad data for two weeks; root cause took days to find. The platform has dbt for transformations, Airflow for orchestration, Spark jobs for features, and Kafka with no schema registry for app events. Design changes that would have (a) prevented the change from shipping silently, (b) caught it within an hour if it shipped anyway, (c) stopped it reaching the model, and (d) made root-cause and impact analysis take minutes.

Answer

(a) Prevent. Put the purchase event under a data contract owned by the mobile-commerce team: field names, types and units ("amount in major units, decimal") with a change policy. Introduce a schema registry for app events with BACKWARD_TRANSITIVE compatibility and a contract check in the app's CI. A unit change is semantic, not structural — the type stays numeric — so the contract's convention must be that a unit change requires a new field (amount_minor) and a major version, and reviewers of event schema changes include the data team. The CI check also prints the downstream impact list from lineage, so the change's blast radius is visible in the pull request.

(b) Detect. Statistical checks on silver purchases, computed hourly: median and 99th percentile of amount, per client version, against seasonal bands.
