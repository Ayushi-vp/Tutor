A mid-sized company's data platform bill doubles in six months. Nobody launched anything big. The finance team asks the data team what happened, and the data team does not know: the cloud invoice says "BigQuery analysis: $61,000", "Cloud Storage: $19,000", "Dataproc: $14,000", "Networking: $8,000" — but not which pipeline, dashboard or person caused each line. After two weeks of digging through query logs they find the story. A popular dashboard was switched from a daily aggregate table to the raw events table "for flexibility" and refreshes every 15 minutes for 40 viewers. A feature backfill was run three times because of bugs, on the largest cluster available. Iceberg snapshots have never been expired, so storage holds eleven copies of some tables. And a team in Europe reads a US bucket every night, paying for cross-region transfer.

None of these is a technical failure; every job worked. They are failures of cost awareness: not knowing how each engine charges, what drives the charge, and which engine fits which job. In a data platform, cost is a design dimension like latency and correctness. The same feature pipeline can cost $15 or $750 a month depending on the engine, and the same dashboard $50 or $5,000 depending on the table it reads. ML teams are especially exposed: training-set builds scan large histories, backfills are frequent, and experiments multiply everything.

This lesson makes cost concrete. It breaks a data bill into its lines; explains the main pricing models — per-byte scanning (BigQuery on-demand, Athena), capacity credits and slots (Snowflake, BigQuery reservations), cluster and serverless Spark — and what each one actually counts; introduces single-node engines (DuckDB and Polars) and why they are often the cheapest option; covers the levers that cut cost on any engine (pruning, projection, clustering, caching, materialised views, incremental models); and then works through one pipeline's monthly bill on three engines with real list prices, dated October 2026. The case is Uber's account of making its open-source data platform cheaper.

The mental model: paying for a library, a taxi and a car

Think of three ways to pay for getting information from books. A pay-per-page library charges for every page you photocopy, no matter how long you stay: reading one paragraph of a huge book is cheap if you copy one page and expensive if you copy the whole book. That is scan pricing — BigQuery on-demand and Athena charge for the bytes a query reads. A taxi charges by the minute while it is working for you and nothing when you are not using it: you pay for compute time, whether the trip is efficient or not. That is capacity pricing — Snowflake warehouses, BigQuery slots, and Spark clusters charge for compute time. Owning a car costs the same whether you drive it or not, cheaper per trip if you drive a lot: that is reserved or committed capacity, and a single machine you keep running.

The right choice depends on how you travel. A few long trips a month: taxi. Daily commutes: a car, or a monthly pass. Occasional quick look-ups in a reference book: the pay-per-page library, as long as you copy only the pages you need. And everyone pays rent for the shelves the books live on — storage — whether or not anyone reads them.

Two lessons transfer directly. First, under per-page pricing, what you read is the cost: reading fewer columns, fewer partitions and fewer rows saves money linearly, while making the engine faster saves nothing. Under per-minute pricing, how long you run is the cost: an efficient query that reads the same bytes in half the time halves the bill. Second, the vehicle should match the trip: hiring a 40-seat coach (a 200-executor Spark cluster) to carry one passenger (a 20 GB aggregation) is the most common waste in data platforms, and the opposite mistake — a hatchback for a house move — is the most common source of jobs that never finish.

Where the analogy breaks: data engines also differ in speed, concurrency and capability, not just in billing, and the people who operate them cost far more than any single line on the bill. A cheaper engine that the team cannot operate reliably is not cheaper.

The rest of the lesson builds the cost picture in the order you would use it on a real platform: first the lines of the bill, then how each pricing model counts usage, then the engines that sit behind those models, then the levers that cut cost on any engine, then one pipeline costed three ways, and finally the habits — attribution, guardrails, reviews — that keep a platform cheap after the first round of savings. Throughout, two numbers matter more than any other: how many bytes a job really touches after pruning and projection, and how long the capacity you pay for spends doing useful work.

1. The lines of a data bill

Before choosing engines or tuning queries, know what you pay for. Four lines cover almost every platform:

  • Storage: bytes held per month, in object storage or in a warehouse's managed storage, across all copies — every layer (bronze, silver, gold), every retained table-format snapshot, every backup and every team's private extract. Verified list prices (2026-10-08): Amazon S3 Standard in us-east-1 is $0.023 per GB-month for the first 50 TB, $0.022 for the next 450 TB and $0.021 above 500 TB (AWS price list published 2026-09-28). Warehouse storage is in the same range per GB of logical or physical data, depending on the billing model chosen.
  • Compute or scan: either bytes processed (scan pricing) or time on provisioned compute (capacity pricing), detailed below.
  • Requests and network: object-store requests (S3 lists $0.005 per 1,000 PUT, COPY, POST or LIST requests and $0.0004 per 1,000 GET requests in us-east-1 — negligible for large files, significant for millions of small ones, de2) and data transfer: free within a region in most cases, charged per GB across regions and to the internet.
  • People: the engineering time to build, operate and debug pipelines — usually the largest real cost, and the reason a slightly more expensive managed service is often the right choice.

Prices change and differ by region, edition and contract; the numbers in this lesson are list prices checked on 2026-10-08 and are there to make the arithmetic concrete, not to quote a bill.

2. Scan pricing: paying per byte processed

BigQuery on-demand charges for the bytes each query processes. On the price list checked on 2026-10-08, US regions are $6.25 per TiB, with the first TiB each month free per billing account; charges are rounded up to the nearest MB with a minimum of 10 MB processed per table referenced and per query. Crucially, "bytes processed" is computed from the logical size of the columns the query reads — based on data-type sizes (for example 8 bytes per 64-bit integer, and a string's length plus a small overhead) — in the partitions it touches, not the compressed bytes on disk. A column that Parquet-style encoding compresses 10× (de2) is still billed at its logical size. Compression reduces storage cost, not the on-demand scan charge.

Amazon Athena (Trino-based SQL over files in S3) charges per TB scanned — the pricing page's example implies $5 per TB — with charges rounded up to the nearest MB and a 10 MB minimum per query; here the bytes are what the engine actually reads from S3, so compressed columnar files and pruning reduce it directly. Athena also offers provisioned capacity at $0.30 per DPU-hour for predictable workloads.

Under scan pricing, three levers decide the cost of a query, and they multiply (the primer's funnel):

  1. Partition pruning (de3): filter on the partition column and only matching partitions are read. 30 days of a 3-year table is 1/36 of it.
  2. Column projection (de2): read only the columns needed. 4 of 40 similar-width columns is a tenth.
  3. Clustering and statistics (de3): within partitions, skip blocks whose statistics exclude the filter. A filter on an event type that is 2% of rows, on data clustered by event type, reads a few percent of blocks.

And the trap: SELECT * on a raw table without a partition filter reads everything, every time. A dashboard doing that every 15 minutes over a 50 TiB table is 96 × 50 = 4,800 TiB a day — $30,000 a day at $6.25 per TiB. Engines provide guardrails: BigQuery's maximum bytes billed setting fails a query that would exceed a limit, partitioned tables can require a partition filter, and dry runs report the bytes a query would process before it runs.

3. Capacity pricing: paying for compute time

Snowflake runs queries on virtual warehouses — named compute clusters of a chosen size. Each size consumes credits per hour, doubling with each step: X-Small 1, Small 2, Medium 4, Large 8, and so on. Billing is per second with a 60-second minimum each time a warehouse starts, and warehouses can auto-suspend when idle and resume on the next query. The price per credit depends on edition, cloud and region (check the current price list for yours). The cost of a query is therefore warehouse size × running time: a query that takes 4 minutes on Medium (4 credits/h) costs 4 × 4/60 ≈ 0.27 credits; the same query might take 2 minutes on Large (8 credits/h) — the same 0.27 credits, faster. Doubling the size usually halves the time for parallelisable work, so cost stays similar while latency improves — until the work stops parallelising, after which bigger warehouses just cost more. Idle time is the main waste: a warehouse kept running for a dashboard that is queried twice an hour pays for 58 idle minutes unless it suspends quickly.

BigQuery capacity pricing sells slots (virtual CPUs) per slot-hour, with editions and optional commitments; queries then cost nothing per byte, and the question becomes whether the reserved slots are busy. The break-even with on-demand is where monthly bytes processed × $6.25 per TiB equals the cost of the slots needed for the peak.

Spark costs the machines it runs on, for as long as they run, plus any platform fee. On a managed cluster (Amazon EMR, Google Dataproc, Databricks), you pay for VM-hours plus a per-vCPU or per-unit fee — for example, Dataproc's management fee is $0.01 per vCPU-hour on top of the VMs. On serverless Spark, you pay for resources while the job runs: Google Cloud's serverless Spark bills in Data Compute Units (DCUs), with each vCPU counted as 0.6 DCU and each GB of memory up to 8 GB per vCPU as 0.1 DCU, at $0.06 per DCU-hour for the standard tier in us-central1 (checked 2026-10-08); an executor with 4 vCPUs and 16 GB is therefore 2.4 + 1.6 = 4 DCUs, $0.24 an hour. Spark's cost levers are de5's and de6's: fewer bytes read, smaller shuffles, no skew or spill, right-sized partitions — all of which shorten the run — plus cheaper capacity: spot or preemptible instances at large discounts for fault-tolerant batch work, and committed use discounts for steady load.

4. Single-node engines: DuckDB and Polars

A modern server has 32–192 cores, hundreds of gigabytes to terabytes of RAM, local NVMe, and tens of gigabits of network. A vectorised columnar engine running on one such machine can scan and aggregate Parquet at gigabytes per second. DuckDB (version 1.5.6 as of September 2026) is an in-process analytical SQL database — a library, like SQLite, but columnar and vectorised — that reads Parquet, CSV and table formats from local disk or object storage with projection and filter pushdown, uses all cores, and spills to disk for larger-than-memory operations. Polars (version 2.0.0 released in October 2026) is a DataFrame library written in Rust whose lazy API builds a query plan, applies predicate and projection pushdown and runs it multi-threaded, with a streaming engine for data larger than memory.

Why they are often cheaper and faster than Spark for medium data: no cluster to start (seconds of start-up versus minutes), no network shuffle (joins and aggregations happen in shared memory), no JVM and serialisation overhead, and one VM billed per second. The limits are those of one machine: total memory for large hash tables (spilling helps but slows), network bandwidth from object storage (a 16-vCPU VM might sustain on the order of 1–3 GB/s), and no fault tolerance — a crash restarts the job. A practical rule of thumb: if the data a job actually reads after pruning and projection is under a few hundred gigabytes and its intermediate state fits in a large machine's memory, try a single-node engine first. Many teams also use DuckDB and Polars for development and testing of pipelines that run on Spark in production, and for ML feature work that ends in a pandas or NumPy hand-off anyway.

5. Caching, materialised views and incremental models

The cheapest query is the one not run against raw data. Three mechanisms move cost from repeated reads to occasional writes.

Result caches. Warehouses cache query results and return them for identical queries on unchanged tables, often at no charge (BigQuery's cached results are not billed; Snowflake reuses results for 24 hours by default). Dashboards that many people open benefit automatically — as long as the query text is identical and the underlying tables have not changed. A table appended to every five minutes defeats the cache every five minutes.

Materialised views. A stored, precomputed result — typically an aggregate — refreshed when base tables change, ideally incrementally (only the new partitions). Worked: a dashboard computes daily revenue by country over a 100 TB fact table; each load of the dashboard scans 2 TiB of logical data for the last 90 days — $12.50 at on-demand prices. Opened 400 times a day, that is $5,000 a day. A materialised daily aggregate of a few megabytes, refreshed once a day by processing only the new day (perhaps 25 GiB, $0.15), reduces the dashboard's cost to almost nothing. The calculation that decides: refresh cost × refreshes versus query savings × queries. Materialising a view refreshed every minute and read once an hour loses.

Incremental models. The same idea for pipelines: transform only what is new or changed since the last run and merge it into the output (dbt's incremental models, table-format change feeds, de4), instead of rebuilding everything nightly. A gold table rebuilt from 3 years of silver data every night reads 330 TB; an incremental version reads one day's 300 GB plus whatever late data arrived. The price is correctness care: late-arriving data (de7's lookback), upstream corrections that must trigger targeted rebuilds, and periodic full rebuilds to catch drift.

For ML specifically, the training-set builder (de12) is a candidate for both: materialise feature tables once and reuse them across experiments rather than recomputing features from raw events in every training job, and build training sets incrementally by week with explicit invalidation.

Now add the line most teams forget: storage of the event table. At 300 GB a day kept for a year, about 110 TB; at object-storage list prices around $0.02–0.023 per GB-month, roughly $2,200–2,500 a month — larger than any compute option above. Three years kept in hot storage triples it, and unexpired table-format snapshots or duplicate copies multiply it further. Lifecycle rules (moving data older than 90 days to an infrequent-access class), snapshot expiry, compaction that drops unused columns (as Uber did, below) and deleting unused copies often save more than any engine choice.

The ranking flips with circumstances. If 40 analysts also run ad hoc queries on the same data, the warehouse's concurrency and zero operations may be worth its premium. If the job grew to read 30 TB per run, a single VM would take hours and Spark becomes the right tool. If the company already pays for a committed Snowflake warehouse that sits idle at 3 a.m., the marginal cost of running the job there is close to zero. Engine choice is a per-workload calculation, and open table formats (de4) are what make it possible to choose per workload without copying data.

7. Choosing an engine: a checklist

Engine decisions are often made by habit ("we are a Spark shop") or by benchmark headlines. A short checklist, applied per workload, gives better answers.

  1. How much data does one run touch after pruning and projection? Measure it from query plans or bytes-scanned metrics, not from table sizes. Under a few hundred gigabytes with modest intermediate state: a single-node engine is a strong candidate. Terabytes with large joins or shuffles: a distributed engine.
  2. How many people or services query it at once, and how fast must answers be? Dozens of concurrent interactive users favour a warehouse, whose concurrency scaling, caching and governance are hard to reproduce; a nightly batch job has one user.
  3. How does the workload's shape match the pricing model? Spiky and infrequent work suits scan pricing or serverless; steady, heavy work suits reserved capacity; a job that reads little but computes a lot (complex UDFs, ML inference) is poorly matched to scan pricing's assumptions and well matched to time pricing.
  4. What does it need besides SQL? Python and ML libraries, custom code, GPU inference or streaming push towards Spark, Flink or single-node Python engines; pure SQL transformations run anywhere.
  5. What already exists, and what is the marginal cost? Idle committed capacity, an existing cluster with headroom or a warehouse contract can make the marginal cost of a job nearly zero on one engine and full price on another.
  6. Who will operate it? A cheaper engine that the team cannot debug at 3 a.m. is not cheaper. Managed services price in operations; self-managed engines move that cost to people.

Because open table formats let every engine read the same tables, the answer can differ per workload: BI on the warehouse, heavy ETL and backfills on Spark, development, small batch jobs and ML data preparation on DuckDB or Polars. The anti-pattern is copying data into each engine's private storage, which turns an engine decision into a storage and consistency problem.

8. Attribution and guardrails

Costs that nobody owns grow. The foundation of cost control is attribution: label every cluster, job, warehouse and query with the team, pipeline and purpose (cloud resource labels, query labels or tags, separate service accounts per pipeline), export billing data to a table, and join it with query logs so that "BigQuery analysis: $61,000" becomes a ranked list of pipelines, dashboards and users. Report unit costs where possible — dollars per thousand training examples built, per dashboard view, per gigabyte ingested — so growth in cost can be compared with growth in usage.

Guardrails stop accidents before they are expensive: maximum bytes billed per query or per user, required partition filters on large tables, per-project quotas, warehouse auto-suspend after a minute or two of idleness, statement timeouts, cluster autoscaling limits, budgets with alerts at 50%, 80% and 100%, and dry runs that show a query's cost before it runs. And a review habit: each month, look at the top 20 cost drivers. In most platforms a small number of jobs and dashboards account for most of the spend, and each has a specific fix — a missing partition filter, a dashboard on a raw table, an oversized cluster, a never-expired table.

Why logical-byte billing changes design

Because BigQuery on-demand bills logical bytes, the optimisations that matter are those that avoid reading columns and blocks, not those that compress them: narrow tables or column selection, partitioning and clustering, and pre-aggregation. Wide string columns are expensive to scan even when they compress well; storing a long JSON payload in a frequently scanned table makes every query that touches it pay its full logical size. Engines that bill physical bytes read (Athena on compressed Parquet) or compute time (Snowflake, Spark) reward compression too. A design that is cheap on one pricing model can be expensive on another, which is a reason to know the billing basis before choosing layouts — and a reason that moving a workload between engines needs a fresh cost estimate, not an extrapolation.

Elasticity, utilisation and the cost of idleness

The economic promise of cloud data platforms is elasticity: pay only while work runs. It is realised only if capacity actually scales down. Static Spark clusters sized for the nightly peak sit idle twenty hours a day; Snowflake warehouses with long auto-suspend timers bill idle minutes after each query; GPU training nodes wait on data loaders (de12). Uber's post sets an explicit utilisation target (about 80% average) because, on owned hardware, unused capacity is the dominant waste. In the cloud the equivalent metric is useful compute-hours divided by billed compute-hours, and raising it — autoscaling, serverless for spiky work, scheduling batch jobs to fill troughs, bin-packing jobs onto shared clusters — is often worth more than any query optimisation.

The single-node renaissance and its limits

Distributed engines were built when a server had a few cores and a few gigabytes of RAM; today one machine has more memory than many early Hadoop clusters. Combined with columnar formats and vectorised execution, that means a large fraction of real analytical jobs — most papers on production query logs find that the vast majority of queries touch modest amounts of data — fit on one node. DuckDB and Polars exploit this, and cloud warehouses themselves increasingly run small queries on single nodes internally. The limits are real, though: a single node has a single failure domain, fixed memory for hash tables and sorts, and bounded network bandwidth from object storage; very large joins, multi-terabyte shuffles and petabyte backfills still need distribution. The senior position is not "Spark or DuckDB" but "measure the data a job touches, then pick the smallest engine that handles it reliably".

Hidden costs: egress, cross-cloud and copies

Network charges hide in architecture decisions. Reading a US bucket from European compute pays cross-region transfer per GB on every run; a "multi-cloud" design where one cloud's warehouse reads another cloud's lake pays internet-egress prices; copying data into a warehouse's managed storage doubles storage and adds load jobs. Lakehouse designs with one copy in open formats reduce copies but make compute location matter: put compute in the same region as the data, and replicate data deliberately (and incrementally) when consumers really are elsewhere. A useful habit in design reviews is to draw every arrow that crosses a region or cloud boundary and attach a GB-per-month number to it.

Cost as a feedback loop for ML teams

ML work multiplies data costs: every experiment rebuilds features, every hyperparameter sweep rereads training data, every backfill reprocesses history. Three practices keep it under control. Materialise and share feature tables rather than recomputing features per experiment. Cache training sets as versioned shards (de12) so repeated training reads cheap local or object-storage copies rather than rerunning joins. And attach a cost estimate to experiment plans — "this sweep rereads 2 TB per trial for 50 trials" — so the decision to run it is made with the bill in view. The same attribution that serves the finance team lets ML teams compare the marginal value of a model improvement with its marginal data cost.

Comparing engines fairly

Published benchmarks rarely answer "which engine is cheapest for my workload". They use standard queries (TPC-H, TPC-DS) on idealised data, tuned by the vendor who publishes them, and report either speed or cost on one configuration. A fair internal comparison runs your top queries or jobs, on your data layout, measuring cost per run at the price you actually pay (including discounts, start-up time and minimum charges), with warm and cold caches reported separately, and repeated enough times to see variance. Include operational costs — time to set up, to debug a failure, to upgrade — even if only as estimates. And re-run the comparison when data volume or engine versions change materially; single-node engines in particular improve quickly, and the size at which a distributed engine becomes necessary keeps moving upwards.

How data costs go wrong

The runaway dashboard. A dashboard on a raw table with a short refresh interval and many viewers; or a BI tool that issues one query per tile per filter change. Symptoms: scan costs concentrated in one service account. Fixes: gold tables or materialised views, longer refresh intervals, result caching, required partition filters.

Backfill blow-ups. A backfill run on the largest cluster, rerun several times because of bugs; or 730 daily runs each re-reading overlapping windows (de7). Symptoms: a spike on one day's bill. Fixes: test on a sample first, use range jobs instead of many daily runs, size clusters for the job, approve large backfills with a cost estimate.

Storage creep. Unexpired table snapshots and orphan files (de4), raw data retained forever, duplicated extracts. Symptoms: storage growing faster than data volume; a large gap between bytes stored and bytes referenced by current table versions. Fixes: snapshot expiry, orphan cleanup, lifecycle rules, retention policies per tier (Uber's tier-5 datasets deleted after a period when unused, de11).

Idle capacity. Clusters that never scale down; warehouses that never suspend; committed capacity bought for a peak that came once. Symptoms: low utilisation in monitoring. Fixes: autoscaling, auto-suspend, serverless for spiky workloads, right-sizing commitments to steady load.

Small-file request costs. Millions of tiny objects multiply request charges and slow every engine. Fix: compaction (de3).

What to measure

Cost per pipeline, table, dashboard and team per day; unit costs (per training example, per query, per GB ingested); utilisation of provisioned capacity; bytes scanned per query for top queries; storage bytes versus bytes referenced by current table versions; cross-region transfer per month. Review the top cost drivers monthly and track savings against them.

The case: Uber's cost-efficient big data platform (2021)

In "Cost-Efficient Open Source Big Data Platform at Uber" (Uber Engineering blog, 11 August 2021, by Zheng Shao and Mohammad Islam), Uber describes how it reduced the cost of a platform built on HDFS, YARN, Hive, Spark and Presto — mostly by changing how data is stored and how capacity is used, rather than by switching engines.

On storage, they recompressed Parquet data from gzip to zstd: 8% smaller at zstd level 9 and 12% at level 19, with data recompressed to level 9 after one month and to level 19 after three months, when it is rarely rewritten (the same work de2's case describes from the file-format side). They found that manually tuned row ordering — typically by user ID, then timestamp — reduced the size of their largest Hive tables by more than 50%, because sorted columns encode and compress far better (de2 and de3). They dropped unneeded columns — debugging metadata and fields removed for compliance — without recompressing the remaining columns. And they evaluated erasure coding (3+2 and 6+3 schemes, storing 1.67× or 1.5× the data instead of 3× replication), which could nearly halve disk requirements. Apache Hudi's incremental processing, they note, dramatically reduced the compute needed compared with full recomputation (de1's case).

On compute, they focused on utilisation: a "dynamic MAX" policy for YARN queues, with each queue's guaranteed minimum set to 125% of its average usage, targeting around 80% average cluster utilisation; plans to shift load out of the daily peak with time-based multipliers rather than buy capacity for it; and running low-priority maintenance work such as recompression on non-guaranteed capacity that would otherwise sit idle — effectively free. On query engines, nested-column pruning in Spark and Presto and rewriting rank-based queries to use MAX_BY improved performance, and workloads were moved between Hive-on-Spark and Presto according to cost.

The lessons match this lesson's numbers: storage layout (codec, sort order, columns kept, replication) and utilisation of paid capacity dominate; engine choice matters per workload; and the most effective cost work is systematic — measure, find the biggest lines, change the defaults — rather than one-off tuning.

Goal: build a small cost calculator and see which assumptions move a monthly bill the most. Python 3.12 standard library; about 15 minutes.

  1. Write a function bytes_touched(stored_tb_per_day, days, cols_needed, cols_total, cluster_factor) returning compressed TB read, and logical_tib(compressed_tb, expansion) for scan-billed engines.
  2. Write three cost functions with dated constants (from this lesson, 2026-10-08): bq_on_demand(tib) at $6.25/TiB; serverless_spark(vcpus, hours, gb_per_vcpu) with 0.6 DCU per vCPU plus 0.1 DCU per GB (up to 8 GB per vCPU) at $0.06 per DCU-hour; single_vm(hours, price_per_hour=1.048).
  3. Add storage_month(tb, price_per_gb=0.023).
  4. Reproduce the worked bill: 300 GB/day, 30 days, 4 of 40 columns, cluster factor 1.0, expansion 4.5; 200 vCPUs for 0.25 h; the VM for 0.5 h; 30 runs per month; 110 TB stored.
  5. Sensitivity: vary one input at a time — cluster factor 0.25, expansion 2 and 8, columns needed 12, runs per day 24 (an hourly job), retention 3 years — and print the monthly total for each engine.

What to look for. The base case should reproduce roughly $750, $90 and $16 for compute and about $2,500 for storage at $0.023/GB-month. Clustering cuts the scan-billed option the most; the expansion factor moves only the scan-billed option (logical bytes); an hourly schedule multiplies everything by 24 and makes the warehouse option very expensive; three-year retention triples storage and dominates all else. That ordering — retention and layout first, engine second — is the practical lesson.

Keep the calculator next to your design docs: re-running it with real numbers before a migration or a new pipeline is a five-minute habit that prevents most cost surprises.

Estimate and decide: where should the training-set build run?

An ML team builds a weekly training set: it reads 8 weeks of impressions (each week 1.5 TB of compressed Parquet, 60 columns, of which the build needs 15), joins user features (400 GB per weekly snapshot, needs all columns) and writes 600 GB of shards. Today it runs as a BigQuery SQL job using on-demand pricing and costs about $1,100 a week; the team wants to run 10 variants a week for experiments. Options: stay on BigQuery on-demand, move the build to Spark on a serverless or ephemeral cluster, or use a single large VM with DuckDB. Estimate each, note the constraints, and recommend.

Answer

Bytes touched. Impressions: 8 × 1.5 TB × 15/60 = 3 TB compressed; user features: 400 GB. About 3.4 TB compressed per build. If logical size is about 4× compressed, BigQuery bills roughly 13–14 TiB of reads per build, about $85 at $6.25/TiB — so the observed $1,100 a week means the job reads far more than it needs: probably all 60 columns, or no partition pruning on the 8-week range, or a cross join stage. First fix: check bytes billed per stage and the query's column list. A corrected query might cost around $85 a build; 10 variants a week would then be about $850 a week, roughly $3,700 a month.

Spark. A shuffle join of 3 TB with 400 GB on user ID (de6), plus writing shards. Say 400 vCPUs with 4 GB each for 30 minutes: 200 vCPU-hours × 1.0 DCU × $0.06 = $12 a build, $120 a week for 10 variants. Better: materialise the joined, projected training table once per week and run the 10 variants as cheap reads and filters on it, so most variants cost a few dollars.

Single VM. 3.4 TB through one machine at about 1–2 GB/s is roughly 30–60 minutes of reading, but the join on user ID with 400 GB of features needs a hash table far larger than a 128 GiB machine; it would spill heavily. A 512 GiB machine (for example n2-highmem-64 at about $4.19/h on the same price page) might manage in 1–2 hours, about $8 a build — cheap, but with no fault tolerance and little headroom as data grows.

Recommendation. Fix the query first (likely a 10× saving). Then, for 10 variants a week, restructure rather than re-engine: one weekly Spark (or corrected BigQuery) job builds a materialised, projected, point-in-time-joined base table; variants are filters, samples or feature subsets over it, cheap on any engine — DuckDB on a modest VM reading the base table's shards is ideal for those. Keep the heavy join on Spark (or BigQuery) where it is reliable at scale, and attribute costs per variant so the team sees the price of each experiment.
