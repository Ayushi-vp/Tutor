An ML engineer writes a feature job in PySpark: read a year of events, filter to purchases, join to the users table, group by user and compute twenty aggregates. On a sample it runs in a minute. On the full data it runs for three hours and then fails with "ExecutorLostFailure … exceeding memory limits". They double the cluster. It runs for two and a half hours and fails the same way. A colleague opens the Spark UI, scrolls to one stage, and points: 3,999 tasks finished in about 40 seconds each, and one task has been running for two hours with 80 GB of shuffle read. One user ID — a test account that a load-testing tool used — has 400 million events. No amount of hardware would have fixed it, because one key goes to one task on one core.

Spark is the workhorse of large-scale data engineering and of ML data preparation: feature pipelines, training-set joins, deduplication of text corpora, embedding backfills. It hides an enormous amount — you write something that looks like pandas or SQL and it runs across hundreds of machines. That is wonderful until it is slow or broken, and then the abstraction becomes the problem: the code says nothing about stages, shuffles, partitions or memory, yet those decide whether the job takes minutes or hours. The engineers who are good with Spark are not the ones who know the most configuration keys; they are the ones who can picture what the cluster is physically doing for each line of code, and who can read the Spark UI to confirm it.

This lesson builds that picture from the bottom. It starts with the parts of a Spark application (driver, executors, cores) and lazy evaluation, then how a query becomes a graph of stages, why some operations cut stages and others do not, how files become partitions and tasks, what a shuffle actually writes and reads, how the Catalyst optimiser rewrites your query, how adaptive query execution re-plans at runtime, how executor memory is divided and what spill is, and finally how to read the Spark UI. The public case is the paper that started it all — the 2012 RDD paper — and the 2015 Spark SQL paper that introduced Catalyst. de6 then applies all of this to the hardest operation, the join.

The mental model: a kitchen brigade

Picture a large restaurant kitchen preparing a banquet. The head chef reads the menu (your program), works out the most efficient way to produce it, breaks it into tasks, and assigns tasks to cooks; the head chef cooks nothing. The cooks, grouped at stations, each work on one task at a time: chop this crate of onions, sear this tray of steaks. Many cooks work in parallel on identical tasks with different ingredients.

Some steps can be done start to finish at one station: wash, peel and chop a crate of potatoes — each crate is independent. Those are narrow steps, and a cook does them one after another without handing anything over. Other steps need everything of one kind gathered in one place: to plate "all the dishes for table 12", every station must send table 12's items to one plating station. That gathering is a shuffle: every station sorts its output by table and sends each pile to the right plating station, and the plating cannot start until every station has finished sending. The kitchen's work naturally divides into phases separated by these gathering moments — those are stages.

The kitchen's problems map exactly onto Spark's. If one table ordered 400 dishes and every other table 10, one plating station is buried while the rest idle — skew. If a station runs out of counter space, it stacks trays on the floor and works slower — spill. If the head chef insists on tasting every dish personally, the whole kitchen waits on one person — a driver bottleneck. And a smart head chef will rewrite the menu's instructions — "only fetch the vegetables we actually use; filter out the spoiled ones at the market, not after cooking" — that is the optimiser.

The analogy breaks in one place: in Spark, if a cook collapses, the head chef can re-do that cook's work from the original ingredients because the recipe (the lineage) is written down and the ingredients (input files) are still in the pantry. Real kitchens cannot un-cook a dish, which is why Spark's fault tolerance is so cheap compared with copying every intermediate result.

The pieces in one picture

Two more words are worth fixing now because the rest of the lesson leans on them. A partition is the chunk of data one task processes, and the number of partitions decides how many tasks a stage has. A slot is one executor core: the cluster in the figure can run twelve tasks at a time, so a stage with 120 tasks runs in ten rounds, or waves. Nearly every Spark performance question reduces to these numbers — how many partitions, how big each one is, how many slots there are, and whether any one partition is much bigger than the rest.

A reader who stops here should hold three ideas: the driver plans and executors do; work is split into stages at every point where data must be regrouped by key; and the slowest task of a stage decides when the stage ends.

1. Why distribute at all

A single modern server core can scan and decode Parquet at very roughly a few hundred megabytes per second, and a large machine might sustain a few gigabytes per second from local NVMe. A nightly job over 10 TB of compressed Parquet at 2 GB/s is 5,000 seconds — over 80 minutes — before any joining or aggregation, and that assumes the data is on local disk rather than object storage. And memory: a join or group-by over billions of rows needs hash tables or sort buffers far larger than one machine's RAM. Splitting the work across many cores on many machines turns 80 minutes into a few minutes, as long as the work divides evenly and the cost of moving data between machines does not eat the gain. Spark is a framework for exactly that division. (For data that fits on one machine — often anything below a few hundred gigabytes — single-node engines such as DuckDB or Polars are frequently faster and cheaper; de13 compares them.)

2. The anatomy of a Spark application

A Spark application is one program — a PySpark script, a Scala job, a notebook session — with its own processes. The driver is the process running your main program. It holds the SparkSession, turns your DataFrame code or SQL into plans, divides the work, schedules it, and gathers results. Executors are worker processes (Java virtual machines) launched on cluster machines for this application; each has a number of cores (spark.executor.cores) and a memory size (spark.executor.memory, 1 GB by default — always set explicitly in practice). A cluster manager — Kubernetes, YARN or Spark's standalone manager — supplies the machines or containers. In PySpark, the Python code runs in the driver, and the executors are still JVMs; Python worker processes appear on executors only when you use Python functions on rows (UDFs, section 9).

Each executor core runs one task at a time, so the cluster's parallelism is executors × cores: 20 executors × 8 cores = 160 slots. A task is the smallest unit of work: one stage's operators applied to one partition of data. When a stage has more tasks than slots, they run in waves: 4,000 tasks on 160 slots is 25 waves.

3. Lazy evaluation, actions and the DAG

Spark operations come in two kinds. Transformations — filter, select, withColumn, join, groupBy(...).agg(...) — return a new DataFrame and do nothing else; they only record what should be computed. Actions — write, count, collect, show, toPandas — require a result, so they trigger execution. Each action creates a job.

Laziness lets Spark see the whole computation before running it and optimise it as a unit, which is the job of Catalyst (section 7). It also means the record of transformations, the lineage, is always available. This was the central idea of the RDD paper: instead of replicating intermediate data to survive failures, remember how each piece was computed from stable input, and recompute only the lost pieces if a machine dies. The cost of laziness is that every action re-runs its lineage from the source unless you explicitly cache() or persist() an intermediate result. A common accident is calling df.count() for logging and then df.write... — the whole pipeline runs twice.

When an action fires, the driver turns the plan into a DAG (directed acyclic graph) of stages. To see where stage boundaries fall, you need the distinction between narrow and wide transformations.

4. Narrow and wide transformations, stages and shuffles

A dataset in Spark is divided into partitions — chunks, each processed by one task. (These are Spark's in-memory partitions, not the table's storage partitions from de3, although reading a table creates them from its files.)

A narrow transformation computes each output partition from exactly one input partition. filter(col("event_type") == "purchase") on partition 17 produces output partition 17; it never needs to look at partition 18. So does select, withColumn, and a map over rows. Spark chains consecutive narrow transformations into one task: read a row group, filter it, compute a column, write it — all inside one task, row batch by row batch, with no intermediate data written anywhere. This chaining is called pipelining.

A wide transformation needs, for some output partition, rows from many input partitions. To compute "total spend per user", all of user 553's rows must end up in one place, but they are scattered across all input partitions. So Spark must shuffle: every task of the first stage splits its output by key into as many buckets as there will be output partitions, and every task of the next stage collects its bucket from all of them. groupBy, join (unless one side is broadcast), distinct, orderBy, window functions with partitionBy and explicit repartition are wide.

A stage is a maximal chain of pipelined narrow work; every shuffle ends one stage and begins another. A typical feature query — read events, filter, join to users, group by user, write — has three stages: stage 1 reads and filters events and writes them shuffled by user_id; stage 2 reads users and writes them shuffled by user_id (stages 1 and 2 can run in parallel); stage 3 reads both shuffles, joins, aggregates (the aggregation can reuse the join's partitioning, so no further shuffle) and writes the output. Stage 3 cannot start any task until stages 1 and 2 have finished completely, because any of their tasks might hold some of the rows a stage-3 task needs.

5. From files to partitions to tasks

For a file-based scan (Parquet, ORC, CSV), Spark's planner lists the input files (or gets them from a table format's metadata, de4) and divides them into read partitions. Large splittable files are cut into pieces of up to spark.sql.files.maxPartitionBytes — 128 MB by default in Spark 4.2 — aligned to Parquet row groups. Small files are packed together into one partition up to the same size, with each file charged an extra spark.sql.files.openCostInBytes (4 MB) to account for the cost of opening it. So:

  • 500 GB in 1,000 files of 500 MB → about 4,000 read tasks of ~128 MB.
  • 500 GB in 500,000 files of 1 MB → each file "costs" 5 MB for packing, so about 25 files per task, ~20,000 tasks, each opening 25 files — the small-files problem of de2 surfacing as task count and request count.

Note that the 128 MB is measured on the files as stored. With column projection (de2), a task assigned 128 MB of file may actually read 20 MB of it, if the query needs three of thirty columns. Tasks are cheap to read in that case, and Spark's task count can look high for the work done.

After a shuffle, the number of partitions is set by spark.sql.shuffle.partitions, 200 by default — a number chosen long ago for small clusters and wrong for most real jobs in both directions: a 2 TB shuffle into 200 partitions makes 10 GB per reduce task (spill, OOM), and a 50 MB shuffle into 200 partitions makes 250 KB tasks (pure overhead) and 200 tiny output files. Adaptive query execution (section 8) mostly fixes this at runtime.

6. What a shuffle really does

Follow one shuffle with real numbers: stage 1 has 4,000 map tasks; the shuffle has 400 reduce partitions; the total shuffled data after filtering and projection is 200 GB.

Map side. Each map task processes its input and, for every output row, computes its target partition: hash(user_id) mod 400. It buffers rows in memory sorted by target partition (Spark's default sort-based shuffle), spills sorted runs to disk if the buffer fills, and at the end writes one data file containing its 400 partitions back to back, plus an index file giving each partition's offset. Each map task writes on average 200 GB ÷ 4,000 = 50 MB. Shuffle output is compressed by default (spark.shuffle.compress is true).

Reduce side. Once every map task has finished, each of the 400 reduce tasks fetches its partition from all 4,000 map outputs — 4,000 blocks of on average 50 MB ÷ 400 = 125 KB each — over the network, keeping up to 48 MB of fetches in flight by default (spark.reducer.maxSizeInFlight). It merges them and runs its operators: here a join and an aggregation over 200 GB ÷ 400 = 500 MB of input. Across the whole shuffle there are 4,000 × 400 = 1.6 million blocks.

The shuffle is expensive in four ways. Bytes: 200 GB is serialised, compressed, written to local disk, read back, sent over the network and decompressed — at least twice through disk and once through the network. Block count: millions of small fetches stress the network and the executors serving them; very many map tasks times very many reduce partitions is a classic cause of slow shuffle reads. Synchronisation: no reduce task starts until the slowest map task finishes. Fragility: shuffle files live on executors' local disks; if an executor dies after the map stage, its outputs are lost and those map tasks must be re-run before the reduce stage can continue (unless an external shuffle service or a remote shuffle store keeps them). Reducing shuffle volume — filtering and projecting before the shuffle, pre-aggregating on the map side (which Spark does automatically for groupBy().agg() with a partial aggregation before the shuffle), broadcasting small tables instead of shuffling large ones — is the single most effective optimisation in Spark.

7. Catalyst: how your query is rewritten

The Spark SQL paper (Armbrust et al., SIGMOD 2015) introduced Catalyst, the optimiser behind DataFrames and SQL. It works in phases, each visible with df.explain(mode="extended"):

  1. Parsed (unresolved) logical plan: the operators you wrote, with names not yet checked.
  2. Analysed logical plan: names resolved against the catalog and the DataFrame schemas, types checked, functions bound. Errors like "column not found" come from here.
  3. Optimised logical plan: rule-based rewrites applied repeatedly until nothing changes. The important ones: predicate pushdown (move filters as close to the data source as possible, into the Parquet reader where de2's statistics can skip row groups), column pruning (read only columns used anywhere downstream), constant folding and expression simplification, combining adjacent filters and projections, and eliminating unnecessary operators.
  4. Physical plan: concrete algorithms chosen. For joins this is where Spark chooses between broadcast hash join, sort-merge join and shuffled hash join, using size estimates (de6). Exchanges (shuffles) are inserted where required.
  5. Code generation: whole-stage code generation, from the Tungsten project, compiles each stage's chain of operators into a single Java function with a tight loop over rows, instead of calling each operator per row through virtual function calls. In explain() output, operators prefixed with *(n) belong to generated code block n.

Here is what that looks like for a simple query (Needs Spark; abridged and illustrative — exact operator names, IDs and fields vary by version):

df = (spark.read.parquet("s3://lake/silver/events")
        .filter("event_type = 'purchase' AND date >= '2026-10-01'")
        .groupBy("user_id").agg(F.sum("price").alias("spend")))
df.explain()

== Physical Plan ==
AdaptiveSparkPlan isFinalPlan=false
+- HashAggregate(keys=[user_id], functions=[sum(price)])
   +- Exchange hashpartitioning(user_id, 200)
      +- HashAggregate(keys=[user_id], functions=[partial_sum(price)])
         +- Project [user_id, price]
            +- Filter (event_type = purchase)
               +- FileScan parquet [user_id, price, event_type, date]
                    PartitionFilters: [date >= 2026-10-01]
                    PushedFilters: [EqualTo(event_type,purchase)]
                    ReadSchema: struct<user_id:bigint,price:double,event_type:string>

Read it bottom up. The scan reads only four of the table's columns (column pruning: ReadSchema), prunes storage partitions by date (PartitionFilters) and pushes the event-type filter into the Parquet reader (PushedFilters), so row groups without purchases are skipped using statistics. A partial aggregation runs before the shuffle, so each map task sends one partial sum per user it saw rather than every purchase row. Exchange hashpartitioning(user_id, 200) is the shuffle — the stage boundary. The final aggregation combines partial sums. AdaptiveSparkPlan isFinalPlan=false says AQE may still change the plan at runtime.

Catalyst cannot optimise what it cannot see. A Python UDF is a black box: filters on its output cannot be pushed into the scan, and its cost is unknown. Neither can Catalyst fix a logically wasteful query — a cross join written by mistake, or a distinct() on a 2 TB table nobody needed.

8. Adaptive query execution

The plan Catalyst picks before running is based on estimates, and estimates after filters and joins are often wrong by orders of magnitude. Adaptive query execution (AQE) — on by default since Spark 3.2 (spark.sql.adaptive.enabled) — re-optimises the remaining plan each time a shuffle stage completes, using the actual size of every shuffle partition. It does three main things.

Coalescing shuffle partitions. If the 200 post-shuffle partitions turn out to hold 6 GB in total, AQE merges adjacent partitions towards the advisory size, spark.sql.adaptive.advisoryPartitionSizeInBytes (64 MB by default): about 94 tasks instead of 200. In Spark 4.2 the default coalescePartitions.parallelismFirst=true means it prioritises keeping enough tasks to use the cluster's parallelism over hitting the advisory size exactly, with a minimum partition size of 1 MB. This is why teams can set spark.sql.shuffle.partitions high (say 2,000) and let AQE coalesce down — you get enough partitions for big days and no tiny tasks on small days.

Switching join strategies. If a join was planned as a sort-merge join because the estimate for one side was 5 GB, but after filtering that side is 8 MB, AQE can convert it to a broadcast hash join (de6) — or at least read the shuffle locally to avoid network transfer.

Splitting skewed partitions. A partition is considered skewed if it is larger than skewedPartitionFactor (5.0) times the median partition size and larger than skewedPartitionThresholdInBytes (256 MB). For a sort-merge join, AQE splits such a partition into several sub-partitions and duplicates the matching partition of the other side for each, so the work for one hot key range runs as several tasks. de6 works through this with numbers.

AQE's limits: it acts only at shuffle boundaries (it cannot fix a skewed scan or a skewed task inside a stage without a shuffle), its skew handling covers specific join types, and it cannot split a single key's rows if the downstream operator genuinely needs them together (a groupBy on a hot key with a non-decomposable aggregate).

9. Executor memory and spill

An executor container's memory is divided into regions, and knowing them explains most out-of-memory errors.

Work through a 16 GB executor heap with 8 cores. Spark reserves 300 MiB. Of the rest, spark.memory.fraction (0.6) forms the unified region: about 9.4 GB, shared by execution memory (sort buffers, hash tables for joins and aggregations, shuffle buffers) and storage memory (cached DataFrames, broadcast variables). Execution can borrow all of it and can evict cached blocks, except for a protected share set by spark.memory.storageFraction (0.5 of the unified region) when it is in use. The remaining 40% of the heap (about 6.3 GB) is user memory for your own data structures and Spark's internal metadata. Outside the heap, memory overhead — 10% of executor memory with a minimum of 384 MiB by default, so about 1.6 GB here — covers JVM internals, thread stacks, network buffers and, crucially for PySpark, the Python worker processes. The container size requested from Kubernetes or YARN is heap plus overhead: about 17.6 GB.

The 9.4 GB unified region is shared by up to 8 concurrently running tasks, so each task can count on roughly 1.2 GB of execution memory. A reduce task whose sort-merge join has a 500 MB partition is comfortable. One with a 6 GB skewed partition is not. When an operator cannot acquire more execution memory, it spills: it sorts or flushes what it has to local disk, frees the memory, continues, and merges the spilled runs at the end. Spill is safe but slow — it adds serialisation and disk I/O, often several times the data's size. The Spark UI reports "spill (memory)" (the in-memory size of what was spilled) and "spill (disk)" (its serialised size). Some structures cannot spill gracefully — a broadcast table that does not fit, a single huge row, a UDF holding a large list — and those produce Java OutOfMemoryErrors. When native memory outside the heap grows (Python workers, Arrow buffers, off-heap allocations) past the overhead, the cluster manager kills the container, and the error says so rather than mentioning a Java OOM.

10. Reading the Spark UI

The Spark UI (live on the driver while the job runs, and through the history server afterwards) is how you see all of the above. The tabs that matter most:

  • Jobs: one row per action, with its stages and duration. Start here to see which job is slow.
  • Stages: for each stage, the number of tasks, input size, shuffle read and write, and — on the stage detail page — the summary metrics table: min, 25th percentile, median, 75th percentile and max of task duration, GC time, input, shuffle read, spill. Plus an event timeline showing tasks on executors over time.
  • SQL / DataFrame: the physical plan as a graph, annotated with runtime metrics per operator — rows output, data size, time, and for scans the number of files and bytes read. Here you see that the filter passed 2% of rows, or that a join produced 10× more rows than its input (an exploding join on duplicated keys).
  • Executors: per-executor task time, GC time, shuffle and storage use, and failures.
  • Storage: what is cached and how much of it fits in memory.

11. A worked job, end to end

A nightly feature job reads 30 days of events — 9 TB on disk, of which the 4 needed columns are 1.2 TB — filters to purchases (2% of rows), joins to a 40 GB users table on user_id, aggregates per user, and writes 3 GB. The cluster has 25 executors of 8 cores and 32 GB heap: 200 slots, each with about 2.3 GB of execution memory when all run at once.

Stage 1 (scan events). Storage partitions prune to 30 days; the planner makes about 9 TB ÷ 128 MB ≈ 70,000 read tasks. With column projection each task reads about 17 MB, and with the pushed-down event-type filter on well-clustered files (de3), most row groups are skipped. Suppose each task takes 3 seconds: 70,000 tasks ÷ 200 slots = 350 waves × 3 s ≈ 18 minutes. Output after filtering, projection and partial aggregation: about 20 GB of shuffle write. The time here is dominated by task count, which hints at a layout fix — if clustering on event_type lets whole files be skipped via table-format statistics, the task count itself drops.

Stage 2 (scan users). 40 GB, about 320 tasks, a minute; 40 GB of shuffle write (or none, if users is broadcast — at 40 GB it is far too large, de6).

Stage 3 (join and aggregate). 60 GB of shuffle read. With spark.sql.shuffle.partitions set to 2,000 and AQE coalescing towards 64 MB, about 940 tasks of 64 MB, each well within 2.3 GB of execution memory — about 5 waves of a few seconds each. Unless a hot user key makes one partition 30 GB, in which case AQE's skew handling must split it, or the stage waits on one task.

Total: about 20–25 minutes, of which most is stage 1's scan overhead. The lesson of the arithmetic is typical: the scan and its file layout dominate, the shuffle is modest because filtering and partial aggregation happen first, and the risk sits in skew. Each of those is visible in the UI within the first few minutes of the job.

Lineage, determinism and fault tolerance

The RDD paper's key insight was that coarse-grained transformations — the same function applied to every element of a partition — can be logged cheaply as lineage, so fault tolerance does not need data replication. When a task fails, Spark re-runs it (up to spark.task.maxFailures, 4 by default). When an executor is lost, Spark re-runs the tasks whose outputs were stored on it, walking back up the lineage as far as needed. This works only if recomputation gives the same result, which is why non-determinism is dangerous: a repartition() followed by a non-deterministic operation, a UDF calling random() or the current time, or a limit without an order, can produce different rows on recomputation, and if only some partitions are recomputed, the output can contain duplicates or gaps. Spark marks some such stages as indeterminate and retries the whole stage, but the safe rule is to make data-dependent randomness deterministic (seed it from row content) and to avoid relying on row order without a sort.

Why the shuffle is sort-based, and what remote shuffle changes

Early Spark used hash-based shuffle, where each map task wrote a separate file per reduce partition: 4,000 maps × 4,000 reduces was 16 million files. Sort-based shuffle writes one file plus an index per map task by sorting output by partition ID, keeping file counts linear in map tasks while still letting reducers fetch contiguous ranges. The remaining scalability problem is the many-to-many fetch pattern — millions of small random reads from executors' disks — and the loss of shuffle data with executors, which hurts on Kubernetes and spot instances. Push-based shuffle (merging blocks per reducer on the map side, from Spark 3.2 on YARN) and remote shuffle services (Apache Celeborn and Apache Uniffle are open-source examples) move shuffle data to dedicated servers that merge it per partition, so reducers do a few large sequential reads and executors can be released or pre-empted without losing shuffle outputs. They add a service to operate; they matter most for very large shuffles on elastic clusters.

Whole-stage codegen and vectorisation

Tuple-at-a-time interpretation (each operator calls the next for every row through a virtual interface) spends most of its time on function call overhead and branch mispredictions rather than useful work. Whole-stage code generation fuses a stage's operators into one generated function, so a filter-project-aggregate chain becomes a loop with the predicate inlined and the aggregate state in local variables — closer to hand-written code. Parquet scans use a separate vectorised reader that decodes column batches directly. Some operators break codegen (certain complex expressions, Python UDFs, very wide schemas past a method-size limit), falling back to interpreted execution; in explain() output, operators without the *(n) prefix are outside generated code, which is a hint when a stage is unexpectedly CPU-bound. Native execution engines (such as Apache Gluten, incubating, with the Velox library, and vendor engines like Databricks Photon) go further by replacing the JVM operators with vectorised C++ ones — a sign that CPU efficiency, not I/O, has become the bottleneck for many Spark workloads.

Statistics and the cost-based optimiser

Catalyst's rules are mostly heuristics; join ordering and strategy depend on size estimates. Spark can collect table and column statistics (ANALYZE TABLE … COMPUTE STATISTICS FOR COLUMNS) and use a cost-based optimiser (spark.sql.cbo.enabled, off by default) to reorder multi-way joins and estimate selectivities. In practice many teams rely on AQE's runtime statistics instead, because stored statistics go stale and estimates after several joins are unreliable anyway. Table formats increasingly provide row counts and column bounds from metadata, which helps the static planner without explicit ANALYZE runs.

Dynamic allocation and cluster sizing

With dynamic allocation, Spark requests more executors when tasks are queued and releases idle ones, which suits jobs with very different stage sizes. Its catch is shuffle data: an executor holding shuffle files cannot be released without losing them, unless an external shuffle service (on YARN) or shuffle tracking/decommissioning (on Kubernetes) preserves or migrates them. Executor sizing is a trade: few large executors (many cores, big heaps) share broadcast data and cached blocks efficiently but suffer longer garbage-collection pauses and bigger blast radius on failure; many small executors isolate failures but duplicate broadcasts and add overhead. Four to eight cores and a heap sized for at least a few gigabytes of execution memory per core is a common middle ground; tune from the UI, not from folklore.

Python, Arrow and Spark Connect

PySpark's DataFrame API is a thin layer: your Python builds a plan that runs in the JVM, so most PySpark code is as fast as Scala. Python cost appears only when rows must cross into Python — UDFs, toPandas(), collect(). Arrow-based pandas UDFs and mapInArrow move whole column batches instead of rows. Spark Connect, introduced in Spark 3.4, separates the client from the driver: a thin client sends unresolved plans over gRPC to a remote Spark server, which suits notebooks, IDEs and services that should not host a driver — and makes driver-side mistakes (collecting huge results) visible as network transfers rather than local OOMs.

How Spark jobs fail and slow down in production

Skewed stages. One task runs for hours while thousands finished in seconds. In the UI: max shuffle read far above median. Causes: hot keys (null keys, test accounts, a "default" category), skewed joins, groupBy on a low-cardinality column. Fixes in de6: AQE skew join handling, salting, isolating hot keys, broadcasting.

Executor OOM and container kills. Java OutOfMemoryError in executor logs, or the cluster manager killing containers for exceeding memory. Causes: partitions too large, oversized broadcasts, data skew, Python workers or Arrow buffers exceeding overhead, collecting large lists in UDFs. Fixes: more and smaller partitions, larger overhead for PySpark, avoiding per-row Python, executors sized with enough memory per core.

Driver OOM and driver bottlenecks. collect() or toPandas() on large results, broadcasting a large table (the driver builds broadcasts), planning over millions of files, or very large query plans built in loops. spark.driver.maxResultSize (1 GB by default) fails a job whose collected results exceed it — a useful safety net, not a limit to raise casually.

Lost executors and FetchFailed. Spot interruptions or node failures lose shuffle files; reduce stages fail with FetchFailed and map stages are recomputed, sometimes repeatedly. Fixes: graceful decommissioning, external or remote shuffle, on-demand nodes for the shuffle-heavy part, fewer, larger shuffles.

Too many small tasks and files. Millisecond median task times, tens of thousands of tasks, and thousands of tiny output files. Fixes: compaction of inputs, AQE coalescing, repartition before writes (de3).

Plan regressions. The same code becomes slower because statistics or sizes crossed a threshold: a broadcast join becomes a sort-merge join, or the reverse a broadcast that is now too large times out (spark.sql.broadcastTimeout, 300 s). Keep the physical plans of important jobs and diff them when performance changes.

What to watch

Per job run: duration per stage, input bytes and files, shuffle read and write, spill, GC time fraction, task duration percentiles, failed and retried tasks, executors lost. Plot them across runs; regressions are easiest to catch as a change in shape (a new long-tail task, a doubled shuffle) the day they appear. The Spark event log, kept by the history server, holds all of this and can be parsed for automated checks.

The case: the RDD paper and the Spark SQL paper

"Resilient Distributed Datasets: A Fault-Tolerant Abstraction for In-Memory Cluster Computing" (Zaharia et al., UC Berkeley, NSDI 2012) introduced the design every later Spark feature rests on. The paper's motivation is that MapReduce-style frameworks made data reuse across computations expensive — the only way to share data between two MapReduce jobs was to write it to a distributed file system, with replication, disk I/O and serialisation — while iterative machine-learning algorithms (logistic regression, k-means, PageRank) and interactive data mining reuse the same data again and again. RDDs are read-only, partitioned collections built only by deterministic, coarse-grained transformations from stable storage or other RDDs; because a program cannot reference an RDD it cannot reconstruct, fault tolerance comes from recomputing lost partitions from lineage instead of replicating data. Users control persistence (keep this RDD in memory, spill to disk if needed) and partitioning (hash-partition two datasets the same way so they join without a shuffle — the same idea as de3's bucketing). The paper reports Spark up to 20× faster than Hadoop for iterative applications, a real-world analytics report sped up 40×, and interactive scans of a 1 TB dataset with 5–7 seconds of latency.

"Spark SQL: Relational Data Processing in Spark" (Armbrust et al., SIGMOD 2015) added the layer most engineers use today. It introduced the DataFrame API — relational operations that integrate with procedural code and are evaluated lazily so they can be optimised — and Catalyst, an extensible optimiser written with Scala pattern-matching rules, with phases for analysis, logical optimisation, physical planning and code generation. DataFrames also store data in a compact columnar format rather than as Java or Python objects. The trajectory from these two papers — lineage for fault tolerance, then declarative plans for optimisation, then adaptive re-planning at runtime — explains why modern Spark advice is "write declarative DataFrame or SQL code, avoid row-level Python, and read the plan and the UI", rather than hand-tuning RDD operations.

Goal: simulate one shuffle by hand to see stage boundaries, block counts and skew. Python 3.12 standard library; about 15 minutes. A real Spark version follows, marked Needs Spark.

  1. Generate 1,000,000 (user_id, amount) rows where 1% of rows belong to user 0 and the rest to random users 1–100,000. Split them into 40 "map partitions" of 25,000 rows each.
  2. Map stage: for each map partition, compute a partial sum per user (a dict), then split it into 16 buckets by hash(user_id) % 16. Record bytes per bucket as len(pickle.dumps(bucket)).
  3. Reduce stage: for each of the 16 reduce partitions, gather its bucket from all 40 map outputs and merge the sums. Print the number of blocks (40 × 16), total shuffle bytes, and rows per reduce partition.
  4. Repeat without the partial aggregation (send raw rows) and compare shuffle bytes.
  5. Needs Spark: df.groupBy("user_id").agg(F.sum("amount")).explain() should show a partial_sum HashAggregate below an Exchange hashpartitioning(user_id, 200) and a final HashAggregate above it.

What to look for. With partial aggregation, shuffle bytes shrink by the ratio of rows to distinct users per map partition — user 0's 10,000 rows become 40 partial sums. Without it, the reduce partition holding user 0 receives about 10,000 more rows than its peers: that is skew, and it is invisible in the totals and obvious in the per-partition counts. The 640 blocks is the map × reduce product that grows painfully at real scale.

Keep the script: the same three numbers — blocks, bytes, largest partition — are what the Spark UI's stage page reports for every real shuffle.

Diagnose: the 20-minute job that now takes 4 hours

A nightly Spark job builds per-merchant features: read 7 days of transactions (about 1.4 TB on disk), join to a merchants dimension table, aggregate per merchant, write. Until last week it ran in 20 minutes on 40 executors of 8 cores. Now it takes 4 hours, with no code change. The Spark UI for last night's run shows: stage 1 (scan transactions) 6 minutes, as before; stage 2 (scan merchants) 40 seconds; stage 3 (join and aggregate) 3 hours 50 minutes. Stage 3 summary metrics: 1,800 tasks; median duration 9 s; 75th percentile 11 s; max 3 h 41 min; median shuffle read 180 MB; max shuffle read 410 GB; max spill (disk) 620 GB. The SQL tab shows a SortMergeJoin between the two. The merchants table grew from 9 MB to 14 MB last week. What happened, and what do you do?

Answer

Two changes, one symptom. First, the plan: a 9 MB merchants table was below the 10 MB broadcast threshold (spark.sql.autoBroadcastJoinThreshold), so the join was almost certainly a broadcast hash join — no shuffle of the 1.4 TB transactions side, and no skew problem, because each task joined its own slice of transactions against a full in-memory copy of merchants. At 14 MB it crossed the threshold, and Catalyst planned a sort-merge join instead: both sides are now shuffled by merchant_id. Second, the data's shape: the max task read 410 GB while the median read 180 MB, so one merchant_id — a huge marketplace or payment aggregator, or a placeholder ID for unknown merchants — holds about a third of all transactions. In a sort-merge join all of that key's rows go to one reduce task on one core, which sorts 410 GB with about 2 GB of execution memory, spilling 620 GB to disk. That single task is the 3 h 41 min.

Why AQE did not save it. The partition clearly qualifies as skewed (410 GB is far above 5 × the 180 MB median and above 256 MB), and AQE can split a sort-merge join's skewed partition into sub-tasks, each joining a slice of the hot side against a copy of the matching merchants rows. But the aggregation that follows groups by merchant_id and relies on the join's output being partitioned by that key; splitting the hot partition would break that and require an extra shuffle, and by default AQE declines skew optimisation that introduces an extra shuffle (spark.sql.adaptive.forceOptimizeSkewedJoin is false). The SQL tab shows whether a skew-join split was applied. AQE could also have switched to a broadcast join at runtime, but only if the merchants side measured under the adaptive broadcast threshold.

Fixes, in order. (1) Restore the broadcast: a 14 MB table is trivially broadcastable; add a broadcast(merchants) hint or raise the threshold to, say, 50 MB, since executors have gigabytes of memory. That removes the transactions shuffle entirely and with it the skew — the job returns to about 20 minutes. (2) Investigate the hot key: if it is a placeholder for "unknown merchant", filter or handle it separately; it is probably also distorting the features. (3) Guard against recurrence: alert when stage 3's max-to-median task ratio exceeds, say, 20, and keep the physical plan of the job under review so a strategy change shows up in a diff. de6 covers join strategies and skew handling in depth.
