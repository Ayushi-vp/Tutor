At Google in the mid-2000s, engineers analysing logs and crawl data had a frustrating loop. A question such as "how many web pages contain this kind of signal, grouped by language?" meant writing a MapReduce job, launching it on thousands of machines, and waiting hours, because every job read every byte of every record even when it needed two fields out of hundreds. In 2010 Google published the design of Dremel, a system it had run internally since 2006, which answered such questions interactively, aggregating over tables of a trillion rows in seconds. The paper's own comparison is the clearest demonstration of the idea at the heart of this lesson. On a table of 85 billion records, a MapReduce job reading records in their usual row-by-row format read 87 TB. The same job reading a columnar copy of the data, only the fields the query needed, read about 0.5 TB and went from hours to minutes. Dremel itself, which combined the columnar format with a tree of servers scanning in parallel, brought the same query down to seconds. Dremel became the engine behind BigQuery, and its columnar encoding of nested data inspired Apache Parquet.

The underlying problem is that two kinds of workload want opposite things from storage. A checkout service reads and writes individual rows: this order, this customer, this inventory item, thousands of times a second, each touching every column of a few rows. An analyst asks for total revenue by region by month over three years, touching two or three columns of hundreds of millions of rows, once. The first is OLTP (online transaction processing); everything in db1 to db11 has been about serving it well. The second is OLAP (online analytical processing), and serving it well needs a different layout (columns rather than rows), different compression, a different execution engine, a different schema style and, almost always, a different system fed from the first.

This lesson explains that second world from the bytes up. You will see why storing a table by column reduces the data a query reads by an order of magnitude or more, how columns compress so well, how analytical engines skip, vectorise and delay work, what Parquet files contain, how data flows from production databases into warehouses, and how to model a warehouse with facts, dimensions, star and snowflake schemas and history-preserving dimensions. The worked examples use a million-row sales table and an LLM usage warehouse, the kind of analytics every AI product needs for cost, latency and quality.

Filing by row versus filing by column

Imagine a company's sales records written on index cards, one card per sale, each card listing the date, region, product, quantity, price, customer and a note. Filed this way, by row, answering "what did sale 81,422 look like?" means pulling one card. But answering "what was total revenue last year?" means pulling every card, reading just two numbers from each, and putting it back. Most of the effort goes into handling the parts of the card you don't need.

Now file the same information differently: one long scroll for dates, another for regions, another for quantities, another for prices, all in the same order, so the 81,422nd entry on each scroll belongs to the same sale. That is filing by column. Total revenue now means unrolling only the quantity and price scrolls and multiplying entry by entry. The other five scrolls stay in the drawer. And the scrolls themselves become compressible: the region scroll says "north, north, south, north…" and can be written in a short code; the date scroll, if sales are filed in time order, says the same date thousands of times in a row and can be written as "day 100 × 3,000".

The analogy breaks where it should: looking up one complete sale now means visiting seven scrolls and finding the same position on each, and adding a new sale means appending to seven scrolls. Column layout makes whole-row operations expensive, which is why it is used for analysis, not for transaction processing.

Two systems, one pipeline

Because the layouts conflict, most organisations run two kinds of system. The production databases (PostgreSQL, MySQL, DynamoDB) are row-oriented and serve the application. A data warehouse (BigQuery, Snowflake, Redshift, ClickHouse, or an open-source engine such as DuckDB or Trino over Parquet files in a data lake) is column-oriented and serves analysts, dashboards and ML pipelines. Data flows from the first to the second continuously or in batches. And because analytics is about counting and slicing facts by their circumstances, warehouses use a schema style built for that, with a central table of facts surrounded by tables describing the who, what, where and when: the star schema. The rest of the lesson takes each piece in turn: layout, compression, execution, files, pipelines and modelling.

A sense of scale

It helps to put rough numbers on the two worlds. A busy OLTP database might handle 10,000 transactions a second, each reading or writing a handful of rows: tens of thousands of rows touched per second, each in a few milliseconds. A single analytical query over a year of that history touches hundreds of billions of row-values, once, and the user is happy to wait a few seconds. Reading 100 GB in five seconds needs 20 GB/s of throughput, which no single disk provides, but a cluster of machines each scanning its own compressed column slices does. That is why the analytical side is built around reading as little as possible, in parallel, and why the same hardware that runs one workload well runs the other badly.

When a query touches most rows, there is nothing to find; the cost is reading and processing bytes, so the winning moves are to read fewer bytes (only needed columns, compressed), skip blocks that cannot match, and process the remaining bytes as fast as the CPU allows.

2. Column storage and what a query reads

In a column store, each column of a table is stored separately, in the same row order, so the i-th value of every column belongs to row i. The lab builds a million-row sales table with seven columns. A revenue query needs two of them, qty and price. In row form, a disk-based engine reads every row (about 31 MiB at the lab's rough row width); in column form, it reads two 4-byte integer columns, 8 MiB, before any compression. Real warehouse tables are often 50 to 200 columns wide, and typical queries touch fewer than ten, so reading only the needed columns routinely cuts I/O by ten to fifty times. That is the first and biggest effect, and it is the one Dremel's 87 TB versus 0.5 TB comparison shows.

The costs are the mirror image. Reconstructing a full row means fetching the i-th value from every column, so "show me sale 81,422" touches as many files as there are columns. Inserting a row means appending to every column, and updating or deleting means rewriting or marking positions in each. Column stores therefore buffer writes: C-Store, the 2005 research system that became Vertica, paired a small write-optimised store with a large read-optimised column store and merged them in the background, and modern warehouses accept data in batches or micro-batches and write immutable compressed column segments, handling deletes with deletion vectors or tombstones that are compacted later, exactly the LSM idea (db5) applied to columns.

3. Encoding: why columns compress so well

A column holds values of one type, often with few distinct values or with order, so it compresses far better than rows that interleave dates, strings and numbers. Analytical engines use lightweight, type-aware encodings, chosen per column and often per block:

  • Run-length encoding (RLE) stores runs of repeated values as (value, count). The lab's day column, sorted by time with 3,000 sales per day, compresses from 1,000,000 values to 334 pairs, about 3,000 times fewer. Sorting a table by its most-filtered low-cardinality columns makes RLE effective on them.
  • Dictionary encoding replaces each value with a small integer code into a dictionary of distinct values. The lab's region column of four strings goes from about 4.3 MiB of text to 0.24 MiB of 2-bit codes. Comparisons like region = 'west' become integer comparisons on codes.
  • Bit-packing stores small integers in as few bits as their range needs: quantities 1-4 need 2 bits, not 32.
  • Delta encoding stores differences between consecutive values; increasing timestamps or ids become small deltas that then bit-pack well.

A general-purpose compressor such as Zstandard or Snappy is often applied on top. Typical analytical tables compress five to ten times overall; sorted, low-cardinality columns far more. Crucially, many operations run on encoded data directly. A SUM over an RLE column multiplies each run's value by its count; a filter on a dictionary-encoded column is evaluated once per distinct value and then becomes a lookup on codes; a COUNT DISTINCT on a dictionary column can count dictionary entries. So compression is not just storage savings; it is less work.

4. Skipping data: zone maps and partition pruning

The next best thing to reading fewer bytes is not reading blocks at all. Column stores and formats keep small statistics per block of each column, at least the minimum and maximum, often null counts and sometimes Bloom filters. These zone maps let a scan skip any block whose min-max range cannot satisfy the filter. A query on day BETWEEN 100 AND 130 over a table sorted by day reads only the blocks for those 31 days; the rest are eliminated from metadata alone. Zone maps are only as good as the data's order: on a column with random order, every block's range spans nearly everything and nothing is skipped. That is why warehouse tables are partitioned by date (whole partitions pruned by directory or metadata) and sorted or clustered within partitions by the next most common filters (tenant, region), the warehouse equivalent of choosing a clustered index (db6). BRIN indexes in PostgreSQL (db6) are the same idea inside a row store.

5. Execution: vectorised and late-materialised

Even after reading only the right bytes, a classic row-at-a-time engine (the iterator model of db7) spends most of its time on overhead: a function call per row per operator, type checks, branches. For billions of rows that overhead dominates. Vectorised execution, pioneered by the MonetDB/X100 research project and now used by DuckDB, ClickHouse, Snowflake and others, passes batches of values between operators, typically around 1,000 to 2,000 values of one column at a time. Each operator then runs a tight loop over a plain array (multiply these 1,024 quantities by these 1,024 prices), which the CPU executes with SIMD instructions, few branches and excellent cache behaviour. The difference is often ten times or more per core. (The lab's pure-Python loops cannot show this effect, since Python's own per-value overhead swamps the layout; the byte counts it prints are the part that transfers.)

Late materialisation delays building rows. To evaluate SELECT customer, price FROM sales WHERE region = 'west' AND day = 100, the engine scans the day column to produce the positions where day = 100, then reads the region column only at those positions to narrow further, and only then fetches customer and price for the surviving positions. Values that fail early filters are never decompressed or copied. Combined with zone maps, a selective analytical query may read a tiny fraction of even the columns it references.

Finally, analytical engines are massively parallel. Data is split into many chunks (files, row groups, partitions), each scanned and pre-aggregated by a separate thread or node, with partial results merged at the end, exactly Dremel's serving tree. Because analytical queries are scans and aggregations rather than coordinated transactions, they parallelise almost perfectly, which is how warehouses scale to petabytes.

6. Parquet and the data lake

Apache Parquet is the open columnar file format used across the data ecosystem. A Parquet file is divided into row groups (horizontal slices of rows, typically sized in the hundreds of megabytes); within each row group, each column is stored as a column chunk, divided into pages that carry encoded and compressed values. The file footer holds the schema and, for every column chunk, statistics such as min, max and null count, so a reader can open the footer, decide which row groups and columns it needs, and read only those byte ranges, the zone-map idea at file level. Parquet also encodes nested and repeated fields using Dremel's repetition and definition levels, so JSON-like records can be stored column by column.

Storing Parquet files in cheap object storage (S3, GCS, Azure Blob) and querying them with separate compute engines is the data lake architecture. Plain files lack transactions, schema evolution and efficient updates, so table formats (Apache Iceberg, Delta Lake, Apache Hudi) add a metadata layer that tracks which files make up each version of a table, supporting atomic commits, time travel, partition evolution and row-level deletes. That combination is called a lakehouse. For an engineer, the practical upshot is that analytical data often lives as Parquet files plus table metadata, readable by many engines: Spark, Trino, DuckDB, Snowflake, BigQuery, and pandas or Polars in a notebook.

7. Getting data from OLTP into the warehouse

Warehouses are fed from operational systems, and the pipeline's design decides how fresh and how correct analytics are. The traditional approach is batch ETL (extract, transform, load): every night, extract changed rows from sources (using an updated_at column or a full dump), transform them into the warehouse schema, and load them. Modern warehouses are powerful enough that ELT is now common: load raw extracts first, then transform inside the warehouse with SQL (tools such as dbt manage those transformations as versioned code).

Batch extraction by timestamp misses deletes and can miss updates if timestamps are unreliable. Change data capture (CDC) reads the database's own log, PostgreSQL's logical decoding or MySQL's row-based binlog (db10), and emits every committed insert, update and delete in commit order, usually into a stream such as Kafka, from which they are landed in the lake or warehouse within seconds to minutes. CDC gives fresh, complete data without load on the source's tables, at the cost of operating the stream and handling schema changes. Whatever the mechanism, the loading step must be idempotent (replaying the same changes gives the same result, by merging on source primary key and change sequence) and reconciled (row counts and key totals compared with the source daily), because pipelines fail and restart.

Keeping analytics separate is not only about speed. Long analytical queries on a PostgreSQL primary hold old snapshots that block vacuum (db10), compete for buffer cache, and can be cancelled on standbys; a warehouse removes all of that coupling.

8. Dimensional modelling: facts, grain and dimensions

Warehouse schemas are designed differently from OLTP schemas, following the dimensional modelling approach associated with Ralph Kimball. The design starts from business processes (taking orders, shipping, serving an LLM request) and for each one asks: what is measured, and at what level of detail?

A fact table records the measurements of one process. Its first and most important design decision is the grain: exactly what one row represents, stated in business terms, such as "one row per order line" or "one row per LLM call". Every column must be true at that grain. Measures (facts) are usually numeric and additive: quantity, amount, tokens, cost, latency. Mixing grains is the classic error: putting an order-level shipping fee on every order-line row means anyone who sums it counts it once per line. Choose the most atomic grain the source provides; you can always aggregate up, never down.

A dimension table holds the descriptive context used to filter and group facts: date, product, customer, store, model, tenant. Dimensions are wide (dozens of attributes such as product name, brand, category, department, size, colour), relatively small (thousands to millions of rows, versus billions of facts) and deliberately denormalized: the category and department names sit directly on each product row. Each dimension row gets a surrogate key, an integer generated in the warehouse, independent of source-system ids, which lets the warehouse keep history (section 10) and merge entities from several sources. The fact table holds a foreign key to each dimension plus its measures. A date dimension, one row per calendar day with attributes such as weekday, week, month, quarter, fiscal period and holiday flags, is nearly universal, because "group by fiscal quarter" or "exclude holidays" then becomes a join and a filter rather than date arithmetic in every query.

9. Star and snowflake schemas

A fact table with its dimensions arranged around it forms a star schema: the fact at the centre, one join to each dimension. A typical query filters dimensions, joins to the fact, and aggregates measures:

SELECT d.month, p.category, s.region, SUM(f.amount) AS revenue
FROM fact_sales f
JOIN dim_date d    ON d.date_key = f.date_key
JOIN dim_product p ON p.product_key = f.product_key
JOIN dim_store s   ON s.store_key = f.store_key
WHERE d.year = 2026 AND p.department = 'Electronics'
GROUP BY 1, 2, 3;

A snowflake schema normalizes dimensions further: product references a category table, which references a department table. It removes repeated names and can make shared hierarchies easier to maintain, but it adds joins to every query and complicates the SQL that analysts and BI tools generate. Because dimensions are small relative to facts, the redundancy a star keeps costs little, and most practitioners prefer stars, snowflaking only very large or shared sub-dimensions. Columnar engines also reduce the cost of wide denormalized tables, since unused columns are never read, which is why some teams go further and build fully flattened "one big table" models for specific dashboards.

When several fact tables share dimensions with the same keys and meanings (the same dim_date, dim_customer and dim_product for orders, shipments and returns), the dimensions are called conformed, and results from different processes can be combined reliably, for example return rate by product category, which divides one fact's aggregate by another's at the same dimensional grain.

10. Slowly changing dimensions

Dimension attributes change: a customer moves city, a product changes category, a tenant upgrades plan, a model's price per million tokens changes. The question is whether old facts should be reported under the old or the new value. Slowly changing dimension (SCD) techniques answer it. Type 1 overwrites the attribute in place: simple, no history; appropriate for corrections and for attributes whose history nobody needs. Type 2 preserves history: when the attribute changes, the current dimension row is closed (valid_to set, is_current false) and a new row is inserted with a new surrogate key, the new value and valid_from = now. New facts get the new surrogate key; old facts keep pointing to the old row. Now "revenue by customer city at the time of sale" is correct, and "revenue by customer's current city" is a join to the current row by the durable source id.

Type 2 is why warehouses use surrogate keys rather than source ids: the same source customer id corresponds to several dimension rows over time. The loading step must look up the dimension row valid at each fact's event time, an as-of join, not the current one. For an LLM cost warehouse, the model dimension is a natural type 2 candidate: if prices change on 1 March, calls before and after must be costed at their own prices, and a type 1 overwrite would silently revalue history.

11. Pre-aggregation: rollups, materialized views and cubes

Even a fast columnar scan of billions of rows costs seconds and money, and dashboards ask the same questions repeatedly. Warehouses therefore precompute. A rollup or aggregate table stores the fact table summarised at a coarser grain, for example revenue and units by day, product category and region instead of by order line. If the base fact has 2 billion rows and the rollup 3 million, every dashboard query against the rollup reads a thousandth of the data. The rollup must be maintained: rebuilt on a schedule, or incrementally updated for the most recent partitions, the same derived-copy discipline as db2's denormalization, with the base fact as the source of truth and a reconciliation check that rollup totals equal base totals.

Materialized views automate this inside the engine: the warehouse stores a query's result and keeps it up to date, and some engines (BigQuery, Snowflake, ClickHouse's materialized views and aggregating tables) can rewrite a query against the base table to read the view automatically when it answers the question. Classic OLAP cubes took the idea to its limit, precomputing aggregates for every combination of dimensions (a GROUP BY CUBE, db3) so any slice was a lookup; they are fast but grow exponentially with the number of dimensions, which is why modern columnar engines mostly replaced them with fast scans plus a few targeted rollups.

Some measures cannot be rolled up naively. Sums and counts add across groups, but averages must be stored as sum and count, distinct counts cannot be added (users active on Monday plus users active on Tuesday double-counts people active on both), and percentiles cannot be combined at all. Approximate sketches solve the distinct and percentile cases: HyperLogLog sketches can be merged to estimate distinct counts across groups, and t-digest or similar sketches can be merged to estimate latency percentiles. A rollup of LLM calls by day and model that stores token sums, call counts, a HyperLogLog of user ids and a latency sketch can answer cost, volume, distinct users and p95 latency for any date range by merging stored values.

12. A worked design: LLM usage analytics

Pull the lesson together on the warehouse an AI platform needs. The process is serving LLM calls; the grain is one row per call. The fact table fact_llm_call carries surrogate keys to dim_date, dim_tenant (type 2: plan and region change), dim_user, dim_model (type 2: provider, context window and price per million input and output tokens) and dim_prompt_version (prompt hash, template name, author, release date), plus measures: input tokens, output tokens, cost (computed at load time from the model row valid at the call's time), latency, status and cache-hit flag. A second fact table at the grain of one evaluation result shares dim_model and dim_prompt_version, so cost and quality can be compared per prompt version, a conformed-dimension join.

Physical design follows the access patterns: partition fact_llm_call by date (old partitions are cheap to drop or archive), cluster or sort by tenant within partitions so per-tenant dashboards skip most blocks, store it as Parquet or in a columnar warehouse, and feed it from the application's call log via CDC so dashboards are minutes behind production. The questions finance and product ask, cost per tenant per model per day, p95 latency by model and region, error rate after a prompt release, quality versus cost per prompt version, all become "filter dimensions, group by dimensions, aggregate measures" over columns that compress extremely well (dates, model keys and statuses run-length or dictionary encode to almost nothing).

Dremel's nested columnar encoding

Flat relational tables are straightforward to store by column. Google's data, however, was nested: protocol-buffer records with repeated and optional fields, such as a document with several links, each with several languages. Dremel's contribution was a lossless columnar representation of such records. For each field path, it stores the values in a column together with two small integers per value: a repetition level, which says at which nesting level a repeated field started repeating (so the reader knows whether a value starts a new record, a new parent element, or continues a list), and a definition level, which says how many of the optional or repeated fields on the path were actually present (so missing values at different depths can be told apart). With these, a query reading only Links.Forward reads one column and can still reconstruct which record and which link each value belonged to. Parquet adopted the same scheme. The paper also measured where columnar storage stops paying off: reading time grows with the number of fields, and the crossover with record-oriented storage often lay at dozens of fields, which is why columnar formats suit analytical queries that touch a small subset of a wide record.

Why vectorisation beats tuple-at-a-time

The iterator model calls next() on each operator for each row, so for a simple filter-and-sum over a billion rows the engine makes billions of virtual function calls, each with branching on types and NULLs. Modern CPUs execute simple arithmetic in a fraction of a nanosecond but pay many nanoseconds for mispredicted branches and cache misses. Vectorised engines amortise the per-call overhead over a vector of values and write each operator as a loop over primitive arrays with no per-value type dispatch, which compilers turn into SIMD instructions processing 4 to 16 values per instruction. The vector size is chosen to keep the working set in the CPU's L1 or L2 cache (around a thousand values). An alternative, used by HyPer and Umbra, is to compile each query into machine code that fuses operators into tight loops ("data-centric code generation"). Both approaches reach similar speeds and both are far faster than tuple-at-a-time interpretation; DuckDB, ClickHouse, Velox and DataFusion are vectorised, while several commercial systems combine both techniques.

Joins in a star schema

Star-schema queries have a characteristic shape: one huge fact table joined to several small, filtered dimensions. Engines exploit it. Each filtered dimension is small enough to build a hash table on (db7), so the fact table is scanned once and probed against each dimension's hash table in a pipeline. Better, the dimension filters can be pushed into the fact scan before the join: build a Bloom filter or a set of qualifying dimension keys from each filtered dimension and use it to skip fact rows, or, with zone maps, whole fact blocks, before any join work. This is sometimes called a semi-join reduction or runtime filter, and it means a query for one department and one month reads a small fraction of the fact table even though the filter was written on dimensions. Classic data warehouses called the same idea a star join or star transformation.

HTAP and the convergence of OLTP and OLAP

Running two systems with a pipeline between them adds latency and operational cost, so there is steady effort to serve both workloads from one system, called HTAP (hybrid transactional/analytical processing). Approaches include keeping a row store for transactions plus an in-memory column copy for analytics, kept in sync automatically (Oracle's in-memory column store, SQL Server columnstore indexes, SAP HANA); a row store replicated to a columnar replica (TiDB's TiFlash, AlloyDB's columnar engine); and columnar extensions for PostgreSQL. They work well for moderate scales and for "operational analytics" over fresh data. At large scale most organisations still separate the workloads, because isolation of resources and failure domains, different retention policies, and data from many sources favour a dedicated warehouse or lakehouse.

Inmon, Kimball and the data vault

There are competing philosophies of warehouse architecture. Kimball's dimensional approach builds star schemas per business process, tied together by conformed dimensions, and is optimised for query simplicity. Inmon's corporate information factory builds a normalized, enterprise-wide integrated warehouse first and derives dimensional data marts from it, favouring integration and consistency. The data vault methodology models hubs (business keys), links (relationships) and satellites (time-stamped attributes), insert-only, optimised for auditability and for loading from many changing sources, and is then typically projected into star schemas for consumption. Modern ELT practice often mixes them: raw landed data, a cleaned and integrated layer, and dimensional or wide "mart" tables for consumption, sometimes described as bronze, silver and gold layers in lakehouse terminology.

Dremel's numbers, and what they mean for your warehouse

Dremel's evaluation, on system instances running in shared data centres alongside other applications, makes the columnar argument with real workloads. On a table of 85 billion records using 3,000 nodes, a MapReduce job over record-oriented storage read 87 TB; the same job over columnar storage read about 0.5 TB and dropped from hours to minutes; Dremel, adding its serving tree and interactive design, answered in seconds. The paper reports that in a typical month most queries finished in under 10 seconds and some scanned close to 100 billion records per second. The lesson for any warehouse is the ordering of the levers: first read fewer bytes (columns, compression, pruning), then parallelise. A warehouse bill or a slow dashboard almost always traces to bytes scanned, which is why cloud warehouses such as BigQuery bill by bytes processed and why partitioning and clustering choices are cost decisions.

Common warehouse failures

Grain errors produce double-counting that looks plausible: a header-level amount repeated on line-level rows, or a join to a dimension that is accidentally not unique (two current rows for one customer after a buggy type 2 load), which fans out facts exactly like the db3 fan-out bug. Defences are uniqueness tests on dimension keys and current flags, and reconciliation of fact totals against source totals. Late-arriving data: events that arrive hours after their event time land in already-aggregated partitions, so pipelines must reprocess recent partitions or merge late rows. Schema drift: a source column renamed or retyped silently breaks transformations; CDC pipelines need schema-change handling and alerts. Small files: streaming ingestion that writes thousands of tiny Parquet files per hour destroys scan efficiency, since each file has fixed overhead; table formats provide compaction jobs to merge them. Unbounded costs: an analyst's SELECT * over a multi-terabyte table in a bytes-billed warehouse is a real bill, so require partition filters on large tables and set per-user quotas.

Data quality and contracts

Because warehouses aggregate data from many producers, quality problems compound. Teams increasingly treat important tables as products with data contracts: declared schemas, freshness targets (for example, yesterday's data complete by 06:00), and tests (non-null keys, unique keys, accepted values, referential integrity between facts and dimensions, row-count bounds) run on every load by tools such as dbt tests or Great Expectations. Failing tests block publication of downstream tables rather than letting a dashboard silently show a wrong number. For ML, the same tables feed training sets, so a silent quality regression becomes a model regression; point-in-time correctness (training features joined as of the label's time, the as-of join of type 2 dimensions) is the warehouse discipline that prevents leakage (hd8).

Analytics for AI products

AI products generate analytical data at high volume: every model call, retrieval, tool invocation, user rating and evaluation result. Three practical recommendations follow from this lesson. Log calls in the OLTP or logging path with stable ids and timestamps, and get them into a columnar store quickly via CDC or a stream, rather than running analytics on the production database. Model them dimensionally, with model and prompt versions as type 2 dimensions so cost and quality history stays correct when prices change or prompts are revised. And keep large payloads (prompts, completions, retrieved documents) out of the fact table: store them in object storage or a separate table keyed by call id, because columnar engines handle wide text columns poorly and most analytical queries never need them. Done this way, questions like "did the new prompt version reduce cost per resolved conversation for enterprise tenants without hurting ratings?" become a single star-schema query that runs in seconds.

Goal: measure the bytes a row layout and a column layout must read for the same query, see how much run-length and dictionary encoding shrink real-looking columns, and run a star-schema query. Python 3.12 standard library (SQLite itself is a row store; it stands in for the warehouse only to show the query shape). Save as db12.py; it builds a million rows, so allow a few seconds.

import array, random, sqlite3, time

random.seed(12)
N = 1_000_000
regions = ["north", "south", "east", "west"]
# one fact table: (day, region, product_id, qty, price_cents, customer_id, note)
rows = [(i // 3_000, random.choice(regions), random.randrange(500), random.randrange(1, 5),
         random.randrange(100, 10_000), random.randrange(100_000), "promo" if i % 10 == 0 else "")
        for i in range(N)]

# Row layout: each query walks whole rows. Column layout: one array per column.
cols = {"qty": array.array("i", (r[3] for r in rows)),
        "price": array.array("i", (r[4] for r in rows))}

s1 = sum(r[3] * r[4] for r in rows)
s2 = sum(q * p for q, p in zip(cols["qty"], cols["price"]))
assert s1 == s2                          # same answer; what differs is what must be read
row_bytes = N * (4 * 6 + 8)              # rough: six 4-byte numbers/codes + a short string
col_bytes = 2 * 4 * N                    # only the two columns the query needs
print(f"bytes a disk-based engine must read: rows ~{row_bytes/2**20:.0f} MiB vs columns {col_bytes/2**20:.0f} MiB")

# Compression that columns make possible
day_col = [r[0] for r in rows]                         # sorted by time: long runs
runs = 1 + sum(1 for a, b in zip(day_col, day_col[1:]) if a != b)
print(f"day column: {N:,} values -> {runs} run-length pairs ({N/runs:,.0f}x fewer)")
region_codes = {r: i for i, r in enumerate(regions)}   # dictionary encoding: 2 bits/value
print(f"region column: text ~{sum(len(r[1]) for r in rows)/2**20:.1f} MiB -> dictionary codes {N*2/8/2**20:.2f} MiB")

# A star schema query in SQLite
db = sqlite3.connect(":memory:")
db.executescript("""
CREATE TABLE dim_product (product_id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, category TEXT);
CREATE TABLE fact_sales (day INT, region TEXT, product_id INT, qty INT, price_cents INT);
""")
db.executemany("INSERT INTO dim_product VALUES (?, ?)", [(p, ["books", "games", "music"][p % 3]) for p in range(500)])
db.executemany("INSERT INTO fact_sales VALUES (?,?,?,?,?)", (r[:5] for r in rows))
q = """SELECT p.category, f.region, SUM(f.qty * f.price_cents) / 100 AS revenue
       FROM fact_sales f JOIN dim_product p USING (product_id)
       WHERE f.day BETWEEN 100 AND 130 GROUP BY 1, 2 ORDER BY revenue DESC LIMIT 3"""
t0 = time.perf_counter(); top = db.execute(q).fetchall()
print(f"star query (top category/region, days 100-130): {top}  {1000*(time.perf_counter()-t0):.0f} ms")

What to look for.

  1. About 31 MiB must be read in row form for a revenue query versus 8 MiB for the two needed columns, before compression: the column-pruning effect alone is about 4x here, and grows with table width.
  2. The time-sorted day column collapses from 1,000,000 values to 334 run-length pairs, about 3,000x; the four-value region column shrinks from about 4.3 MiB of text to 0.24 MiB of 2-bit dictionary codes.
  3. The star query returns the top three category-region pairs by revenue for days 100-130. In a columnar engine it would read the day, region, product and two measure columns, skip every block outside days 100-130 using zone maps, and join to the 500-row product dimension through a small hash table.

Try next. Shuffle the rows before computing the day runs (random.shuffle(rows)) and watch RLE collapse to nearly one run per value: order is what makes RLE and zone maps work. If you can install DuckDB in a virtual environment of your own (it is not installed here), write the table to Parquet and run the same query with EXPLAIN ANALYZE to see vectorised scans and filter pushdown.

Design: from an OLTP schema to a warehouse

An e-commerce PostgreSQL database has orders(order_id, customer_id, created_at, shipping_fee, status), order_lines(order_id, line_no, product_id, qty, unit_price), products(product_id, name, category_id), categories(category_id, name, department) and customers(customer_id, name, city, segment). Analysts want revenue and units by day, category, department, customer segment and city, with city as it was at the time of the order, plus average shipping fee per order by segment. Design the warehouse model, the load process, and point out the trap in the shipping fee.

Answer

Grain. The main process is selling products, so the fact table fact_order_line is one row per order line: keys to dim_date (order date), dim_product, dim_customer; degenerate dimension order_id (kept on the fact for counting distinct orders); measures qty, revenue = qty * unit_price.

Dimensions. dim_product as a star: product name, category name and department flattened onto each product row (snowflaking categories would add a join for little benefit). dim_customer as SCD type 2 on city and segment: surrogate key, source customer_id, attributes, valid_from, valid_to, is_current. dim_date with day, week, month, quarter, holiday flags.

The trap. Shipping fee is an order-level fact. Putting it on every order-line row means SUM(shipping_fee) counts it once per line. Either create a second fact table at order grain, fact_order(order_key, date_key, customer_key, shipping_fee, line_count), conformed on the same dimensions, or allocate the fee across lines (by revenue share) if a line-level number is truly needed and everyone agrees on the rule. Average fee per order by segment then comes from fact_order.

Load. CDC from PostgreSQL logical decoding into the lake; customer changes processed first into dim_customer (close the current row, insert the new one); order lines looked up against the customer row valid at orders.created_at (an as-of join), not the current row, so city-at-time-of-order is correct. Merges keyed on (order_id, line_no) make reloads idempotent; status changes (cancellations) handled either as updates in a mutable recent window or as negative adjustment rows. Physical: partition both facts by order date, cluster by customer segment; daily reconciliation of revenue and line counts against the source.
