A small online shop starts with one spreadsheet-shaped table because it is the quickest thing to build. Each row is one item in one order: the order number and date, the customer's id, email and city, the shipping region for that city, the product id, its SKU and price, and the quantity. Every report is a single SELECT. It works well for six months.

Then a customer, Ana, moves from Pune to Mumbai and updates her address. The update statement changes the city on her most recent order row but the developer forgot that her previous 37 orders also carry a city. Now the database says Ana lives in two cities, and the delivery report, which reads whichever row it finds first, sends a parcel to the old address. Next, the shop wants to add a new product to the catalogue before anyone has bought it, and finds there is nowhere to put it: every row is an order line, so a product with no orders cannot exist. Finally, a customer who placed a single order asks for it to be cancelled and deleted; when the row goes, so does the only record that the customer existed and where they live.

Those three failures have names, the update, insertion and deletion anomalies, and one cause: the table stores several different facts in one row, so each fact is repeated as many times as the rows it is mixed into. The theory that diagnoses this precisely is normalization. It gives you a way to write down which facts determine which (functional dependencies), an algorithm to find the keys from those rules, a test for whether splitting a table loses information, and a series of normal forms, each of which removes one kind of redundancy.

The theory also has an honest second half. Every split adds a join, and some read paths, such as a feed that shows comment counts or an analytics query over a billion rows, are better served by data that is deliberately stored redundantly. William Kent's well-known 1983 guide to the normal forms says it directly: there is no obligation to fully normalize when actual performance requirements are taken into account. This lesson teaches both halves: how to normalize correctly, step by step, on one real table, and how to denormalize on purpose without bringing the anomalies back.

One fact, one place

Think of the facts a business knows as a set of index cards. "Customer 7's email is ana@example.com." "Pune is in the West region." "Product 10 has SKU MUG-01 and costs ₹900." "Order 100 contains 2 of product 10." If each card is kept in exactly one drawer, filed under what it is about, then changing a fact means rewriting one card, adding a fact means adding one card, and removing one never accidentally removes another. Normalization is the discipline of filing every fact under the thing it is actually about.

The flat order table breaks this because its rows are not about one thing. A row is "about" an order line, but it also carries facts about the customer, the city and the product. Those facts are copied onto every order line they touch. The amount of copying is easy to estimate: if Ana has 38 order lines, her email and city are stored 38 times; if MUG-01 has been sold 10,000 times, its price is stored 10,000 times. Every copy is a place where the fact can go stale.

The analogy breaks in one place. Cards in drawers do not tell you what they are about; you decide when filing them. In a database, what a fact is "about" is captured by functional dependencies, rules of the form "if you know the customer id, you know the email". Writing those rules down is the real work of normalization; once they are written, the rest is mechanical.

The rules, in plain words

The normal forms are a ladder, each rung stricter than the one below. First normal form (1NF) says every cell holds one value: no lists of products in a column. Second normal form (2NF) says that when a table's key has several columns, no other column may depend on only some of them. Third normal form (3NF) says no column may depend on another non-key column. Kent's summary of 2NF and 3NF has stuck for forty years: every non-key field must provide a fact about the key, the whole key, and nothing but the key. Boyce-Codd normal form (BCNF) tightens 3NF to cover an edge case in which part of a key depends on something else. Fourth and fifth normal forms deal with facts that have several values, and they are rarer in practice.

How you get there

You never jump straight to the answer. You list the attributes, write down the functional dependencies from the business rules, compute the keys, then repeatedly find a dependency that breaks the target normal form and split the table along it: the dependency's left side plus what it determines becomes a new table, and the original keeps the left side as a foreign key. Each split must be lossless (joining the pieces gives back exactly the original rows), which is guaranteed when you split along a dependency like this. The body of this lesson walks the shop's flat table through every step.

And then you decide where to stop, or to go back. Normalization is the right default for systems that change data one row at a time (OLTP: online transaction processing), because there the cost of an anomaly is a wrong answer. Systems that load data in bulk and read it in aggregate (OLAP: online analytical processing, db12), or specific hot read paths, are where deliberate denormalization pays.

The common cause is that the row mixes facts with different subjects. To fix it systematically we need a precise way to say what each fact is about.

2. Functional dependencies

A functional dependency (FD) X → Y, read "X determines Y", says: any two tuples that agree on all attributes in X must agree on all attributes in Y. X is the determinant. For the shop, the business rules give these FDs:

  1. order_id → placed_at, customer_id (an order is placed once, by one customer)
  2. customer_id → email, city (a customer has one email and one city)
  3. city → region (each city belongs to one shipping region)
  4. product_id → sku, price (a product has one SKU and one current price)
  5. sku → product_id (SKUs are unique to products)
  6. order_id, product_id → qty (each product appears once per order, with one quantity)

Two things to notice. First, FDs come from the meaning of the data, not from looking at it. In the five rows above, email → city happens to hold, and so does placed_at → region if you squint, but neither is a rule, and a schema built on them would break with the next row. Second, a dependency like {order_id, product_id} → product_id is trivial: the right side is part of the left, so it is always true and tells you nothing.

FDs can be combined to derive others. The rules for doing so, Armstrong's axioms, are three: reflexivity (if Y ⊆ X then X → Y, the trivial ones), augmentation (if X → Y then XZ → YZ for any Z) and transitivity (if X → Y and Y → Z then X → Z). Useful derived rules follow: union (X → Y and X → Z give X → YZ) and decomposition (X → YZ gives X → Y and X → Z). For example, from rules 1 and 2 by transitivity, order_id → email; then with rule 3, order_id → region. Every FD that logically follows from a set can be derived this way, and nothing else can.

3. Attribute closure: computing keys by hand

Deriving FDs one at a time is tedious. The practical tool is the closure of a set of attributes X, written X⁺: every attribute X determines. The algorithm is a loop: start with result = X; repeatedly look for an FD whose left side is entirely inside result and add its right side; stop when nothing changes.

Compute {order_id}⁺. Start: {order_id}. Rule 1 applies (left side order_id is in result): add placed_at, customer_id. Rule 2 now applies (customer_id is in): add email, city. Rule 3 applies (city): add region. Rules 4, 5 and 6 need product_id or sku, which are not in result. No more changes. So {order_id}⁺ = {order_id, placed_at, customer_id, email, city, region}: six of the ten attributes, missing product_id, sku, price and qty. Therefore order_id alone is not a key.

Now {order_id, product_id}⁺. Start with both; rules 1, 2 and 3 add the order and customer attributes as before; rule 4 adds sku and price; rule 6 adds qty. That is all ten attributes, so {order_id, product_id} is a superkey. Is it minimal? Removing product_id gives the six-attribute closure above; removing order_id gives {product_id}⁺ = {product_id, sku, price}. Neither alone is a superkey, so {order_id, product_id} is a candidate key. By the same method, {order_id, sku}⁺: rule 5 gives product_id, then everything as before, so {order_id, sku} is a second candidate key. The lab's script finds exactly these two by computing the closure of every subset.

The closure is also how you test whether a dependency is implied: X → Y holds exactly when Y ⊆ X⁺. And it gives the vocabulary the normal forms need. A prime attribute belongs to some candidate key (here order_id, product_id and sku); every other attribute is non-prime.

4. Decomposition, and how to split without losing information

Normalizing means decomposing a relation into smaller ones, each with a subset of the attributes. The danger is that a careless split loses information. Take three order lines (order 100 with 2 of product 10 and 1 of product 12; order 101 with 1 of product 10) and split them into (order_id, qty) and (qty, product_id, sku). Rejoining on the shared column qty gives five rows, not three: order 100 now appears to contain 1 of product 10 as well as 2, and order 101 appears to contain product 12. Those invented rows are spurious tuples, and the decomposition is called lossy, which is a confusing name, since it produced more rows; what is lost is the knowledge of which rows were real.

The test for a lossless split of R into R1 and R2 is simple: the shared attributes R1 ∩ R2 must be a superkey of R1 or of R2 (equivalently, (R1 ∩ R2)⁺ includes all of R1 or all of R2). Splitting on qty fails, because qty determines nothing. Splitting into (product_id, sku) and (order_id, product_id, qty) passes: the shared attribute product_id is the key of the first part, so each order line joins to exactly one product row and nothing is invented. The lab shows both rejoins.

There is a recipe that is always lossless: when an FD X → Y violates a normal form, split R into (X ∪ Y) and (R − Y). The shared attributes are X, which is a key of the first part by construction. Every normalization step below uses this recipe.

A second, separate property is dependency preservation: every original FD can be checked within one of the resulting tables. It matters because an FD that spans tables can only be enforced by a join, a trigger or application code, whereas an FD inside a table is enforced by a key or unique constraint. Lossless is mandatory; dependency preservation is highly desirable and, as section 8 shows, occasionally impossible together with BCNF.

5. First normal form

A relation is in 1NF when every attribute holds a single atomic value. The classic violation is a repeating group: an order row with products = 'MUG-01:2, CAP-RED:1', or columns product1, product2, product3. Both are bad for the same reasons. The database cannot enforce that each product exists (no foreign key into a string), cannot index "orders containing CAP-RED", and cannot add a fourth product without a schema change or a string edit. The fix is one row per product, which is exactly what our order_flat already does, so it is in 1NF.

"Atomic" is relative to how you use the data. A date is atomic for most applications even though it has a year, a month and a day; a full name stored in one column is fine if you never sort by surname. The test is practical: if you need to query, constrain or update a part of the value on its own, the part should be its own attribute or row.

6. Second normal form: the whole key

A relation is in 2NF if it is in 1NF and no non-prime attribute depends on a proper subset of a candidate key, a partial dependency. 2NF only matters when a key has several attributes. In order_flat the candidate key is (order_id, product_id), and the dependency diagram shows the partial dependencies: order_id → placed_at, customer_id (and through it email, city, region) and product_id → sku, price.

Split along each with the recipe. On order_id → placed_at, customer_id, email, city, region (using the closure): new table orders(order_id, placed_at, customer_id, email, city, region), key order_id. On product_id → sku, price: new table product(product_id, sku, price), keys product_id and sku. What remains is order_line(order_id, product_id, qty), key (order_id, product_id), with foreign keys to orders and product. All three tables are in 2NF: orders and product have single-attribute keys, so partial dependencies are impossible, and in order_line qty depends on the whole key.

Check the anomalies. The product price is now stored once, so a price change is one update and the new PEN-BLK product can be inserted before anyone orders it. But Ana's email and city are still repeated on each of her orders, so the update anomaly for customers is still there.

7. Third normal form: nothing but the key

A relation is in 3NF if, for every non-trivial FD X → A that holds in it, either X is a superkey or A is a prime attribute. The everyday reading: no non-prime attribute depends on anything other than a key, so there are no transitive dependencies such as key → B → C where B is not a key.

The orders table has two: order_id → customer_id → email, city and customer_id → city → region. Split on customer_id → email, city, region (its closure within this table): customer(customer_id, email, city, region) and orders becomes orders(order_id, placed_at, customer_id). The customer table still has city → region with city not a key, so split again: city(city, region) and customer(customer_id, email, city).

The final schema has five tables:

city(city PK, region)
customer(customer_id PK, email UNIQUE, city FK→city)
orders(order_id PK, placed_at, customer_id FK→customer)
product(product_id PK, sku UNIQUE, price)
order_line(order_id FK→orders, product_id FK→product, qty, PRIMARY KEY (order_id, product_id))

Every original FD is now the key (or a unique constraint) of exactly one table, so the decomposition preserves dependencies, and each split used the lossless recipe. Replay the anomalies: changing Ana's email is one row in customer; a customer with no orders can be inserted; deleting order 102 leaves Li, Delhi and North intact. You may notice the schema is exactly what an ER model of the shop gives (db1). That is not a coincidence: a good ER model usually produces a schema in 3NF, and normalization is the check that you did not miss a dependency.

8. Boyce-Codd normal form, and the case where it costs something

A relation is in BCNF if for every non-trivial FD X → Y, X is a superkey. Compare with 3NF: BCNF drops the escape clause "or A is prime". For most tables the two coincide; the five shop tables are all in BCNF (in product, sku → product_id has sku as a candidate key, so it is fine). They differ only when a non-key attribute determines part of a candidate key.

The standard example: a tutoring service where each student takes courses, each tutor teaches exactly one course, and for each course a student has one tutor. Relation enrol(student, course, tutor) with FDs student, course → tutor and tutor → course. Closures: (student, course)⁺ = all; (student, tutor)⁺ = {student, tutor, course} = all; (course, tutor)⁺ = {course, tutor}, missing student. So there are two candidate keys, (student, course) and (student, tutor), and every attribute is prime.

3NF check: tutor → course has a non-superkey on the left, but course is prime, so 3NF allows it. BCNF check: tutor is not a superkey, so BCNF is violated. And there is real redundancy: if tutor T1 teaches Databases to 200 students, "T1 teaches Databases" is stored 200 times.

Decompose with the recipe on tutor → course: tutor_course(tutor, course) with key tutor, and student_tutor(student, tutor) with key (student, tutor). It is lossless (shared attribute tutor is the key of tutor_course) and both parts are in BCNF. But the FD student, course → tutor now spans two tables: nothing in either table stops student S1 being assigned tutors T1 and T2 who both teach Databases. To enforce "one tutor per course per student" you now need a join in a trigger or a check in the application. This is the theorem in miniature: every relation has a lossless, dependency-preserving decomposition into 3NF, but not always into BCNF. When the lost dependency is an important rule, staying in 3NF is a legitimate design decision.

9. Denormalization: what it is and when it pays

Denormalization is deliberately storing a fact redundantly, or storing a precomputed join or aggregate, so that reads are cheaper. It is a performance decision, so it starts with a measured read path, not a feeling. Four forms are common.

Copying an attribute across a relationship. Storing customer_name on orders so the order list does not join customers. Usually not worth it: a primary-key join costs a few page reads. It becomes worth it when the join crosses a service or shard boundary.

Storing an aggregate. A posts.comment_count column, so a feed of 50 posts does not run 50 COUNT(*) queries or one large grouped join. Worth it when the aggregate is read far more often than the underlying rows change.

Materializing a query. A materialized view stores the result of a query and is refreshed on a schedule or on demand. In PostgreSQL, REFRESH MATERIALIZED VIEW CONCURRENTLY rebuilds it without blocking readers, provided the view has a unique index. Good for dashboards that can be minutes stale.

Embedding a child collection. A document database (db11) storing an order with its lines inside, or a JSON column holding a small list that is always read with its parent.

The cost of any of these is that writes now have two places to update, and the anomalies come back unless the copy has a defined maintenance mechanism. Work the comment-count example through. Options, from tightest to loosest consistency: increment the counter in the same transaction that inserts the comment (always correct, but every comment on a viral post now updates one hot row, and the comment insert and the counter update share a lock); a trigger on comment that does the same thing automatically (same behaviour, harder to forget, also harder to see); an asynchronous job that consumes comment events and applies increments in batches (no hot-row contention, count lags by seconds); or a periodic full recount (simplest, stale between runs). Whichever you choose, keep the normalized truth (the comment rows) and run a reconciliation query, SELECT p.post_id FROM posts p WHERE p.comment_count <> (SELECT count(*) FROM comment c WHERE c.post_id = p.post_id) on a sample, to detect drift.

A useful middle ground for values derived from the same row is a generated column, such as total_cents integer GENERATED ALWAYS AS (qty * unit_price_cents) STORED: the database computes it on every write, so it can never drift, and it can be indexed.

10. Normalizing out loud: a method for interviews

Interviewers who ask normalization questions are usually testing whether you can reason from rules rather than recite definitions. A reliable method has five steps, and saying each step out loud is half the answer.

Step 1: name the attributes and ask for the rules. Do not guess dependencies from sample rows. Ask: can a customer have two emails? Can a product's price change, and do old orders keep the old price? Can one product appear twice in an order? Each answer is a functional dependency or the absence of one, and the questions themselves show the interviewer you know FDs come from meaning.

Step 2: write the FDs as a numbered list with single attributes or small groups on the left. Mark anything that is derived (like a date from a timestamp) or historical (the price at the time of sale) because those are where most mistakes hide.

Step 3: find the candidate keys with closures. A quick shortcut: any attribute that never appears on the right side of an FD must be in every key, so start from those and add attributes until the closure covers everything. In the shop example, order_id and product_id (or sku) never appear on a right side except via sku ↔ product_id, which is why the keys contain order_id plus one of them.

Step 4: walk the FDs and classify each one. Left side a superkey: fine. Left side part of a key and right side non-prime: partial dependency, a 2NF violation. Left side non-key and right side non-prime: transitive, a 3NF violation. Left side not a superkey but right side prime: allowed by 3NF, a BCNF violation only.

Step 5: split with the recipe and check both properties. For each violating X → Y, create (X ∪ Y) and remove Y from the original. Then state that each split is lossless because X is the key of the new table, and check whether every FD still lives inside one table. If one does not, say so and offer the 3NF alternative.

Finish by naming the trade-off you would make in the real system: which read paths might need a denormalized copy, and how you would maintain it. That one sentence turns a textbook answer into an engineering answer.

11. A walk-through: normalizing an LLM evaluation log

An AI team has been logging evaluation runs to one wide table: eval_log(run_id, run_started_at, model_name, model_provider, price_per_mtok, prompt_version, prompt_text, case_id, case_input, expected_output, output, score). Write down the FDs: run_id → run_started_at, model_name, prompt_version; model_name → model_provider, price_per_mtok; prompt_version → prompt_text; case_id → case_input, expected_output; run_id, case_id → output, score. The key is (run_id, case_id).

The anomalies are real and expensive. A 2,000-case suite run 300 times stores each prompt text 2,000 times per run, and each test case's input and expected output 300 times; a price change for a model must update every row for that model, so cost reports silently mix old and new prices; and deleting old runs deletes the only copy of retired test cases. Normalizing gives model(model_name, provider, price_per_mtok), prompt(prompt_version, prompt_text), eval_case(case_id, input, expected_output), eval_run(run_id, started_at, model_name, prompt_version) and eval_result(run_id, case_id, output, score). Notice the subtle point about price: if reports need the price at the time of the run, that is a different fact, about the run, and belongs on eval_run as price_per_mtok_at_run. Normalization does not decide that; the meaning of the fact does.

The dashboard that shows mean score per prompt version per day is then a materialized view over eval_run joined to eval_result, refreshed after each run: denormalized for reading, derived from a normalized source.

Canonical covers and the 3NF synthesis algorithm

Real schemas come with dozens of FDs, some redundant. A canonical (minimal) cover is an equivalent set with three properties: every right side is a single attribute, no left side has an attribute that could be removed without changing what the set implies, and no FD can be removed entirely. You compute it with closures. For example, given A → B, B → C, A → C, the last is redundant: compute A⁺ using only the other two and it still contains C. Given AB → C and A → C, the B in AB → C is extraneous, because A alone already reaches C. The 3NF synthesis algorithm (due to Bernstein, 1976) then builds the schema directly: for each group of FDs in the cover with the same left side X, make a table X ∪ (their right sides); if no table contains a candidate key of the original relation, add one table holding a key; remove tables contained in others. The result is always in 3NF, always lossless (thanks to the key table) and always dependency-preserving (each FD has its own table). That guarantee is why textbooks say 3NF is "always achievable" while BCNF is not.

Fourth normal form: independent multi-valued facts

Functional dependencies cover single-valued facts. Some facts are multi-valued: an employee has several skills and, independently, speaks several languages. Storing both in emp_skill_lang(employee, skill, language) forces a row for every combination, because leaving one out would falsely imply a link between that skill and that language. With 3 skills and 4 languages, 12 rows hold 7 facts, and adding a fifth language means adding 3 rows. The table has no non-trivial FDs at all (all three columns form the key), so it is in BCNF, yet it is clearly redundant. The concept that captures it is the multivalued dependency employee ↠ skill: for a given employee, the set of skills is independent of the other attributes. 4NF requires that every non-trivial multivalued dependency's left side is a superkey; the fix is (employee, skill) and (employee, language). The practical signal is a table whose key is all its columns and which combines two lists about the same thing.

Fifth normal form: when only three tables will do

Kent's example is agents, companies and products. If the rule is simply "agent A sells product P for company C", the three-column table is the only correct representation: Smith sells Ford cars and GM trucks but not Ford trucks, so no pair of two-column tables could reconstruct that. But suppose a business rule says: if an agent represents a company, and that company makes a product, and the agent sells that product type, then the agent sells it for that company. Then the three-column table is redundant, and it can be split into (agent, company), (company, product) and (agent, product), whose three-way join reproduces it exactly, while no two of them would. That is a join dependency, and 5NF (also called project-join normal form) says every join dependency is implied by the candidate keys. It is rare in practice because it depends on such symmetric rules, but it is the reason "lossless" is defined in terms of joins of any number of projections, not just two.

Normalization and NULLs

The normal forms are defined over relations without NULLs, and NULLs muddy them. Is customer_id → email violated if two rows for customer 7 have email 'a@x' and NULL? SQL's unique constraints treat NULLs as distinct by default (PostgreSQL 15 added NULLS NOT DISTINCT to change that), so a unique index on email permits many NULLs. A common design consequence is to move optional, sparsely populated attributes into their own table with a one-to-one relationship, which removes the NULLs and keeps every column's meaning crisp; the extreme form of this, one table per attribute keyed by entity and time, is sometimes called 6NF and is used in temporal and data-vault warehouse designs.

Why analytics flips the trade-off

Normalization assumes, as Kent noted, that every non-key field may be updated frequently. That is true of OLTP systems, where a user changes an address and the change must be visible everywhere immediately. Analytical systems invert the assumptions: data arrives in bulk, is rarely updated in place, and is read by queries that scan millions of rows and group them. In that world, a join to a dimension table for every row scanned is a real cost and update anomalies are rare, because history is appended rather than edited. That is why star schemas (db12) keep a normalized fact table of events but deliberately denormalized dimension tables (a product dimension holding category and department names inline), and why ML feature tables are wide, flat rows keyed by entity and time. The redundancy is managed by the pipeline that rebuilds them, not by the database's constraints, which is acceptable precisely because the tables are derived, not authoritative.

Normal forms and performance, concretely

It helps to put rough numbers on the join cost that people fear. Fetching an order and its customer by primary key in a B-tree index costs a handful of page reads per table (db5), almost all from memory in a warm database, so microseconds. Denormalizing that join saves almost nothing. Where joins hurt is fan-out and aggregation: rendering a feed of 50 posts, each with an author, a comment count and a like count, could mean 150 lookups, or one large grouped query over the comments table. That is where a stored count wins by orders of magnitude. The general rule is to denormalize aggregates and cross-boundary joins, and keep row-to-row primary-key joins normalized.

Drift in denormalized data

The characteristic production failure of denormalization is silent drift: the copy and the truth disagree, and nothing alerts. Causes are mundane. A new code path inserts comments through a bulk import that bypasses the service that increments the counter. A transaction updates the counter and then fails after the counter update was made in a separate connection. An asynchronous consumer is down for an hour and its offset is reset past unprocessed events. A developer runs a manual DELETE to remove spam. In each case the stored count is now wrong forever unless something recomputes it. The defence is a reconciliation job that recomputes the derived value for a sample (or all) of rows, compares, logs and repairs differences, and alerts when the drift rate rises. Teams that denormalize without one usually discover the drift from a customer.

Hot rows

A counter column on a popular row is a concurrency bottleneck, independent of correctness. Each increment is an update to the same row, so concurrent transactions serialise on the row lock; in PostgreSQL each update also writes a new row version that vacuum must later clean up (db10). At a few hundred increments per second on one row, transactions start queueing and latency climbs; at a few thousand, timeouts. Common fixes: keep the source events as rows and update the count asynchronously in batches (one update per second carrying +487 instead of 487 updates); split the counter into N sub-rows chosen at random and sum them on read; or keep the hot counter in an in-memory store and persist it periodically. All of these accept a slightly stale count in exchange for removing the contention.

Materialized view refresh in practice

A plain REFRESH MATERIALIZED VIEW in PostgreSQL takes a lock that blocks reads of the view for the whole refresh, which for a dashboard over a large table may be minutes. The CONCURRENTLY option avoids blocking readers by computing the new result and applying only the differences, but it requires a unique index on the view covering all rows, and it does more work overall, so it is slower when much of the view changes. Refreshes are full recomputations (PostgreSQL has no built-in incremental maintenance), so a view over a billion-row table refreshed every five minutes may never finish before the next one starts. Watch refresh duration as a metric and alert when it approaches the refresh interval.

Normalizing a live table

Moving from a denormalized design to a normalized one in production is a migration with the same shape as any other (db14): create the new tables; backfill them from the old table while resolving conflicts (the GROUP BY ... HAVING COUNT(DISTINCT region) > 1 query finds the cities already stored with two regions); write to both for a while; switch reads; stop writing the old columns; drop them. The conflict resolution step is where teams discover how much drift the denormalized design has already caused, which is itself a useful argument for the project.

JSON columns as a release valve

Modern relational databases store JSON efficiently (PostgreSQL's jsonb is a parsed binary format that can be indexed with GIN), so teams increasingly keep a metadata jsonb column for sparse, evolving attributes. That is a pragmatic 1NF exception, and it works when the JSON is read as a whole and filtered on by a few known keys. The production trouble starts when important fields migrate into JSON: no type checking, no NOT NULL, no foreign keys, statistics that do not see inside the document so the planner misestimates filters on it, and updates that rewrite the whole value. A healthy pattern is to promote a field to a real column once queries depend on it, with an expression index or generated column as the bridge.

Feature and logging tables for AI systems

AI applications produce two kinds of table that tempt denormalization. Logs of LLM calls (db1) are append-only and huge, so storing the model name and prompt version on every row as short strings is fine, but copying the full prompt text or model pricing onto each row repeats kilobytes or invites drift; reference them. Feature tables for ML models are wide by design and are best treated as derived artefacts: rebuilt by pipelines from normalized sources, versioned, and never edited by hand. Getting this boundary right (normalized sources of truth, denormalized derived views) is the same principle as the comment counter at a larger scale.

Goal: compute attribute closures and candidate keys for the shop's flat table, list its BCNF violations, and see a lossy decomposition invent rows. Python 3.12 standard library only. Save as db2.py and run it.

from itertools import combinations
import sqlite3

# --- Part 1: functional dependencies, closures and keys -------------------
R = {"order_id", "placed_at", "customer_id", "email", "city", "region",
     "product_id", "sku", "price", "qty"}
FDS = [({"order_id"}, {"placed_at", "customer_id"}),
       ({"customer_id"}, {"email", "city"}),
       ({"city"}, {"region"}),
       ({"product_id"}, {"sku", "price"}),
       ({"sku"}, {"product_id"}),
       ({"order_id", "product_id"}, {"qty"})]

def closure(attrs, fds):
    result = set(attrs)
    changed = True
    while changed:
        changed = False
        for lhs, rhs in fds:
            if lhs <= result and not rhs <= result:
                result |= rhs
                changed = True
    return result

def candidate_keys(rel, fds):
    keys = []
    for size in range(1, len(rel) + 1):
        for combo in combinations(sorted(rel), size):
            s = set(combo)
            if any(k <= s for k in keys):
                continue            # contains a smaller key: superkey, not minimal
            if closure(s, fds) >= rel:
                keys.append(s)
    return keys

print("{order_id}+ =", sorted(closure({"order_id"}, FDS)))
print("candidate keys:", [sorted(k) for k in candidate_keys(R, FDS)])
for lhs, rhs in FDS:
    ok = closure(lhs, FDS) >= R
    print(f"  {sorted(lhs)} -> {sorted(rhs)}: {'ok' if ok else 'BCNF violation'}")

# --- Part 2: a lossy and a lossless decomposition -------------------------
db = sqlite3.connect(":memory:")
db.executescript("""
CREATE TABLE line(order_id, product_id, sku, qty);
INSERT INTO line VALUES (100,10,'MUG-01',2),(100,12,'CAP-RED',1),(101,10,'MUG-01',1);
CREATE TABLE a AS SELECT DISTINCT order_id, qty FROM line;          -- lossy split
CREATE TABLE b AS SELECT DISTINCT qty, product_id, sku FROM line;
CREATE TABLE p AS SELECT DISTINCT product_id, sku FROM line;        -- lossless split
CREATE TABLE ol AS SELECT DISTINCT order_id, product_id, qty FROM line;
""")
lossy = db.execute("SELECT order_id, product_id, qty FROM a JOIN b USING (qty)").fetchall()
good = db.execute("SELECT order_id, product_id, qty FROM ol JOIN p USING (product_id)").fetchall()
print("original rows :", 3)
print("lossy rejoin  :", len(lossy), sorted(lossy))
print("lossless      :", len(good), sorted(good))

What to look for.

  1. {order_id}+ lists six attributes, exactly the closure computed by hand in section 3: order_id is not a key.
  2. The candidate keys are [order_id, product_id] and [order_id, sku]. The search skips any set containing a smaller key, which is what "minimal" means.
  3. Every dependency except the one on the whole key is reported as a BCNF violation. Each line is a split you would make; compare with the decomposition tree in the body.
  4. The lossy rejoin returns 5 rows from 3, including (100, 10, 1) and (101, 12, 1): order lines that never existed. The lossless rejoin on product_id returns exactly the 3 originals.

Normalize: a clinic appointments sheet

A clinic exports appointments as one table: appt(appt_id, appt_time, patient_id, patient_name, patient_phone, doctor_id, doctor_name, specialty, room, room_floor, fee). Rules: each appointment has one patient, one doctor and one room at one time; a doctor has one specialty; the fee is set by the specialty; each room is on one floor; a doctor is assigned one room for the whole day, so (doctor_id, date of appt_time) → room. List the FDs, find the key, name the normal-form violations, and give the normalized schema.

Answer

FDs. appt_id → appt_time, patient_id, doctor_id, room; patient_id → patient_name, patient_phone; doctor_id → doctor_name, specialty; specialty → fee; room → room_floor; (doctor_id, appt_date) → room, where appt_date is derived from appt_time.

Key. appt_id⁺ reaches every attribute through the chains, so appt_id alone is the key. With a single-attribute key there are no partial dependencies, so the table is in 2NF.

Violations. All transitive: appt_id → patient_id → name, phone; appt_id → doctor_id → specialty → fee; appt_id → room → floor. So it is not in 3NF. The room rule is the interesting one: room depends on (doctor_id, date), not on the appointment as such, so storing room per appointment lets two appointments of the same doctor on one day disagree.

Schema (3NF and BCNF).

patient(patient_id PK, name, phone)
specialty(specialty PK, fee)
doctor(doctor_id PK, name, specialty FK)
room(room PK, floor)
doctor_day(doctor_id FK, day, room FK, PRIMARY KEY (doctor_id, day))
appointment(appt_id PK, appt_time, patient_id FK, doctor_id FK)

The room for an appointment is found by joining appointment to doctor_day on (doctor_id, date(appt_time)). Fee note: if the fee charged must stay fixed even when the specialty fee changes later, it is a fact about the appointment (the price agreed), so fee_charged belongs on appointment as well. Say that distinction out loud in an interview; it shows you reason from meaning, not just from the FD list.
