You have designed a neural network with 25 million weights. To train it you need one piece of information for each weight: if I nudged this weight up a tiny amount, would the model's error go up or down, and by how much? That number is the weight's gradient, and gradient descent (ml4) cannot move without it. The obvious way to get it is to try: increase weight number 1 by a hair, run the whole network on the data, and see how the error changed; then weight number 2; and so on. That is 25 million and one runs of the network for a single step of training. If one run takes 10 milliseconds, one step takes almost three days — and a real training job takes hundreds of thousands of steps.

Backpropagation gets all 25 million numbers for the price of roughly two or three runs of the network. Not two or three per weight: two or three in total. That is a saving of about ten million times, and it is the reason neural networks with millions or billions of weights can be trained at all. Everything else in deep learning — bigger models, better architectures, GPUs, distributed training — sits on top of that one algorithm. The trick is not deep mathematics; it is bookkeeping. The error at the end of the network is passed backwards, and every layer re-uses the work of the layer after it, so nothing is computed twice.

Understanding it well is not only for theory questions. The same mechanism explains why a job that trains happily at 1 billion parameters dies with an out-of-memory error at 7 billion — and dies specifically at the moment loss.backward() starts, not before. It explains why PyTorch refuses to run when you modify a tensor "in place", why calling backward() twice makes gradients double, why one forgotten detach() can leave half of a model silently untrained, and why training costs about three times as much compute as inference. When an interviewer asks "your model isn't learning — what do you check?" or "how would you fit a bigger batch on this GPU?", the answer starts with a picture of the computation graph.

This lesson builds that picture from scratch. We start with the chain rule on one variable and check it with real numbers, then turn a small network into a graph and run a complete forward and backward pass by hand — every number shown, and verified against PyTorch. We see how the same rules become matrix formulas, why softmax and cross-entropy combine into the tidy p − y, why going backwards is so much cheaper than going forwards, what autograd stores and why that becomes the memory bottleneck, how gradient checkpointing trades compute for memory, and how PyTorch's autograd works and breaks. The public case is Chen et al.'s 2016 paper that shrank the memory of a 1,000-layer network from 48 GB to 7 GB.

A train of gears

Picture a chain of gears. Turn the first gear by one degree and the second turns by, say, 2 degrees; the second drives the third, which turns 3 degrees for every degree of the second; the third drives the fourth at a ratio of 0.5. How far does the last gear turn per degree of the first? You multiply the ratios: 2 × 3 × 0.5 = 3 degrees. That multiplication of local ratios along a chain is the chain rule of calculus, and it is the only mathematical idea in backpropagation.

Now imagine the last gear is attached to a dial that shows the model's error, and the first gear is a weight in the network. The question training asks — "if I turn this weight a tiny bit, how much does the error dial move?" — is answered by multiplying the ratios of all the gears in between. Backpropagation is a smart way of doing that multiplication for every weight at once: it starts at the dial and works backwards, and at each gear it keeps a running product, so the gears near the dial are multiplied only once no matter how many weights sit further back.

Where the analogy breaks: real networks are not single chains. A value can be used in several places (one gear driving two others), and paths that split must be added back together. And a "gear ratio" here is a slope measured at the current settings, valid only for tiny turns — after the weights change, the ratios change too, so the whole measurement must be repeated at every training step.

The pieces, defined

A neuron takes some inputs, multiplies each by a weight, adds them up with a bias (a learned constant), and passes the result through a non-linear activation function: output = activation(w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + … + b). A layer is many neurons side by side reading the same inputs; in matrix form h = activation(W x + b). A network stacks layers, so the outputs of one become the inputs of the next.

  • Input layer: the features (pixels, word embeddings, numbers).
  • Hidden layers: intermediate representations the network invents for itself.
  • Output layer: the prediction — one number for regression, or class scores (logits) that softmax turns into probabilities for classification.

A network made only of fully connected layers is a multi-layer perceptron (MLP). With enough hidden units and a non-linear activation it can approximate any reasonable function (the universal approximation theorem); the practical questions are whether training can find good weights and whether they generalise.

The derivative of a function at a point is its slope there: how much the output changes per tiny change of the input. If f(x) = x², the derivative is 2x: at x = 3 the slope is 6, so raising x from 3 to 3.001 raises f from 9 to about 9.006. When a function has several inputs, its partial derivative with respect to one input is the slope along that input with the others held fixed, and the vector of all partial derivatives is the gradient.

One training step, four moves

  1. Forward pass: feed an input through the layers to get a prediction.
  2. Loss: compare the prediction with the target and reduce the mismatch to one number (ml5).
  3. Backward pass (backpropagation): compute the gradient of the loss with respect to every weight.
  4. Update: an optimiser nudges each weight against its gradient.

Only the third step is backpropagation. It does not change any weight; it only produces the slopes. That separation matters: what to do with the gradients (plain gradient descent, momentum, Adam) is the subject of dl5, and this lesson is entirely about how the gradients are produced.

The smallest possible example

One neuron with no activation: ŷ = w·x, and the squared-error loss L = (ŷ − y)². Let x = 2, target y = 10, and weight w = 3. Forward: ŷ = 6, error d = ŷ − y = −4, L = 16. Backward, gear by gear from the loss: the last operation is squaring, whose local slope is 2d = −8, so ∂L/∂d = −8. Subtracting the constant y has slope 1, so ∂L/∂ŷ = −8 as well. Multiplying by x has slope x with respect to w, so ∂L/∂w = −8 × 2 = −16. (It also has slope w with respect to x: ∂L/∂x = −8 × 3 = −24, useful when x is the output of an earlier layer.) The negative gradient says "raising w lowers the loss"; with learning rate 0.05, w becomes 3 + 0.8 = 3.8, ŷ becomes 7.6, and the loss falls from 16 to 5.76.

In a deep network the chain rule simply goes on: the gradient arriving at a layer is multiplied by that layer's local slope and passed further back. Frameworks record the operations while the forward pass runs and replay them backwards automatically — that machinery is autograd (automatic differentiation), and understanding what it records is the second half of this lesson.

1. The chain rule on numbers

Before graphs, one small check that "multiply the local slopes" really works. Let y = 3x and z = y², so z is a function of x through y. At x = 2: y = 6 and z = 36. The local slopes are dy/dx = 3 and dz/dy = 2y = 12. The chain rule says dz/dx = dz/dy × dy/dx = 12 × 3 = 36. Test it by brute force: nudge x to 2.001, so y = 6.003 and z = 36.036009. The change in z is 0.036009 for a change of 0.001 in x — a slope of 36.009, matching 36 (the small excess is the curvature the tiny nudge cannot avoid). The slope of the whole chain really is the product of the slopes of its links.

Two rules cover everything else in this lesson.

  • Chains multiply. If x affects z only through y, then ∂z/∂x = (∂z/∂y)·(∂y/∂x).
  • Branches add. If x affects z through two separate routes, the total effect is the sum of the effects along each route. Section 3 shows this with numbers.

2. The computation graph and the forward pass

Any computation a network performs can be written as a list of simple operations, each with one or two inputs and one output: add, multiply, matrix multiply, ReLU, exp, log. Draw each value as a node and each operation as the arrows that connect its inputs to its output, and you get a computation graph: a directed graph with no cycles. The forward pass evaluates it from the inputs to the loss. Because every operation is simple, its local derivative — the slope of its output with respect to each of its inputs — is known in closed form. A small table covers most of deep learning:

OperationOutputLocal slopes
Adda + b∂/∂a = 1, ∂/∂b = 1 — passes the gradient through unchanged to both
Multiplya · b∂/∂a = b, ∂/∂b = a — each input gets the gradient times the other input
Squarea²2a
ReLUmax(0, a)1 where a > 0, else 0 — a gate that passes or blocks the gradient
Sigmoidσ(a) = 1/(1 + e⁻ᵃ)σ(a)·(1 − σ(a)); at most 0.25, at a = 4 only 0.018
Tanhtanh(a)1 − tanh²(a); 1 at a = 0, 0.42 at a = 1
Exp / Logeᵃ / ln aeᵃ / 1/a
Matrix multiplyY = X·Wsee section 5

Two features of this table are worth noticing now. First, several local slopes are computed from values that existed in the forward pass: the multiply node needs the other input, the ReLU node needs to know which inputs were positive, the sigmoid node needs its own output. If the backward pass is to run later, those values must be kept. That is the entire reason training needs so much memory (section 8). Second, the ReLU row shows that a local slope can be exactly zero: the gradient stops there. Sigmoid's slope is never bigger than 0.25, so a stack of sigmoids shrinks gradients at every layer — the seed of the vanishing-gradient problem in dl3.

3. The backward pass: gradient in, gradient × local slope out

Write v̄ for "the gradient of the loss with respect to the value v" — how much L changes per unit change of v. (Automatic-differentiation papers call this the adjoint of v; deep-learning code calls it v.grad.) The backward pass has one starting fact and one rule.

  • Start: the loss's gradient with respect to itself is 1 (L̄ = 1): change L by one unit and L changes by one unit.
  • Rule: for an operation with output o and input v, the gradient arriving at v from this operation is ō × (∂o/∂v). If a value feeds several operations, sum the contributions from all of them.

Nodes are visited in reverse of the order in which the forward pass created them, so a node's gradient is complete (all consumers have contributed) before it is passed further back. This reverse order is what "back" means in backpropagation.

Branches add — a worked example. Let a = 3 and b = 2. Compute p = a·b = 6, q = a + b = 5 and r = p·q = 30. The value a is used twice (in p and in q); b is too. Backward, starting from r̄ = 1: the multiply node r = p·q sends p̄ = q = 5 and q̄ = p = 6. Now a receives a contribution from each consumer: through p (a multiply by b) it gets 5 × b = 10; through q (an add) it gets 6 × 1 = 6. Its total is ā = 10 + 6 = 16. Likewise b gets 5 × a = 15 from p and 6 from q: b̄ = 21. Check by algebra: r = ab(a + b) = a²b + ab², so ∂r/∂a = 2ab + b² = 12 + 4 = 16 ✓ and ∂r/∂b = a² + 2ab = 9 + 12 = 21 ✓.

4. A full worked example: a 2-layer network, every number

Now a real (tiny) network, with numbers small enough to follow by hand. Two inputs, two hidden ReLU units, one linear output, one training example, squared-error loss. Inputs x = (1, 2), target y = 2. Parameters:

W1 = [[ 0.50, 0.25],      b1 = [ 0.10, -0.40]      (row i = weights of hidden unit i)
      [-0.25, 0.50]]
w2 = [1.0, 0.5]           b2 = 0.2

Forward pass, node by node (values are kept — we will need them):

  1. Pre-activations z = W1·x + b1: z₁ = 0.5·1 + 0.25·2 + 0.1 = 1.10; z₂ = −0.25·1 + 0.5·2 − 0.4 = 0.35.
  2. ReLU: h = (max(0, 1.10), max(0, 0.35)) = (1.10, 0.35). Both units are active.
  3. Output ŷ = w2·h + b2 = 1.0·1.10 + 0.5·0.35 + 0.2 = 1.10 + 0.175 + 0.2 = 1.475.
  4. Loss L = (ŷ − y)² = (1.475 − 2)² = 0.525² = 0.2756.

Backward pass, from the loss backwards, using only the local slopes of the table and the saved forward values:

  1. ŷ̄ = ∂L/∂ŷ = 2(ŷ − y) = 2 × (−0.525) = −1.05. The negative sign says: the output is too low, so raising ŷ would lower the loss.
  2. Output layer. Because ŷ = w2₁h₁ + w2₂h₂ + b2, the slope of ŷ with respect to each weight is the matching input and with respect to b2 is 1:
    ∂L/∂w2 = ŷ̄ · h = −1.05 × (1.10, 0.35) = (−1.155, −0.3675) and ∂L/∂b2 = −1.05.
  3. Passing the signal to the hidden layer: the slope of ŷ with respect to each hidden value h_i is w2_i, so h̄ = ŷ̄·w2 = −1.05 × (1.0, 0.5) = (−1.05, −0.525).
  4. Through ReLU: the slope is 1 where z > 0. Both z values are positive, so the gate is open and z̄ = h̄ = (−1.05, −0.525).
  5. First layer. z_i = Σⱼ W1_ij·xⱼ + b1_i, so ∂L/∂W1_ij = z̄_i · xⱼ — the outer product of z̄ with x — and ∂L/∂b1 = z̄:
    ∂L/∂W1 = [[−1.05·1, −1.05·2], [−0.525·1, −0.525·2]] = [[−1.05, −2.10], [−0.525, −1.05]], ∂L/∂b1 = (−1.05, −0.525).

Take the step. With learning rate 0.05, each parameter moves by −0.05 × its gradient: w2 becomes (1.0578, 0.5184), b2 becomes 0.2525, W1 becomes [[0.5525, 0.355], [−0.22375, 0.5525]], b1 becomes (0.1525, −0.37375). Run the forward pass again: z = (1.415, 0.5075), ŷ = 2.0123, L = 0.00015. One step took the loss from 0.2756 to 0.00015 — the update moved every parameter in exactly the direction that raises ŷ.

Check it two ways. First, by brute force: nudge W1₂₁ (the weight from x₁ into hidden unit 2) by ±0.00001, recompute the loss both times and divide the difference by 0.00002. The result is −0.525000000004, agreeing with the backprop value −0.525 to ten digits. Second, PyTorch autograd on the same numbers in float64 returns exactly the gradients above. (The lab at the end reproduces all of this with a from-scratch autograd.)

What if a ReLU had been closed? Suppose z₂ had been −0.35 instead. Then h₂ = 0 and the ReLU gate for unit 2 is 0, so z̄₂ = 0, and therefore every gradient belonging to unit 2 — its row of W1 and its bias — is zero for this example. The unit contributes nothing to the output and receives no learning signal. If that happens for every example the unit never changes again: a "dead ReLU", discussed in dl2. The output-layer weight w2₂ still gets a gradient equal to ŷ̄·h₂ = 0 as well, since h₂ = 0. Backprop faithfully reports that the loss does not depend on a value the forward pass ignored.

5. From scalars to matrices: the linear layer

Real layers process a whole batch of examples at once with matrix multiplication. A linear layer computes Y = X·W + b where X has shape (batch, in), W has shape (in, out) and b has length out (added to every row). Suppose the gradient arriving from above, dY (shape (batch, out)), is known. The three gradients are:

dX = dY · Wᵀ        shape (batch, in)     — sent to the layer below
dW = Xᵀ · dY        shape (in, out)       — given to the optimiser
db = dY summed over the batch   shape (out)

These are the scalar rules of section 4, written for many neurons and many examples at once: dW = Xᵀ·dY is "every weight's gradient is the input it multiplied times the gradient of the output it fed, summed over examples". A tiny batch makes it concrete. Let X = [[1, 2, 0], [0, 1, 3]] (2 examples, 3 features), W = [[1, 0], [0, 1], [2, −1]] and b = (0.5, −0.5). Forward: Y = XW + b = [[1.5, 1.5], [6.5, −2.5]]. Suppose the gradient from above is dY = [[1, −1], [0.5, 2]]. Then:

dX = dY·Wᵀ = [[ 1.0, -1.0,  3.0],      dW = Xᵀ·dY = [[1.0, -1.0],      db = (1.5, 1.0)
              [ 0.5,  2.0, -1.0]]                     [2.5,  0.0],
                                                       [1.5,  6.0]]

Shapes are a powerful check: dW must have the same shape as W, dX the same shape as X, because each gradient says how the loss changes per unit change of that very tensor. Two facts follow directly. First, the backward pass of a linear layer needs two matrix multiplications — the same size as the forward one — where the forward pass needed one. That is why backward costs about twice as much as forward, and why training a transformer costs roughly 2N + 4N = 6N FLOPs per token for N parameters (gpu3). Second, computing dW = Xᵀ·dY needs X, the layer's input from the forward pass. Every linear layer's input must therefore stay in memory until its backward step runs.

6. Softmax and cross-entropy: why p − y is so tidy

For classification the network's last layer produces logits z, one per class, and softmax turns them into probabilities pᵢ = e^{zᵢ} / Σⱼ e^{zⱼ} that are positive and sum to 1. The cross-entropy loss is L = −ln p_c, where c is the true class: it is small when the model puts high probability on the right answer and huge when it puts almost none. (ml5 discusses why.) With logits z = (2.0, 1.0, 0.1) and true class 0:

e^z = (7.389, 2.718, 1.105)   sum = 11.213
p   = (0.659, 0.242, 0.099)   L = −ln 0.659 = 0.417

Working out the local derivatives of softmax (a 3×3 Jacobian) and then of the logarithm and multiplying them looks messy, but the whole thing collapses to one line: ∂L/∂zᵢ = pᵢ − yᵢ, where y is the one-hot target vector (1 for the true class, 0 elsewhere). Here that is (0.659 − 1, 0.242, 0.099) = (−0.341, 0.242, 0.099). Read it in words: the gradient pushes the correct logit up by (1 − p_c) and every wrong logit down by its own probability. The more wrongly confident the model is about a class, the harder it is pushed down. When the model is already right and confident (p_c ≈ 1) the gradient vanishes, so the update naturally slows.

The same expression appears for logistic regression (ml6) and for the output of every language model, which is one cross-entropy over a vocabulary of tens or hundreds of thousands of tokens. It is also why libraries offer a fused cross_entropy(logits, target) that takes logits, not probabilities: fusing avoids computing the log of a tiny probability (numerically unstable) and produces the stable gradient p − y directly.

7. Why go backwards? The cost argument

A neural network is a function from millions of numbers (the parameters) to one number (the loss). There are three ways to get its gradient with respect to all n parameters.

  • Finite differences. Nudge each parameter and re-evaluate. Cost: n + 1 forward passes (or 2n for the more accurate centred version), and each result is only an approximation. With n = 25 million, that is the three-days-per-step of the opening.
  • Forward-mode differentiation. Carry a derivative along with every value as the computation runs forward. This is exact, but each pass tracks the derivative with respect to one chosen input, so you still need n passes.
  • Reverse-mode differentiation (backprop). One forward pass, one backward sweep. The sweep computes the derivative of the one output with respect to every input.

The asymmetry is the whole point. Think of the graph as a network of pipes: forward mode asks "if I twist this one input, how does every output respond?"; reverse mode asks "for this one output, how sensitive is it to every input?". A loss is one output, so reverse mode answers everything with one sweep. In the standard analysis of automatic differentiation (Griewank & Walther), the cost of the reverse sweep is a constant c times the cost of the function, where c is provably below 6 and in practice between 2 and 3, independent of n. For a transformer the count is exact enough to state: the forward pass is 2N FLOPs per token and the backward pass 4N, so the whole gradient is about 3× the price of inference.

Reverse mode's price is storage: it needs the forward values, so memory grows with the number of operations executed — the topic of section 8.

8. What backward needs to remember: activation memory

During training four things live in GPU memory: the parameters, their gradients, the optimiser's state, and the activations — every forward-pass tensor that some backward step still needs. The first three are a fixed number of bytes per parameter (about 16 with mixed-precision Adam, gpu3). The activations depend on the batch: they grow with batch size × sequence length × hidden width × number of layers. Backprop needs them because, as sections 4–5 showed, each local slope is computed from forward values: X for dW, the ReLU's pattern of positive z, the sigmoid's output.

For a transformer layer with hidden size h processing b sequences of s tokens, gpu3 gives about 34·s·b·h bytes of saved activations per layer in 16-bit precision with a fused attention kernel. For a 7B model (h = 4,096, 32 layers) at s = 4,096 and b = 1, that is 34 × 4,096 × 4,096 ≈ 570 MB per layer, and about 18 GB over 32 layers — from a single sequence. At batch size 8 it is 146 GB, more than an 80 GB GPU holds even if the weights were free. Parameters are constant; activations are what you scale with your data, which is why they are what runs out.

Gradient checkpointing (also called activation checkpointing or rematerialisation) breaks the deal: save only some activations and recompute the rest when needed. Split an n-layer network into segments; during forward, keep only each segment's input and free everything inside the segment; during backward, when reaching a segment, re-run its forward pass from the saved input to regenerate the activations, do the segment's backward step, and free them again. With n = 100 layers and one unit of memory per layer, storing everything costs 100 units. Cutting into 10 segments of 10 layers costs 10 saved inputs plus 10 units for the one segment currently being rebuilt: 20 units — a 5× reduction. In general the best split has √n segments of √n layers, so memory is about 2√n rather than n: for n = 1,000 that is about 63 units instead of 1,000. The price is that every layer's forward runs twice, so a step costs roughly one extra forward pass. Since forward is one third of forward-plus-backward (2N versus 4N FLOPs), that is about +33% compute. In practice transformers checkpoint at block boundaries, storing a block's 2·s·b·h-byte input instead of its 34·s·b·h bytes of internals, a 17× cut in activation memory (gpu9 works through the FSDP and sharding side).

9. How PyTorch does it: autograd

PyTorch builds the graph while your code runs ("define by run"). Each tensor created from operations on tensors that requires_grad carries a grad_fn: an object that knows which operation produced it, which saved tensors it needs, and links to the grad_fns of its inputs. Calling loss.backward() walks those links in reverse topological order, calls each node's backward function with the incoming gradient, and adds the results into the .grad attribute of the leaf tensors (the parameters). Because the graph is rebuilt on every forward pass, ordinary Python if statements and loops just work, and the graph may differ from one step to the next.

x = torch.tensor(2., requires_grad=True)
y = x * 3 + 1          # y.grad_fn is AddBackward0, linked to MulBackward0
z = y ** 2             # z.grad_fn is PowBackward0
z.backward()           # x.grad == dz/dx == 2*y*3 == 42.0  (y = 7)

A complete training step, with what each line does to the graph:

model.train()
for x, y in loader:
    x, y = x.to(device), y.to(device)
    optimizer.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)   # clear .grad left from the previous step
    logits = model(x)                       # forward: builds the graph, saves activations
    loss = F.cross_entropy(logits, y)
    loss.backward()                         # backward: fills .grad on every parameter, frees the graph
    torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)
    optimizer.step()                        # update weights using .grad

model.eval()                                # dropout off, BatchNorm uses running stats
with torch.no_grad():                       # no graph, no saved activations
    preds = model(x_val).argmax(-1)

The four behaviours that trip people up are all direct consequences of this design.

  • Gradients accumulate. backward() adds into .grad. Call it three times on (a**2).sum() with a = (1, 2) and you get (2, 4), then (4, 8), then (6, 12). This is essential when a parameter is used in several places (branches add) and it is what makes gradient accumulation over micro-batches possible — but it makes zero_grad() mandatory once per step. set_to_none=True frees the buffers instead of writing zeros.
  • The graph is freed by backward. The saved activations are released as soon as they have been used. A second loss.backward() on the same loss raises "Trying to backward through the graph a second time". retain_graph=True keeps them, at a memory cost.
  • detach() and no_grad() cut the graph. t.detach() returns a tensor sharing data with t but with no history, so no gradient flows to whatever produced t. torch.no_grad() turns recording off inside a block: no grad_fns are created and nothing is saved, which is why inference under no_grad (or the slightly faster inference_mode) uses so much less memory than a training forward pass. Using a detached tensor where you needed gradients is silent: the parameters simply stop learning.
  • In-place operations can invalidate saved tensors. Every tensor has a version counter that increments when it is modified in place. When backward is about to use a saved tensor, autograd compares versions; on a mismatch it raises: "one of the variables needed for gradient computation has been modified by an inplace operation … is at version 1; expected version 0 instead." A concrete case: h = torch.sigmoid(a); h.mul_(2); h.sum().backward() fails, because sigmoid's backward needs its own output h and mul_ overwrote it. Not every in-place op is dangerous: relu_() works because ReLU's backward only needs to know which outputs are positive. When in doubt use the out-of-place form.

What about non-differentiable spots? ReLU has a kink at 0; PyTorch simply defines its slope there as 0 (any value in [0, 1] is a valid subgradient, and exact zeros are rare). Truly non-differentiable operations such as argmax or rounding have zero gradient almost everywhere, so nothing flows through them; tricks to get around this (the straight-through estimator) are in the deep section.

10. Checking and debugging a backward pass

Gradient checking. Compare autograd against finite differences on a tiny model. For a parameter w compute (L(w + ε) − L(w − ε)) / 2ε — the centred difference, which is more accurate than nudging one way — and compare with the analytic gradient using a relative error |a − n| / max(|a|, |n|). Use float64 and ε around 10⁻⁶: in float32 the subtraction of two nearly equal losses discards most digits, and a big ε makes the estimate coarse. A correct implementation agrees to about 10⁻⁷ or better; 10⁻² means a bug. torch.autograd.gradcheck does this for you. Avoid points where the function has a kink (ReLU at exactly 0).

Debugging a network that does not learn. Work down this list, cheapest first:

  1. Overfit one small batch. A correct model, loss and loop can drive the loss on 8–32 examples to almost zero. If it cannot, the bug is in the model, loss or data pipeline, not in the hyperparameters.
  2. Look for parameters with .grad is None or exactly zero after backward() — a detached branch, a layer not used in forward, or dead ReLUs.
  3. Check the update happens: print a weight's norm before and after optimizer.step(); make sure zero_grad is called once per step and that the optimiser was given the right parameters.
  4. Log the global gradient norm and per-layer gradient norms. Norms shrinking towards zero in early layers, or exploding, point at dl3's problems.
  5. Check train/eval modes and that inference code runs under no_grad.

Jacobians and vector–Jacobian products: why the graph never stores a matrix of slopes

For a function f: ℝⁿ → ℝᵐ, the Jacobian J is the m × n matrix of all partial derivatives ∂fᵢ/∂xⱼ. The chain rule for a composition is a product of Jacobians. Consider one 4,096-to-4,096 layer: its Jacobian per example has 4,096 × 4,096 ≈ 16.8 million entries, and for a batch it is far bigger. Backprop never builds it. It needs only the product of the incoming gradient row vector with the Jacobian, v̄ᵀ · J, called a vector–Jacobian product (VJP), and every primitive operation implements exactly that: given the upstream gradient, return the gradient for each input. For ReLU the VJP is an elementwise multiplication by a 0/1 mask (the Jacobian is a diagonal matrix that never needs to exist); for a matmul it is the pair of matmuls of section 5. Forward-mode differentiation uses the mirror-image Jacobian–vector product (JVP), J · v: with a chosen direction v it tells you how all the outputs move when the inputs move along v. A JVP costs about one extra forward pass and gives one column of J; a VJP costs about one backward pass and gives one row. A scalar loss has a Jacobian with a single row, so one VJP gives the entire gradient. Where you need Hessian-vector products (curvature information for second-order methods, or sharpness measures) the standard trick is to run forward-mode over reverse-mode; JAX and torch.func expose jvp, vjp, jacfwd and jacrev as composable functions.

The mathematics of checkpointing, and what it looks like in practice

With n layers and a budget of k checkpoints, the stored activations are k plus one segment of n/k layers being recomputed: total ≈ k + n/k, minimised at k = √n, giving 2√n. Chen et al. also showed a recursive scheme reaching O(log n) memory at O(n log n) compute, and Griewank's earlier "revolve" algorithm finds the provably optimal schedule for a given number of checkpoints, but the √n scheme, or simply "checkpoint every transformer block", is what production code uses because the layers of a transformer are identical and the extra compute is a predictable third. Selective recomputation (Korthikanti et al., 2022) refines this by noting that not all activations are equal: attention's s×s score matrices and dropout masks take a lot of memory but cost few FLOPs to recompute, while matmul outputs are cheap to store relative to their compute cost. Recomputing only the former cut activation memory about 5× for a few percent of compute; with sequence parallelism it raised model FLOPs utilisation on a 530B model from 42% (full recomputation) to 54% (gpu9). FlashAttention applies the same idea inside a single kernel: it never stores the attention matrix at all, and recomputes it from Q, K and the saved softmax statistics during backward — more FLOPs, far less memory traffic, and faster overall (gpu7).

Frozen layers still need half a backward pass

If a layer's weights are frozen (LoRA, adapter fine-tuning, or a frozen encoder), backprop must still pass gradients through it to reach trainable layers earlier in the network, so dX = dY·Wᵀ is required. But the weight gradient dW = Xᵀ·dY is not, and neither is the storage of X for that purpose. So backward through a frozen layer costs about the same as its forward pass (one matmul, not two), and no gradient or optimiser state exists for it. This is why parameter-efficient fine-tuning cuts state memory by orders of magnitude yet cuts activation memory and compute far less — the forward pass and the input-gradient path are unchanged. Freezing the earliest layers is cheaper still: if nothing before a layer needs gradients, backward can stop there entirely.

Custom gradients and non-differentiable operations

You can define a new operation by writing torch.autograd.Function with a forward and a hand-written backward, or in JAX with custom_vjp. Reasons to do so: a fused kernel (FlashAttention, fused layer norm) whose backward is more efficient than the composition of primitives; numerical stability; and a deliberately wrong gradient. The best-known case is the straight-through estimator: quantisation, rounding and hard argmax have zero gradient almost everywhere, which would stop learning, so the backward function pretends the operation was the identity and passes the gradient through unchanged. Quantisation-aware training and the codebook lookup of vector-quantised autoencoders (dl10) rely on it. The estimator is biased — the gradient it reports is not the gradient of anything — and works because the direction is usually useful; it is a heuristic to know about, not a theorem.

Higher-order gradients

Passing create_graph=True to backward or torch.autograd.grad makes the backward pass itself recorded in a graph, so you can differentiate the gradient. Uses: gradient penalties (WGAN-GP penalises the norm of the discriminator's gradient with respect to its inputs, dl10), meta-learning (MAML differentiates through an inner training step), sharpness-aware minimisation and Hessian-vector products. The cost is a backward pass through a backward pass, and memory for everything saved by both.

Define-by-run, define-and-run, and compilers

Early frameworks (Theano, TensorFlow 1) built a static graph first and executed it later, which allowed global optimisation but made debugging painful; PyTorch popularised define-by-run, which lets you use a debugger and Python control flow. The price is that the framework sees only one operation at a time and cannot fuse across them. torch.compile recovers the optimisation: it captures the forward graph, uses AOTAutograd to trace the corresponding backward graph ahead of time, and hands the joint graph to a compiler (Inductor) that fuses elementwise operations into single kernels and decides which intermediates to save and which to recompute — effectively automatic selective checkpointing. Data-dependent Python control flow causes "graph breaks" that limit the gains (gpu6).

Gradient accumulation done correctly

To emulate a batch of B·k from k micro-batches of B, sum the gradients of the k micro-batch losses and take one optimiser step. The gradient of a mean loss over the big batch equals the mean of the micro-batch mean gradients, so divide each micro-batch loss by k. With variable-length sequences, per-micro-batch means over-weight short sequences; divide the summed token losses by the total token count of the whole accumulated batch instead. Under distributed data parallelism every backward() would trigger a gradient all-reduce, so you suppress it on all but the last micro-batch (model.no_sync()) to avoid k-fold communication. Layers whose output depends on batch statistics (BatchNorm, dl4) see only the micro-batch, so accumulation is not exactly equivalent to a large batch for them.

Numerics of the backward pass

Gradients span a wider range than activations: they are often tiny. In float16 (max ≈ 65,504, smallest normal ≈ 6·10⁻⁵) small gradients underflow to zero, so loss scaling multiplies the loss by a large constant (or a dynamically adjusted one) before backward and divides the gradients by it before the update; bfloat16 shares float32's range and normally needs none (gpu4). Log-softmax uses the log-sum-exp trick — subtract the maximum logit before exponentiating — so that e^z cannot overflow, and fused cross-entropy computes the gradient p − y directly rather than differentiating through a log of a possibly tiny probability. Classic sources of NaN gradients are sqrt(0) (slope infinite), log(0), 0·inf, and dividing by a variance that underflows to zero; gradient clipping (dl3, ml4) and an epsilon inside the operation are the standard defences.

What backprop means for LLM training budgets

The 6·N·D FLOPs estimate for training a transformer of N parameters on D tokens is exactly the forward (2ND) plus backward (4ND) of section 5 — nothing more. Activation checkpointing adds a recomputed forward (about 2ND more), which is why hardware FLOPs utilisation can exceed model FLOPs utilisation (gpu3, gpu9). In pipeline parallelism (dl9) each micro-batch in flight holds its activations until its own backward runs, so the schedule (one-forward-one-backward, 1F1B) is chosen to bound how many micro-batches' activations are alive at once. Activation memory is why long-context training needs sequence or context parallelism: it is linear in s, and per-layer attention would otherwise be quadratic. All of this follows from one fact — reverse mode trades memory for speed — and each memory-saving technique in modern training is a different way of renegotiating that trade.

The public case: 1,000 layers in 7 GB

By 2016, GPU memory was the practical limit on network depth. Chen, Xu, Zhang and Guestrin (University of Washington, Dato, MIT) observed that the memory needed to store feature maps and their gradients grew linearly with depth, and asked how much of it was really necessary. Their paper, "Training Deep Nets with Sublinear Memory Cost" (arXiv:1604.06174), applied exactly the ideas of section 8 in the MXNet framework: a computation-graph analysis to share memory between tensors and do operations in place, and then an algorithm that drops the outputs of cheap layers and recomputes them during backward, choosing segments so that memory grows as O(√n) for an n-layer network at the cost of one additional forward pass. The headline result, from the abstract: "we can reduce the memory cost of a 1,000-layer deep residual network from 48G to 7G on ImageNet problems". Their ResNet experiments used a batch of 32 images at 224 × 224; with the system optimisations alone (in-place operations and memory sharing) memory still grew linearly and about 200 layers was the limit of the best GPU available to them, while with the sublinear plan a 1,000-layer network trained in under 7 GB. The price they measured on a Titan X was about 30% more runtime per batch, consistent with the analysis: an extra forward pass is roughly a third of a training step. Today the idea is a one-line switch in every framework (torch.utils.checkpoint, gradient_checkpointing_enable() in Hugging Face Transformers), and it is what lets a 7B model train with sequences of thousands of tokens.

The deeper historical case is Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams's 1986 Nature paper, which showed that backpropagating errors through hidden layers let networks learn useful internal representations; the chain-rule idea itself had been used in control theory and earlier work, but this paper made it the default way to train multi-layer networks. That is worth saying in an interview as one sentence, and no more: the point is that the algorithm is old and the engineering around its memory and numerics is where the recent progress has been.

Where training memory peaks, and how to see it

Memory during a training step rises through the forward pass, peaks at its end (all activations alive) and just after backward begins (activations plus the first gradients), then falls as backward frees each layer's saved tensors. That is why an out-of-memory error at loss.backward() means activations, and an error at the first optimizer.step() usually means optimiser state: Adam allocates its moment buffers lazily on the first step, so a model that survived a whole forward and backward can still fail there. Tools: torch.cuda.memory_summary(), torch.cuda.max_memory_allocated() around each phase, and the memory-snapshot recorder (torch.cuda.memory._record_memory_history()), which shows every live tensor and the stack trace that allocated it. Check the largest single terms first: activations, the logits tensor (sequence × vocabulary: 8,192 × 128,256 floats in fp32 is 4.2 GB per sequence, plus a gradient of the same size), and allocator fragmentation (reserved memory far above allocated).

Failure modes you will actually meet

The graph-holding leak. losses.append(loss) stores a tensor that still points at the whole graph of its step, so memory grows every iteration until OOM after a few hundred steps. Log loss.item() (a plain Python float) or loss.detach(). The same happens with metrics accumulated as tensors.

Evaluating with a graph. A validation loop without torch.no_grad() builds and saves a graph for every batch. It "works" but uses several times the memory and time it should; the symptom is a validation loop that OOMs at a batch size that trains fine.

Silent non-learning. A detached tensor, a layer defined but not called in forward, a parameter missing from the optimiser's parameter list, or requires_grad=False left on after a freezing experiment: the loss barely moves and nothing crashes. The diagnostic is to print, after backward(), every parameter's name and gradient norm and to look for None and zeros. Under DistributedDataParallel, parameters that receive no gradient in a step raise "Expected to have finished reduction in the prior iteration…" unless the model is wrapped with find_unused_parameters=True — an error that is really reporting a disconnected branch.

Accumulation bugs. Forgetting to divide the loss by the number of accumulation steps makes the effective learning rate k times too large (Adam partly masks this, plain SGD does not); calling zero_grad inside the accumulation loop discards earlier micro-batches; and calling the optimiser scheduler once per micro-batch rather than once per step stretches the schedule.

NaNs and infs. torch.autograd.set_detect_anomaly(True) re-runs backward checking each operation for NaN and, on failure, prints the stack trace of the forward operation that created the offending node. It is slow, so use it for a few steps at the point of failure, not for whole runs. Also cheap and effective: log the global gradient norm every step (clip_grad_norm_ returns it) and watch for spikes preceding loss spikes; use register_hook on a suspect tensor to print its gradient statistics.

The train/eval switch. Forgetting model.eval() leaves dropout active and BatchNorm using batch statistics at test time (dl4, dl8) — not a backprop bug, but the most common reason a model that trained well predicts badly, and the reason "check train/eval mode" is on every debugging list.

What to monitor

Log per step: the loss, the global gradient norm (before clipping), the fraction of steps where clipping fired, peak GPU memory, and the learning rate. Log periodically: per-layer gradient norms and the ratio of update size to weight size (about 10⁻³ is typical; a ratio near 10⁻⁶ means a layer is barely learning, near 10⁻¹ means it is unstable). Karpathy's "A Recipe for Training Neural Networks" is the best short public list of the habits behind this: overfit a single batch first, verify the loss at initialisation (for a 1,000-class classifier it should start near ln 1000 ≈ 6.9), and inspect data and gradients before trusting a training curve.

Goal: build a small autograd engine on NumPy arrays, reproduce the lesson's hand-computed gradients, prove them with finite differences, see the accumulation behaviour, and train an MLP on XOR with it. About 90 lines, NumPy only, Python 3.12. It was tested on Python 3.12 with NumPy 2.x.

import numpy as np

class T:
    """A tensor that remembers how it was made, so backward() can walk the graph in reverse."""
    def __init__(self, data, parents=(), op=""):
        self.data = np.asarray(data, dtype=np.float64)
        self.grad = np.zeros_like(self.data)
        self.parents, self.op, self._back = parents, op, lambda: None

    def __matmul__(self, o):
        out = T(self.data @ o.data, (self, o), "matmul")
        def back():
            self.grad += out.grad @ o.data.T          # dX = dY Wᵀ
            o.grad += self.data.T @ out.grad          # dW = Xᵀ dY
        out._back = back
        return out

    def __add__(self, o):                              # o may be a bias row that is broadcast
        out = T(self.data + o.data, (self, o), "add")
        def back():
            self.grad += out.grad
            o.grad += out.grad.reshape(-1, *o.data.shape).sum(axis=0) if o.data.ndim else out.grad.sum()
        out._back = back
        return out

    def relu(self):
        out = T(np.maximum(self.data, 0), (self,), "relu")
        def back(): self.grad += out.grad * (self.data > 0)
        out._back = back
        return out

    def mse(self, y):
        d = self.data - y
        out = T(np.mean(d ** 2), (self,), "mse")
        def back(): self.grad += out.grad * 2 * d / d.size
        out._back = back
        return out

    def backward(self):
        order, seen = [], set()
        def visit(t):                                  # topological order: parents before children
            if id(t) not in seen:
                seen.add(id(t)); [visit(p) for p in t.parents]; order.append(t)
        visit(self)
        for t in order:
            if t.parents: t.grad = np.zeros_like(t.data)              # intermediate nodes start fresh; leaves keep accumulating
        self.grad = np.ones_like(self.data)            # dL/dL = 1
        for t in reversed(order): t._back()

def forward(params, x, y):
    W1, b1, W2, b2 = params
    return ((T(x) @ W1 + b1).relu() @ W2 + b2).mse(y)

# ---- 1. the worked example from the lesson: 2 inputs -> 2 ReLU units -> 1 output
params = [T([[0.5, -0.25], [0.25, 0.5]]), T([0.1, -0.4]), T([[1.0], [0.5]]), T([0.2])]
x, y = np.array([[1.0, 2.0]]), np.array([[2.0]])
loss = forward(params, x, y); loss.backward()
print("loss", loss.data)
for name, p in zip(["W1", "b1", "W2", "b2"], params): print(name, "grad", p.grad.round(4).tolist())

# ---- 2. check every gradient against finite differences
def numeric_grad(params, i, x, y, eps=1e-6):
    g = np.zeros_like(params[i].data)
    for idx in np.ndindex(*g.shape):
        old = params[i].data[idx]
        params[i].data[idx] = old + eps; up = forward(params, x, y).data
        params[i].data[idx] = old - eps; dn = forward(params, x, y).data
        params[i].data[idx] = old
        g[idx] = (up - dn) / (2 * eps)
    return g
for i, p in enumerate(params):
    n = numeric_grad(params, i, x, y)
    print(f"param {i}: max relative error {np.max(np.abs(p.grad - n) / np.maximum(1e-8, np.abs(p.grad) + np.abs(n))):.1e}")

# ---- 3. gradients accumulate: a second backward without zeroing doubles them
loss.backward()
print("after 2nd backward, b2 grad =", params[3].grad.tolist())

# ---- 4. train on XOR to show the same machinery learning something
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X = np.array([[0., 0.], [0., 1.], [1., 0.], [1., 1.]]); Y = np.array([[0.], [1.], [1.], [0.]])
P = [T(rng.normal(0, 1, (2, 8))), T(np.zeros(8)), T(rng.normal(0, 1, (8, 1)) * 0.5), T(np.zeros(1))]
for step in range(501):
    for p in P: p.grad[:] = 0                          # zero_grad
    loss = forward(P, X, Y); loss.backward()
    for p in P: p.data -= 0.1 * p.grad                 # SGD update
    if step % 100 == 0: print(f"step {step:3d}  loss {loss.data:.4f}")
pred = (T(X) @ P[0] + P[1]).relu() @ P[2] + P[3]
print("predictions:", pred.data.round(2).ravel().tolist())

What to look for.

  1. Part 1 prints loss 0.2756 and gradients W1 = [[−1.05, −0.525], [−2.1, −1.05]] (the transpose of the lesson's table, because this code stores W as (in, out) so that it computes X·W), b1 = (−1.05, −0.525), W2 = (−1.155, −0.3675), b2 = −1.05. These are the numbers you computed by hand — the engine is just the section-3 rule (gradient in × local slope out) applied in reverse topological order.
  2. Part 2 prints relative errors around 10⁻¹¹ between backprop and centred finite differences in float64. Change eps to 1e-1 and then to 1e-12 and watch the error rise in both directions: truncation error on one side, floating-point cancellation on the other.
  3. Part 3 shows the b2 gradient going from −1.05 to −2.1 after a second backward(): leaf gradients accumulate. This is exactly why the training loop in part 4 zeroes them before every step. Delete that line and the loss stops falling cleanly.
  4. Part 4 starts near loss 0.44, drops below 0.01 by step 100 and is essentially 0 by step 300, with XOR predictions of 0, 1, 1, 0 — a network that a single neuron could never fit, trained purely by the gradients this engine computes.

Work it out — where does the memory go?

You are training a 32-layer transformer with hidden size h = 4,096, batch size 8, sequence length 2,048, in bf16 with a fused attention kernel. Using the rule of about 34·s·b·h bytes of saved activations per layer (gpu3), estimate the activation memory, then what gradient checkpointing at block boundaries saves and costs.

Answer

One (batch, seq, hidden) tensor in bf16: 8 × 2,048 × 4,096 × 2 bytes = 134 MB. The per-layer rule gives 34 × 2,048 × 8 × 4,096 ≈ 2.28 GB, which is about 17 such tensors' worth of saved intermediates (the inputs of the matmuls, the activation-function inputs, dropout and normalisation statistics).

Without checkpointing: 32 layers × 2.28 GB ≈ 73 GB of activations — before weights, gradients or optimiser state, and on an 80 GB GPU. This is why activation memory, not parameter count, is what usually OOMs a run.

With checkpointing at block boundaries: save only each block's input, 134 MB × 32 = 4.3 GB, plus one block's full internals (2.28 GB) while it is being recomputed: about 6.6 GB, an 11× reduction overall (the per-layer ratio is 17×). During backward each block's forward is re-run once, so a step costs one extra forward pass: about +33% compute (forward is 2N of the 6N FLOPs per token).

The follow-up to volunteer: activation memory scales with batch × sequence, while weights, gradients and optimiser state do not. If you halve the batch to fit you halve only one term; if the fixed terms (16 bytes per parameter with Adam) already exceed VRAM, no batch size fits and you need sharding (FSDP), offload, or parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA.

Diagnose it — a layer that never learns

You add an auxiliary head to a working model. Training runs, the main loss falls, but the auxiliary head's weights are bit-for-bit identical after 1,000 steps, and its .grad is None for every parameter. Give the most likely causes, in order, and how you would confirm each.

Answer

.grad is None (rather than zeros) means backward never reached these parameters, which is a graph problem, not a numerical one. In order of likelihood:

  • The head's input was detached — for example the features were computed under torch.no_grad() or with .detach() (fine for the backbone, fatal if the head also sits inside the block). Confirm by printing head_out.requires_grad and head_out.grad_fn; False and None mean the graph is cut before the head.
  • The head's loss isn't part of the loss you call backward() on — it is computed and logged but never added to the total. Confirm by checking that total = main + w * aux is what gets backward().
  • The parameters aren't in the optimiser — gradients exist but are never applied. This one would give non-None grads, so it is ruled out by the observation, but it explains "weights identical" alone.
  • The head was never called in forward — or is called under a branch that is never taken; then the parameters take no part in the graph.

The fastest check is to overfit one batch and print [(n, p.grad is None) for n, p in model.named_parameters()] after backward(). The lesson: None means "no route from the loss to this tensor", zero means "a route existed but the gate was shut" (dead ReLU, zero-weighted loss).
