Ask a classifier to look at a photograph and it says "cat". Now ask the opposite question: make me a photograph of a cat that has never existed. The second task is far harder, and for a long time it was out of reach. To draw a cat, a program must have absorbed what makes a picture of a cat likely: fur texture, ears, eyes, perspective, lighting, and the enormous number of ways they can combine. An image is not a handful of numbers; a 512×512 colour picture is 786,432 of them, and the overwhelming majority of possible arrangements of 786,432 numbers look like television static. Somewhere inside that vast space is a thin, twisting sheet of arrangements that look like photographs. Generating an image means learning the shape of that sheet from examples and then landing on it at random. That is what "learning a data distribution" means, and doing it for images, audio, video, molecules and text is the core of generative AI.

The history of the field is a history of four different tricks for making that hard problem trainable. Autoencoders squeeze data through a bottleneck and rebuild it, on the hope that a good compressed code is also a good place to generate from. Variational autoencoders (2013) made that hope rigorous with a probabilistic twist and a clever way to differentiate through randomness. Generative adversarial networks (2014) had two networks play a forgery game, and for a few years produced the sharpest images anyone had seen. Then diffusion models (2015 in embryo, 2020 in practice) turned generation into a long sequence of small, easy denoising steps, and by 2022 had taken over: DALL·E 2, Imagen and, crucially, the openly released Stable Diffusion, which ran on a gaming graphics card. The same ideas now generate video, audio, molecules and robot trajectories, and a close relative (flow matching) powers several of the newest image models.

For an AI engineer these are more than history. You need to be able to explain why a plain autoencoder cannot generate but a VAE can, what the discriminator and generator are optimising and why it goes wrong, and above all how a diffusion model trains and samples — with the formulas at your fingertips, because interviewers ask for them. You also need the practical picture: why Stable Diffusion runs its loop on 64×64×4 latents rather than pixels, what classifier-free guidance costs, how sampling is made 10–50× faster, how quality is measured with FID, and what the safety and legal issues are.

This lesson builds all of it from the ground up, with numbers you can check. We start with what a generative model is and a map of the families, then the autoencoder and the VAE with its ELBO worked on a two-dimensional example, the GAN with its optimal discriminator, and then diffusion in depth: the closed-form noising with a real schedule, the noise-prediction loss, one reverse step by hand, a tiny diffusion model trained in NumPy that turns static into a two-peaked distribution, conditioning, guidance, latent diffusion, fast sampling and flow matching. The public cases are the DDPM paper and Stable Diffusion, checked against their sources, and the lab lets you watch a model learn.

Creating instead of classifying

Most models you meet first answer questions about data: is this email spam? Which digit is this? These are discriminative models: they learn p(y | x), the probability of a label given an input. A generative model learns what the data itself looks like, p(x), well enough to sample new examples from it: a new face that belongs to nobody, a new sentence, a synthetic medical scan for training. Language models are generative models of text; this lesson is about the main families for continuous data such as images and audio.

Three big ideas, in plain words

  • Compress and rebuild (autoencoders, VAEs): squeeze data into a short code and learn to rebuild it. If the space of codes is well organised, choosing a random code and rebuilding it creates something new.
  • Forger against detective (GANs): a forger network makes fakes; a detective network tries to spot them; both improve until the fakes are convincing.
  • Remove noise step by step (diffusion): learn to clean slightly noisy images; start from pure static and clean it a little at a time until an image appears. This is how most modern image generators work.

A worked intuition for diffusion

Take a photo and add a little random noise, then a little more, a thousand times; by the end it is pure static. Train a network on the question: "here is an image at noise level 500 — what noise was added to it?" If it can answer at every level, you can run the process backwards: start from static, ask for the noise, subtract a bit, repeat, and a new image emerges from the static like a photograph developing. Add a text description as an extra input during training and the network learns to steer the cleaning towards "a red bicycle on a beach". The analogy of a sculptor removing marble to reveal a figure is a good one, with the limit that the sculptor here starts from a random block of static and the figure it reveals is different every time.

Why this is hard

A 512×512 colour image is 786,432 numbers, and almost every arrangement of them is meaningless static. The images that look like photographs occupy a vanishingly thin sliver of that space, and a generative model must find it from examples alone, with no labels telling it what is good. Each of the three families above is a different way of making that search trainable with ordinary gradient descent.

What "learning a distribution" gives you

Once a model has learned p(x) you can do things a classifier cannot: sample (make new data), complete partial data (inpainting: fill a masked region consistently), translate (turn a sketch into a picture), and sometimes score how likely a given example is (anomaly detection). Every family below is a different answer to the same question: since we cannot write p(x) down for images, how do we build something we can train and sample from?

1. The problem and a map of the families

A dataset gives us samples x₁, x₂, … drawn from an unknown distribution p_data over a space of enormous dimension (786,432 for a 512×512 RGB image). We want a model p_θ from which we can draw new samples that look like they came from p_data. The difficulty is that there is no formula for p_data, and naive approaches do not scale: a histogram of 786,432 dimensions is impossible, and a Gaussian model would produce blobs. Every family introduces some trick. The four you must be able to compare:

2. Autoencoders

An encoder network compresses an input x into a short vector z, the latent code; a decoder network rebuilds x̂ from z; training minimises the reconstruction error ‖x − x̂‖². The bottleneck forces the code to keep the most important information: an MNIST digit (784 numbers) squeezed through a 32-dimensional code is a 24-fold compression that still rebuilds a recognisable digit. Autoencoders are useful for compression, for denoising (train to rebuild the clean input from a corrupted copy), for anomaly detection (unusual inputs rebuild badly) and for learning features. But they are not generators. Nothing in the loss says which parts of the latent space are used: codes of the training images can sit in scattered clusters, and a random z falls into the gaps where the decoder has never been trained, giving garbage. To generate we need a latent space whose shape we know, so that we can sample from it: that is what the VAE adds.

3. Variational autoencoders

A VAE (Kingma & Welling, 2013) makes two changes. First, the encoder outputs not a point but a distribution over codes: a mean μ and a standard deviation σ for each latent dimension, q(z | x) = N(μ, σ²). Second, the loss includes a term that pulls every such distribution towards a standard normal prior p(z) = N(0, I). The derivation is short and worth knowing. We want to maximise the likelihood of the data, log p(x) = log ∫ p(x | z) p(z) dz, which is intractable. For any distribution q(z | x) over codes, Jensen's inequality gives a lower bound, the evidence lower bound (ELBO):

log p(x)  ≥  E_{z ~ q(z|x)} [ log p(x | z) ]  −  KL( q(z | x) ‖ p(z) )
              reconstruction term               regulariser: stay close to the prior

(The gap between the two sides is exactly KL(q(z|x) ‖ p(z|x)), how far the encoder is from the true posterior, so maximising the ELBO also improves the encoder.) Training minimises its negative: reconstruction error + KL. For Gaussians the KL term has a closed form per latent dimension, ½(μ² + σ² − log σ² − 1). Worked example with a two-dimensional latent: μ = (0.8, −0.5) and σ = (0.6, 1.2). Dimension 1: ½(0.64 + 0.36 − ln 0.36 − 1) = ½(0.64 + 0.36 + 1.022 − 1) = 0.511. Dimension 2: ½(0.25 + 1.44 − ln 1.44 − 1) = ½(0.25 + 1.44 − 0.365 − 1) = 0.163. Total KL = 0.674 nats. It is zero only when μ = 0 and σ = 1 (the prior itself), it grows as the encoder becomes more certain (σ → 0 makes −log σ² large) and as the code moves away from zero. That is the tension in the loss: the reconstruction term wants each image's code to be sharp and distinctive, the KL term wants all codes to look like the prior.

The reparameterisation trick. The reconstruction term involves an expectation over a random z, and sampling is not differentiable — gradients cannot flow through "draw a random number". The fix is to write the sample as a deterministic function of the parameters and an independent noise: z = μ + σ ⊙ ε, with ε ~ N(0, I). Now randomness is an input (ε) and z is a differentiable function of μ and σ. With ε = (0.5, −1.0), z = (0.8 + 0.6·0.5, −0.5 + 1.2·(−1.0)) = (1.1, −1.7), and the derivatives are simply ∂z/∂μ = 1 and ∂z/∂σ = ε, so backprop (dl1) passes gradients to the encoder. Generation then needs only the decoder: draw z ~ N(0, I) and decode. Because training pushed all encodings towards this prior, the sample lands in a region the decoder knows.

Behaviour and failure modes. VAE samples tend to be blurry: with a Gaussian likelihood of fixed variance the decoder outputs the mean of everything compatible with z, which averages plausible details together. Posterior collapse happens when a powerful decoder learns to ignore z and the KL term goes to zero: the latent carries no information. Remedies are KL annealing (start with a small KL weight), weaker decoders, free-bits constraints. A weight β on the KL term (β-VAE) trades reconstruction quality for a smoother, more factorised latent space. VQ-VAE replaces the continuous code by a choice from a learned codebook (discrete tokens), the basis of image tokenisers for autoregressive models. And the VAE's greatest current role is as the compressor inside latent diffusion (section 6): its decoder is what turns a denoised latent back into an image.

4. Generative adversarial networks

Goodfellow et al. (2014) had a different idea: skip the density altogether and learn to sample. A generator G maps random noise z to a fake sample G(z). A discriminator D takes a sample and outputs the probability D(x) that it is real. They play a two-player game with the value function

min_G max_D  V(D, G) = E_{x~data}[ log D(x) ]  +  E_{z~noise}[ log(1 − D(G(z))) ]

D wants both terms large (say "real" on real data, "fake" on fakes); G wants the second term small (make D say "real" on fakes). In practice training alternates: a few gradient steps for D on a mixture of real and generated batches, then one step for G. The theory is elegant. For a fixed G, the best discriminator is D*(x) = p_data(x) / (p_data(x) + p_g(x)): at a point where the real data density is 0.6 and the generator's is 0.2, D* = 0.6/0.8 = 0.75; where the generator has matched the data, D* = ½. Plugging D* back in, the game minimises (twice) the Jensen–Shannon divergence between p_data and p_g, whose minimum is reached exactly when p_g = p_data; at that equilibrium D = ½ everywhere and the value is −log 4 = −1.386. So a perfect generator leaves the discriminator guessing.

The saturating-loss problem, with numbers. Early in training the generator is bad and D is confident that fakes are fake: D(G(z)) ≈ 0.01, a logit of about −4.6. The gradient of the generator's loss log(1 − D(G(z))) with respect to that logit is −σ(a) = −0.01: nearly zero, so the generator learns almost nothing exactly when it most needs to. The paper's fix is to have the generator instead maximise log D(G(z)), the "non-saturating" loss, whose gradient with respect to the logit is −(1 − σ(a)) = −0.99. Same goal, usable gradient.

Why GANs are hard. The two networks chase a moving target, so the optimisation is not a descent on one loss but a search for an equilibrium, which can oscillate or diverge. Mode collapse: the generator finds a few outputs that fool the current discriminator and produces only those, ignoring the rest of the distribution; the discriminator then learns to reject those, the generator jumps to other ones, and the cycle continues. Vanishing gradients when the real and generated distributions have essentially disjoint supports, so the discriminator separates them perfectly and gives no useful signal. Evaluation is hard because there is no likelihood. Progress came from engineering: the DCGAN design rules (strided convolutions, batch normalisation, ReLU/LeakyReLU, no fully connected layers), Wasserstein GANs (Arjovsky et al., 2017), which replace the classifier by a critic estimating the Earth-Mover distance under a Lipschitz constraint (weight clipping, later a gradient penalty, WGAN-GP) and give meaningful gradients even for disjoint distributions, spectral normalisation, and progressive growing; StyleGAN (Karras et al., 2018) remains a landmark for faces. Their great virtue is speed: one forward pass makes an image.

5. Diffusion models, in depth

The forward (noising) process. Choose a small variance β_t for each step t = 1…T. Each step shrinks the previous image a little and adds a little Gaussian noise:

x_t = √(1 − β_t) · x_(t−1) + √β_t · ε,        ε ~ N(0, I)

Ho et al. (2020) used T = 1,000 with β_t increasing linearly from 10⁻⁴ to 0.02 ("These constants were chosen to be small relative to data scaled to [−1, 1]"). The forward process has no learnable parameters. Because a sum of independent Gaussians is Gaussian, the steps compose into a closed form. Write α_t = 1 − β_t and ᾱ_t = α₁·α₂·…·α_t. Then

x_t = √ᾱ_t · x₀  +  √(1 − ᾱ_t) · ε,        ε ~ N(0, I)          (jump straight to any step t)

This line is the heart of practical diffusion: to train, pick a random t and noise ε and form x_t in one step, no simulation. How much of the picture is left at each step? For the DDPM schedule (the lab computes these):

Worked noising. A one-pixel "image" with clean value x₀ = 0.8 and noise draw ε = −0.5 at t = 500: x₅₀₀ = 0.280 × 0.8 + 0.960 × (−0.5) = 0.224 − 0.480 = −0.256. The pixel has gone from a bright 0.8 to a slightly negative number that is 86% noise by weight.

The learned reverse process. The reverse of a small noising step is well approximated by a Gaussian step, but its mean depends on the data distribution and must be learned. Ho et al. train a network ε_θ(x_t, t) to predict the noise ε that was used to make x_t. The training objective, after simplification, is a plain mean-squared error:

loss = E_{x₀, t, ε} ‖ ε − ε_θ(√ᾱ_t·x₀ + √(1 − ᾱ_t)·ε , t) ‖²
training step: pick a real image x₀, a random t ∈ {1…T}, noise ε ~ N(0,I); form x_t; predict ε; minimise the squared error

Why is predicting the noise the same as predicting the clean image? Because x_t = √ᾱ x₀ + √(1−ᾱ) ε, knowing x_t, t and ε gives x₀ = (x_t − √(1−ᾱ_t)·ε)/√ᾱ_t. The noise target has the convenient property of having unit variance at every level t, so one network can handle all noise levels with a well-scaled loss. Training is stable, has no adversary and needs only ordinary supervised machinery.

Sampling. Start from x_T ~ N(0, I) and for t = T…1 compute the mean of the previous step from the noise prediction and add a small amount of fresh noise:

x_(t−1) = (1/√α_t) · ( x_t − β_t/√(1 − ᾱ_t) · ε_θ(x_t, t) )  +  σ_t · z,        z ~ N(0, I)  (z = 0 at the last step)

with σ_t² = β_t in DDPM. Worked reverse step. At t = 500 with the noisy pixel x₅₀₀ = −0.2557 and, suppose, a perfect noise prediction ε_θ = −0.5: β₅₀₀ = 0.01, α₅₀₀ = 0.99, √(1 − ᾱ) = 0.960. Mean = (1/√0.99)(−0.2557 − 0.01/0.960 × (−0.5)) = 1.005 × (−0.2557 + 0.0052) = −0.2517. The lab confirms that this equals exactly the theoretical posterior mean of x_(t−1) given the true x₀ (both −0.2517): predicting the noise is predicting the clean image, and the step moves the pixel a small distance towards it. Repeating 1,000 such steps (each with a fresh network evaluation) takes static to an image.

A complete model you can run. The lab builds this whole pipeline in NumPy for a one-dimensional "image" with two modes, data drawn from N(−2, 0.3²) or N(+2, 0.3²) with equal probability, using T = 100 steps, a small MLP that takes (x_t, t) and predicts the noise, and 4,000 Adam steps. The figure shows the histograms of 4,000 samples as they are denoised: at t = 100 they form a single wide bell of static, by t = 50 the bell has split, by t = 25 two bumps at ±2 are clear, and at t = 0 there are two sharp modes matching the data (46.5% of the samples above zero against the true 50%, standard deviation 2.00 against 2.02). The network never saw a generated sample; it only learned to predict noise, and the sampler assembled the distribution from those predictions.

6. Conditioning, guidance and control

To generate what the user asks for, feed a condition c to the denoiser: ε_θ(x_t, t, c). For a class label c is an embedding added to the time embedding (or used in adaptive normalisation, dl4); for text, a pretrained text encoder (CLIP or T5) turns the prompt into a sequence of vectors that the U-Net attends to through cross-attention layers: queries come from the image features, keys and values from the text embeddings. Classifier-free guidance (Ho & Salimans, 2022) strengthens the effect: during training the condition is randomly replaced by a null token (about 10–20% of the time), so one network learns both the conditional prediction ε_c and the unconditional ε_u. At sampling time combine them:

ε̂ = ε_u + w · (ε_c − ε_u)        (w = guidance scale; Stable Diffusion's default is 7.5)

With ε_u = 0.2 and ε_c = 0.5: w = 0 gives 0.20 (unconditional), w = 1 gives 0.50 (the plain conditional), w = 3 gives 1.10, w = 7.5 gives 2.45. The extrapolation beyond the conditional prediction pushes the sample further in "the direction that the prompt adds", which improves adherence and sharpness but lowers diversity and, if too large, oversaturates colours and distorts images. Each step now needs two network evaluations (batched together), doubling the cost. (Ho & Salimans write the same thing as (1 + w)ε_c − wε_u, so their w is one less than the scale above.) Beyond text, the same denoiser supports inpainting (at each step overwrite the known pixels with their noised original and let the model fill the rest), image-to-image (noise a given image part-way, then denoise with a new prompt: the "strength" parameter), and structural control via adapters (ControlNet for edges, depth or pose; IP-Adapter for image prompts), with LoRA and DreamBooth fine-tuning for styles and specific subjects (dl11).

7. Latent diffusion and Stable Diffusion

Running the denoiser on raw pixels is expensive: a 512×512 image has 786,432 values and the convolutional and attention layers must process every one, at every step. Latent diffusion (Rombach et al., 2021) first trains an autoencoder to compress images to a much smaller latent tensor, then runs diffusion in that space. In Stable Diffusion v1, the autoencoder has a downsampling factor f = 8: a 512×512×3 image becomes a 64×64×4 latent, so 786,432 values become 16,384: 48 times fewer. The convolutions see 64× fewer positions (4,096 instead of 262,144), and self-attention's matrix, quadratic in positions, is about 4,096 times smaller. The denoising U-Net has about 860 million parameters, the CLIP ViT-L/14 text encoder about 123 million, and by the project's own description it "runs on a GPU with at least 10GB VRAM"; it was trained on 512×512 images from a subset of the LAION-5B dataset.

The autoencoder is trained separately with a reconstruction loss plus a perceptual loss and a patch-based adversarial loss (a GAN discriminator, section 4), with a very small KL weight so the latent stays close to a Gaussian. The Rombach paper studied the downsampling factor: too small (f = 1–2) and training is slow because the latent is nearly as big as the image, too large (f = 32) and fidelity stagnates because too much detail is lost; factors of 4–16 strike a good balance, and f = 4 already reconstructs with far less loss than the more aggressive DALL-E (f = 8) and VQGAN (f = 16) autoencoders it compared with. The limitation to remember: the VAE bounds the achievable detail, which is why small text and faces are often imperfect, and why newer models use a wider latent (16 channels) or a better autoencoder.

8. Making sampling fast

Diffusion's cost is sampling: T (or 2T with guidance) network evaluations per image, against one for a GAN. Four levers have brought it down from 1,000 to as few as 1–4 steps.

  • Better samplers. DDPM sampling is a stochastic differential equation solver; the same trained model defines a deterministic probability-flow ODE with the same marginals. DDIM (Song et al., 2020) samples that ODE with no retraining and reports "high quality samples 10× to 50× faster in terms of wall-clock time" than DDPM; higher-order solvers (DPM-Solver, Heun) reach good quality in 15–25 steps because the ODE trajectory is smooth.
  • Distillation. Train a student to match the teacher in fewer steps: progressive distillation (Salimans & Ho) repeatedly halves the step count, down to about 4; consistency models (Song et al., 2023) learn to map any point on a trajectory straight to its clean endpoint, allowing "fast one-step generation by design" with optional multi-step refinement; adversarial distillation adds a discriminator for sharp few-step samples; guidance distillation folds the CFG's two predictions into one.
  • Straighter paths: flow matching and rectified flow (section 9) learn trajectories that need fewer solver steps.
  • Engineering: latent space, half precision, compiled kernels (gpu6), batching, cached text embeddings, smaller or quantised U-Nets.

Every one trades something (diversity, fine detail, controllability), so evaluate after each change.

9. Flow matching, rectified flow and diffusion transformers

A modern reformulation drops the noise-schedule machinery. Flow matching (Lipman et al., 2022) and rectified flow (Liu et al., 2022) define a path between a data point x₀ and a noise sample ε as a straight line, x_t = (1 − t)·x₀ + t·ε for t ∈ [0, 1], along which the position moves with constant velocity v = ε − x₀. The network v_θ(x_t, t) is trained to regress that velocity with an ordinary squared error; sampling integrates the ODE dx/dt = v_θ from noise back to data with a handful of Euler steps, and straighter paths need fewer steps. Diffusion models correspond to a particular (curved) choice of path, and the two formulations are closely related. Stable Diffusion 3 uses rectified flow with a multimodal diffusion transformer (MM-DiT) with separate weights for image and text tokens. The other architectural shift is from U-Nets to transformers: the diffusion transformer (DiT; Peebles & Xie, 2022) cuts the latent into patches (a patch embedding is a strided convolution, dl6), runs a standard transformer with the timestep and class injected through adaptive layer normalisation, and shows that image quality improves consistently with forward-pass compute; its largest model reached an FID of 2.27 on class-conditional ImageNet 256×256, better than all prior diffusion models. Transformers bring the scaling behaviour and tooling of language models to image and video generation.

10. Evaluating generative models

There is no single number for "good samples". FID (Fréchet Inception Distance; Heusel et al., 2017) embeds real and generated images with a pretrained Inception network (2,048-dimensional features), fits a Gaussian to each set, and computes the Fréchet distance between the Gaussians: ‖μ_r − μ_g‖² + Tr(Σ_r + Σ_g − 2(Σ_rΣ_g)^½). Lower is better. In one dimension it reduces to (μ_r − μ_g)² + (σ_r − σ_g)²: for real N(0, 1) and generated N(0.5, 1.5²), 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5. It rewards both quality (feature means) and diversity (feature covariance), but it depends on the chosen feature network, on image resizing and on the number of samples (typically 10,000–50,000), and it can miss semantic errors. DDPM's headline was a CIFAR-10 FID of 3.17 and Inception score of 9.46. Complementary measures: precision and recall for generative models (fidelity versus coverage), the CLIP score (image–text agreement), likelihood where available (VAEs, flows, diffusion bounds), and human preference studies, which remain the ground truth. Use automatic metrics to compare systems built the same way, not to certify quality, and always look at samples.

Score matching and the SDE view

The score of a density is ∇ₓ log p(x), the direction of steepest increase in log-density; knowing it at every noise level lets you sample by following it uphill with noise (Langevin dynamics). Denoising score matching (Vincent, 2011) shows that the score of the noised distribution can be learned by regressing on the score of a single noising step, which for x_t = √ᾱ x₀ + √(1−ᾱ) ε is −(x_t − √ᾱ x₀)/(1 − ᾱ) = −ε/√(1 − ᾱ_t). So an ε-predicting network is a score estimator up to the known factor −1/√(1−ᾱ_t): at t = 500 with ε = −0.5 the conditional score is 0.5/0.960 = 0.52. Song et al. (2021) unified DDPMs and score models as discretisations of a stochastic differential equation that noises data continuously, dx = f(x, t)dt + g(t)dW, whose time-reversal dx = [f − g² ∇ₓ log p_t(x)]dt + g dW̄ depends only on the score; the same paper shows a deterministic probability-flow ODE with the same marginals, which is why ODE solvers (DDIM, DPM-Solver) can sample in 10–25 steps: the ODE's trajectory is smooth so large steps have small truncation error, while the SDE injects noise at every step that must be averaged out. Sampling in the continuous limit is solving an ODE from noise to data; a discretisation with N steps is an N-evaluation solver.

Parameterisation, schedules and loss weighting

The network can predict ε, the clean image x₀, or the velocity v = √ᾱ_t·ε − √(1−ᾱ_t)·x₀ (Salimans & Ho, 2022); they are algebraically interchangeable but weight noise levels differently in the loss. Predicting x₀ is poorly behaved at high noise (the target is a blurry average), ε-prediction is poorly behaved at low noise (a tiny change of x_t implies a big change in ε), and v interpolates, which is why many recent models use it. The noise schedule matters: the linear schedule wastes many steps at very high noise and makes the image structure vanish fast, so Nichol and Dhariwal proposed the cosine schedule. A subtle flaw: with the linear schedule ᾱ_T = 4×10⁻⁵ is not zero (the table above), so training never sees pure noise but sampling begins from it, which shows up as models that cannot produce very dark or very bright images; enforcing zero terminal SNR (rescaling the schedule and using v-prediction; Lin et al., 2023) fixes it. Loss weighting across timesteps (min-SNR weighting) changes convergence speed and the balance between coarse structure and fine detail, and "noise offset" tricks address brightness. Resolution matters too: for a given schedule, the same noise level destroys much less information in a large image than in a small one, so schedules are shifted for high resolutions (an ingredient of SD3).

The role of the noise levels: what each step learns

Different noise levels teach different things. At high noise (t near T) the network decides global layout and colour: which large blobs go where. At medium noise it forms shapes and objects; at low noise it refines texture and removes residual grain. A diffusion model is therefore a curriculum over scales trained jointly, and this is also why it covers modes so well: at high noise every mode of the data is reachable from any starting point, and the network only has to nudge the sample towards one of them, while low-noise steps only refine. It also explains classifier-free guidance's behaviour: guidance at high noise steers global composition, at low noise it sharpens detail, and applying it only over a middle range of steps (as in "guidance interval" methods) improves diversity.

VAE refinements and the ELBO's tightness

The ELBO is tight only if q(z|x) equals the true posterior; a factorised Gaussian encoder cannot match a complex posterior, leaving a gap. Remedies include importance-weighted autoencoders (a tighter bound by averaging several samples), normalising-flow posteriors, hierarchical VAEs with many latent layers (NVAE, VDVAE: deeper stacks reach image quality competitive with GANs and better likelihoods), and discrete VQ-VAE codes. A diffusion model can itself be read as a hierarchical VAE with T latent layers, a fixed encoder (the noising process) and a learned decoder, whose ELBO decomposes into a sum of KL terms per step; the simple ε-loss is a re-weighted version of that bound, which is why diffusion models have a valid (if loose) likelihood. Ho et al. note their models' log-likelihoods are not competitive with the best likelihood models while their samples are the best of their time, a reminder that likelihood and perceptual quality can diverge.

GAN theory: what goes wrong, and Wasserstein

The Jensen–Shannon divergence between two distributions with disjoint supports is a constant (log 2), so if real and generated data occupy different low-dimensional manifolds (as images plausibly do in a huge ambient space) the discriminator can be perfect and the generator gets no gradient: the theoretical origin of the vanishing-gradient problem. The Earth-Mover (Wasserstein-1) distance stays informative for disjoint supports because it measures how far mass must move. Its dual form needs a critic that is 1-Lipschitz, enforced by clipping weights (crude), a gradient penalty (WGAN-GP: penalise (‖∇ₓ D(x)‖ − 1)²), or spectral normalisation. R1 regularisation (penalising the discriminator's gradient norm on real data) is now common and makes GAN training locally convergent. Mode collapse remains an issue for weaker recipes; StyleGAN-family models combine these tricks with architectural innovations (style-based generator, progressive growing) and remain the state of the art for speed-critical single-domain generation such as faces. Adversarial losses also survive inside other systems: the autoencoder of latent diffusion, super-resolution and few-step distillation of diffusion models.

Guidance, geometry and failure modes

Guidance can be understood as sampling from a sharpened conditional distribution p(x|c)·(p(c|x))^s — the classifier gradient added to the score — with the implicit classifier obtained from the difference between the conditional and unconditional scores. High scales make the sample lie in regions of very high implicit-classifier probability, which look prototypical, saturated and low in diversity; dynamic thresholding (clipping predicted x₀ to a percentile), guidance limited to a range of steps and guidance-distilled models mitigate it. Negative prompts replace the unconditional branch by the embedding of the unwanted content, so ε̂ = ε_neg + w(ε_c − ε_neg) pushes away from it. Compositionality failures (binding attributes to objects, counting, text rendering) come from the text encoder's limited fidelity and the denoiser's cross-attention, and are why newer models add larger text encoders (T5) and multi-modal transformers.

Memorisation, privacy and provenance

Large generative models can reproduce training data. Carlini et al. (2023) generated many images from diffusion models using captions of training images and "extract over a thousand training examples", including over a hundred near-identical replicas from Stable Diffusion and Imagen, ranging from personally identifiable photographs to trademarked company logos. Memorisation is concentrated in images that appear many times in the training set (deduplication reduces it sharply) and in smaller datasets relative to model size; it is not a property of every image. Mitigations: deduplicate training data (near-duplicate as well as exact), limit training epochs per image, differentially private or unlearning methods (immature), filtering of outputs, and opt-out mechanisms. For provenance, watermarking (visible or invisible, embedded in the sampling process) and metadata standards (C2PA content credentials) help identify generated content but can be stripped, so they complement, not replace, detection and policy. Copyright and likeness questions are legal rather than technical, and evolve by jurisdiction.

Beyond images

The same machinery generates video (a spatio-temporal latent, with temporal attention or 3D patches), audio (spectrograms or codec latents), molecules and proteins (diffusion on 3-D coordinates with equivariant networks), and robot action sequences (diffusion policies). For text, autoregressive models dominate because tokens are discrete and the chain-rule factorisation gives exact likelihood and efficient training; diffusion and masked-discrete-diffusion language models exist but have not displaced them, and multimodal models increasingly combine an autoregressive backbone with a diffusion or flow head for images.

The public case: DDPM (2020) and Stable Diffusion (2022)

Jonathan Ho, Ajay Jain and Pieter Abbeel's "Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models" (NeurIPS 2020, arXiv:2006.11239) took an idea from Sohl-Dickstein et al. (2015) that had been ignored for five years and showed it produced excellent samples. Their setup, as reported in the paper: T = 1,000 diffusion steps, forward-process variances increasing linearly from 10⁻⁴ to 0.02, a U-Net denoiser with self-attention (35.7 million parameters for CIFAR-10, 114 million for the 256×256 LSUN and CelebA-HQ models), images scaled to [−1, 1], and training on the simplified noise-prediction loss they derived from a "novel connection between diffusion probabilistic models and denoising score matching with Langevin dynamics". Result: on unconditional CIFAR-10 an Inception score of 9.46 and a state-of-the-art FID of 3.17, and on 256×256 LSUN "sample quality similar to ProgressiveGAN". They also noted that their log-likelihoods were not competitive with the best likelihood-based models, evidence that likelihood and sample quality are different objectives. The cost was sampling speed: 1,000 sequential network evaluations per image. DDIM (Song et al., 2020) removed most of that cost with a deterministic sampler ("10× to 50× faster"), and Nichol and Dhariwal, then Dhariwal and Nichol ("Diffusion models beat GANs") showed diffusion overtaking GANs on ImageNet.

The second half of the story is Rombach et al. (2021), "High-Resolution Image Synthesis with Latent Diffusion Models" (arXiv:2112.10752), the paper behind Stable Diffusion. Their observation, in the abstract's words, was that pixel-space diffusion models "often consume hundreds of GPU days" to optimise and have expensive inference, so they train the diffusion model "in the latent space of powerful pretrained autoencoders", which "allows for the first time to reach a near-optimal point between complexity reduction and detail preservation", with cross-attention layers to condition on text or other inputs. The public Stable Diffusion v1 release (August 2022, from CompVis, Stability AI and LAION) used a downsampling-factor-8 autoencoder, an 860M-parameter U-Net and a 123M-parameter CLIP text encoder, was trained on 512×512 images from a subset of LAION-5B, and ran on a single GPU with at least 10 GB of memory — the first high-quality text-to-image model that people could run on their own hardware. Within months it had spawned fine-tuning methods (DreamBooth, LoRA, textual inversion), control adapters (ControlNet), and a large open ecosystem; it also brought the copyright, consent and safety debates of training-data provenance into public view.

Serving generative models: failure modes and what to watch

Latency and cost. Each image is steps × (1 or 2 with guidance) network evaluations plus a text encoding and a VAE decode. An unoptimised 50-step, guidance-on pipeline that takes 8 seconds (0.16 s per step) is 100 network evaluations; halving the steps with a better sampler halves the latency, distillation to 4 steps gives 10×. Track p50/p95 latency, images per GPU-hour and cost per image; batch requests, use half precision, compile the U-Net, and cache prompt embeddings.

Numerical failures. The VAE decoder in half precision can overflow and produce black or NaN images (a widely reported issue with several Stable Diffusion VAEs, fixed by running the decoder in float32 or using a patched VAE); classifier-free guidance at a high scale can overflow pixel ranges (dynamic thresholding); and mismatched sampler/schedule pairs give washed-out or over-saturated images. Test every deployment on a fixed set of prompts and seeds and compare images and metrics, not just "it runs".

Reproducibility. The same prompt with a different seed, sampler, step count, guidance scale, resolution or library version gives a different image; store all of them with each output, and pin versions. Non-determinism in GPU kernels means bit-exact reproduction may not be possible across hardware.

Quality regressions. Monitor with a fixed evaluation set: FID (against a reference set, with the caveats above), CLIP score for prompt adherence, aesthetic scores, and a small rotating human review. Sudden changes usually come from a sampler, VAE, text-encoder or tokenizer change, not the U-Net.

Safety and abuse. Input filters (prompt classifiers), output classifiers for unsafe content and faces of real people, rate limits, provenance watermarking and metadata (C2PA), audit logs, and a process for takedown and user reports. Open-weight releases can have their filters removed, so downstream deployers are responsible for their own.

Data and legal. Deduplicate training data; record provenance and licences; honour opt-outs; test for training-data extraction (the Carlini et al. procedure) before release; keep a documented data card.

Common product failure modes. Anatomy errors (hands), unreadable rendered text, wrong counts, attribute binding ("a red cube on a blue sphere" swapping colours), prompt-following drift at high guidance, and out-of-distribution styles. They guide model choice (a larger text encoder or transformer backbone) and prompt engineering (structured prompts, negative prompts) and are why human review stays in the loop for high-stakes use.

What to monitor

Latency percentiles and GPU utilisation per stage (text encode, denoise loop, decode); failure rates (NaNs, black images, timeouts); image statistics (mean brightness and saturation drift) and safety-classifier hit rates; distribution of prompts and guidance settings; and offline quality metrics (FID on a fixed set, CLIP score, human ratings) on every model or sampler change.

Goal: compute the numbers of the lesson (the DDPM schedule, a noising step, one reverse step, the VAE's KL and reparameterisation, the GAN's optimal discriminator and gradient saturation, FID in one dimension, classifier-free guidance), and then train a complete diffusion model from scratch: a small NumPy MLP learns to predict noise for one-dimensional data with two modes, and a 100-step sampler turns Gaussian static into that distribution. NumPy only, Python 3.12, about 25 seconds (4,000 training steps of a 128-unit network).

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
sig = lambda z: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))

# ---- 1. the DDPM noise schedule (Ho et al.): T = 1000, beta linear from 1e-4 to 0.02
betas = np.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, 1000); abar = np.cumprod(1 - betas)
print("t     abar_t    signal sqrt(abar)  noise sqrt(1-abar)   SNR = abar/(1-abar)")
for t in (1, 100, 250, 500, 750, 1000):
    a = abar[t - 1]; print(f"{t:4d}  {a:9.5f}  {np.sqrt(a):12.4f}  {np.sqrt(1 - a):16.4f}  {a / (1 - a):16.4g}")
x0, eps, t = 0.8, -0.5, 500                                                    # noise one "pixel" straight to step 500 (no loop needed)
xt = np.sqrt(abar[t - 1]) * x0 + np.sqrt(1 - abar[t - 1]) * eps
print(f"\nx_500 = sqrt(abar)*0.8 + sqrt(1-abar)*(-0.5) = {xt:.4f}")
a_t, ab, ab_prev = 1 - betas[t - 1], abar[t - 1], abar[t - 2]                   # one reverse step if the network predicted the noise perfectly
mean = (xt - betas[t - 1] / np.sqrt(1 - ab) * eps) / np.sqrt(a_t)
posterior = (np.sqrt(ab_prev) * betas[t - 1] / (1 - ab)) * x0 + (np.sqrt(a_t) * (1 - ab_prev) / (1 - ab)) * xt
print(f"reverse-step mean from the noise prediction = {mean:.4f}; exact posterior mean given x_0 = {posterior:.4f} (equal: predicting the noise is predicting x_0)")

# ---- 2. VAE: KL to the prior in closed form, and the reparameterisation trick
mu, sd = np.array([0.8, -0.5]), np.array([0.6, 1.2])
kl = 0.5 * (mu ** 2 + sd ** 2 - np.log(sd ** 2) - 1)
e = np.array([0.5, -1.0]); z = mu + sd * e
print("\nKL per latent dim:", np.round(kl, 4), " total", round(kl.sum(), 4), " | z = mu + sigma*eps =", z, " (dz/dmu = 1, dz/dsigma = eps)")

# ---- 3. GAN: optimal discriminator, equilibrium value, and why the generator loss is changed
pdata, pg = 0.6, 0.2
print("\nD*(x) at a point with p_data=0.6, p_g=0.2:", pdata / (pdata + pg), " | at equilibrium (p_g = p_data): 0.5, value V = -log 4 =", round(-np.log(4), 4))
a = -4.6                                                                        # discriminator logit for a generated sample it is sure is fake: D = sigmoid(a) ~ 0.01
print(f"D(G(z)) = {sig(a):.3f}: gradient of log(1-D) wrt the logit = {-sig(a):.3f} (saturates); of -log D = {-(1 - sig(a)):.3f} (non-saturating)")

# ---- 4. FID in one dimension: Frechet distance between two Gaussians
mr, sr, mg, sg = 0.0, 1.0, 0.5, 1.5
print("\n1-D Frechet distance:", (mr - mg) ** 2 + (sr - sg) ** 2, "= mean gap^2 + std gap^2  (the image FID uses 2048-d Inception features and a matrix version)")

# ---- 5. classifier-free guidance: extrapolate from the unconditional to the conditional prediction
e_u, e_c = 0.2, 0.5
for w in (0, 1, 3, 7.5): print(f"guidance scale {w}: eps = eps_u + w*(eps_c - eps_u) = {e_u + w * (e_c - e_u):.2f}")

# ---- 6. a complete tiny diffusion model: learn the noise for 1-D data with two modes (-2 and +2), then sample
T = 100; b = np.linspace(1e-3, 0.06, T); al = 1 - b; ab = np.cumprod(al)
def data(n): return np.where(rng.random(n) < 0.5, -2.0, 2.0) + 0.3 * rng.normal(size=n)
def feats(x, t):
    tt = t / T; return np.stack([x, tt, np.sin(np.pi * tt), np.cos(np.pi * tt), np.sin(2 * np.pi * tt), np.cos(2 * np.pi * tt)], 1)
H = 128
P = {"W1": rng.normal(0, 1 / np.sqrt(6), (6, H)), "b1": np.zeros(H), "W2": rng.normal(0, 1 / np.sqrt(H), (H, H)), "b2": np.zeros(H),
     "W3": rng.normal(0, 0.1 / np.sqrt(H), (H, 1)), "b3": np.zeros(1)}
M = {k: np.zeros_like(v) for k, v in P.items()}; V = {k: np.zeros_like(v) for k, v in P.items()}
def net(F):
    h1 = np.tanh(F @ P["W1"] + P["b1"]); h2 = np.tanh(h1 @ P["W2"] + P["b2"]); return (h2 @ P["W3"] + P["b3"])[:, 0], (F, h1, h2)
for step in range(1, 4001):                                                     # training: predict the noise that was added
    x0 = data(512); t = rng.integers(0, T, 512); eps = rng.normal(size=512)
    xt = np.sqrt(ab[t]) * x0 + np.sqrt(1 - ab[t]) * eps
    out, (F, h1, h2) = net(feats(xt, t)); d = (2 * (out - eps) / 512)[:, None]  # loss = mean (eps - eps_theta)^2
    G = {"W3": h2.T @ d, "b3": d.sum(0)}
    d2 = (d @ P["W3"].T) * (1 - h2 ** 2); G["W2"], G["b2"] = h1.T @ d2, d2.sum(0)
    d1 = (d2 @ P["W2"].T) * (1 - h1 ** 2); G["W1"], G["b1"] = F.T @ d1, d1.sum(0)
    for k in P:                                                                 # Adam
        M[k] = 0.9 * M[k] + 0.1 * G[k]; V[k] = 0.999 * V[k] + 0.001 * G[k] ** 2
        P[k] -= 2e-3 * (M[k] / (1 - 0.9 ** step)) / (np.sqrt(V[k] / (1 - 0.999 ** step)) + 1e-8)
    if step % 1000 == 0: print(f"step {step}: noise-prediction loss {((out - eps) ** 2).mean():.3f}")
x = rng.normal(size=4000); snaps = {T: x.copy()}                                # sampling: start from static, denoise T times
for t in range(T - 1, -1, -1):
    e, _ = net(feats(x, np.full(len(x), t)))
    x = (x - b[t] / np.sqrt(1 - ab[t]) * e) / np.sqrt(al[t]) + (np.sqrt(b[t]) * rng.normal(size=len(x)) if t > 0 else 0)
    if t in (75, 50, 25, 10): snaps[t] = x.copy()
snaps[0] = x
bins = np.linspace(-4, 4, 17)
print("\nhistogram of 4,000 generated samples :", np.histogram(x, bins)[0])
print("histogram of 4,000 real data samples :", np.histogram(data(4000), bins)[0])
print(f"generated: fraction above 0 = {(x > 0).mean():.3f} (data 0.5), std = {x.std():.2f} (data 2.02)")
np.save("dl10_snaps.npy", np.stack([snaps[k] for k in (T, 75, 50, 25, 0)]))

What to look for.

  1. The schedule table matches the lesson: ᾱ₅₀₀ = 0.0786 (weights 0.280 on the clean value and 0.960 on the noise), ᾱ₁₀₀₀ = 4×10⁻⁵. The noised pixel is −0.2557, the reverse mean from a perfect noise prediction is −0.2517, and it equals the exact posterior mean: predicting noise is predicting the clean image.
  2. VAE: KL = (0.511, 0.163), total 0.674; z = (1.1, −1.7) for ε = (0.5, −1.0).
  3. GAN: D* = 0.75 where p_data = 0.6 and p_g = 0.2; the equilibrium value −1.386; for a discriminator sure that a sample is fake (D = 0.01) the saturating loss's gradient is −0.010 and the non-saturating one's is −0.990.
  4. FID in 1-D is 0.5; guidance gives 0.20, 0.50, 1.10 and 2.45 for scales 0, 1, 3 and 7.5.
  5. The diffusion model. The noise-prediction loss hovers around 0.36–0.42 (it cannot reach zero because the noise is unpredictable); the generated histogram has two clear modes at about ±2 with 46.5% of the samples above zero (data: 50%) and a standard deviation of 2.00 (data 2.02). Change the data (three modes; unequal weights), the number of steps T, the network size, or the training steps and see what breaks first: with too little training the modes blur into each other; with too few sampling steps the samples are noisy.

Explain and choose

(a) A plain autoencoder trained on faces reconstructs well, but decoding random latent vectors gives noise. Why, and what change fixes it? (b) Your team needs to generate synthetic product photos on a white background conditioned on a text description, and also fill in missing regions of existing photos. Which family, and how does text conditioning work? (c) Sampling takes 8 seconds per image with 50 steps and guidance; the product needs under 1 second. Options?

Answer

(a) Nothing in training organises the latent space: encodings can sit in scattered clusters, and random vectors land in regions the decoder never saw. A VAE fixes it by encoding distributions (μ, σ), sampling with the reparameterisation trick and adding a KL penalty towards N(0, I), so the region you sample from is exactly the region the decoder was trained on.

(b) A (latent) diffusion model. Text is encoded by a text encoder (CLIP or T5); the denoising network attends to the embeddings through cross-attention at every step; classifier-free guidance (scale about 5–8) strengthens adherence. Inpainting is native: at each step keep the known pixels (noised to the current level) and let the model generate only the masked region, or use a model fine-tuned with the mask as an extra input. Fine-tune on your own product photos (LoRA) for the white-background style.

(c) 8 s for 50 steps with guidance is 100 network evaluations at about 80 ms each. Options in order of effort: a faster sampler (DPM-Solver++ at 15–20 steps) cuts it to about 2.4 s; guidance distillation removes the doubled cost (another 2×); a distilled few-step model (consistency or adversarial distillation, 1–4 steps) reaches well under 1 s; lower resolution plus a fast upscaler; fp16/bf16, a compiled U-Net (gpu6), batching, and cached text embeddings. Measure quality (FID/preference) after each change; few-step models trade some diversity.

Work it out — latent diffusion economics

A pixel-space diffusion U-Net processes 512×512×3 images; a latent-diffusion model uses an autoencoder with downsampling factor 8 and 4 latent channels. Compute the compression factor of the data, the reduction in spatial positions, and the reduction in the size of a self-attention matrix if attention operates on every position. What does the autoencoder cost you?

Answer

Values: 512 × 512 × 3 = 786,432 versus 64 × 64 × 4 = 16,384: 48× fewer. Positions: 262,144 versus 4,096: 64× fewer, so convolution cost (proportional to positions) falls about 64× (channel widths in the U-Net differ, so the real saving depends on the architecture). Attention matrix: proportional to the square of the positions: (262,144)² ≈ 6.9×10¹⁰ versus (4,096)² ≈ 1.7×10⁷, about 4,096× smaller. Together these are why a diffusion model with high-resolution outputs and text conditioning became trainable and runnable on one GPU.

The cost: the autoencoder is lossy, so it caps fidelity: small text, thin structures and faces at small scale can be wrong however good the diffusion model is, an extra network (the VAE decoder) must run once per image, it can fail numerically in half precision, and any bias or artefact in the VAE is inherited by every generated image. Better autoencoders (more latent channels) and pixel-space refinement stages address it.
