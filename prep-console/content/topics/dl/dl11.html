A hospital wants software that flags pneumonia on chest X-rays. It has 800 labelled X-rays, each one read and annotated by a radiologist, which took months and cost a great deal. It trains a neural network from scratch. The network learns the training set perfectly, memorising the scanner artefacts, the patient-position quirks and even the hospital's watermark, and on new patients it is barely better than a coin toss. Eight hundred examples is simply not enough to teach a network with millions of weights what an edge, a texture, a rib or a shadow is, let alone what pneumonia looks like.

Meanwhile, somebody else has already trained a network on 1.28 million everyday photographs. That network never saw an X-ray, and pneumonia is not among its thousand categories. But to tell a tabby from a tiger it had to learn to detect edges, then corners, then textures, then repeated patterns, then parts of objects, and those skills are useful for X-rays too. If the hospital starts from those weights instead of from random numbers, it needs to learn only the last step: what combination of these already-available features means pneumonia. That takes a few hundred examples, minutes of training, and works.

The same story played out in language, and it changed the field. Until 2018, most language tasks were solved by training a model from scratch on that task's labelled data: sentiment on movie reviews, question answering on Wikipedia, entailment on sentence pairs. Then two groups showed that a model first trained to read enormous amounts of unlabelled text could be adapted to nearly any of these tasks with a small labelled set, and beat everything built specially for each task. One of those models, BERT, set new records on eleven tasks at once and could be fine-tuned in about an hour. Every large language model you use today, from a chat assistant to a coding tool, is built on this idea: an expensive general pretraining run that a few organisations can afford, followed by cheap adaptation that everyone can.

The economics are the whole point. Pretraining a modern language model costs millions of dollars of GPU time (the GPU track puts numbers on it). Adapting one to your task should cost a few dollars and an afternoon, and it must not break what the model already knows. This lesson is about how to do that adaptation well: which of the five or six options to choose (do nothing, train only a small classifier, train part of the model, train all of it, or train a tiny add-on such as LoRA), why the naive approach of "just keep training with the same settings" often fails, what a good pretrained representation actually is, and how much memory each option needs for a 7-billion-parameter model. You will also run a small experiment that reproduces the effect on a toy problem, so that the numbers are yours rather than a claim.

The apprentice analogy

Think of someone who has spent years learning to read and write. They can already parse sentences, recognise words and follow arguments. Ask them to become a contract lawyer and they do not start by learning the alphabet: they learn legal vocabulary and conventions on top of the reading skills they have. A person who never learned to read would need years longer. A pretrained neural network is the first person: its weights (the millions or billions of numbers that define what the network computes) already encode a lot of general skill. Transfer learning means reusing those weights as the starting point for a new task, called the target task, instead of starting from random ones. The task the weights were originally trained on is the source task.

Two more words. Training on the big general dataset first is called pretraining; the later, small-scale training on your data is fine-tuning when it changes the pretrained weights, and everything after pretraining is loosely called adaptation. A task that comes after pretraining is called downstream, so "downstream accuracy" means accuracy on the target task.

Anatomy of a pretrained model

A network for classifying images or text is a stack of layers: each layer takes numbers in and produces numbers out. The layers near the input do simple, general processing; the layers near the output combine those results into task-specific answers. We split the network into two parts:

  • The backbone (also called the encoder or the body): almost all of the layers. It turns an input, such as a photo or a sentence, into a list of numbers called an embedding or feature vector. A ResNet-50 backbone, for instance, outputs 2,048 numbers for each image. Those numbers are the network's own description of the input, and the descriptions of similar inputs are close together. This description is called the representation.
  • The head: a small final part, often a single linear layer, which turns the embedding into the answer for a specific task, such as scores for 1,000 ImageNet classes. When you move to a new task with 10 classes, you throw away the old head and put a fresh, randomly initialised 10-class head on the backbone.

To freeze a part of the network means to hold its weights fixed: no updates. To unfreeze it means to allow training to change them. Freezing also has a cost benefit: a frozen weight needs no gradient and no optimiser state, which, as the memory section below shows, is most of the memory of training.

Five ways to adapt, from cheapest to dearest

Everything in this lesson is a point on one ladder, drawn below. Higher rungs allow the model to change more, which means a higher ceiling on quality, and cost more compute, more memory, more data and more risk.

  1. Zero-shot or prompting. Use the model as it is. A language model is given instructions and examples in its prompt; an image–text model such as CLIP compares an image with the sentence "a photo of a cat". No weights change.
  2. Linear probe (feature extraction). Freeze the backbone, compute the embeddings of your labelled data once, and train only a small linear classifier on them. Training takes seconds to minutes, on a CPU.
  3. Partial fine-tuning. Unfreeze the last few layers (the most task-specific) and keep the rest frozen.
  4. Full fine-tuning. Update every weight. Highest ceiling, and highest cost: about 16 bytes per weight for mixed-precision Adam.
  5. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT). Freeze the model, attach small trainable pieces (adapters, LoRA matrices), and train only those. Often close to full fine-tuning in quality at a small fraction of the trainable parameters and memory.

A small example with real numbers

Suppose you have a two-class problem and only 10 labelled examples. Training a small network from scratch on such data means guessing dozens of parameters from ten points: it will fit them, and it will be wrong on new points. In this lesson's lab, a toy problem constructed so that a pretraining task and a target task share hidden structure gave these test accuracies with 10 labelled examples: 0.643 for a network trained from scratch, 0.775 for a linear probe on the frozen pretrained features. With 160 examples the from-scratch network catches up to 0.846 and the probe reaches 0.866, and a carefully fine-tuned model reaches 0.893. That pattern (a large gain when labelled data is scarce, a shrinking gain as it becomes plentiful) is the signature of transfer learning, and it is why the recipe of "start from a pretrained model" is the default in almost every applied deep learning project.

What the rest of the lesson covers

We first see why features transfer, then walk up the ladder rung by rung, working out by hand what goes wrong when you fine-tune carelessly and what the standard fixes do. Then we look at where good representations come from (supervised, self-supervised and contrastive pretraining), at LoRA and QLoRA with a memory calculation for a 7B model, and finally at the trade-offs and failure modes you meet in practice, including what fine-tuning can and cannot teach a language model.

Why features transfer at all

The reason is that deep networks build their answers in stages. Look at a convolutional network trained on photos (the CNN lesson, dl6): the first layer learns small detectors for edges at various angles and blobs of colour. The next layers combine edges into corners, curves and textures; middle layers combine those into repeated patterns and object parts such as wheels or eyes; the last layers assemble parts into whole-object evidence and finally into the class scores. Edges and textures are just as useful for X-rays, satellite pictures and product photos as for cats, so those layers transfer directly. The last layers were shaped by the exact 1,000 classes of the source task and are the least reusable.

Yosinski et al. measured this in 2014. They trained one network on half of the ImageNet classes and another on the other half, then built hybrid networks by copying the first n layers of one into the other and either freezing them or letting them keep training. Features copied from the first layers worked almost perfectly, features from the last layers worked worst, and they identified two separate causes of losses: specialisation (higher-layer neurons are tuned to the source task) and, less expected, fragile co-adaptation (neighbouring layers had learned to depend on each other in ways that break if you split them apart). They also found that transferability falls as the distance between source and target tasks grows, but that even a distant source initialisation was better than random weights after fine-tuning.

A second, more abstract reason has been found for large language models: Aghajanyan et al. showed that fine-tuning a pretrained model has a very low intrinsic dimension. Optimising only 200 parameters that are randomly projected into the full weight space was enough for a RoBERTa model to reach 90% of full fine-tuning accuracy on one benchmark (MRPC). Pretraining leaves the network in a place where a few directions of change are enough to adapt it. This is the empirical backbone of LoRA, which we meet below.

Rung 1: zero-shot and prompting

The cheapest adaptation is to not adapt. A model that was pretrained on a broad enough objective can perform new tasks from a description alone. CLIP, from OpenAI in 2021, trained an image encoder and a text encoder on 400 million (image, caption) pairs so that the embedding of a photo is close to the embedding of its caption. To classify photos into your own classes, you write each class as a sentence ("a photo of a dog", "a photo of a truck"), embed those sentences, and pick the class whose sentence embedding is closest to the image's. No training on your data, and no fixed label set. The paper reports matching the accuracy of the original ResNet-50 on ImageNet without using any of its 1.28 million labelled training images. For language models the equivalent is prompting: describing the task and maybe including a few examples in the prompt. If zero-shot is good enough, stop here: you have no training pipeline to maintain.

Rung 2: a linear probe on frozen features

If zero-shot is not enough and you have some labels, the next step is the linear probe. Run each labelled image through the frozen backbone once, store the resulting embedding, and fit a linear classifier (logistic regression, from the ML track) on the embeddings. Because the embeddings are computed once and the classifier is tiny, this trains in seconds, can be cross-validated properly (ml9), and cannot damage the backbone.

Count the numbers. For a ResNet-50 backbone with 2,048 outputs and a 12-class problem, the head is a 2,048×12 weight matrix plus 12 biases, so 24,588 trainable parameters, against about 25.6 million in the backbone. Even on a very small dataset this is a model that can be fitted without heavy overfitting, especially with an L2 penalty (ml3). For a 512-dimensional embedding and the same 12 classes, 512×12 + 12 = 6,156 parameters.

A probe is also the standard measurement of a representation's quality. If a linear classifier on a frozen embedding does well, the information is available in a simple, usable form. That is why papers on self-supervised learning report "linear probe accuracy on ImageNet". A linear probe is strictly limited by what the frozen features contain and by preprocessing: the backbone must see images normalised, resized and cropped exactly as during pretraining, and text tokenised with its own tokeniser. A mismatch here silently degrades everything and is among the most common transfer bugs.

Rung 3: fine-tuning, and what goes wrong when you do it naively

Fine-tuning goes further: it lets gradient descent change the backbone too, so the features can adapt to your data, not just be re-read. That is where the gains at larger dataset sizes come from, and also where the danger is. A worked example makes the danger visible.

Take the smallest possible network with a body and a head: one pretrained body weight w and one new head weight v, so the prediction is ŷ = v·w·x. The body weight is w = 0.8, from pretraining, and it is good: the right head would make everything work. But the head is new and random, v = −1.2 (by chance the wrong sign). One training example: x = 1, target t = 1, loss L = ½(ŷ − t)².

  1. Forward: ŷ = (−1.2)(0.8)(1) = −0.96. The error is ŷ − t = −1.96.
  2. Backward: ∂L/∂v = error·w·x = −1.96 × 0.8 = −1.568, and ∂L/∂w = error·v·x = −1.96 × (−1.2) = +2.352.
  3. Update with one learning rate of 0.1 for both: v becomes −1.2 − 0.1(−1.568) = −1.043, and w becomes 0.8 − 0.1(2.352) = 0.565.

Look at the second gradient. The body's gradient is proportional to the head weight, which is garbage. In one step the good body weight changed by 29% (0.8 to 0.565), and in the wrong direction: the head still has the wrong sign, and the gradient is telling the body to shrink because the random head cannot be fixed as fast as the body can be broken. In a real network with millions of weights this happens in every layer: a random head sends large, wrong-headed gradients back into the pretrained layers, which spend the first steps of training being rewritten to fit a random head instead of the data.

There are three standard remedies, each visible in the numbers:

  • A smaller learning rate for the body. Give the body one thirtieth of the head's rate (0.003 against 0.1): the body update is 0.003 × 2.352 = 0.007, so w changes from 0.800 to 0.793, less than 1%. The head still moves at full speed towards a sensible value.
  • Train the head first. With the body frozen, gradient descent on v alone drives it to v = t/w = 1.25, at which point the error is zero and the gradient on the body is zero too. Then you can unfreeze the body and the gradients that reach it are small and pointed in the right direction. This head-first-then-everything recipe is called LP-FT (linear probe, then fine-tune).
  • Warmup. Start the learning rate near zero and raise it over the first steps so the head has time to become sensible before the body sees big updates.

These are not hypothetical. The lab measures them on a toy problem (figure below). Fine-tuning the whole network with the same learning rate for body and head (3e-3) ended up no better than training from scratch (0.645 against 0.643 with 10 labelled examples: the initialisation was wrecked before it could help). Fine-tuning with the body learning rate cut to 1e-4 (a factor of 30) gave 0.744 at 10 examples and the best of all methods from 40 examples upwards: 0.841 at 40, 0.872 at 80 and 0.893 at 160, against 0.780, 0.833 and 0.846 from scratch. With few labels, the frozen probe wins (0.775 at 10, 0.807 at 20); with more labels, a carefully fine-tuned body eventually overtakes it. The crossover is the main practical lesson: start with the probe, and fine-tune only when there are enough labels to justify it.

Discriminative learning rates, gradual unfreezing and the slanted triangle (ULMFiT)

Howard and Ruder's ULMFiT (2018) packaged these ideas into a recipe for language models. It has three stages: (a) pretrain a language model, a network that predicts the next word, on a big general corpus (Wikitext-103, 28,595 Wikipedia articles and 103 million words); (b) fine-tune that language model on the unlabelled text of the target task; (c) add a classifier head and fine-tune on the labelled target data. Stages (b) and (c) use three tricks:

  • Discriminative learning rates. Lower layers hold more general features, so they should move less. The last layer gets a rate η, and each layer below gets the rate of the layer above divided by 2.6 (the paper's empirical choice). With η = 0.01 for four layers: 0.01, 0.0038, 0.0015, 0.00057.
  • Slanted triangular learning rate (STLR). The rate climbs linearly for a short fraction of training (cut_frac = 0.1) up to η_max = 0.01 and then decays linearly for the rest, ending at η_max/ratio with ratio = 32. For T = 1,000 steps: η = 0.0003 at step 0, 0.0052 at step 50, 0.0100 at step 100, 0.0052 at step 550 and 0.0003 at step 1,000. It is the fine-tuning form of warmup plus linear decay, with the "short rise, long fall" shape the authors found important.
  • Gradual unfreezing. First train only the last layer; after one epoch unfreeze the next layer down and train the unfrozen ones; continue down to the first layer. The body changes last and least.

The ablation (validation error on IMDb movie reviews) shows each helping: 6.99% with no language-model fine-tuning, 5.86% with plain fine-tuning of the language model, 5.55% with discriminative rates added, 5.00% with the slanted triangle too. Modern LLM fine-tuning uses lower rates, warmup and decay, but has mostly replaced gradual unfreezing with parameter-efficient methods, which reach the same goal (change the model a little) more directly.

How BERT is fine-tuned

BERT, published in 2018 by Devlin et al., is the other reference case. Its fine-tuning recipe shows how mundane the procedure becomes once pretraining does the hard part. For each of the GLUE language-understanding tasks the authors added one linear layer on top of the pretrained encoder and trained everything with a batch size of 32 for 3 epochs, choosing the learning rate from {5e-5, 4e-5, 3e-5, 2e-5}. Compare the pretraining learning rate of 1e-4 for a million steps: fine-tuning uses a rate two to five times lower and only 3 passes over the data instead of a million steps. On the small datasets they found BERT-large sometimes unstable and ran several random restarts, picking the best on the development set; this is a real phenomenon (fine-tuning a big model on a small dataset has high variance between seeds), and a reminder to report the spread and not one lucky run.

Practical details that matter in fine-tuning

  • Normalisation layers. A BatchNorm backbone (dl4) carries running means and variances estimated on the pretraining data. With small fine-tuning batches those running statistics get noisy; the usual fix is to keep BatchNorm layers in evaluation mode (or frozen) or switch to GroupNorm. LayerNorm and RMSNorm, used in transformers, compute statistics per example and have no running statistics, so the problem does not arise.
  • Regularisation (dl8). With a few hundred examples, use weight decay, dropout, data augmentation and early stopping on a validation split. Small data plus high capacity means memorisation is the default outcome.
  • Optimiser. AdamW (dl5) is the standard; the optimiser states start empty, and warmup is essential because Adam's early estimates are noisy.
  • Layer-wise learning-rate decay. A generalisation of discriminative rates used for vision transformers and BERT-style encoders: each layer's rate is the layer above's times a factor like 0.75 to 0.95.
  • The new head. Initialise it small (or with the linear probe's solution, as in LP-FT) so the loss gradient into the body is small at the start.

Where good representations come from

What is transferred is a representation, so it is worth knowing how representations are produced. There are three families.

Supervised pretraining learns from labels, as ImageNet classification did. It produces excellent features, but it needs a large labelled dataset and the features are biased towards the label set.

Self-supervised pretraining (ml1) makes the labels from the data itself. In a language model the task is to predict the next token from the previous ones (GPT-style) or to predict masked tokens from both sides (BERT: 15% of tokens are hidden, and of those chosen 80% are replaced by a [MASK] symbol, 10% by a random token and 10% left unchanged, so the model cannot rely on seeing [MASK]). Predicting the missing part forces the network to learn grammar, facts and reasoning patterns, using text nobody had to label. BERT was trained for 1,000,000 steps with batches of 256 sequences of 512 tokens on a corpus of 3.3 billion words; BERT-base has 110 million parameters and BERT-large 340 million.

Contrastive pretraining learns by comparison: embeddings of two things that belong together (two augmented views of one image, or an image and its caption) should be close, and those of unrelated things far apart. In CLIP, a batch contains N image–caption pairs, and a matrix S of N×N similarity scores is computed, where Sij is the (scaled) similarity of image i and caption j. The matching pairs are on the diagonal, and the loss for each row is a softmax cross-entropy asking the correct caption to win. A worked case with N = 3 and scores

            caption 1  caption 2  caption 3
image 1        4          1          0
image 2        0          3          1
image 3        1          0          5

Row 1: e⁴ = 54.60, e¹ = 2.72, e⁰ = 1.00 sum to 58.32, so the probability of the right caption is 54.60/58.32 = 0.936 and the loss is −ln 0.936 = 0.066. Row 2: e⁰ = 1, e³ = 20.09, e¹ = 2.72 sum to 23.80, the probability is 0.844 and the loss 0.170. Row 3: 2.72, 1, e⁵ = 148.41 sum to 152.13, the probability is 0.976 and the loss 0.025. The average loss is (0.066 + 0.170 + 0.025)/3 = 0.087. The gradient pushes each diagonal score up and each off-diagonal score down; a large batch supplies many negatives and makes the task harder and the embeddings sharper, which is why SimCLR's linear probe reached 76.5% top-1 accuracy on ImageNet (matching a supervised ResNet-50) with large batches. Contrastive pretraining makes embedding models for search and retrieval-augmented generation: the same idea with a query and its relevant passage as the pair.

Rung 4: parameter-efficient fine-tuning and LoRA

Full fine-tuning of a big model is a problem of arithmetic. The GPU track (gpu3, gpu9) derived it: with mixed-precision Adam, every weight costs 16 bytes (bf16 weight and gradient, fp32 master copy, momentum and variance), so a 7-billion-parameter model needs 7×10⁹ × 16 = 112 GB before a single activation is stored. And every task you fine-tune for produces a complete new copy of the model to store and serve. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) freezes the model and trains a small number of new parameters. Older variants inserted small adapter layers in the middle of each block (which add a little latency at inference) or trained only extra "prompt" vectors prepended to the input. The variant that took over is LoRA.

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation, Hu et al., 2021) starts from an observation: the change that fine-tuning makes to a weight matrix is small, and can be approximated by a low-rank matrix. The rank of a matrix is the number of independent directions it uses; a d×d matrix of rank r can be written as a product of a d×r matrix B and an r×d matrix A. Instead of learning the full update ΔW (d² numbers), LoRA learns B and A (2dr numbers):

h = W₀x + ΔW x = W₀x + (α/r) · B(Ax)

W₀ is frozen. A (r×d) is initialised with small random numbers and B (d×r) with zeros, so ΔW = 0 at the start and the model is exactly the pretrained model. r is the rank (typically 8 to 64) and α a constant that scales the update by α/r, which makes the learning rate needed roughly independent of r.

A hand example. Use d = 4 and r = 1. Let x = (1, 2, 0, 1), A = (0.5, −0.5, 0, 1) (one row) and, after some training, B = (0.2, 0, −0.4, 0.1)ᵀ (one column). Then Ax = 0.5 − 1 + 0 + 1 = 0.5, a single number (the bottleneck), and B(Ax) = 0.5 × (0.2, 0, −0.4, 0.1) = (0.1, 0, −0.2, 0.05). With α = r the scale is 1, so this is what LoRA adds to W₀x. The equivalent full update is ΔW = BA, a 4×4 matrix with 16 entries whose rows are multiples of A: (0.1, −0.1, 0, 0.2), then a zero row, then (−0.2, 0.2, 0, −0.4), then (0.05, −0.05, 0, 0.1). It has rank 1 and needs 8 numbers (4 in A and 4 in B) instead of 16. At d = 4,096 and r = 8 the ratio is 2dr/d² = 65,536/16,777,216 = 0.39%.

Why B = 0 and A random. The gradient of the loss with respect to B is (upstream gradient) × (Ax)ᵀ, non-zero from the first step. The gradient with respect to A is Bᵀ × (upstream gradient) × xᵀ, which is zero while B = 0. So B learns first, and once it is non-zero A starts to move too. If both were zero, both gradients would be zero and nothing would ever change; if both were random, the model would start with a random perturbation of the pretrained function.

What it buys. After training, ΔW can be merged into the base weight (W = W₀ + (α/r)BA) so that inference has zero extra latency, or the adapter can be kept separate: one shared frozen base model and many small adapters, one per customer or task, swapped in and out. In the LoRA paper the numbers for GPT-3 175B were a training memory of 1.2 TB reduced to 350 GB, a checkpoint per task of 350 GB reduced to 35 MB (with r = 4 on the query and value matrices), and a 25% training speed-up; on DeBERTa-XXL, LoRA with 4.7 million trainable parameters scored 91.3 on GLUE against 91.1 for full fine-tuning of 1,500 million.

Counting for a 7B model. A Llama-style 7B model has 32 layers with hidden size 4,096. LoRA with r = 8 on the query and value projections adds 32 layers × 2 matrices × 2 × 4,096 × 8 = 4.19 million parameters, 0.06% of the model. With r = 16 on every linear layer (attention and feed-forward) it is 40.0 million, 0.59%. The QLoRA authors found that adapting all linear layers is needed to match full fine-tuning, and that with all layers adapted the rank r hardly matters.

The memory table for a 7B model

Now the arithmetic. The frozen base needs no gradients and no optimiser state, so only the adapters pay the 16 bytes:

The frozen bf16 weights are 96% of the LoRA total, which is why the next step is to shrink them. QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023) stores the base weights in 4 bits using a data type called NormalFloat (NF4), designed for weights that are roughly normally distributed, and applies double quantisation: the per-block scaling constants that quantisation needs are themselves quantised, saving about 0.37 bits per parameter (about 3 GB for a 65B model). During the forward and backward passes each layer's weights are dequantised on the fly to bf16 for the matrix multiplication, the gradient flows through the frozen 4-bit weights into the LoRA matrices, and only those matrices are updated. A third feature, paged optimisers, lets optimiser states spill into CPU memory during memory spikes. The paper reports fine-tuning a 65B model on a single 48 GB GPU, where regular 16-bit full fine-tuning needs more than 780 GB (under the 16-bytes-per-parameter accounting of this course the number would be 1,040 GB; the paper's figure is a lower bound, and the point is the same: it does not fit on a single device). Activations are extra in all three rows and depend on batch size and sequence length; gradient checkpointing (gpu9) reduces them.

Choosing a rung

Decision rules that hold up in practice:

  • Try zero-shot or prompting first, since it costs nothing to test.
  • With a small labelled set (tens to a few thousand examples) and a domain not too far from the pretraining data, start with a linear probe or with LoRA. Both leave the backbone intact.
  • With more data, or when the target domain differs a lot, fine-tune more layers, ideally with LP-FT or a small body learning rate.
  • If the domain is very different (medical text, source code in a rare language, a new natural language) and unlabelled data is plentiful, continue the self-supervised pretraining on it first (domain-adaptive pretraining), then fine-tune.
  • Whatever the method, check on data that resembles deployment, not only on a random split of your labels.

Fine-tuning distorts features: the LP-FT result in detail

The chain example above generalises into a theorem-backed result. Kumar et al. (2022) compared full fine-tuning and linear probing on ten benchmarks where the test data differs from the training data (distribution shift): several ImageNet variants, CIFAR to STL, DomainNet, satellite images (FMoW), and others. Full fine-tuning was about 2% better on average in distribution (same distribution as the fine-tuning data) but 7% worse out of distribution, when the pretrained features were good and the shift large. Their explanation: with a randomly initialised head, the gradient signal in early training is large and points in an arbitrary direction, so the lower layers change while the head is still being learned, and the changes are specific to the fine-tuning distribution: features get distorted. Directions in feature space that the fine-tuning data never exercises are left untouched by the head but altered by the body, so any shift that uses those directions breaks. LP-FT (fit the head with the body frozen, then fine-tune everything) starts with a good head, so the gradients into the body are small and the features change less. It was 1% better in-distribution and 10% better out-of-distribution than full fine-tuning.

A cheaper relative is WiSE-FT (Wortsman et al., 2021), for models such as CLIP: after fine-tuning, average the weights of the original zero-shot model and the fine-tuned model, θ = (1−λ)θ₀ + λθ₁. It needs no extra training, it improved accuracy under distribution shift on ImageNet by 4 to 6 points over prior work while raising accuracy on ImageNet itself by 1.6 points, and it works because, empirically, the zero-shot and fine-tuned weights lie in a connected region of low loss, so points between them are good models too. The same idea (averaging weights of models fine-tuned from the same starting point) underlies model merging of LLM adapters and fine-tunes.

Forgetting: what it is and what regularises it

Catastrophic forgetting is the price of moving shared weights. The classical remedies are algorithmic: regularise towards the pretrained weights (an L2 penalty on the distance from the starting point instead of from zero, sometimes called L2-SP; note that standard weight decay, including AdamW's, pulls weights towards zero, which is the wrong anchor for a fine-tuned model, a point worth making in an interview); elastic weight consolidation, which penalises movement in proportion to each weight's estimated importance for the old task; replay, mixing a fraction of old-task or general data into every batch; and simply using a low learning rate and few steps.

LoRA is itself a regulariser. Biderman et al. (2024) compared LoRA and full fine-tuning on code and mathematics, in both instruction fine-tuning (around 100 thousand prompt–response pairs) and continued pretraining (20 billion tokens). Their finding is nuanced: at standard ranks LoRA underperforms full fine-tuning on the target domain, but it forgets less of the base model's abilities elsewhere, more than weight decay or dropout do, and it keeps generations more diverse. They also measured the rank of the weight updates that full fine-tuning learns and found it 10 to 100 times higher than the typical LoRA rank, a possible reason for the gap on hard, data-rich domains. So the two ends behave as a dial: less capacity to change means less learned and less forgotten.

Why low rank is enough (and when it is not)

The low intrinsic dimension result (Aghajanyan et al.) says that the number of directions in weight space needed to fine-tune is small, and that larger pretrained models have lower intrinsic dimension. The LoRA paper looked inside: the adaptation matrices for rank 8 and rank 64 share their top singular directions (the strongest directions of the update), so a rank-1 or rank-2 update already captures most of the useful change on their GPT-3 tasks, and the other directions look like noise. Two limits follow. First, this is measured on tasks close to the pretraining distribution (instruction following, classification, summarisation): teaching the model a substantially new skill needs more capacity, as the Biderman et al. results show. Second, rank is not the only knob: which matrices carry adapters matters more than r (all linear layers, per QLoRA), and the scale α/r interacts with the learning rate.

The lab's toy LoRA demonstrates the rank logic exactly: for a frozen 64×64 matrix whose true change has rank 2, a rank-1 adapter (128 weights, 3.1% of the matrix) reduces the squared error from 0.4111 to 0.1428, and rank-2 (256 weights, 6.2%) and rank-4 adapters reach zero, because the needed change fits in the adapter. If the true change had rank 30, a rank-2 adapter would fail, which is the situation of hard domain shifts.

Domain-adaptive pretraining and the adaptation ladder for LLMs

Gururangan et al. (2020) tested whether a second phase of self-supervised pretraining on text from the target domain helps: across four domains (biomedical and computer-science papers, news, reviews) and eight classification tasks, it did, in both high- and low-resource settings, and adapting further to the unlabelled text of the task itself helped again on top. For an LLM the ladder therefore reads:

  1. Prompting and few-shot examples: no training, and no permanent change.
  2. Retrieval-augmented generation: fetch relevant documents at query time and put them in the prompt. Best for facts that change or must be cited.
  3. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT), including instruction tuning: train on (prompt, response) pairs. Best for behaviour: output format, tone, following a policy, calling tools. The loss is usually computed only on the response tokens, so that the model learns to produce answers, not to reproduce prompts. The prompt must use exactly the chat template the deployment will use.
  4. Continued pretraining: next-token prediction on a large unlabelled corpus for a new language or domain, when billions of tokens exist.

The evidence for fine-tuning teaching form more than facts comes from two directions. LIMA (Zhou et al., 2023) fine-tuned a 65B LLaMA model on just 1,000 carefully selected prompt–response pairs and, in human comparisons, its responses were equivalent or preferred to GPT-4's in 43% of cases: the model already knew nearly everything and only needed to learn the format of a helpful answer. Ovadia et al. (2023) tested injecting knowledge and found retrieval consistently better than unsupervised fine-tuning, for facts the model had seen and for entirely new ones; fine-tuning on documents helps somewhat and is unreliable in leading to answers. That is why production systems commonly fine-tune for behaviour and retrieve for knowledge.

Distillation: transferring behaviour to a different model

Transfer can also go from a big model to a smaller one that has a different architecture. In distillation (Hinton et al., 2015) the student is trained to match the teacher's probability distribution and not only the hard label. The teacher's wrong-answer probabilities carry information ("this cat looks a bit like a dog, not at all like a truck"). Dividing the logits by a temperature T softens the distribution: logits (4, 1, 0) give (0.936, 0.047, 0.017) at T = 1 and (0.543, 0.257, 0.200) at T = 4. The student minimises cross-entropy to the soft targets computed at temperature T (multiplied by T² so that gradient magnitudes stay comparable across temperatures), usually together with the ordinary hard-label loss. In LLM practice distillation appears as training a small model on text generated by a large one, which is transfer by imitation, and it is limited by the teacher's licence terms and by how faithfully the student can reproduce the teacher's reasoning.

Choosing a source model, and when transfer fails

  • Domain gap. Features from natural photos help less on radar or spectrograms, and BERT trained on general text helps less on clinical notes than a model trained on biomedical text. Continued pretraining bridges some of the gap.
  • Negative transfer. With enough in-domain data, a poorly matched source can be worse than scratch; test against a from-scratch baseline when data is large.
  • Task mismatch. Representations trained for classification throw away spatial detail that segmentation needs; contrastive image features can throw away colour or orientation if the augmentations made them invariant to it.
  • Cheap model selection. Before fine-tuning candidate backbones, run a linear probe on each with your data; the ranking usually holds and costs minutes.

A fine-tuning checklist that avoids the usual failures

  1. Evaluate before you train. Build a held-out set that resembles deployment (same sources, time period, difficulty), keep it out of every training run, and measure the pretrained model with prompting and with a linear probe on it. If you cannot beat these baselines, fine-tuning is not the problem you have.
  2. Check contamination. The pretraining corpus of a public model may include your test set (benchmarks and well-known datasets leak). An eye-catching gain on a public benchmark may be memorisation, so favour private held-out data.
  3. Match the input pipeline exactly. Image resolution and normalisation constants, tokeniser and its special tokens, and for chat models the chat template. Mismatches produce a model that trains fine and behaves oddly; a wrong template is a common cause of a fine-tuned chat model "ignoring" instructions.
  4. Set conservative hyperparameters. Full fine-tuning: peak learning rate around 1e-5 to 5e-5 for encoder-size models, warmup over 3 to 10% of steps, linear or cosine decay, 1 to 3 epochs. LoRA: 1e-4 to 2e-4, r = 8 to 64 on all linear layers, α around r to 2r, dropout 0.05 to 0.1 on the adapters. Tune the learning rate first; it matters more than anything else.
  5. Look at the loss curve. A spike in the first steps indicates a learning rate too high for the body or a missing warmup. A training loss that keeps falling while validation rises is memorisation: reduce epochs or rank, add weight decay, or add data.
  6. Regression-test general behaviour. Keep a small suite of general prompts (or a standard benchmark) and run it on every fine-tuned checkpoint to catch forgetting, including safety behaviours that a narrow fine-tune can erode.
  7. Run several seeds on small datasets. Variation between runs with a few hundred examples can exceed the gain you are chasing.

Data quality beats quantity

The LIMA result (1,000 curated examples were enough for a strong instruction-following model) is a production lesson: a few hundred to a few thousand clean, consistent, representative examples usually beat tens of thousands of noisy ones. Duplicates and contradictory labels are common, as is skew: if 95% of examples have a short answer the model will produce short answers. The cheapest improvement is often reading 100 training examples by hand.

Serving adapters

Because a LoRA adapter is a few megabytes to a few hundred megabytes, one frozen base model in GPU memory can serve many tenants: the serving system loads the right adapter for each request, and many requests with different adapters can be batched together at a small cost per token. The alternative, merging the adapter into the weights, has zero overhead for a single-task deployment but produces a full-size copy for each task. Keep the adapter, base model version, tokeniser, chat template and training-data hash together under one version identifier; an adapter trained on one base checkpoint is meaningless on another.

Cost and capacity planning

Use the memory table: full fine-tuning of a 7B model does not fit on one 80 GB GPU (112 GB), so it needs sharding across GPUs (ZeRO or FSDP, gpu9); LoRA on a bf16 base fits on a 24 GB GPU with small batches (14.6 GB plus activations); QLoRA fits in under 6 GB plus activations, a consumer card. The time cost is set by the tokens processed (about 6 × parameters FLOPs per token for a full pass, gpu3), and LoRA does not reduce the forward and backward FLOPs of the base, only the weight-gradient computation and memory (the LoRA paper's 25% speed-up on GPT-3 175B). QLoRA is slower per step than LoRA because of dequantisation.

When not to fine-tune

  • The problem is facts that change often: use retrieval.
  • You have fewer than a few dozen examples and prompting with examples already works.
  • You cannot maintain the evaluation and regression suite: an unmonitored fine-tune degrades silently.
  • The base model's provider updates frequently and your adapter would need retraining each time.

Monitoring after launch

Track the same metrics as before launch on live traffic samples, watch for drift between the fine-tuning data and real inputs (new products, new language), and keep the ability to roll back to the base model or the previous adapter. Log the adapter version with each response so a regression can be attributed to a specific change.

Goal: reproduce transfer learning on a toy world built so that a pretraining task and a new target task share hidden structure, and compare training from scratch, a frozen linear probe and two fine-tuning learning rates as the number of labelled examples grows; then build a LoRA update by hand and count the parameters and memory for a 7B model. NumPy only, Python 3.12, about 40 seconds.

import numpy as np
sig = lambda z: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))

# ================= 1. transfer learning: scratch vs linear probe vs fine-tuning =================
D_IN, K = 100, 4
def make_world(seed):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed); A = r.normal(size=(K, D_IN)) / np.sqrt(K); Wt = r.normal(size=(K, 8))
    def gen(n, rng):                                                             # inputs are a nonlinear (sine) mixing of K hidden factors z, plus noise
        z = rng.normal(size=(n, K)); return np.sin(3.0 * (z @ A)) + 0.3 * rng.normal(size=(n, D_IN)), z
    return gen, Wt
def adam(ps, gs, st, t, lrs):
    for i, (p, g) in enumerate(zip(ps, gs)):
        st[0][i] = 0.9 * st[0][i] + 0.1 * g; st[1][i] = 0.999 * st[1][i] + 0.001 * g * g
        p -= lrs[i] * (st[0][i] / (1 - 0.9 ** t)) / (np.sqrt(st[1][i] / (1 - 0.999 ** t)) + 1e-8)
def new_state(ps): return [np.zeros_like(p) for p in ps], [np.zeros_like(p) for p in ps]
def pretrain(gen, Wt, rng, steps=2500, H=16):                                    # source task: 8 related labels (the "ImageNet" of this toy world)
    W1 = rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(1 / D_IN), (D_IN, 32)); b1 = np.zeros(32); W2 = rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(1 / 32), (32, H)); b2 = np.zeros(H)
    W3 = rng.normal(0, 0.3, (H, 8)); b3 = np.zeros(8); ps = [W1, b1, W2, b2, W3, b3]; st = new_state(ps)
    for t in range(1, steps + 1):
        x, z = gen(256, rng); y = (z @ Wt > 0).astype(float)
        h1 = np.tanh(x @ W1 + b1); h2 = np.tanh(h1 @ W2 + b2); d = (sig(h2 @ W3 + b3) - y) / len(y)
        d2 = (d @ W3.T) * (1 - h2 ** 2); d1 = (d2 @ W2.T) * (1 - h1 ** 2)
        adam(ps, [x.T @ d1, d1.sum(0), h1.T @ d2, d2.sum(0), h2.T @ d, d.sum(0)], st, t, [3e-3] * 6)
    return [(W1, b1), (W2, b2)]                                                  # keep the feature extractor, drop the source head
def fit(x, y, rng, enc, train_enc, enc_lr, steps=400, lr=3e-3, wd=1e-2):        # target task: new binary label, tiny labelled set
    enc = [(W.copy(), b.copy()) for W, b in enc]; hd = rng.normal(0, 0.1, enc[-1][0].shape[1]); hb = np.zeros(1)
    ps = [hd, hb] + [a for W, b in enc for a in (W, b)]; st = new_state(ps); lrs = [lr, lr] + [enc_lr] * (len(ps) - 2)
    for t in range(1, steps + 1):
        hs = [x]
        for W, b in enc: hs.append(np.tanh(hs[-1] @ W + b))
        d = (sig(hs[-1] @ hd + hb[0]) - y) / len(y); gs = [hs[-1].T @ d + wd * hd, np.array([d.sum()])]; gl = []
        d2 = np.outer(d, hd) * (1 - hs[-1] ** 2)
        for i in reversed(range(len(enc))):
            W = enc[i][0]; gl = [hs[i].T @ d2 + wd * W, d2.sum(0)] + gl
            if i > 0: d2 = (d2 @ W.T) * (1 - hs[i] ** 2)
        adam(ps, gs + (gl if train_enc else [np.zeros_like(a) for W, b in enc for a in (W, b)]), st, t, lrs)
    def predict(xx):
        h = xx
        for W, b in enc: h = np.tanh(h @ W + b)
        return (h @ hd + hb[0] > 0).astype(float)
    return predict
methods = {"from scratch": (None, True, 3e-3), "linear probe (frozen)": ("enc", False, 0), "fine-tune, same LR": ("enc", True, 3e-3),
           "fine-tune, encoder LR 1e-4": ("enc", True, 1e-4)}
sizes = (10, 20, 40, 80, 160); res = {}
for seed in range(6):
    gen, Wt = make_world(seed); rng = np.random.default_rng(100 + seed); enc = pretrain(gen, Wt, rng)
    w_new = np.random.default_rng(500 + seed).normal(size=K); xt, zt = gen(3000, rng); yt = (zt @ w_new > 0).astype(float)
    scratch = [(rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(1 / D_IN), (D_IN, 32)), np.zeros(32)), (rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(1 / 32), (32, 16)), np.zeros(16))]
    for n in sizes:
        x, z = gen(n, rng); y = (z @ w_new > 0).astype(float)
        for name, (init, train_enc, elr) in methods.items():
            res.setdefault((name, n), []).append((fit(x, y, rng, enc if init else scratch, train_enc, elr)(xt) == yt).mean())
print("test accuracy (mean of 6 worlds) by number of labelled target examples")
print(f"{'method':30}" + "".join(f"{n:>8}" for n in sizes))
for name in methods: print(f"{name:30}" + "".join(f"{np.mean(res[(name, n)]):8.3f}" for n in sizes))
np.save("dl11_curves.npy", np.array([[np.mean(res[(m, n)]) for n in sizes] for m in methods]))

# ================= 2. LoRA: a low-rank update recovers a low-rank change with few parameters =================
r0 = np.random.default_rng(1); d = 64
W0 = r0.normal(size=(d, d)) / np.sqrt(d); true_delta = 0.5 * r0.normal(size=(d, 2)) @ r0.normal(size=(2, d)) / np.sqrt(d)   # the new task changes W by a rank-2 matrix
X = r0.normal(size=(400, d)); Y = X @ (W0 + true_delta)
print("\nLoRA on a frozen 64x64 layer whose true change has rank 2  (full fine-tuning would train", d * d, "weights)")
for rank in (1, 2, 4):
    B = np.zeros((d, rank)); A = r0.normal(0, 0.1, (rank, d)); ps = [B, A]; st = new_state(ps)   # B = 0, so the adapted model starts identical to the frozen one
    start = ((X @ W0 - Y) ** 2).mean()
    for t in range(1, 2001):
        E = (X @ (W0 + B @ A) - Y) / len(X) * 2; adam(ps, [X.T @ E @ A.T, B.T @ X.T @ E], st, t, [1e-2, 1e-2])
    print(f"  rank {rank}: {2 * d * rank:5d} trainable weights ({2 * d * rank / (d * d):5.1%}), MSE {start:.4f} -> {((X @ (W0 + B @ A) - Y) ** 2).mean():.6f}")

# ================= 3. how many parameters and how much memory for a 7B model? =================
d, ffn, L = 4096, 11008, 32                                                     # Llama-7B shapes
lora = lambda din, dout, r: r * (din + dout)
qv8 = L * 2 * lora(d, d, 8); all16 = L * (4 * lora(d, d, 16) + 3 * lora(d, ffn, 16))
print(f"\nLoRA parameters on a 7B model: rank 8 on q and v: {qv8 / 1e6:.2f} M ({qv8 / 6.74e9:.2%});  rank 16 on every linear layer: {all16 / 1e6:.1f} M ({all16 / 6.74e9:.2%})")
print(f"memory: full fine-tune with Adam {16 * 7e9 / 1e9:.0f} GB;  LoRA (bf16 base 14 GB + adapters with 16 B/param) {14 + 16 * all16 / 1e9:.1f} GB;  QLoRA (4-bit base 7e9 x 0.5 B) {7e9 * 0.5 / 1e9 + 16 * all16 / 1e9:.1f} GB  (before activations)")

What to look for.

  1. The sample-efficiency table should match the lesson: from scratch 0.643, 0.714, 0.780, 0.833, 0.846 for 10, 20, 40, 80, 160 labelled examples; the frozen probe 0.775 to 0.866; fine-tuning at the head's learning rate 0.645 to 0.848, indistinguishable from scratch; fine-tuning with an encoder rate of 1e-4 from 0.744 to 0.893, best at 40 labels and above.
  2. Try changing the encoder learning rate (1e-3, 3e-5): higher rates move towards the from-scratch row, much lower ones towards the probe row. Try fewer pretraining steps or fewer related pretraining labels: the whole advantage shrinks, because there is less shared structure to transfer.
  3. LoRA rank: for a frozen 64×64 matrix whose true change has rank 2, rank 1 leaves an error of 0.143 (from 0.411), and ranks 2 and 4 reach 0.000000. The count is 128, 256 and 512 trainable weights, 3.1%, 6.2% and 12.5% of the matrix.
  4. 7B counts: rank 8 on query and value = 4.19 million parameters (0.06%); rank 16 on all linear layers = 40.0 million (0.59%); memory 112 GB full, 14.6 GB LoRA, 4.1 GB QLoRA before activations.

Limits. The "world" is synthetic and small (100 inputs, 16 features), so the exact accuracies are a property of this construction; the pattern (probe wins when labels are few, careful fine-tuning wins later, careless fine-tuning wastes the pretraining) is what carries over.

Plan it

You must classify 12 types of defects in factory images. You have 1,500 labelled images (some classes have only 40 examples) and 200,000 unlabelled images from the same cameras. A colleague proposes training a ResNet-50 (25.6 million parameters) from scratch. Propose a plan and justify each step. A second part: the labelling budget is fixed but the company also wants a chatbot for the inspectors that answers in a fixed report format and uses the plant's 30,000 pages of manuals; the only GPU is one 24 GB card and the chosen model has 13 billion parameters (40 layers, hidden size 5,120, feed-forward size 13,824). What do you do?

Answer

Part 1: the vision classifier.

  1. Do not train from scratch. 1,500 images cannot teach 25.6 million weights what an edge or a texture is, and 40-example classes make it worse. This lesson's experiment shows the size of the gap: with 10 labels a transferred model beat scratch by 13 points of accuracy.
  2. Baseline in an hour: a frozen pretrained backbone (a strong self-supervised model such as DINOv2, or an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet or ConvNeXt), the exact preprocessing it was pretrained with, and a logistic regression on the embeddings. A 2,048-dimensional embedding and 12 classes is a 24,588-parameter head. Stratified cross-validation, grouped by production line or camera so that near-identical frames do not leak between folds (ml9). It tells you how far transfer alone goes.
  3. Domain-adaptive pretraining: continue self-supervised training (a DINO or masked-image-modelling objective) on the 200,000 unlabelled factory images, so the features adapt to this lighting, texture and camera. This is often the largest gain when the domain differs from ImageNet, and it uses data you already own.
  4. Fine-tune starting with LP-FT (probe first, then unfreeze), a body learning rate 10 to 100 times lower than the head's or layer-wise decay, weight decay, augmentation that matches real variation (rotation, lighting, blur, but not flips or crops that change what a defect means), class-balanced sampling or loss weights for the 40-example classes, and early stopping. Keep BatchNorm layers in evaluation mode if batches are small.
  5. Use the unlabelled pool further: pseudo-label high-confidence predictions and retrain, and use active learning (send the most uncertain images to inspectors) focused on the rare classes.
  6. Evaluate per-class recall with confidence intervals on a held-out set grouped by line and camera, and check it against the baseline in step 2; with 40-example classes, a few images change a class's recall by several points.

Part 2: the inspector chatbot.

  1. Knowledge from the manuals: retrieval, not fine-tuning. The 30,000 pages change, need citations, and fine-tuning teaches facts unreliably (Ovadia et al.). Index them and retrieve passages into the prompt.
  2. Report format and tone: LoRA. Full fine-tuning of 13B parameters needs about 13 × 10⁹ × 16 = 208 GB. A bf16 base alone is 26 GB, which does not fit in 24 GB. QLoRA: the 4-bit base is 6.5 GB; with rank 16 on all linear layers the adapters have 40 layers × (attention 4 × 2 × 5,120 × 16 + feed-forward 3 × 16 × (5,120 + 13,824)) = 62.6 million parameters, or about 1.0 GB at 16 bytes each. That is 7.5 GB before activations, leaving about 16 GB for activations, which is enough for a small batch with gradient checkpointing.
  3. Data: a few hundred to a few thousand curated (question plus retrieved passages, report-format answer) examples, with the loss on response tokens only and the deployment chat template. Evaluate faithfulness to the retrieved text and run a general-behaviour regression suite to catch forgetting.
