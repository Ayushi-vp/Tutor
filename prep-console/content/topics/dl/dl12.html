A payments company runs a good fraud model. It looks at each new account on its own: how old the email address is, whether the name matches the card, how many payments it made in the last hour, how far the shipping address is from the card's billing address. One Monday, forty new accounts sign up. Each one looks clean. Each one has a different name, a different email and a modest first purchase. Each one passes. By Friday all forty have cashed out stolen cards and vanished.

Afterwards an analyst draws a picture. The forty accounts used only six phones between them. Those six phones logged in from three home internet connections. Twelve of the accounts shipped to the same two addresses. Drawn as dots and lines, the forty "independent" customers are one tight knot. The fraud was invisible in every row of the table and obvious in the picture. The signal was never in any account; it was in the connections between accounts.

This happens all over machine learning. Whether two people will become friends depends mostly on how many friends they already share. Whether a scientific paper is about machine learning is strongly hinted at by the papers it cites. Whether a molecule is poisonous depends on how its atoms are bonded, not on a list of which atoms it contains: the same atoms connected differently make a different drug. Pinterest's recommendations, Google Maps' travel-time estimates, drug-discovery screening and social networks' friend suggestions all have this shape. The data is a graph: things (called nodes) and relationships between them (called edges).

The neural networks you have met so far cannot eat a graph. A multilayer perceptron (MLP) wants a fixed-length vector. A convolutional network (dl6) wants a grid, where every pixel has exactly eight neighbours in fixed positions. A sequence model (dl7) wants an order. A graph has none of these: one account has two neighbours and another has two thousand; the neighbours have no "left" or "right"; and the same graph can be written down with its nodes listed in any order. You can hand-engineer graph features (count shared devices, compute friends-in-common) and feed them to an ordinary model, and that is a strong baseline you should always try. But it means a person must guess every useful pattern in advance.

Graph neural networks (GNNs) learn those patterns instead. This lesson builds them from one idea. We first describe a graph as numbers: an adjacency matrix and a feature matrix. Then we meet message passing, the step that every GNN repeats: each node collects information from its neighbours and updates itself. We derive the most famous GNN, the graph convolutional network (GCN), first from the theory of signals on graphs and then as the plain neighbour averaging it turns out to be, and we compute one layer by hand on a 4-node graph. Then we fix GCN's two practical limits with GraphSAGE (sampling, so it scales to billions of nodes and works on nodes it has never seen) and GAT (attention, so neighbours can matter unequally). We look at why GNNs stay shallow (over-smoothing), what they provably cannot see, how graphs with several kinds of node are handled, and finally how GNNs are actually served in fraud detection, recommendations, People You May Know and chemistry, where the hard part is rarely the model and usually the graph.

The village gossip analogy

Picture a village where nobody reads the newspaper. Every evening, each villager talks to the people they know and comes away with a blend of what they heard and what they already thought. After one evening you know what your friends think. After two evenings you have absorbed, second-hand, what your friends' friends think. After three, news from three handshakes away has reached you. Nobody ever talks to a stranger, yet information spreads across the whole village in a few rounds, and what you end up believing depends on who you are connected to.

A graph neural network is this village, run by arithmetic. Every node holds a vector of numbers (its current "opinion"). In each layer (one evening), every node receives a message from each neighbour (a vector computed from that neighbour's opinion), combines all its messages into one vector, and uses that, together with its own previous vector, to compute its new vector. The way messages are computed and combined uses learned weights, which are trained by ordinary backpropagation (dl1) to make the final vectors useful for the task: flagging fraud, predicting friendships or classifying molecules. After k layers, each node's vector is a learned summary of everything within k hops.

Where the analogy breaks is instructive. Real gossip distorts; GNN messages are precise learned functions. And real villages do not all end up believing the same thing; but a GNN that runs too many rounds of averaging does exactly that, which is a real failure called over-smoothing that we will measure later.

Graphs as numbers

A graph is a set of nodes (also called vertices: accounts, papers, atoms, road segments) and a set of edges (links between pairs of nodes: "paid with this card", "cites", "is bonded to"). Edges can be undirected (friendship goes both ways) or directed (a paper cites another; following someone on a social network), and they can carry a weight or their own features (the bond type, the amount of a payment). The number of edges attached to a node is its degree.

Two matrices describe a graph for a neural network. The adjacency matrix A is an n×n table, where n is the number of nodes, with Aᵢⱼ = 1 if nodes i and j are linked and 0 otherwise. For an undirected graph it is symmetric. The feature matrix X has one row per node and one column per feature: for an account, perhaps its age in days and the number of payments it has made; for a paper, a bag-of-words vector of its abstract. Throughout this lesson we use one tiny graph: four people, Ana (node 0), Ben (node 1), Cai (node 2) and Dev (node 3), where Ben knows the other three and they do not know each other. What GNNs are asked to predict

Graph tasks come in three sizes. In node classification you predict something about each node: is this account fraudulent, what subject is this paper about. In edge prediction (or link prediction) you predict something about a pair of nodes: will these two people connect, will this user save this pin. In graph classification (or regression) you predict one thing about an entire graph: is this molecule toxic, what is its energy. All three use the same machinery: message passing produces a vector per node; a node task puts a classifier on each node vector, an edge task scores a pair of node vectors (often with a dot product, as in two-tower retrieval, rec4), and a graph task first pools all node vectors into one, a step called the readout.

Two more words you will meet constantly. A transductive task has the whole graph available during training, including the nodes you want to label; only their labels are hidden. Cora is used this way: all 2,708 papers and their links are visible, only 140 of them (5.2%) have labels, and the model labels the rest. An inductive task requires the model to work on nodes, or whole graphs, it never saw in training: a brand-new account, a new molecule. Whether a method is inductive turns out to matter enormously in production.

1. Why a graph needs its own kind of network

Start with what goes wrong if you try an ordinary network. Suppose you flatten the 4×4 adjacency matrix of our village into 16 numbers and append the 8 feature values. Now number the people differently, say Ben as node 0 instead of node 1. The graph is the same, but the 24 numbers are in a different order, so a multilayer perceptron sees a different input and gives a different answer. With n nodes there are n! ways to number them (24 for our 4 people, an astronomically large number for 2,708 papers), and the network would have to learn that all of them mean the same thing. It also could not handle a graph with a fifth person at all, because its input layer has a fixed size, and for a sparse graph it wastes n² inputs on mostly zeros.

The property we want has a name. A function on the nodes is permutation equivariant if renumbering the nodes simply renumbers its outputs in the same way: Ben's prediction is Ben's prediction whatever number he has. A function of a whole graph or of a set is permutation invariant if renumbering does not change it at all. GNNs get these properties by construction, from two design choices. First, every node is processed by the same learned function with the same weights, exactly as a CNN slides the same kernel over every pixel. Second, a node combines its neighbours' messages with an operation that ignores order, such as a sum, a mean or a maximum: 2 + 5 + 1 is 8 however you list the numbers.

This is also where a graph differs from an image. A pixel in a CNN has exactly eight neighbours in fixed positions, so a 3×3 kernel can give "the pixel above" its own weight and "the pixel to the left" a different one. Graph neighbours have no positions: Ana is not "above" Ben. So a graph layer cannot have a separate weight per neighbour position. It must treat all neighbours with the same weights and let something else, such as degree or attention, decide how much each one counts. A useful way to remember it: an image is a very regular graph (each pixel linked to its eight neighbours), and a CNN is a GNN that is allowed to exploit that regularity.

2. Message passing: the one idea behind every GNN

Gilmer et al. (2017) noticed that a dozen published graph networks were all instances of one recipe, which they called message passing. Write hᵥ⁽ᵏ⁾ for node v's vector after layer k, starting with hᵥ⁽⁰⁾ = xᵥ, its input features. Each layer does three things for every node v:

  1. Message. For each neighbour u of v, compute a message m = MSG(hᵤ⁽ᵏ⁾, hᵥ⁽ᵏ⁾, eᵤᵥ), a vector that may depend on the sender, the receiver and the edge's features eᵤᵥ. In the simplest case it is just the sender's vector multiplied by a weight matrix: m = W hᵤ.
  2. Aggregate. Combine all of v's incoming messages into one vector with an order-independent function: sum, mean, max, or a weighted sum. This is the step that handles any number of neighbours, two or two thousand.
  3. Update. Compute v's new vector from its old vector and the aggregate: hᵥ⁽ᵏ⁺¹⁾ = UPDATE(hᵥ⁽ᵏ⁾, aggregate), typically a linear layer followed by a nonlinearity such as ReLU (dl2).

All nodes do this at the same time, using the previous layer's vectors, and the weights are shared by every node. Stacking layers spreads information: after layer 1, Ben's vector contains Ana's, Cai's and Dev's features; after layer 2, Ana's vector contains Ben's layer-1 vector and therefore, second-hand, Cai's and Dev's features too. The set of nodes whose features can influence a node's output is its receptive field, and for a k-layer network it is exactly the k-hop neighbourhood, as the computation tree in the primer shows. Note what "depth" means here: in a CNN, more layers mean more abstract features; in a GNN, more layers mostly mean farther reach. That difference will matter when we discover that GNNs work best with two or three layers.

Training is ordinary. Put a task head on the final vectors (for node classification, a linear layer and softmax per node), compute the loss on the nodes that have labels, and backpropagate. Gradients flow back through the aggregations into the shared weights. On Cora only the 140 labelled nodes contribute to the loss, yet all 2,708 nodes take part in the forward pass, because unlabelled nodes carry messages. That is why GCN's paper is titled "semi-supervised": the unlabelled part of the graph is doing real work.

3. The spectral route to graph convolution

Before GCN was a formula it was a question: what does "convolution" mean on a graph? On a grid, the convolution theorem says that convolving a signal is the same as transforming it into frequencies (with the Fourier transform), multiplying each frequency by a number, and transforming back. A blur filter keeps low frequencies (slow changes) and suppresses high ones (sharp edges). To borrow that idea, we need "frequencies" for a graph.

They come from the graph Laplacian, the matrix L = D − A, where D is the diagonal matrix of degrees. Its key property is a single identity: for any vector x that puts one number on each node, xᵀLx = Σ over edges (xᵢ − xⱼ)². That sum is small when connected nodes have similar values (a smooth signal) and large when values jump across edges (a rough signal). Check it on our village with feature f₁ = (1, 0, 1, 2) for Ana, Ben, Cai, Dev: the three edges are Ana–Ben, Ben–Cai and Ben–Dev, so xᵀLx = (1−0)² + (0−1)² + (0−2)² = 1 + 1 + 4 = 6. The eigenvectors of L (math9) are the graph's "frequencies": the eigenvector with eigenvalue 0 is constant on every node (perfectly smooth), and eigenvectors with larger eigenvalues change more and more sharply across edges. Our star graph's Laplacian has eigenvalues 0, 1, 1 and 4.

A spectral filter then does what a Fourier filter does: write the node signal in the eigenvector basis, scale each component by a learned number, and transform back. Bruna et al. (2014) built networks this way. They work, but they have three serious problems: computing all eigenvectors of an n×n matrix costs O(n³), which is hopeless for a million nodes; the filter is a dense n×n operation, so it is not local (every node affects every other); and the learned numbers are tied to one graph's eigenvectors, so the model cannot be applied to a different graph.

Defferrard et al. (2016, ChebNet) removed the eigenvectors by approximating the filter as a polynomial in L: θ₀x + θ₁Lx + θ₂L²x + … up to degree K. Multiplying by L once mixes each node with its direct neighbours, so a degree-K polynomial only mixes nodes within K hops: the filter became local, cheap (just repeated sparse products) and eigenvector-free. Kipf and Welling then took the most drastic simplification: keep only K = 1, use the normalised Laplacian, and tie the two remaining parameters into one. What is left is θ(I + D^(−1/2) A D^(−1/2)) x. One last problem remained: that operator has eigenvalues between 0 and 2, so stacking many layers can multiply some components by up to 2 each time, and the numbers explode or vanish (dl3). Their fix, the renormalisation trick, adds the identity to the adjacency matrix first (Ã = A + I, a self-loop on every node) and normalises afterwards with the degrees of Ã. The resulting matrix, Â = D̃^(−1/2) Ã D̃^(−1/2), has its largest eigenvalue at exactly 1 (on our star its eigenvalues are 1, 0.5, 0.5 and −0.25), so repeated application neither explodes nor dies. Generalising from one feature and one parameter to many features and a weight matrix gives the GCN layer:

H⁽ᵏ⁺¹⁾ = σ( Â · H⁽ᵏ⁾ · W⁽ᵏ⁾ )

where H⁽ᵏ⁾ is the n×f matrix of node vectors (row v is hᵥ), W⁽ᵏ⁾ is an f×f′ learned weight matrix shared by all nodes, and σ is a nonlinearity. The spectral view tells you what this layer does to signals: it is a low-pass filter. It keeps the part of each feature that varies smoothly over the graph and damps the part that jumps across edges. On our graph, applying Â to f₁ = (1, 0, 1, 2) gives (0.5, 1.414, 0.5, 1.0); the "roughness ratio" xᵀLx / xᵀx falls from 6/6 = 1.00 to 1.84/3.50 = 0.53. One layer roughly halved how much the feature jumps across edges. That is useful when connected nodes tend to share labels, and it is the seed of over-smoothing when applied too often.

Three things are worth noticing in these numbers. First, there are only 4 learned numbers in W however many nodes the graph has: weight sharing, as in a CNN. A real Cora layer has a 1,433×16 matrix, about 23,000 weights, for 2,708 nodes. Second, the cost of the aggregation is proportional to the number of edges times the feature size, because Â is sparse: one multiply-add per non-zero entry per feature. Third, the degree normalisation changes the answer. A plain mean (dividing by degree, D̃⁻¹Ã, called row normalisation) would give Ben (4, 2)/4 = (1.0, 0.5). The symmetric version gives each message the weight 1/√(dᵢdⱼ): a message from a high-degree node is damped (a celebrity's one connection to you is weak evidence about you), and a message to a high-degree node is damped too, so hubs do not end up with vectors far larger than everyone else's.

Kipf and Welling's full model is two of these layers: H¹ = ReLU(Â X W⁰) with 16 hidden units, then a softmax over Â H¹ W¹ to predict each paper's class. They trained it with dropout of 0.5, L2 regularisation of 5·10⁻⁴ on the first layer (dl8), learning rate 0.01, for at most 200 epochs with early stopping, using all nodes in every step (full-batch training). It reached 81.5% on Cora, 70.3% on Citeseer and 79.0% on Pubmed, ahead of every earlier method, with 5.2%, 3.6% and 0.3% of nodes labelled respectively.

5. GraphSAGE: learn a function, and sample the neighbours

GCN as published has two limits that bite immediately in industry. The first is scale. Full-batch training keeps every node's vector for every layer in memory: fine for 2,708 nodes, impossible for a social network with a billion. Switching to minibatches of target nodes does not save you by itself, because of neighbourhood explosion: to compute one node's layer-2 vector you need its neighbours' layer-1 vectors, which need their neighbours' inputs. With an average degree of 100, a two-layer network touches about 1 + 100 + 10,000 nodes for a single target, and a three-layer one about a million. In a social graph a "celebrity" with ten million followers makes even one layer unaffordable.

The second is inductiveness. Earlier node-embedding methods such as DeepWalk and node2vec learn one free vector per node, a lookup table, so a node added tomorrow has no vector. GCN learns a function of features, but its normalisation and training assume a fixed graph, and practitioners often fed it one-hot node ids that make it transductive in practice. A fraud model or a recommender sees new users every second.

Hamilton, Ying and Leskovec's GraphSAGE (2017; the name is short for SAmple and aggreGatE) fixes both. Its layer is

hᵥ′ = σ( W · [ hᵥ ‖ AGG({hᵤ : u ∈ sample of N(v)}) ] ), then hᵥ′ ← hᵥ′ / ‖hᵥ′‖

where ‖ means concatenation (stacking two vectors end to end) and N(v) is v's neighbour set. Three choices differ from GCN, and each has a reason:

  • Self and neighbours are kept apart. Instead of mixing a node's own vector into the average through a self-loop, GraphSAGE concatenates the node's vector with the aggregate, so W has separate halves for "who I am" and "who I know". This is a form of skip connection, it matters when neighbours are unlike the node (heterophily, below), and it slows over-smoothing.
  • Several aggregators. The paper tried a mean, an LSTM (the gated recurrent network from dl7) run over a random ordering of neighbours (not truly order-independent, so it is randomised at each step), and a pooling aggregator that passes each neighbour through a small neural layer and takes the elementwise maximum. The pooling and LSTM aggregators did best on its benchmarks.
  • Fixed-size sampling. At each layer, each node uses a uniformly random sample of its neighbours, not all of them. With K = 2 layers and sample sizes S₁ = 25 and S₂ = 10, the paper's defaults, one target node needs at most 1 + 25 + 25×10 = 276 nodes, whatever the graph's degree distribution. The authors found S₁·S₂ ≤ 500 enough for high accuracy, with diminishing returns from larger samples.

GraphSAGE can be trained with labels, or without them using a graph-based unsupervised loss: nodes that co-occur on short random walks should have similar embeddings (high dot product), and randomly sampled "negative" nodes should not. That is the same positive-versus-negative idea as two-tower retrieval with negative sampling (rec4), and the next lesson on self-supervised learning builds on it.

The public case: PinSage at Pinterest. Ying et al. (2018) took GraphSAGE into production on a graph of pins (saved images) and boards (user collections): 3 billion nodes and 18 billion edges, trained on 7.5 billion examples. Three of its changes are worth knowing because they are general lessons. First, instead of sampling neighbours uniformly, PinSage ran short random walks from each pin and took the most frequently visited nodes as its neighbourhood, weighting each by its normalised visit count (the paper calls this importance pooling; the visit counts approximate personalised PageRank). That gives a fixed-size neighbourhood of the most relevant nodes, which matters for hubs, whose random neighbours are mostly noise. Second, it trained with a ranking loss (a related pin must score higher than an unrelated one by a margin) and, as training progressed, added hard negatives: pins ranked between 2,000 and 5,000 by personalised PageRank from the query, which are similar but not related, forcing finer distinctions. Third, it separated the systems work: a large-memory CPU "producer" sampled neighbourhoods and built minibatches while GPUs consumed them, and after training a MapReduce job computed embeddings for all pins, sharing computation between nodes with common neighbours. The embeddings then fed a nearest-neighbour index for recommendations. The paper reports more than 40% better offline ranking than the best baseline, recommendations preferred about 60% of the time in side-by-side human evaluations, and 30% to 100% engagement gains in A/B tests.

6. GAT: let the model decide which neighbours matter

In GCN, how much a neighbour counts is fixed by the graph: 1/√(dᵢdⱼ). In a mean aggregator it is 1/degree. Neither can express "for deciding Ben's interests, Cai matters more than Dev". Veličković et al.'s Graph Attention Network (GAT, 2018) learns these weights. For a node i and each neighbour j (including i itself):

  1. Transform both vectors with a shared matrix: Whᵢ and Whⱼ.
  2. Score the pair with a tiny learned network: eᵢⱼ = LeakyReLU(aᵀ [Whᵢ ‖ Whⱼ]), where a is a learned vector of length 2F′ and the LeakyReLU has a negative slope of 0.2 (a ReLU that lets 20% of negative values through).
  3. Turn scores into weights with a softmax over i's neighbours: αᵢⱼ = exp(eᵢⱼ) / Σₖ exp(eᵢₖ). They are positive and sum to 1.
  4. Output the weighted sum: hᵢ′ = σ(Σⱼ αᵢⱼ Whⱼ).

Work it for Ben. Using our W, the transformed vectors XW are Ana (1, −1), Ben (0.5, 1), Cai (1.5, 0) and Dev (2, −2). Suppose the scoring network produces e = 1.0 for Ana, 0.5 for Ben himself, 2.0 for Cai and 0.0 for Dev. The exponentials are 2.718, 1.649, 7.389 and 1.000, summing to 12.756, so the attention weights are 0.213, 0.129, 0.579 and 0.078. Ben's output (before σ) is 0.213·(1, −1) + 0.129·(0.5, 1) + 0.579·(1.5, 0) + 0.078·(2, −2) = (1.303, −0.241). Cai now supplies 58% of Ben's information, where GCN gave every neighbour the same weight. If the scores were all equal, GAT would reduce to a plain mean.

As in transformers, GAT uses several heads: independent copies of the attention with their own W and a, whose outputs are concatenated (in the last layer, averaged). Multi-head attention stabilises training and lets different heads attend for different reasons. The Cora model used 8 heads of 8 features (64 features) with an ELU nonlinearity (exponential linear unit, a smooth relative of the ReLU from dl2) in the first layer, and one head for the classifier, reaching 83.0% against GCN's 81.5%. GAT is also inductive (the weights depend on features, not node ids) and needs no matrix computed from the whole graph.

If you know the transformer (lm2), you have already seen GAT. Self-attention computes a weight for every pair of tokens in a sequence; that is attention on a complete graph, where every token is linked to every other. GAT is the same computation with a mask that allows only the graph's edges, and with an additive (concatenate-then-score) scoring function instead of a dot product. Later GNNs ("graph transformers") run full attention over all nodes and inject the graph structure as positional information, trading the cost of n² attention for long-range reach.

7. Reading out a whole graph, and how expressive this all is

For a graph-level task, such as predicting whether a molecule is toxic, you need one vector per graph. The readout pools the final node vectors with an order-independent function: sum, mean or max over all nodes, optionally followed by an MLP. For a molecule the nodes are atoms (features: element, charge) and the edges are bonds (edge features: single, double, aromatic), and Gilmer et al.'s message-passing networks predicted quantum-chemical properties this way. Training batches of small graphs are built by placing them side by side as one big disconnected graph (a block-diagonal adjacency matrix), with a vector recording which node belongs to which graph so the readout pools correctly.

Which aggregator should you pick? Xu et al. (2019) asked what message passing can distinguish at all. Each node only ever sees its own vector and the multiset of its neighbours' vectors (a multiset is a set that counts repeats). If two different neighbourhoods produce the same aggregate, no amount of training can tell them apart. Mean and max lose information: neighbours {a, a} and {a} have the same mean and the same max, so a node with two identical neighbours is indistinguishable from one with one. Sum keeps the count. They showed that sum aggregation followed by an MLP is injective on multisets (different multisets give different outputs) and proposed the Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN) built this way. That is why sum is the usual choice for chemistry, where "two hydrogens" and "one hydrogen" are different molecules, while mean is often better for social graphs, where a node's degree varies wildly and what matters is the proportion of neighbours with some property.

They also proved a ceiling: no message-passing GNN can distinguish two graphs that the classical 1-Weisfeiler–Lehman test cannot. That test repeatedly relabels each node by its old label plus the multiset of its neighbours' labels, which is exactly what message passing does with learned functions. We will see in the deep section what it misses and what that means in practice.

Over-smoothing: why GNNs stay shallow

Repeat GCN's propagation, ignoring the weights and nonlinearities for a moment, and you are computing Âᵏ X. By the power-iteration argument from math9, multiplying any vector by Â again and again pushes it towards Â's top eigenvector, which for the renormalised matrix is proportional to √(degree) on each node of a connected graph. Every column of features converges to the same shape, so every node's vector ends up pointing in the same direction, differing only by its degree. Measured on our village, the smallest cosine similarity between any two people's vectors is 0 at the start (Ana's (1, 0) and Ben's (0, 1) are perpendicular), then 0.764 after one step, 0.945 after two, 0.996 after four and 1.000 after eight. By then Ana, Cai and Dev all have the vector (0.80, 0.48) and Ben has √2 times it. A classifier on top cannot tell them apart. Li, Han and Wu (2018) named this over-smoothing and showed that GCN's propagation is exactly a form of Laplacian smoothing, which helps while it pulls same-class neighbours together and hurts once it pulls different classes together too.

The experiment in Kipf and Welling's Appendix B says the same with real data: on Cora, Citeseer and Pubmed the best results came from 2 or 3 layers, and beyond about 7 layers training without residual connections became difficult. Several remedies work by keeping some of each node's own information in every layer. Residual connections add the layer's input to its output (H⁽ᵏ⁺¹⁾ = σ(ÂH⁽ᵏ⁾W) + H⁽ᵏ⁾), as in ResNets. Initial-residual propagation, used by APPNP (Approximate Personalized Propagation of Neural Predictions, Klicpera et al. 2019), separates prediction from propagation: an MLP predicts from each node's own features, then the predictions are propagated with H ← (1 − α)ÂH + αH⁰, mixing a fraction α (0.1 in the paper) of the original prediction back in at every step. This is personalised PageRank, a random walk that teleports home with probability α, and it can propagate for 10 or more steps without collapse because it never forgets the start. GraphSAGE's concatenation of self and neighbours is a milder version of the same idea. Random edge dropping during training (DropEdge) and normalisation layers that keep node vectors spread apart (PairNorm) attack it from other sides.

Over-squashing: too much information through too small a pipe

Over-smoothing is about vectors becoming alike; over-squashing (Alon and Yahav, 2021) is about information from far away never arriving intact. If a task needs a node to know about something 6 hops away, the model needs at least 6 layers, and in a graph with degree 10 that 6-hop neighbourhood can contain a million nodes, all summarised into one vector of perhaps 64 numbers, passed through a bottleneck of a few edges. The signal from any one distant node is crushed. They showed that GNNs which absorb all incoming edges equally, such as GCN and GIN, suffer most, and that even a simple fix of making the last layer fully connected (every node can message every other node) improved results on long-range tasks. In practice people add a virtual node connected to everything, rewire the graph to add shortcuts, or use graph transformers with global attention. In production the more common answer is to bring long-range information in as precomputed features (a node's PageRank, its cluster from an offline community-detection job) rather than through many layers.

What message passing cannot see

The 1-WL ceiling has concrete consequences. Take two graphs with six nodes each: two separate triangles, and one ring of six. Every node in both has exactly two neighbours. If the nodes start with identical features (no features, or the same atom type), every node receives the same two messages at every layer, every node ends with the same vector, and any readout gives the two graphs the same embedding. Yet one has triangles and the other does not, which in chemistry is the difference between ring structures, and in fraud detection triangles of shared devices are exactly the dense knots you are hunting. A standard GNN also cannot count triangles at a node, or tell whether two nodes are in the same connected component when they have similar local views.

The practical fixes are cheap. Add structural features to the input: the number of triangles through each node, local clustering coefficient, degree, random-walk return probabilities (the chance a k-step random walk ends where it started, which differs between a triangle and a hexagon). Add positional encodings such as each node's values in the first few Laplacian eigenvectors, the graph analogue of a transformer's position embeddings. Or, at higher cost, use higher-order GNNs that pass messages between pairs or small subgraphs. Note that rich input features already break most ties in real data: two accounts with different ages and payment histories are distinguishable even if their neighbourhoods have the same shape. The WL ceiling bites hardest when features are poor and structure is the signal.

Homophily, heterophily and what averaging assumes

Averaging over neighbours is useful when neighbours resemble each other: homophily. Citation graphs are strongly homophilous, papers mostly cite papers on the same topic, which is why GCN shines on Cora. Many valuable graphs are not. In fraud, a fraudster's edges often point at legitimate victims or at innocent merchants, and fraudsters deliberately connect to good accounts to borrow their reputation (camouflage). In a dating graph, connected people tend to differ in some attributes. Under heterophily a GCN pulls the fraudster's vector towards the honest majority around it, erasing the signal. Remedies follow from the mechanism: keep self and neighbour information separate (GraphSAGE's concatenation, so the model can learn "I am unlike my neighbours"), use attention or learned edge weights that can down-weight camouflage edges, include the difference hᵥ − hᵤ in the message, or aggregate separately over neighbours of different types, which leads to the next point.

Heterogeneous graphs

Real graphs have several kinds of node and edge. A payments graph has accounts, cards, devices, IP addresses and merchants; edges are "account owns card", "account logged in from device", "card paid merchant". Treating them all as one kind of edge mixes things that mean different things. The standard approach, introduced as the Relational GCN (R-GCN, Schlichtkrull et al. 2018) for knowledge graphs, gives each relation type r its own weight matrix: hᵢ′ = σ(W₀hᵢ + Σᵣ Σ over j in Nᵣ(i) of (1/cᵢ,ᵣ) Wᵣhⱼ), where Nᵣ(i) are i's neighbours through relation r and cᵢ,ᵣ is a normalising count. With hundreds of relation types this means hundreds of matrices, so R-GCN shares them through basis decomposition: each Wᵣ is a learned mix of a few shared basis matrices, cutting parameters and overfitting on rare relations. A related tool is the metapath, a typed path such as account → device → account ("two accounts that share a device"), which can be turned into a direct edge so that one message-passing step connects the two accounts that matter. Modern libraries (PyTorch Geometric, and DGL, the Deep Graph Library) represent heterogeneous graphs natively and run a separate message function per edge type.

Spectral versus spatial, and where GCN sits

You will hear GNNs split into "spectral" (defined through the Laplacian's eigenvectors) and "spatial" (defined by aggregating over neighbours). GCN is the bridge: derived spectrally, implemented spatially. The spectral view is still useful as an explanation (GCN is a low-pass filter, so it suits smooth, homophilous labels and suits high-frequency, heterophilous labels badly) and in some scientific applications with a fixed graph, but nearly all production GNNs are spatial message passing, because it is inductive, local, and parallelises over edges.

Where GNNs are used, and what each use demands

Fraud and abuse. The opening story is the canonical case: rings of accounts that share devices, cards, addresses or IPs. The graph is heterogeneous and changes every second, labels arrive weeks late (a chargeback lands after the fraud) and never for payments you blocked (hd4 discusses this feedback problem), the classes are extremely imbalanced, and fraudsters are adversaries who learn to look like their neighbours. In practice teams often use the GNN to produce embeddings or risk scores per entity that become features in the existing gradient-boosted model, rather than replacing it: the tree model keeps its explainability and its velocity features, and the GNN adds the relational signal.

Recommendations. Users and items form a bipartite graph (edges only between the two kinds: user saved item). PinSage is the reference case; later models such as LightGCN stripped GCN down further for collaborative filtering. The GNN's output is an item (or user) embedding served through an approximate-nearest-neighbour index, exactly like a two-tower model (rec4), so the serving path barely changes; the GNN changes how the embeddings are computed offline.

People You May Know and link prediction. Score pairs of users from their embeddings. hd18 covers candidate generation from friends-of-friends; a GNN mainly improves ranking of those candidates and helps users with few friends, whose features and few connections still produce an embedding. The trap here is evaluation: if you hide a random 10% of edges as the test set but leave them in the graph used for message passing, the model simply reads the answer. Remove test edges from the message-passing graph, and prefer a time split: train on edges formed before a date, test on edges formed after.

Molecules and materials. Small graphs (tens of atoms), graph-level labels, sum aggregation, edge features for bonds, and often 3D coordinates as extra features. Here the GNN usually is the whole model, and the hard problems are scarce labels (lab measurements are expensive) and generalising to molecules unlike the training set, so splits are made by molecular scaffold rather than at random.

Travel times. Google Maps' ETA (estimated time of arrival) model (Derrow-Pinion et al., 2021) runs a GNN over "supersegments", sequences of adjacent road segments that share traffic, so that congestion spreading from a junction is modelled directly. The paper reports that deployment cut negative ETA outcomes by over 40% in cities such as Sydney compared with the previous production model, and describes the training-stability work (including MetaGradients to tune the learning rate) needed to make it production-ready.

The serving problems

Neighbourhood fan-out at request time. A two-layer model needs features for up to hundreds of nodes per prediction, fetched from a graph store that is usually sharded across machines. Fetching 276 nodes across several shards inside a 100 ms fraud budget is hard. The common design splits the work: compute embeddings for slowly changing entities (merchants, devices, cards with history) offline or on change, store them in a feature store, and at request time run only the final layer over the transaction's live, direct neighbours. That keeps the online fan-out to one hop while still catching a new ring through its shared device.

Staleness. Precomputed embeddings describe the graph as it was when the batch job ran. In recommendations a few hours of lag is fine; in fraud, a device that turned bad an hour ago still carries a clean embedding. Monitor embedding age per request, recompute incrementally for nodes whose neighbourhoods changed, and keep fresh counting features (shared-device counts over the last hour) alongside the embeddings as a safety net.

Hubs. A merchant with ten million customers, a public Wi-Fi IP address, a celebrity account. Unsampled, a hub blows up memory and latency; sampled uniformly, its few informative edges are lost among noise; and hubs connect unrelated communities, so they spread signals that should not spread (every customer of a large online shop "shares" that merchant). Cap and sample by importance, as PinSage did, drop or down-weight edge types that are near-universal, and evaluate accuracy per degree bucket.

Time leakage in training. If a training example for a payment made on 1 March uses a graph snapshot from 1 April, the model sees edges and labels from the future (the chargeback edge, the device later banned), learns from them, and then fails in production where they do not yet exist. Build training examples from point-in-time snapshots, or store edges with timestamps and only let messages flow along edges that existed before the prediction time. This is the most common reason a GNN that looks excellent offline disappoints in an A/B test.

Cold start and inductiveness. Because new nodes appear constantly, production GNNs must be inductive: features plus neighbourhood, never a learned per-node id embedding alone (or, if id embeddings are used for popular nodes, a feature-based fallback for new ones).

What to watch and what to tune

Track the share of requests with a fresh embedding, the age distribution of embeddings, the distribution of sampled neighbourhood sizes, and model metrics split by node degree and by node age, since GNN gains are usually concentrated in the low-degree, new-node segments, and regressions show up in the hub segment first. The knobs people actually tune are the number of layers (2 or 3, almost never more), the sample sizes per hop, the aggregator (mean for social graphs with varied degree, sum for chemistry), which edge types to include or merge, the negative-sampling scheme for link prediction (mix random and hard negatives, as PinSage and rec4 do), and the embedding refresh schedule. Before any of this, build the baseline: a gradient-boosted model with hand-made graph statistics (degree, shared-device counts, friends-in-common, PageRank). If the GNN does not beat it on a time-split evaluation, ship the baseline.

Goal: compute one GCN layer on the 4-node village by hand in NumPy, compare symmetric and mean normalisation, and watch over-smoothing happen. Python 3.12 with NumPy, under five minutes.

import numpy as np
np.set_printoptions(precision=3, suppress=True)

# Ana(0) - Ben(1) - Cai(2), Ben(1) - Dev(3): Ben is the hub
A = np.zeros((4, 4))
for i, j in [(0, 1), (1, 2), (1, 3)]:
    A[i, j] = A[j, i] = 1
X = np.array([[1., 0], [0, 1], [1, 1], [2, 0]])     # two features per person
W = np.array([[1., -1], [0.5, 1]])                  # a made-up "learned" weight matrix

At = A + np.eye(4)                                  # self-loops
d = At.sum(1)
A_hat = np.diag(d ** -0.5) @ At @ np.diag(d ** -0.5)
print("degrees with self-loops:", d)
print("A_hat:\n", A_hat)
print("A_hat @ X:\n", A_hat @ X)
print("GCN layer ReLU(A_hat X W):\n", np.maximum(A_hat @ X @ W, 0))
print("plain mean (D^-1 At) @ X:\n", (At / d[:, None]) @ X)

def min_cos(H):
    N = H / np.linalg.norm(H, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    return (N @ N.T).min()

for k in [0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16]:
    H = np.linalg.matrix_power(A_hat, k) @ X
    print(f"k={k:2d}  least similar pair cosine = {min_cos(H):.3f}")

Design it

A card-payments company scores every payment in under 100 ms with a gradient-boosted model (hd4). The fraud team suspects rings of accounts that share phones, cards and shipping addresses, and wants to "add a GNN". You have 50 million accounts, 80 million cards, 30 million devices, 2 million merchants, chargeback labels that arrive up to 60 days late, and a graph store that can return an entity's direct neighbours in about 5 ms. Propose the graph, the model, the training data and the serving design, and say how you would know it worked.

Answer
  1. Baseline first. Add hand-made graph features to the existing model: number of accounts sharing this device in the last 24 hours and 30 days, accounts sharing this card or shipping address, the fraction of those with past chargebacks, the size of the connected component through devices. Measure the lift. The GNN must beat this, not the original model.
  2. Graph. Heterogeneous: account, card, device, IP, shipping address and merchant nodes; typed edges (owns, logged-in-from, ships-to, paid-at), each with a first-seen timestamp. Drop or heavily sample near-universal nodes (a big merchant, a mobile carrier's shared IP), which connect unrelated people.
  3. Model. Two layers, relation-specific weights (R-GCN style with a basis decomposition), GraphSAGE-style concatenation of self and neighbours so the model can express "unlike my neighbours" (fraudsters camouflage), sampled neighbours (say 20 then 10, importance-weighted for hubs), node features such as account age, card country and device type, and a classifier on the account or payment node.
  4. Training data. Point-in-time: each example is a payment at time t with only edges first seen before t and only labels known by t + 60 days, so no future edges or labels leak in. Time-split validation (train January to June, test August), skipping July so late labels are complete. Payments that were blocked have no outcome label; treat them separately rather than as legitimate.
  5. Serving. A full 2-hop fetch is up to 1 + 20 + 200 nodes at 5 ms per round trip of shard lookups: too slow and too variable. Instead, refresh embeddings for cards, devices, addresses and merchants every few minutes for entities whose neighbourhoods changed, store them in the feature store, and at request time fetch the payment's direct neighbours (one round, about 5 ms) with their stored embeddings and run only the last layer, about a millisecond on a CPU. Feed the resulting embedding and score into the gradient-boosted model as features. A brand-new ring is still caught because its shared device is a live direct neighbour.
  6. Did it work. Offline: precision at the operating recall on the time-split test set, against the step-1 baseline, split by account age and device-degree bucket. Online: an A/B test on fraud losses, false-decline rate and review-queue volume, plus monitoring of embedding age, neighbour-count distribution and score drift. Expect most of the gain on new accounts and shared-device clusters; if the gain is only on old, high-degree accounts, suspect leakage.
