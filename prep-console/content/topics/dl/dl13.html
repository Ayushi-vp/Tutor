An online furniture shop has 10 million product photos. It wants a model that tags each photo with its style (mid-century, industrial, Scandinavian and forty more), finds visually similar items, and spots photos that break the listing rules. It has paid a team of interior designers to label photos for style, and after three months and a large invoice it has 5,000 labelled images: about a hundred per style, some styles with twenty. A network trained from scratch on 5,000 images memorises them. A network pretrained on ImageNet's labelled photos helps, but ImageNet's thousand categories were chosen to tell dogs from trucks, not walnut veneer from oak, and its features reflect that.

Meanwhile 9,995,000 photos sit unused, because nobody has labelled them. That is the shape of almost every real machine learning problem. Labels are the bottleneck in four ways. They are expensive: a radiologist's time, a lawyer's time, a designer's time. They are slow: new products, new fraud patterns and new slang arrive faster than labelling teams. They are narrow: a label answers one question, so a network trained to predict it learns only what that question needs and may ignore everything else in the picture. And they are noisy: two designers disagree about what counts as "industrial". Unlabelled data, by contrast, is nearly free and enormous: the shop's own photos, every web page, every hour of audio, every sensor stream.

Self-supervised learning is the idea that turns that unlabelled pile into training data. Instead of asking a human for the answer, you invent a task whose answer is already in the data. Hide part of a photo and ask the network to fill it in. Show it two different crops of the same photo and ask it to recognise that they belong together, among thousands of crops of other photos. Hide a word in a sentence and ask for it. Or, the biggest example of all, show the network the beginning of a text and ask for the next word: that is how every large language model is pretrained (lm6). None of these tasks is useful in itself. Their value is that solving them well, over millions of examples, forces the network to learn what objects, textures, shapes, sentences and meanings are, and that knowledge is then reusable with very few labels (dl11).

The idea is easy to state and surprisingly hard to make work. The obvious tasks have cheap shortcuts the network finds first. The most natural loss, "make two views of the same image have the same embedding", has a trivial perfect solution: give every image the same embedding. A large part of this lesson is about the clever and sometimes mysterious tricks that avoid that failure. We start with early pretext tasks and why they disappointed. Then we build contrastive learning (SimCLR and MoCo) and work through its loss, InfoNCE, by hand, including what the temperature parameter really does. We see how BYOL and DINO learn without any negative examples and why they do not collapse, then masked modelling (BERT for text, MAE for images) and I-JEPA's prediction in embedding space. Finally we cover how to tell whether a representation is any good, what goes wrong in practice, and how all of this connects to LLM pretraining and to CLIP.

The fill-in-the-blanks analogy

Think of how a child learns a language long before any grammar lesson. Nobody labels each sentence with its meaning. The child hears millions of sentences and constantly, silently, predicts what comes next and notices when the prediction is wrong. To predict well that "the cup fell and ..." ends in "broke" rather than "sang", you need to know about cups, falling and fragility. The prediction task is a pretext; the knowledge it forces is the point. Self-supervised learning does the same with a neural network: pick a pretext task, a task created automatically from unlabelled data, whose answer you already know because you hid it yourself, and train on it at enormous scale.

The analogy breaks in one important way. A child cannot cheat. A neural network will solve the pretext task by whatever is easiest, and if there is a shortcut that answers the question without learning anything about meaning, it will find it. Much of the art in self-supervised learning is designing tasks without shortcuts.

Three families of method

Modern methods fall into three families, shown below. Learn the shapes first; every method in the lesson is a variation on one of them.

  1. Joint-embedding methods make two different views of the same input produce similar embeddings. A view is a randomly transformed copy, an augmentation (dl8): a random crop, a colour change, a blur. The network learns to ignore what the augmentations change (position, colour, scale) and keep what they preserve (what the object is). Contrastive methods (SimCLR, MoCo) also push views of different inputs apart; non-contrastive methods (BYOL, DINO) use only same-input pairs and prevent collapse by other means.
  2. Masked prediction (also called masked modelling or generative pretraining) hides part of the input and reconstructs it from the rest: a hidden word (BERT), hidden image patches (MAE), the next token (GPT-style language models).
  3. Joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPA) combine the two: hide part of the input, but instead of reconstructing its raw pixels or tokens, predict its embedding, as computed by a separate target network.

How we will know it worked

A self-supervised model is never used for its pretext task. What we want is the encoder, the part that turns an image or sentence into an embedding (a vector of numbers, dl11). So we judge it the way dl11 taught: freeze the encoder and train a linear probe, a single linear classifier, on its embeddings of a labelled dataset; or classify each test image by its nearest labelled neighbours in embedding space (k-NN); or fine-tune it with a small fraction of the labels. The headline benchmark in vision is "ImageNet linear-probe top-1 accuracy": pretrain on ImageNet's 1.28 million images with the labels thrown away, then see how well a linear classifier on the frozen features recovers the 1,000 classes. A supervised ResNet-50, trained with all the labels, scores about 76%. In 2020 SimCLR matched it without using a single label during pretraining (76.5%, with a ResNet-50 four times wider than standard). That result is why self-supervised pretraining became the default way to build vision backbones.

1. Pretext tasks, and the shortcut problem

The first wave of self-supervised vision (roughly 2015–2018) invented puzzles. Context prediction (Doersch et al., 2015): cut a 3×3 grid of patches from an image and ask where a given patch sits relative to the centre one; knowing that an ear goes above an eye requires knowing faces. Jigsaw puzzles (Noroozi and Favaro, 2016): shuffle nine patches and predict the permutation. Colourisation (Zhang et al., 2016): predict colours from a greyscale image; you need to know that grass is green and skies blue. Rotation prediction (Gidaris et al., 2018): rotate an image by 0°, 90°, 180° or 270° and classify which; you cannot tell an upside-down cat from an upright one without knowing what cats look like.

These worked to a degree and taught the field a lesson that still drives design today: networks take shortcuts. Doersch et al. found their network solving context prediction partly by exploiting chromatic aberration, a lens effect that shifts colour channels slightly depending on position in the frame, so it could locate patches from colour fringes without understanding content. They had to add colour-channel tricks to block it. Jigsaw networks could match low-level edge continuity across patch borders, so gaps and jitter were added between patches. Every pretext task is a contract: the network will learn exactly what the task requires and nothing more, and if a cheap low-level cue answers the question, that is what you get. A second lesson was that hand-designed puzzles were each good at learning some features and poor at others, and none matched supervised pretraining. The methods that did were more general: comparing views, and reconstructing large hidden regions.

2. Contrastive learning: the idea

Take a photo of a dog. Make two random augmentations of it: one is a tight crop of the dog's head with the colours shifted towards blue, the other a wide crop of the whole dog, slightly blurred, in greyscale. These two views look different pixel by pixel, but they show the same thing. Call them a positive pair. Now take views of other photos in the same batch: a car, a beach, another dog. These are negatives. Contrastive learning trains an encoder so that the embeddings of a positive pair are close together and the embeddings of negatives are far from the anchor. To succeed, the encoder must produce embeddings that survive crops, colour changes and blur, which means it must capture what is constant across them: shape, parts, texture, identity.

The augmentations are not a detail; they define the task. Chen et al.'s SimCLR (2020) tested pairs of augmentations systematically and found that no single augmentation was enough, and that one pair mattered most: random cropping combined with colour distortion. Without colour distortion, two crops of the same image share their colour histogram, and the network can match them by colour alone: a shortcut again. With crops but colour jitter, the network must recognise content. A useful way to think about it: whatever the augmentations change is information the network is taught to throw away. That is a choice about your downstream task, made before you see any labels.

Why are negatives needed at all? Because a loss that only says "make the two views' embeddings equal" is minimised perfectly by an encoder that outputs the same vector for every input. That is called collapse, and it is the central difficulty of the whole field. Negatives make collapse expensive: if everything maps to one point, the anchor is as close to every negative as to its positive, and the loss stays high.

3. SimCLR, component by component

Augmentation. Random resized crop (then resize back to 224×224 pixels), random colour distortion (brightness, contrast, saturation, hue, sometimes greyscale), and random Gaussian blur, applied twice independently to each image.

Encoder f. A standard ResNet-50 (dl6); its pooled output h is a 2,048-dimensional vector. This is the representation you keep.

Projection head g. A two-layer multilayer perceptron (MLP, a small stack of fully connected layers) that maps h to a 128-dimensional z, on which the loss is computed. This small addition mattered a lot: a nonlinear head beat a linear one by 3% and no head by more than 10% in linear-probe accuracy measured on h. The explanation is that the contrastive loss makes z invariant to everything the augmentations change; if the loss acted directly on h, h would throw that information away too, even though downstream tasks (is this photo in colour? which way is the object facing?) may need it. The head acts as a buffer: g absorbs the invariance, h keeps richer information.

Batch and negatives. A batch of N images gives 2N views. For each view the other view of the same image is its positive, and the remaining 2N − 2 views are its negatives; no extra data or memory structure is needed. SimCLR's default batch is N = 4,096, so 8,192 views and 8,190 negatives per anchor, trained with the LARS optimiser (a variant of SGD that scales each layer's step for very large batches) at learning rate 4.8 (0.3 × batch size / 256). On 128 TPU v3 cores (Google's tensor-processing chips) a 100-epoch run took about 1.5 hours. Contrastive learning benefits from bigger batches and longer training more than supervised learning does, because more negatives make the task harder.

4. The InfoNCE loss, worked by hand

The loss SimCLR uses goes by several names: InfoNCE (from van den Oord et al.'s Contrastive Predictive Coding, 2018, where NCE stands for noise-contrastive estimation), NT-Xent (SimCLR's name: normalised temperature-scaled cross-entropy), or the multi-class N-pair loss. For an anchor embedding z, its positive z₊, and a set of candidates that includes z₊ and the negatives, first compute cosine similarities sⱼ = cos(z, zⱼ) (math8: the dot product of the two vectors after normalising each to length 1, so every s is between −1 and 1). Then:

L = −log [ exp(s₊ / τ) / Σⱼ exp(sⱼ / τ) ]

Read it as a classification problem: the classes are the candidates, the logits are the similarities divided by the temperature τ, and the correct class is the positive. It is exactly the softmax cross-entropy you would use for a classifier, which is why it is easy to implement (a matrix product and a standard cross-entropy call) and why it is the same loss as in-batch-negative retrieval (rec4) and CLIP (mm2).

At τ = 1 the similarities, which all lie between −1 and 1, produce a soft distribution where the positive is barely ahead. As τ falls, the softmax sharpens. But the loss value is not the point; what training does is follow the gradient, and the gradient reveals what the temperature really controls. For the softmax cross-entropy, the derivative of the loss with respect to each negative's similarity sₖ is pₖ/τ, and with respect to the positive's similarity it is (p₊ − 1)/τ. So each negative is pushed away with a force proportional to its own probability. At τ = 1 the three negatives' probabilities are 0.284, 0.191 and 0.141, so the hardest negative (similarity 0.5) receives 0.284 / 0.616 = 46% of the total push on negatives. At τ = 0.5 it receives 59%. At τ = 0.1 its probability is 0.047 against 0.001 and 0.000 for the others, so it receives 98% of the push. The temperature decides how much the loss concentrates on hard negatives, the ones closest to the anchor.

Concentrating on hard negatives is good, up to a point: easy negatives (a beach when the anchor is a dog) are already far away and teach nothing. Too much concentration is bad, because the hardest negatives in a large batch are often not really negatives: another dog of the same breed, a second photo of the same product. Those are false negatives, and pushing them away hurts the representation. SimCLR's Table 5 measured it, with normalised embeddings at batch 4,096 and 100 epochs:

Two more facts about InfoNCE are worth knowing. First, the chance level: if the encoder is useless and all similarities are equal, the positive's probability is 1/N for N candidates and the loss is log N. A collapsed encoder that outputs a constant vector gets exactly that: log 256 = 5.55 for 256 candidates, as the lab shows. So collapse is the worst outcome under InfoNCE, not a perfect one. Second, van den Oord et al. showed that minimising InfoNCE maximises a lower bound on the mutual information between the two views (how much knowing one tells you about the other): I ≥ log N − L. The bound can never exceed log N, which is one theoretical reason why more negatives help. Later work showed that the bound does not fully explain why the representations are good; a more practical lens (Wang and Isola, 2020) is that the loss rewards alignment (positives close together) and uniformity (all embeddings spread evenly over the unit sphere, so information is preserved).

5. MoCo: many negatives without a giant batch

SimCLR's negatives come from the batch, so more negatives means a bigger batch, and a batch of 4,096 high-resolution images needs a lot of accelerators. He et al.'s MoCo (Momentum Contrast, 2020) separated the number of negatives from the batch size. It frames contrastive learning as a dictionary look-up: a query (the embedding of one view) should match its key (the embedding of the other view) among many keys. MoCo keeps a queue of keys from recent batches: each step, the current batch's keys are added and the oldest are removed. With a queue of 65,536 keys, each query has 65,536 negatives whatever the batch size (256 in the paper).

There is a catch. The keys in the queue were computed by the encoder as it was several hundred steps ago. If the encoder changes quickly, old keys and new queries live in slightly different spaces, and the comparison is meaningless. MoCo's fix is the momentum encoder: keys are produced by a separate key encoder whose weights are an exponential moving average (EMA) of the query encoder's weights, θₖ ← m·θₖ + (1 − m)·θ_q, with m = 0.999. Each step, the key encoder moves only 0.1% of the way towards the query encoder, so it effectively averages the query encoder's weights over roughly the last 1 / (1 − m) = 1,000 steps. It changes slowly and smoothly, and keys produced a few hundred steps apart are consistent. The paper's ablation is clear: with m = 0 (copying the query encoder) training failed; m = 0.9 reached 55.2%; m = 0.99, 57.8%; m = 0.999, 59.0%; m = 0.9999, 58.9%. Only the query encoder receives gradients. Its temperature was τ = 0.07, and the ResNet-50 reached 60.6% linear-probe accuracy. A follow-up, MoCo v2, adopted SimCLR's MLP projection head and stronger augmentation and then outperformed SimCLR at a batch size of 256. The momentum encoder turned out to be an idea that outlived the queue: BYOL, DINO and I-JEPA all use one.

6. Non-contrastive learning: BYOL and the collapse puzzle

Negatives cost compute (a large batch or a queue) and bring false negatives. Grill et al.'s BYOL (Bootstrap Your Own Latent, 2020) asked whether they are needed at all, and got a surprising answer. BYOL has two networks. The online network is an encoder, a projector and an extra predictor MLP. The target network has an encoder and projector only, and its weights are an EMA of the online network's (starting at τ_base = 0.996 and rising to 1 over training with a cosine schedule). Given two views of an image, the online network processes view 1 and its predictor tries to output the target network's projection of view 2. The loss is the mean squared error between the two after normalising both to length 1, which equals 2 − 2·cos(prediction, target). The loss is symmetrised by swapping the views. Gradients flow only into the online network; the target is treated as a constant (a stop-gradient).

On paper this should collapse immediately: if both networks output the same constant vector for every image, the prediction matches the target perfectly and the loss is 0. In practice BYOL does not collapse, and its ResNet-50 reached 74.3% linear accuracy (79.6% with a larger ResNet), better than SimCLR's 69.3% for the standard-width ResNet-50. It also proved more robust: it lost much less accuracy than SimCLR when the batch was made smaller, or when the augmentations were reduced to random crops alone.

Why no collapse? The authors' hypothesis was the combination of the predictor and the slow-moving target. Chen and He's SimSiam (2021) stripped the recipe further and found that a plain shared encoder, a predictor and a stop-gradient were enough: no negatives, no large batch, no momentum encoder. Remove the stop-gradient, and it collapses. Their interpretation is that the stop-gradient turns training into an alternating procedure, a little like k-means (ml12): one step fixes the targets and moves the network towards them, the next step recomputes the targets with the updated network. Neither step on its own is ever asked to make everything identical, so the trivial solution, while it exists, is not where the dynamics go. This is an empirical success with a partial theory, and a good interview answer says so honestly.

7. DINO: self-distillation with centring and sharpening

Caron et al.'s DINO (self-DIstillation with NO labels, 2021) took the momentum-teacher idea and phrased it as knowledge distillation (training a student network to match a teacher's output probabilities, dl11), where the teacher is just an EMA of the student. Both networks end in a softmax over K = 65,536 output dimensions, which can be thought of as soft, learned "pseudo-classes" or clusters. The student is trained with cross-entropy to match the teacher's distribution on a different view of the same image.

Two design choices make DINO work. The first is multi-crop: from each image, take two large "global" crops at 224×224 pixels and several small "local" crops at 96×96. The teacher sees only the global crops; the student sees all of them. So the student must predict, from a small patch of a dog's ear, what the teacher says about the whole dog: local-to-global correspondence, a strong push towards semantic features. The second is how it avoids collapse without negatives or a predictor: centring and sharpening of the teacher's output. Collapse can happen in two ways here: every image gets a one-hot output on the same dimension (one pseudo-class wins everything), or every image gets the uniform distribution (no information). The two operations push against these in opposite directions.

  • Centring subtracts a running mean c of the teacher's logits (the scores before the softmax) over recent batches. If one dimension starts winning for all images, its mean rises and centring cancels the advantage. On its own it pushes the output towards uniform.
  • Sharpening uses a low teacher temperature (warming from 0.04 to 0.07 over the first 30 epochs; the student uses 0.1). With logits (2, 1, 0), a temperature of 1 gives probabilities (0.665, 0.245, 0.090); a temperature of 0.1 gives (0.99995, 0.00005, 0.000000002), essentially one-hot. On its own it pushes towards one dominant dimension.

Balanced together, with a momentum teacher (EMA coefficient on a cosine schedule from 0.996 to 1), they avoid both collapses; the paper's ablations show either operation alone collapses. DINO with a ViT-B/8 (a Vision Transformer, mm1, with 8×8-pixel patches) reached 80.1% ImageNet linear accuracy, and a small ViT's frozen features reached 78.3% with a plain k-NN classifier. The paper also found that the self-attention maps of DINO-trained ViTs segment the main object in an image without ever seeing a segmentation label, more clearly than supervised ViTs: a striking demonstration that self-supervision can learn things labels never asked for. Its successor DINOv2 (2023) scaled the recipe to a 1-billion-parameter ViT trained on a large curated image dataset and distilled it into smaller models, whose frozen features beat the best openly available CLIP-style features (OpenCLIP) on most image- and pixel-level benchmarks; DINOv2 backbones are now a common default for frozen visual features. A related family, VICReg and Barlow Twins, avoids collapse explicitly with a loss term: keep the variance of every embedding dimension above a threshold across the batch and decorrelate the dimensions from each other.

8. Masked modelling: BERT for text, MAE for images

The second family hides part of the input and reconstructs it. In language this has been dominant since BERT (Devlin et al., 2018): take a sentence, select 15% of its tokens, replace most of them with a special [MASK] token (80% of the selected ones; 10% become a random token and 10% stay unchanged, so the model cannot rely on seeing [MASK]), and train a transformer encoder to predict the original tokens from both left and right context. The target is a word from a vocabulary of about 30,000, so the prediction is a softmax over a discrete set: cheap and well defined. GPT-style models do the causal version: predict every next token from the ones before it. Both are self-supervised: the targets are the text itself.

Images resisted this for a while. Predicting pixels seemed to waste capacity on detail, and early attempts lagged behind contrastive methods. He et al.'s Masked Autoencoder (MAE, 2022) made it work with two design choices. First, mask a very large fraction: 75% of the patches, chosen at random. Images are far more redundant than text; with 15% hidden, the network can fill each gap by interpolating its neighbours, which teaches nothing. With 75% hidden it must infer what objects are where. Second, an asymmetric encoder–decoder. The ViT encoder processes only the visible patches, with no mask tokens; a small decoder (by default 8 transformer blocks, 512 wide) receives the encoded visible patches plus a learned mask token for each hidden position (with position embeddings so it knows where each one goes) and reconstructs the hidden patches' pixels. The loss is the mean squared error on the masked patches only; predicting pixel values normalised within each patch worked slightly better than raw pixels. After pretraining the decoder is thrown away.

Work the arithmetic for a 224×224 image with 16×16-pixel patches: 14 × 14 = 196 patches. With 75% masked, the encoder sees 49. Self-attention cost grows with the square of the number of tokens (lm2), so the encoder's attention costs about 49² / 196² = 1/16 of attending over every patch, and its other layers about a quarter. The paper reports wall-clock training speed-ups of 3× or more (up to about 4× with a smaller decoder or a larger encoder). That efficiency is why MAE scaled: a ViT-Huge pretrained this way reached 87.8% top-1 when fine-tuned on ImageNet-1K, the best result at the time among methods using only ImageNet-1K data.

MAE's features behave differently from contrastive ones. Its linear-probe accuracy is relatively low (68.0% for ViT-B, 75.8% for ViT-L, 76.6% for ViT-H), while its fine-tuned accuracy is excellent. The authors showed that fine-tuning just the last few blocks closes most of the gap, and that MAE beats MoCo v3 under partial fine-tuning even though MoCo v3 has the higher linear probe. Reconstruction features are strong but not linearly arranged by class; contrastive features are arranged for linear separability because the loss compares embeddings directly. Which is "better" depends on whether you will freeze the encoder or fine-tune it.

9. JEPA: predicting in embedding space

Both families have a weakness. Joint-embedding methods depend on hand-designed augmentations, which encode assumptions about which changes do not matter; those assumptions are tuned for photos and may be wrong for X-rays, satellite images or audio. Reconstruction methods avoid augmentations but must predict every pixel, including unpredictable detail (the exact texture of leaves, sensor noise), so the encoder spends capacity on things no downstream task needs. Assran et al.'s I-JEPA (Image-based Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture, 2023), building on a framework LeCun proposed, takes the masked-prediction recipe and moves the target into embedding space.

From one image, sample four target blocks, each covering 15–20% of the image with an aspect ratio between 0.75 and 1.5, and one large context block covering 85–100% of the image, with any overlap with the targets removed. A context encoder (a ViT) embeds the visible context patches. A target encoder, an EMA of the context encoder, embeds the full image, and the embeddings of the patches in each target block become the targets. A narrow predictor network takes the context embeddings plus position tokens saying where each target block is, and predicts the target embeddings; the loss is the squared error between predicted and actual target embeddings. No augmentations are used to create views; the only "augmentation" is the masking.

Two choices matter, as the paper's ablations show. The target blocks must be large enough to be semantic (a whole part of an object, not a few pixels), and the context must be informative and spread out. And predicting in embedding space lets the target encoder discard unpredictable detail: if leaf texture cannot be predicted, the target embedding can simply not encode it, so the predictor is not forced to guess it. This is the "learn what is predictable" idea in one sentence. The cost is that collapse is again possible (the embedding space is learned, so a constant target is trivially predictable), which the EMA target and the asymmetric predictor prevent, as in BYOL. Results: a ViT-H/14 trained on ImageNet using 16 NVIDIA A100 GPUs in under 72 hours reached 79.3% linear-probe accuracy, against 77.2% for MAE's ViT-H/14 trained for 1,600 epochs, and I-JEPA did well on low-level tasks such as object counting and depth prediction where augmentation-invariant methods are weaker. The same idea has since been applied to video (V-JEPA).

10. Evaluating representations

Since the pretext task is not the goal, evaluation needs care. The standard protocols, from cheapest to most expensive:

  • k-NN on frozen features. Embed the labelled training set once; classify each test image by a vote among its k nearest neighbours by cosine similarity. DINO used k = 20 with each neighbour's vote weighted by exp(similarity / 0.07). No training, no hyperparameters to tune beyond k, and cheap enough to run every epoch during pretraining as a progress monitor.
  • Linear probe. Train a linear classifier on frozen features. The headline ImageNet number. Sensitive to details: the probe's own augmentation, whether features are normalised (a BatchNorm layer before the probe is common), the learning rate and number of epochs. Compare numbers only under the same protocol.
  • Low-label fine-tuning. Fine-tune the whole network with 1% or 10% of ImageNet labels (about 13 or 128 images per class). SimCLR fine-tuned with 1% of labels reached 85.8% top-5 accuracy, beating AlexNet trained on all the labels, with 100 times fewer.
  • Full fine-tuning and transfer to other datasets and tasks (object detection on COCO, the Common Objects in Context dataset; segmentation; depth). MoCo's headline was that its features beat supervised ImageNet pretraining on 7 detection and segmentation tasks.

These measure different things. A linear probe asks "is the label information linearly available as is?", fine-tuning asks "is this a good starting point?", and they can disagree, as MAE shows. Report the one that matches your use: frozen embeddings for retrieval or many small heads, fine-tuning for a single high-stakes task.

11. From here to LLMs and CLIP

Everything in this lesson appears at foundation-model scale. LLM pretraining is masked modelling with a causal mask: every position's target is the next token, so a corpus of trillions of tokens provides trillions of training targets without a single label (lm6). It works so well in text partly because text is already a compact, discrete, meaningful representation: predicting the next token is not wasted on noise, and the softmax over a vocabulary handles uncertainty naturally. Images and audio are continuous and full of unpredictable detail, which is why vision moved towards predicting embeddings (JEPA) or invariances (DINO) rather than raw values. Speech models such as wav2vec 2.0 sit in between: they mask stretches of audio and use a contrastive loss to pick the correct quantised (discretised) speech unit for each masked position among distractors.

CLIP (mm2) is InfoNCE across modalities. The two "views" are an image and its caption, produced by different encoders; the negatives are the other captions in the batch (32,767 of them, with a batch of 32,768); the loss is symmetric (images to captions and captions to images); and the temperature is a learned parameter, initialised to the equivalent of 0.07 (as in MoCo) and clipped so that it never scales the logits by more than 100, which the authors found necessary for stability. Two-tower retrieval with in-batch negatives (rec4) is the same loss again, with users and items as the two views. When you understand InfoNCE and its temperature, you understand the training objective of a large share of the embedding models in production.

Augmentations are your prior, and they can be wrong

A joint-embedding method learns to be invariant to whatever its augmentations change, and invariance means discarding information. Colour jitter teaches the network that colour does not matter, which is right for "is this a dog?" and wrong for "is this flower a buttercup or a daisy?", for "is this fruit ripe?" and for many medical tasks where discolouration is the signal. Horizontal flips teach that left and right are the same, which is wrong for reading text and for chest X-rays where the heart's side matters. Aggressive cropping teaches that a small part implies the whole, which works for object-centric photos like ImageNet and less well for scenes with many objects, where two random crops may show different objects entirely and the "positive pair" is a lie. This is why SimCLR's projection head helps (it absorbs the invariance so the layer before it keeps the information), why methods that need fewer augmentations (MAE, I-JEPA) are attractive in new domains, and why the first thing to do when applying contrastive learning to a new modality is to list what each augmentation would erase.

False negatives and the limits of more negatives

In ImageNet with 1,000 classes, a batch of 4,096 contains about four images of each class on average, so every anchor has several "negatives" that share its class. With a peaked temperature, these get most of the gradient, and the loss actively pushes same-class images apart. The effect grows with batch size and with datasets that have few classes or many near-duplicates (product catalogues with the same item photographed several times). Remedies include deduplicating the data, lowering the weight of the most similar negatives (debiased contrastive losses estimate and subtract the expected false-negative contribution), using supervised contrastive learning when labels exist (all same-label samples become positives), or switching to non-contrastive methods, which have no negatives to be wrong about. The InfoNCE mutual-information bound also caps at log N, so beyond a point more negatives mostly add false negatives and cost.

Dimensional collapse

Complete collapse (every output identical) is easy to spot. Partial collapse is subtler and common: the embeddings vary, but only along a few directions of the 128- or 2,048-dimensional space. Jing et al. (2021) showed this dimensional collapse happens even in contrastive methods, driven by strong augmentation and by the interaction between layers. To measure it, collect embeddings for a few thousand images, compute their covariance matrix, and look at its singular values (math9): a healthy representation has a slowly decaying spectrum, a collapsed one has a few large values and the rest near zero. A single number summary is the effective rank, the exponential of the entropy of the normalised singular values. VICReg's variance and covariance terms and Barlow Twins' decorrelation loss attack exactly this, by forcing every dimension to carry variance and different dimensions to carry different information.

Why stop-gradient and EMA prevent collapse, as far as anyone knows

Tian et al. (2021) analysed BYOL and SimSiam with simplified linear networks and found that the predictor's weights align with the eigenvectors of the embedding correlation matrix, and that the stop-gradient creates dynamics in which the trivial solution is unstable: directions with signal grow while noise directions shrink, rather than everything shrinking together. The EMA target slows the targets down so that this growth can happen before the online network chases its own changes. DINO's analysis is simpler: centring and sharpening are two opposing forces on the output distribution, and the EMA teacher smooths them. In all cases, collapse prevention lives in the optimisation dynamics, not in the loss's minimum, which has a practical consequence: these methods are sensitive to hyperparameters such as the EMA schedule, the predictor's learning rate, and the teacher temperature, and a change that looks harmless can tip a run into collapse. Monitor rather than assume.

Linear separability is not the whole story

The field optimised linear-probe accuracy for years, and MAE showed that it can mislead. Contrastive and DINO-style objectives compare embeddings directly, so their embeddings are arranged by semantic similarity, which makes a linear classifier and k-NN work well out of the box. Reconstruction objectives produce features that contain the information but arrange it less linearly; a nonlinear head or a few tuned blocks unlock it. For systems design this is a real choice: a frozen-embedding service (search, deduplication, clustering, many small heads trained by different teams) wants linearly well-arranged embeddings; a single model fine-tuned for one task wants the best starting point, which may be a masked-modelling backbone. The two families are increasingly combined (iBOT and DINOv2 add a masked-patch prediction term to a DINO objective) to get both.

Data matters as much as the objective

Self-supervised methods were developed on ImageNet, a carefully curated, object-centric, balanced dataset, with its labels discarded. Trained on raw web images, which are dominated by a few concepts (products, screenshots, faces, text), they learn the imbalance. DINOv2's main contribution was a data pipeline: start from a curated seed set and retrieve similar images from a large uncurated pool, deduplicating and balancing, so the self-supervised model sees a diverse, balanced dataset. The same lesson applies in LLM pretraining, where deduplication and quality filtering of the corpus matter as much as model size. "Unlabelled" does not mean "uncurated".

When teams actually use self-supervised learning

Most teams do not pretrain a foundation model; they use one. The common pattern in 2024–2026 is: start with an off-the-shelf self-supervised backbone (DINOv2 for visual features, a CLIP or SigLIP image encoder when text alignment is useful, a pretrained speech or text encoder), measure a k-NN or linear probe on a small labelled set from your domain, and only then decide whether to do more. Running your own self-supervised stage pays off when you have a lot of unlabelled data from a domain unlike web photos (medical scans, satellite imagery, industrial inspection, documents, sensor data), when labels are scarce or slow, and when the public backbone's probe is far below what fine-tuning reaches. In that case the usual move is continued self-supervised pretraining: start from the public weights and run DINO-style or MAE-style training on your in-domain images for a fraction of the original schedule (dl11 calls this domain-adaptive pretraining).

Self-supervised embeddings also power products directly, with no labels at all: near-duplicate detection for listings or uploads, visual search ("find similar items"), clustering a catalogue, and detecting out-of-distribution inputs by their distance from the training data. These uses want embeddings that work frozen, which favours contrastive or DINO-style models.

Failure modes and their symptoms

Collapse. The loss settles at a suspicious value (log N for InfoNCE; near zero for BYOL-style losses), the per-dimension standard deviation of normalised embeddings across a batch drops towards zero, and k-NN accuracy falls to chance. Dimensional collapse shows up as a falling effective rank of the embedding covariance. Log both every few hundred steps.

Shortcuts. The loss falls quickly but downstream metrics do not improve. Typical causes are augmentations that leave an easy cue (colour histograms, image borders, JPEG artefacts, watermarks, timestamps burnt into frames, the same background in both crops), near-duplicate images so that "two views" can be matched by pixel identity, and BatchNorm information leakage. The last is subtle: with per-GPU BatchNorm, both views of an image are normalised with the same batch statistics, and the network can exploit that shared signal to find its partner without learning content. SimCLR uses global BatchNorm (statistics aggregated across all devices), MoCo shuffles samples across GPUs before the key encoder so that the query and key of one image land in different BatchNorm batches, and CLIP's authors replaced BatchNorm in some experiments to avoid the same leak.

Augmentation mismatch. The model is invariant to something the task needs (colour for ripeness, orientation for documents, laterality for X-rays). Symptom: good k-NN accuracy on generic categories, poor on the specific distinctions that matter. Fix: remove or soften the offending augmentation, or use a masked-modelling objective.

Evaluation that does not match use. A team chooses a backbone by ImageNet linear probe, then fine-tunes it on a detection task where a masked-modelling backbone would have been better, or vice versa. Evaluate on your task, under your protocol.

Systems and cost

Contrastive methods need many negatives, so in multi-GPU training each GPU all-gathers the embeddings from every other GPU before computing its similarity matrix, so that each anchor sees the global batch; gradients flow back only through the local embeddings. The similarity matrix itself is small (8,192 × 8,192 = 67 million entries at SimCLR's batch), but the activations of thousands of high-resolution images are not, which is why gradient checkpointing and mixed precision (gpu4) are standard. CLIP's batch of 32,768 needed both. Non-contrastive and masked methods remove the dependency on batch size, which is a large part of their practical appeal: BYOL degrades much less than SimCLR at small batches, and MAE's encoder sees only a quarter of the patches, which cuts compute several-fold. Rough reference points from the papers: SimCLR's 100-epoch ResNet-50 run took about 1.5 hours on 128 TPU v3 cores at batch 4,096, and I-JEPA's ViT-H/14 trained on 16 A100 GPUs in under 72 hours.

The knobs people tune

Temperature (contrastive: 0.07–0.2 is typical; CLIP learns it with a cap), batch size or queue size, augmentation strength and type, the EMA schedule for momentum encoders (0.99–0.999 base, often increased towards 1 over training), the projection-head width and depth, the masking ratio and block shapes for masked methods, and the number of pretraining epochs, which is much larger than for supervised training (MAE kept improving at 1,600 epochs; BYOL used 1,000). The single most useful practice is a cheap, frequent downstream monitor (k-NN on a few thousand labelled images) so that you see the representation improve, plateau or collapse while the run is going rather than after.

Goal: compute the InfoNCE loss on a batch of embeddings, sweep the temperature, and see how it changes the positive's probability and how much of the push lands on the hardest negative; then check what a collapsed encoder scores. Python 3.12 with NumPy, two minutes.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N, D = 256, 128
norm = lambda v: v / np.linalg.norm(v, axis=1, keepdims=True)

def info_nce(a, p, tau):
    logits = a @ p.T / tau                              # row i, column j = cos(anchor i, candidate j) / tau
    logits -= logits.max(1, keepdims=True)              # numerical safety; the softmax is unchanged
    prob = np.exp(logits); prob /= prob.sum(1, keepdims=True)
    loss = -np.log(np.diag(prob)).mean()                # anchor i's positive is candidate i
    acc = (prob.argmax(1) == np.arange(len(a))).mean()  # is the positive ranked first?
    neg = prob.copy(); np.fill_diagonal(neg, 0)
    hardest = (neg.max(1) / neg.sum(1)).mean()          # share of the push on negatives that hits the closest one
    return loss, np.diag(prob).mean(), acc, hardest

anchors = norm(rng.normal(size=(N, D)))
for noise in (0.5, 1.0, 3.0):                           # how different the two "views" are
    positives = norm(anchors + noise * rng.normal(size=(N, D)) / np.sqrt(D))
    print(f"\nnoise {noise}: mean positive cosine {np.mean(np.sum(anchors * positives, 1)):.3f}")
    print(" tau   loss   p(pos)  top-1  hardest-neg share")
    for tau in (1.0, 0.5, 0.2, 0.1, 0.05, 0.02):
        l, pp, acc, h = info_nce(anchors, positives, tau)
        print(f"{tau:5.2f} {l:6.3f} {pp:7.3f} {acc:6.3f} {h:8.3f}")

same = np.tile(anchors[:1], (N, 1))                     # a collapsed encoder: every output identical
print(f"\ncollapsed encoder: loss {info_nce(same, same, 0.1)[0]:.3f}   log(N) = {np.log(N):.3f}")

What to look for.

  1. Loss falls as τ falls for the same embeddings (noise 1.0: about 4.85 at τ = 1, 0.29 at τ = 0.1). The loss value alone says nothing about representation quality; it depends on τ.
  2. Top-1 does not depend on τ: dividing by a positive number never changes which candidate is most similar. Temperature changes the gradient, not the ranking.
  3. Hardest-negative share climbs from about 0.5% at τ = 1 (255 negatives share the push almost evenly) to about 54% at τ = 0.02. That is the hard-negative focusing from the worked example, at batch scale.
  4. Collapse costs log N: the collapsed encoder scores exactly 5.545 = log 256, the chance level. Negatives make collapse the worst outcome.

Try: copy a few anchors into other rows with small noise to create false negatives and watch the low-τ loss for those rows jump.

Diagnose it

A team pretrains a ResNet-50 with a SimCLR-style loss on 3 million unlabelled product photos from their catalogue, on 32 GPUs with batch 256 per GPU, τ = 0.05, the standard SimCLR augmentations, and per-GPU BatchNorm. The training loss drops very quickly in the first epoch and keeps falling. After 200 epochs the k-NN accuracy on a labelled set of 20,000 products (by category) is barely above an ImageNet-pretrained baseline, and the "find similar items" demo returns the same product photographed from another angle at the top, but otherwise unrelated items. List the likely causes, in the order you would check them, and the fixes.

Answer
  1. BatchNorm leakage (the fast first-epoch drop is the clue). With per-GPU BatchNorm both views of an image are normalised with the same local batch statistics, a shared signal the network can use to find its partner. Fix: synchronised (global) BatchNorm across GPUs, or shuffle samples across GPUs as MoCo does, or use a normalisation that does not mix samples (LayerNorm, GroupNorm).
  2. Negatives seen per anchor. If the loss is computed per GPU, each anchor sees only 2 × 256 − 2 = 510 negatives, not 16,382. Fix: all-gather embeddings across the 32 GPUs before the similarity matrix.
  3. False negatives from near-duplicates. Catalogues photograph the same item several times; those photos land in the same batch as negatives, and at τ = 0.05 most of the gradient goes to the closest negatives, which are exactly these duplicates. The model learns to separate photos of the same product, the opposite of what search needs. Fix: deduplicate or group photos by product and treat same-product photos as positives (or exclude them from the negatives); raise τ to around 0.1–0.2 so the push spreads beyond the very closest negatives.
  4. Augmentations versus the task. Colour jitter removes colour, which matters for products ("the red one"); aggressive crops of product shots on white backgrounds may produce views showing only background. Fix: soften colour distortion, constrain crops to contain the object, and check by listing what each augmentation erases.
  5. Evaluation and monitoring. Run the k-NN monitor every few epochs, log per-dimension embedding standard deviation and effective rank to catch partial collapse, and compare against a strong public backbone (DINOv2, a CLIP encoder) with continued pretraining as the alternative plan; on 3 million in-domain images, continued DINO-style training from public weights is likely cheaper and better than training from scratch.
