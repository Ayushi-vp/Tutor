In the 1980s and again in the 2000s, researchers stacked more and more layers into neural networks and found the same disappointing thing: past three or four layers, adding depth made training worse, not better. The networks that could be trained were shallow. For years the standard explanation was that deep networks were simply hard to optimise. The real culprit turned out to be small and unglamorous: the shape of a one-line function applied to every neuron, the activation function.

Every neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs and then passes it through such a function. Choose a curve that goes flat at both ends (sigmoid, tanh, the natural choices in the early days) and, as the error signal travels backwards through ten or twenty layers, it is multiplied at each layer by a slope that is nearly zero. The first layers learn nothing. Choose a function whose slope is exactly 1 for positive inputs — ReLU, "if it's positive keep it, otherwise output zero" — and the signal passes through intact. In 2012 that swap was one of the ingredients that let AlexNet win the ImageNet competition by a wide margin, training on two consumer graphics cards; its authors reported reaching a given training error six times faster than with tanh.

The story did not end there, because ReLU has its own failure: a unit that is pushed into the region where it outputs zero gets zero gradient and never comes back. So the field kept iterating — leaky and parametric versions, smooth curves called GELU and SiLU, and finally gated units such as SwiGLU, which sit inside the feed-forward block of essentially every recent large language model. If you open the configuration of LLaMA, PaLM or Gemma, the activation function is one of the first architecture choices listed, and an interviewer expects you to say why it is that one.

This lesson builds the subject from the ground up. We show, with numbers, why a network without a non-linearity is useless and how two ReLU units solve XOR; then why sigmoid and tanh saturate and what that does to gradients; what makes ReLU work and how units die; how GELU and SiLU smooth it; how gated linear units add a multiplicative gate and why they need a third weight matrix and a 2/3 hidden width; which output activation goes with which loss; and how activation and initialisation must be chosen together. The public case is Shazeer's 2020 controlled comparison that moved the industry from ReLU and GELU to SwiGLU, checked against the paper's own table.

A neuron's last step, and why it matters

Recall the neuron from dl1: it multiplies its inputs by weights, adds a bias, and gets a number z, the pre-activation. The neuron's output is a = f(z), where f is the activation function. If f were the identity (a = z) the neuron would be a plain weighted sum. The whole point of f is to be not a straight line.

An analogy: think of each neuron as a light dimmer switch on a dial. A linear "activation" is a dimmer whose brightness is exactly proportional to the dial: turn it twice as far, twice as bright, forever. A useful neuron behaves more like a real switch — off below a threshold, ramping up above it, perhaps flattening out at full brightness. Stack many such switches in layers and the pattern of which ones are on encodes something about the input: an edge, a shape, a word's role in a sentence. The analogy breaks in one respect: real dimmers are chosen for looks, while activation functions are chosen for how well the error signal travels back through them during training, and that second job decides most of the design.

The four curves that matter

  • Sigmoid, σ(z) = 1/(1 + e⁻ᶻ): squashes every input into the range (0, 1). σ(0) = 0.5, σ(4) ≈ 0.98, σ(−4) ≈ 0.02. Natural as a "probability" or a gate. Rarely used for hidden layers today, but still the output for binary decisions and a building block inside LSTMs and SiLU.
  • Tanh, tanh(z): squashes into (−1, 1), centred on zero. tanh(0) = 0, tanh(2) ≈ 0.96.
  • ReLU (rectified linear unit), max(0, z): ReLU(−3) = 0, ReLU(2) = 2. Cheap, and the default for CNNs and plain MLPs.
  • GELU and SiLU: smooth cousins of ReLU, z·Φ(z) and z·σ(z), used in transformers. They follow ReLU for large inputs but bend smoothly near zero and dip slightly below zero for negative inputs.

Three things to hold in your head

1. Non-linearity gives depth its power. Without it, layers collapse (next section). 2. The slope is the second job. Backprop multiplies each layer's gradient by f′(z) (dl1), so a function whose slope is tiny over a wide range starves the layers behind it. 3. Everything modern is a variation on "keep the slope near 1 where it counts". ReLU does it with a hard corner; GELU and SiLU smooth the corner; gated units multiply the signal by a learned gate. If you can explain why sigmoid's flat tails are a problem and what ReLU changes, you have the core of every interview question on this topic.

Choosing, simply

Hidden layers: ReLU for CNNs and small MLPs; GELU or a gated unit (SwiGLU, GeGLU) for transformers. Output layer: nothing (linear) for a real-valued target, sigmoid for one yes/no, softmax for one-of-many classes. The rest of the lesson explains why.

1. Why stacking linear layers gets you nothing

Take two layers without activations: h = W1·x and y = W2·h. Substitute: y = W2·(W1·x) = (W2·W1)·x. Matrix multiplication is associative, so the two layers are exactly one layer whose matrix is the product. With numbers: W1 = [[1, 2], [0, 1]], W2 = [[2, 0], [1, 1]], x = (1, 1). Layer by layer: h = (3, 1), y = (6, 4). Combined: W2·W1 = [[2, 4], [1, 3]], and [[2, 4], [1, 3]]·(1, 1) = (6, 4). Same answer. A network of 50 such layers is one matrix, and its decision boundaries are straight lines (or hyperplanes). Adding biases does not change this — affine maps compose into affine maps.

The classic proof that this is a real limitation is XOR: output 1 when exactly one of two inputs is 1. Any affine function f(x₁, x₂) = ax₁ + bx₂ + c satisfies f(0,0) + f(1,1) = f(0,1) + f(1,0) (both sides equal a + b + 2c). XOR would need 0 + 0 on the left and 1 + 1 on the right, so no linear model can compute it. Now add a ReLU. Let h₁ = ReLU(x₁ + x₂), h₂ = ReLU(x₁ + x₂ − 1) and output h₁ − 2h₂:

Two hidden ReLU units solve a problem no number of linear layers can. The mechanism: ReLU folds the number line at a chosen point, and a weighted sum of folds can make bumps and zigzags. The universal approximation theorem says that with enough such units a network can approximate any reasonable function; the question deep learning cares about is which activation lets us find the weights by gradient descent.

2. What we want from an activation

A good hidden-layer activation should (a) be non-linear, obviously; (b) have a slope that stays around 1 over the range of inputs we actually see, so gradients neither vanish nor explode as they pass back; (c) be cheap, since it runs on every neuron of every layer of every example; (d) be differentiable, or nearly so, because backprop needs a slope; and (e) ideally keep the layer's output at a reasonable scale (roughly centred and not growing without bound) so the next layer sees well-behaved inputs. Each generation of activation trades these against each other.

The slope of sigmoid is at most 0.25, achieved only at z = 0, and it falls to 0.007 by |z| = 5. Tanh's peak slope is 1 but it, too, collapses: 0.07 at |z| = 2. When a unit's input lands far from zero the unit is saturated — pinned near 0 or 1 (or ±1) — and its slope is almost zero, so a gradient arriving from above is multiplied by a near-zero number and stops. This is the mechanical cause of the vanishing gradient problem, examined in depth in dl3.

The compounding, with numbers. Backprop multiplies one slope per layer (plus the weights). Take the best case for each function and ignore the weights: after k layers the gradient is scaled by 0.25ᵏ for sigmoid. After 5 layers that is 0.001; after 10 layers, 9.5×10⁻⁷. For tanh at a typical |z| ≈ 1 (slope 0.42): 0.013 after 5 layers, 1.7×10⁻⁴ after 10. For an active ReLU path the factor is 1ᵏ = 1. If the first layer's gradient is a millionth of the last layer's, the first layer's weights barely move in any reasonable time, and the network effectively trains only its last few layers.

A second problem: sigmoid is not zero-centred. Its output is always positive. Consider a neuron in the next layer: its weight gradient is ∂L/∂w_j = (upstream gradient) × x_j, where x_j is the sigmoid output feeding it. If every x_j is positive, all the weight gradients for that neuron have the same sign (that of the upstream gradient), so the weights can only all increase or all decrease together in a step. To move in a direction where some go up and others down, the optimiser must zig-zag. Tanh, being centred on zero, avoids this, which is why tanh usually beat sigmoid in hidden layers. Both also need an exponential, which is slow relative to a comparison.

4. ReLU: what changed, and why it worked

ReLU(z) = max(0, z). Its slope is 1 where z > 0 and 0 where z < 0 (at exactly 0 frameworks use 0). Rectifiers were proposed for neural networks by Nair & Hinton (2010) and studied for deep supervised training by Glorot, Bordes & Bengio (2011); AlexNet (2012) made them famous. There are three reasons it works so well.

  • The slope is exactly 1 on active units. The gradient through an active unit passes unchanged, so an active path across a hundred layers does not shrink the signal by activation slopes at all. (Weights still matter — dl3.)
  • It is cheap and sparse. One comparison, no exponential. At initialisation about half of a layer's units output exactly zero for a given input, so information is carried by a sparse subset of active units, and the backward pass for the zeroed units is trivially zero.
  • It is piecewise linear and scale-equivariant. ReLU(c·z) = c·ReLU(z) for c > 0. A network of ReLUs (without biases) scales its output when its input scales, which makes the optimisation landscape better behaved, and inside each region of input space the network is exactly a linear function whose slope is a product of 0/1 gates and weights.

The AlexNet paper's evidence is direct. Its Figure 1 shows that a four-layer convolutional network with ReLUs "reaches a 25% training error rate on CIFAR-10 six times faster than an equivalent network with tanh neurons", and the authors add that they "would not have been able to experiment with such large neural networks for this work if we had used traditional saturating neuron models". Their network had 60 million parameters and trained in five to six days on two GTX 580 GPUs.

5. How a ReLU dies

The price of a slope of exactly 0 on the negative side: a unit whose z is negative for every example in the data outputs 0 and has zero slope, so no gradient reaches its weights or its bias. Nothing can move it back. A worked case: a unit computes z = 1.0·x + b on data with x between 0 and 1. With b = 0.1, z ranges over 0.1 to 1.1: active. Now suppose one large update (a big learning rate, or a rare huge gradient) pushes b to −5. Now z ranges from −5 to −4. The output is 0 for every example; the slope is 0 for every example; ∂L/∂b = ∂L/∂w = 0. The unit is dead, and gradient descent, which only moves parameters in response to gradients, will never revive it. In the lesson's lab, with a learning rate of 3, about 41% of the hidden units in a small ReLU network were silent on all 400 training examples, against 3–6% at moderate learning rates.

A dead unit is not a crash: the loss still falls, using the remaining units. You have simply paid for capacity you do not have. The usual causes are learning rates that are too high, large negative biases, badly scaled inputs, and (with Adam) early steps that are too large; the diagnostic is the fraction of zero activations per layer, logged per step. Remedies:

  • Leaky ReLU (Maas et al., 2013): max(0.01z, z) — a small slope on the negative side so gradient always flows.
  • PReLU (He et al., 2015): the same, but the negative slope is learned. The extra parameters equal the number of channels — negligible — and PReLU networks gave 5.71% top-5 error as a single model and 4.94% as a multi-model result on ImageNet — the first published result to beat the 5.1% error reported for human annotators.
  • ELU (Clevert et al., 2015): z for positive z and α(eᶻ − 1) for negative z — smooth and saturating at −α.
  • Softer training: a smaller learning rate, warmup, and He initialisation (section 8), all of which prevent the big early updates that kill units.

6. GELU and SiLU: smoothing the corner

Transformers moved to smooth ReLU relatives. GELU (Gaussian error linear unit; Hendrycks & Gimpel, 2016) is GELU(z) = z·Φ(z), where Φ(z) is the probability that a standard normal random variable is below z. Read it as: multiply the input by the probability that it "wins" against a random threshold. For large positive z, Φ ≈ 1 and GELU ≈ z; for large negative z, Φ ≈ 0 and GELU ≈ 0; near 0 it bends smoothly. SiLU (also called Swish with β = 1; Ramachandran et al. found it by automated search, Elfwing et al. proposed it independently) is SiLU(z) = z·σ(z), which is very close in shape. Three properties are worth knowing. The curve is non-monotonic: GELU dips to a minimum of about −0.170 near z = −0.75 (SiLU to −0.278 near z = −1.28) and then rises back to 0, so small negative inputs give small negative outputs rather than exactly zero. The slope is never exactly zero for moderate negative inputs (−0.083 at z = −1), so a unit cannot die in the ReLU sense — although for very negative z the slope is again tiny (−0.012 at −3). And the slope can slightly exceed 1 (GELU′(1) = 1.083), unlike ReLU. In practice GELU is computed either exactly with the error function or with the tanh approximation 0.5z(1 + tanh(√(2/π)(z + 0.044715z³))), whose worst-case error is about 5×10⁻⁴; the two are interchangeable for training but a checkpoint trained with one should be served with the same one (see prod).

Why smooth? A common argument is that a non-zero, smoothly varying gradient near zero gives the optimiser more information than ReLU's hard 0/1 switch, and that the negative dip lets units carry a little information about how far below the threshold the input is. Honest summary: BERT and GPT-2 used GELU, Swish/SiLU appear in EfficientNet and many vision models, the empirical gain over ReLU is modest (Ramachandran et al. report +0.9% top-1 ImageNet accuracy for Mobile NASNet-A and +0.6% for Inception-ResNet-v2 from swapping in Swish), and no one has a decisive theory. Say so in an interview.

7. Gated linear units and SwiGLU

The most important recent change is not another pointwise curve but a gate. A transformer's feed-forward block (FFN) processes each token vector x of width d: project up to a wider hidden size, apply an activation, project back down.

standard FFN:   FFN(x)    = W2 · f(W1 · x)                       two matrices: W1 (d×4d), W2 (4d×d)
gated FFN:      SwiGLU(x) = W2 · ( SiLU(Wg · x) ⊙ (Wu · x) )      three matrices: Wg, Wu (d×h), W2 (h×d)

In a gated linear unit (GLU; Dauphin et al., 2016) the layer computes two parallel linear projections of the same input — a gate branch and an up (value) branch — passes the gate through an activation and multiplies the two elementwise (⊙). SwiGLU uses SiLU for the gate, GeGLU uses GELU, ReGLU uses ReLU, and the "bilinear" layer uses no activation at all. The gate decides, for every hidden unit and every token, how much of the value to let through: the multiplication makes the effective transformation depend on the input in a way a fixed pointwise curve cannot.

A worked SwiGLU. Take x = (1, 2), gate matrix Wg = [[0.5, −1], [0.5, 0.25]] and up matrix Wu = [[1, 0.5], [0.5, 1]]. Gate pre-activation: x·Wg = (0.5 + 1.0, −1 + 0.5) = (1.5, −0.5). SiLU: SiLU(1.5) = 1.5 × σ(1.5) = 1.5 × 0.8176 = 1.226; SiLU(−0.5) = −0.5 × 0.3775 = −0.189. Up branch: x·Wu = (1 + 1, 0.5 + 2) = (2, 2.5). Elementwise product: (1.226 × 2, −0.189 × 2.5) = (2.453, −0.472). Now the backward pass, as in dl1: the gradient with respect to the up branch is the gate value (1.226, −0.189) times the incoming gradient — the value path is scaled by how open the gate is — and the gradient with respect to the gate pre-activation is SiLU′ times the up value: SiLU′(1.5) = 1.041, so 1.041 × 2 = 2.083 for the first unit and 0.260 × 2.5 = 0.650 for the second. Each branch's gradient depends on the other branch's value: that is what "multiplicative interaction" means, and why the layer can represent products of features (a bilinear form) that a pointwise nonlinearity of a single projection cannot.

Parameter counting. A standard FFN with hidden width 4d has 2 × d × 4d = 8d² weights. A gated FFN with hidden width h has 3 × d × h. Setting 3dh = 8d² gives h = 8d/3 ≈ 2.67d — "the 2/3 rule", since 2/3 × 4d = 8d/3. For d = 4,096: standard, 2 × 4,096 × 16,384 = 134.2 million weights per layer; gated with h = 10,923, 3 × 4,096 × 10,923 = 134.2 million. In practice h is rounded to a multiple of 256 for hardware efficiency: LLaMA-7B uses 11,008 (3 × 4,096 × 11,008 = 135.3 M). Llama 3 goes further and treats the FFN width as a free hyperparameter: 14,336 for d = 4,096 (about 3.5d), 28,672 for d = 8,192 and 53,248 for d = 16,384, all with SwiGLU. Those are scaling decisions, not consequences of the activation.

Why does gating help? Nobody has a proven answer. Plausible ingredients: the gate gives every hidden unit an input-dependent multiplier (a soft, learned "which features matter for this token" selection), the product creates second-order interactions between features, and the gate branch keeps a healthy gradient path because its non-linearity is applied to one factor while the other passes linearly. Shazeer's paper is honest about it: "We offer no explanation as to why these architectures seem to work; we attribute their success, as all else, to divine benevolence." The evidence is a controlled experiment, which we review in prod.

8. Output activations go with losses

The last layer's activation is chosen from the target, not from fashion. It is always paired with a loss, and in every classification pairing the fused gradient with respect to the logits is p − y (the derivation in dl1, section 6).

Two practical details. First, use the fused "with logits" loss functions (F.cross_entropy, BCEWithLogitsLoss): they take raw logits and apply the log-sum-exp trick internally, avoiding overflow and the log of a tiny probability. Softmax is pᵢ = e^{zᵢ}/Σⱼe^{zⱼ}; computed naively, logits of 1,000 make e^1000 overflow to infinity. Subtracting the largest logit first changes nothing (softmax is shift-invariant) and makes the largest exponent 0. Second, softmax has a temperature: dividing logits by T > 1 flattens the distribution and T < 1 sharpens it — the knob behind LLM sampling.

9. Activation and initialisation must be chosen together

Initial weights are random; their scale decides whether the signal keeps a steady size as it passes through layers. For a layer with n inputs and weights of variance σ², the output pre-activation has variance about n·σ²·E[x²]. For that to equal E[x²] (a steady scale), we would need σ² = 1/n. But a ReLU sets negative values to zero, which cuts the mean square of its output to half that of its input. To compensate, He et al. (2015) use σ² = 2/n for ReLU networks (He initialisation); Glorot & Bengio's Xavier initialisation, 2/(n_in + n_out), targets roughly symmetric activations like tanh. Measured effect: in a quick experiment with 10 ReLU layers of width 1,000 and unit-variance input, He initialisation kept the root-mean-square activation at 0.95, while the 1/n rule shrank it to 0.031 — exactly 2⁻⁵, because half the second moment is lost at each of ten layers. The signal (and the gradient, which sees the same halving in reverse) fades exponentially. dl3 develops this fully.

Why a piecewise-linear network is so expressive

A ReLU network computes a function that is linear on each of many polyhedral regions of input space; each hidden unit introduces a hyperplane where its input crosses zero, and the pattern of on/off units identifies the region. Composing layers folds space repeatedly, and Montúfar et al. (2014) showed that the number of distinct linear regions can grow exponentially with depth for a fixed number of units, while it grows only polynomially with width in a shallow network. That is the theoretical case for depth: a deep ReLU net can carve up its input space into astronomically many pieces with a modest number of parameters. Within a region the network is exactly linear, so its gradient is a product of the weight matrices and 0/1 masks — clean, and free of saturation. The cost is zero curvature almost everywhere and hard "corners" in the loss surface.

Activation sparsity and its use for inference

ReLU produces exact zeros; in ReLU-based LLMs (OPT, for example) a large majority of feed-forward neurons are inactive for any given token, and systems work exploits this by skipping the matmul columns for zeroed units ("contextual sparsity"). SwiGLU and GELU models are much less sparse in the strict sense, because their outputs are almost never exactly zero, which is a real trade-off: the quality advantage of gated units is paid for in lost inference sparsity. This is why some efficiency-focused work returns to ReLU or, as in Nemotron-4 15B, uses squared ReLU (max(0, z)², found by architecture search in the Primer paper), which is still exactly sparse but has a smooth, growing slope. When someone asks "why not ReLU in an LLM?", the two-sided answer is quality (gated units win in controlled tests) versus sparsity and simplicity (ReLU-family wins for cheap inference).

Gates, second-order structure and the bilinear layer

The bilinear layer (xW) ⊙ (xV) has no activation at all and still beats ReLU in Shazeer's Table 1 (1.960 versus 1.997 log-perplexity at 65,536 steps). That says much of the benefit is the multiplication: a product of two linear projections computes a quadratic form in x, i.e. pairwise feature interactions, which a single pointwise non-linearity applied to one projection expresses only indirectly. Adding an activation to the gate (SwiGLU, GeGLU) keeps the multiplicative structure and adds a saturating or bending element that keeps values bounded in the gate path, and those variants come out best. A second view: the gate is a per-token, per-unit soft mask, which is the same mechanism as the gates in LSTMs (dl7) and the routing weights of mixture-of-experts layers. Experts in modern mixture-of-experts models are themselves SwiGLU blocks.

Second derivatives, smoothness, and optimisation

ReLU's second derivative is zero except for a delta function at 0, so the Hessian of a ReLU network's loss has curvature contributions only from the layers' weights interacting, never from the activation itself. Smooth activations contribute their own curvature, which matters to methods that use second-order information and to analyses such as sharpness-aware minimisation, where non-smooth activations complicate the theory. For everyday first-order training the practical consequence is small; the more important effect is the non-zero gradient for small negative inputs, which prevents the permanent death of units.

Fusion: why elementwise cost is hidden

An activation is an elementwise operation: it reads a tensor from memory, applies a function, and writes it back. Its arithmetic is negligible next to the matmul that produced its input, but its memory traffic is not: a standalone GELU over a (tokens × 4d) tensor moves two full passes of data through GPU memory, which is slow relative to the compute (the roofline argument of gpu2). Frameworks therefore fuse the activation into the matmul's epilogue or into a single elementwise kernel with the gating multiply (torch.compile, Triton, or a hand-written SwiGLU kernel; gpu6). Fused, the erf or exp is free; unfused, GELU and SwiGLU are visibly slower than ReLU. The backward pass also needs the activation's input (or output) saved (dl1), which is one reason activation-function choice shows up in the memory arithmetic of gpu3: a SwiGLU block saves the gate branch, the up branch and their product, so its saved activations are larger than a ReLU block's, and selective recomputation (gpu9) often recomputes exactly these cheap elementwise tensors.

Outliers and quantisation

Large transformers develop a few hidden channels with activation magnitudes far larger than the rest; Dettmers et al. (LLM.int8(), 2022) found systematic outlier features emerging at around 6.7B parameters that break naive 8-bit activation quantisation, motivating mixed-precision decomposition and, in SmoothQuant, migrating the difficulty from activations to weights with a per-channel scale. The multiplicative gate of GLU-style FFNs is a natural place for extreme values because a large gate times a large up value gives a very large product. Understanding that the activation choice shapes the distribution of intermediate values, not just their function, is what connects this topic to quantisation (gpu4).

Softmax details that bite

Softmax is shift-invariant (adding a constant to every logit changes nothing) but not scale-invariant (multiplying logits by 2 sharpens the distribution). This is why the numerically stable form subtracts the maximum, and why temperature works. If logits grow large, softmax saturates: one probability approaches 1, the rest approach 0, and the Jacobian diag(p) − ppᵀ goes to zero, so gradients vanish — the same saturation as sigmoid. In attention, where softmax is applied to query-key dot products, unchecked growth of those logits causes exactly this; QK-normalisation and logit soft-capping (used in some LLMs, e.g. Gemma 2) bound the scores to prevent it. A related trick on the output side is label smoothing (dl8), which stops the network from driving logits to extremes.

Approximation mismatches

Because "GELU" names two computations (exact erf and tanh approximation) and "Swish" names a family (x·σ(βx), where SiLU is β = 1), a model can silently use a different variant in training and serving. The per-element difference between the two GELUs is under 5×10⁻⁴, tiny for one layer but not zero after dozens of layers, and it is exactly the kind of small discrepancy that makes a ported model score a fraction of a point lower or diverge from a reference in a layer-by-layer comparison. The remedy is to pin the variant in the configuration and test numerical equivalence on fixed inputs whenever weights cross frameworks.

The public case: Shazeer's controlled comparison and what followed

By 2019 the transformer's feed-forward block still used ReLU (as in the original 2017 paper and in T5) or GELU (BERT, GPT-2). In February 2020 Noam Shazeer (Google) published "GLU Variants Improve Transformer" (arXiv:2002.05202), a four-page test of one question: does replacing the first linear layer and activation of the FFN with a gated unit help? He used the T5 base model (12 encoder and 12 decoder layers, d_model = 768, FFN width 3,072), the C4 text corpus and the span-filling pre-training objective, and kept parameters and compute equal by shrinking the hidden width of gated variants to 2,048 (that is, 3,072 × 2/3). Pointwise activations barely differ (GELU and Swish are within noise of ReLU at 524k steps: 1.679 and 1.683 versus 1.677), so "smooth beats ReLU" is not supported here. Every gated variant beats every pointwise one — including the bilinear layer with no activation — and the best gap, 1.633 versus 1.677, is 0.044 log-perplexity. The paper measured run-to-run standard deviations of 0.003–0.010 (four repeats of the 65,536-step runs), so the gated improvement is many times the noise. Fine-tuning results on GLUE, SuperGLUE and SQuAD were noisier, but the gated variants scored best on most tasks. The paper's conclusion is the quote from section 7: no explanation, just results — which is honest, and the right register for an interview.

Adoption followed: PaLM (Google, 2022) used SwiGLU "because they have been shown to significantly increase quality compared to standard ReLU, GeLU, or Swish activations", noting that it needs three matrix multiplications instead of two; LLaMA (Meta, 2023) replaced ReLU with SwiGLU and set the hidden width to 2/3·4d; Llama 3 lists SwiGLU with FFN widths of 14,336, 28,672 and 53,248 for its 8B, 70B and 405B models; Gemma uses the GeGLU variant. Earlier work stayed with GELU: BERT states that it uses GELU "rather than the standard ReLU, following OpenAI GPT".

A port-mismatch story. When Gemma was released in February 2024, a community pull request to the Hugging Face transformers library, "Gemma bug fixes — Approx GELU, Layernorms, Sqrt(hd)" (#29402), listed differences between the library's implementation and the model as trained: the activation should be the tanh-approximate GELU (gelu_pytorch_tanh), not the exact erf form the config's "gelu" resolved to; the layer norm should be computed in float32; and the embedding scale sqrt(hidden size) needed care. The library's Gemma implementation was subsequently changed to use the approximate GELU through a separate hidden_activation setting. The lesson is not that anyone erred grossly; it is that a per-element difference of 10⁻⁴ in an activation is invisible in a unit test and visible as a small quality drop, so activation variants belong in the checkpoint's configuration and in a layer-by-layer equivalence test.

Monitoring activations in a real run

Cheap statistics per layer, logged every few hundred steps, catch most activation problems: the fraction of exactly-zero outputs (ReLU layers: roughly 50% at initialisation is normal; a climbing fraction or units silent on a whole batch means dead units), the mean and standard deviation of activations (steady across depth if initialisation is right; shrinking or exploding by layer means a scale problem), and for saturating activations the fraction of inputs with |z| > 4 (sigmoid/tanh gates in recurrent networks: a high fraction means the gate is stuck open or closed). PyTorch forward hooks (register_forward_hook) make this a dozen lines. Compare against the gradient-norm-per-layer plot (dl3): dead or saturated units show up as layers whose gradient norm is far below its neighbours'.

Failure modes and their symptoms

A large silent fraction of dead ReLUs after an aggressive first few steps: loss plateaus higher than a peer run, and the activation-zero fraction is high. Fix with warmup, lower peak learning rate, and check bias initialisation (a large positive constant bias can also start many units dead in a badly scaled layer).

NaN in softmax or a custom sigmoid gate at low precision: exp overflow. Float16 overflows at inputs above about 11 for exp (its maximum finite value is 65,504) and float32 above about 88; use the fused, max-subtracted implementations and keep activations that involve exponentials or reductions in float32 while matmuls run in bfloat16 (gpu4).

Slow steps caused by an unfused activation. A profile (gpu11) that shows many small elementwise kernels around each matmul — GELU, the gate multiply, dropout — means the activation is memory-bound and not fused; torch.compile or a fused SwiGLU kernel typically removes it.

Exploding activations in gated FFNs. The product of two unbounded branches can produce rare huge values, seen as loss spikes or as outlier channels that wreck int8 quantisation. Mitigations in practice are a smaller learning rate or longer warmup, weight decay, normalisation before the FFN (dl4), and outlier-aware quantisation.

A wrong output activation. Applying softmax and then a loss that itself applies softmax (double softmax) makes the model train slowly and plateau at poor accuracy — logits are compressed to (0, 1) before the loss sees them. The rule is one activation, applied where the loss expects it; use the fused with-logits losses and feed them raw scores.

Goal: tabulate GELU and SiLU by hand-written formulas, train the same small network with six different activations on data no linear model can fit, and watch dead ReLU units appear as the learning rate rises. NumPy only, Python 3.12; runs in under a minute. The network is 2 → 16 → 16 → 1 with hand-written backprop (exactly the loop of dl1) so you see how f′(z) enters the backward pass.

import numpy as np
from math import erf, sqrt

# ---- activation functions and their derivatives (all vectorised)
erf_v = np.vectorize(erf)
Phi = lambda z: 0.5 * (1 + erf_v(z / sqrt(2)))
phi = lambda z: np.exp(-z ** 2 / 2) / sqrt(2 * np.pi)
sig = lambda z: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))
ACTS = {
    "linear":  (lambda z: z,                lambda z: np.ones_like(z)),
    "sigmoid": (sig,                        lambda z: sig(z) * (1 - sig(z))),
    "tanh":    (np.tanh,                    lambda z: 1 - np.tanh(z) ** 2),
    "relu":    (lambda z: np.maximum(z, 0), lambda z: (z > 0).astype(float)),
    "gelu":    (lambda z: z * Phi(z),       lambda z: Phi(z) + z * phi(z)),
    "silu":    (lambda z: z * sig(z),       lambda z: sig(z) * (1 + z * (1 - sig(z)))),
}

print("z      gelu    silu   sigmoid'   tanh'")
for z in [-2.0, -0.5, 0.5, 2.0]:
    zz = np.array([z])
    print(f"{z:4}  {ACTS['gelu'][0](zz)[0]:7.3f} {ACTS['silu'][0](zz)[0]:7.3f} {ACTS['sigmoid'][1](zz)[0]:8.3f} {ACTS['tanh'][1](zz)[0]:8.3f}")

# ---- data: two concentric circles (not linearly separable)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 400
ang = rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi, n); rad = np.where(np.arange(n) < n // 2, 1.0, 2.0) + rng.normal(0, 0.1, n)
X = np.c_[rad * np.cos(ang), rad * np.sin(ang)]; y = (np.arange(n) >= n // 2).astype(float)[:, None]

def train(act, lr=0.1, steps=2000, width=16, seed=1):
    f, df = ACTS[act]
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    sizes = [2, width, width, 1]
    W = [r.normal(0, sqrt(2 / a), (a, b)) for a, b in zip(sizes, sizes[1:])]
    b = [np.zeros(k) for k in sizes[1:]]
    for _ in range(steps):
        zs, hs = [], [X]                               # forward: keep pre-activations and activations
        for i in range(3):
            zs.append(hs[-1] @ W[i] + b[i])
            hs.append(f(zs[-1]) if i < 2 else zs[-1])   # last layer is linear (logits)
        p = sig(zs[-1])
        d = (p - y) / n                                # dL/dlogits for sigmoid + binary cross-entropy
        for i in (2, 1, 0):                            # backward
            gW, gb = hs[i].T @ d, d.sum(0)
            if i > 0: d = (d @ W[i].T) * df(zs[i - 1])
            W[i] -= lr * gW; b[i] -= lr * gb
    acc = ((p > 0.5) == (y > 0.5)).mean()
    dead = np.mean([(hs[i + 1].max(0) <= 1e-12).mean() for i in range(2)]) if act == "relu" else float("nan")
    return acc, dead

print("\nactivation   accuracy on circles")
for name in ACTS: print(f"{name:9}    {train(name)[0]:.3f}")

print("\nReLU dead units vs learning rate (fraction of hidden units silent on ALL 400 examples)")
for lr in [0.05, 0.3, 1.0, 3.0]:
    acc, dead = train("relu", lr=lr, steps=300)
    print(f"lr={lr:<5} accuracy {acc:.3f}   dead {dead:.2f}")

# ---- parameter count: standard FFN vs SwiGLU with the 2/3 rule
d = 4096; print("\nstandard FFN 4d:", 2 * d * 4 * d, " SwiGLU 8/3 d:", 3 * d * round(8 * d / 3))

What to look for.

  1. The value table reproduces the numbers in the lesson: GELU(−2) = −0.046, SiLU(−2) = −0.238, sigmoid′(±2) = 0.105, tanh′(±0.5) = 0.786.
  2. The accuracy table. The two rings of points cannot be separated by a line, so linear stays near chance (about 0.53) however long it trains — a network with no non-linearity is one linear layer. Tanh, ReLU, GELU and SiLU all reach 1.000 in 2,000 full-batch steps. Sigmoid is clearly behind (about 0.74 in the reference run): its small, saturating slopes and non-zero-centred outputs make it the slowest learner. Set steps=20000 for sigmoid to see it eventually get there.
  3. The dead-unit table. After 300 steps, the fraction of hidden ReLU units that output exactly zero for all 400 examples is about 3–6% at learning rates 0.05–1 and about 41% at 3.0. The network still fits the training data — dead units are silent, not fatal — which is why you must measure them.
  4. The parameter count line confirms the 2/3 rule: the two-matrix FFN and the three-matrix SwiGLU at width 8d/3 hold 134.2 million weights each at d = 4,096.

Work it out

Evaluate ReLU, GELU and SiLU at z = −2, −0.5, 0.5, 2 (use Φ(−2) = 0.02275, Φ(−0.5) = 0.3085, Φ(0.5) = 0.6915, Φ(2) = 0.97725, and σ(−2) = 0.1192, σ(−0.5) = 0.3775, σ(0.5) = 0.6225, σ(2) = 0.8808). Then explain precisely how a ReLU unit dies and why a GELU unit cannot die in the same way.

How a ReLU dies. Its slope is exactly 0 for z < 0. If a large update pushes a unit's bias so negative that z < 0 for every example in the data, the unit outputs 0 and every gradient through it — with respect to its input weights and its own bias — is exactly 0. Gradient descent only moves parameters in response to gradients, so nothing can move it back: it is permanently dead, and with an oversized learning rate this can silently take out a large share of a layer.

Why GELU cannot die this way. Its slope is non-zero for moderate negative inputs (about −0.085 at z = −2 and −0.083 at z = −1), so a gradient still reaches a unit sitting in the negative region, and a suitable update can bring it back. Very negative inputs (below about −4) do give a near-zero slope, so GELU can become practically inactive; it just does not have the exact, permanent zero that ReLU does. The trade-off is cost: GELU needs an erf or tanh and SiLU an exp, which is invisible when fused into a matmul but real when it is not.

Size it up

A team wants to swap the ReLU FFN of a 32-layer model (d = 4,096, FFN width 16,384) for SwiGLU without changing the parameter count. What hidden width do they choose, and how many FFN parameters does the whole model hold before and after?

Answer

Standard FFN per layer: 2 × 4,096 × 16,384 = 134,217,728 weights. SwiGLU has three matrices, so 3 × 4,096 × h = 134,217,728 gives h = 10,922.7. Round to a multiple of 256 for tensor-core friendliness: h = 10,752 or 11,008 (LLaMA's choice; 3 × 4,096 × 11,008 = 135,266,304, +0.8%). Over 32 layers: 4.29 billion FFN weights before (134.2M × 32), 4.33 billion with h = 11,008. Compute per token follows the same count (each weight is one multiply-add, 2 FLOPs, in the forward pass), so the swap is neutral in parameters and FLOPs; what changes is that three smaller matmuls run instead of two larger ones, and an extra elementwise gate multiply that should be fused. If instead they keep h = 16,384, the FFN grows by 50% (201M weights per layer) — a legitimate choice, but then any quality gain is confounded with the extra parameters, which is what the 2/3 rule avoids.
