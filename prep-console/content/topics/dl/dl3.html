An engineer takes a network that works at 8 layers and, reasonably, tries 40. The training loss now sits stubbornly at its starting value. Printing the size of the weight changes shows a striking pattern: the last few layers are updating normally, and the first ten layers are moving by a millionth of what they should — or not at all. In another team, the same kind of change gives the opposite symptom: the loss falls nicely for two hundred steps and then leaps to NaN, with the weights suddenly enormous. Same architectural move, two opposite failures, and both come from the same one-line piece of arithmetic.

The arithmetic is the chain rule (dl1): the gradient that reaches the first layer is the gradient at the loss multiplied by one factor for every layer in between. Multiply forty numbers that are each slightly below 1 and the result is tiny; multiply forty numbers slightly above 1 and it is huge. Depth turns a small per-layer bias into an exponential one. For decades this limited neural networks to a handful of layers: it is why sigmoid networks were hard to train, why recurrent networks could not remember what happened fifty steps ago, and why "deeper" was for years a way of making models worse.

The story has a surprising twist that makes this topic a favourite of interviewers. The deep-network breakthroughs of 2010–2015 — variance-preserving initialisation, normalisation layers, and above all residual connections — are usually credited with "curing vanishing gradients". But the ResNet authors themselves, having trained plain 34-layer networks with normalisation in which they checked that gradients were healthy, still saw those networks train worse than 18-layer ones. The truth is more subtle than the folk story, and being able to say why marks the difference between knowing the slogan and understanding the mechanism.

This lesson starts from the smallest possible model, a chain of scalar multiplications with real numbers, and builds up to matrices, random initialisation and the variance arguments behind Xavier and He initialisation. We then measure a 30-layer network by hand-written backprop to see the effect directly, study each of the fixes (initialisation, normalisation, residual connections, gates, clipping) for what it changes and what it does not, and finish with how loss spikes are handled in real large-model training. The public case is the ResNet paper, checked against its own numbers.

The telephone game

Remember the game where a message is whispered down a line of people. If each person repeats only half of what they heard, the message from the far end is unrecognisable — nothing is left. If each person exaggerates by half, a small rumour becomes a grotesque story. A neural network's error signal travelling from the loss back to the first layer is that message, and each layer is one person in the line, scaling what it receives by some factor. Backprop multiplies those factors together, so what reaches the first layer is the original signal times the product of all the factors.

The numbers make it concrete. Thirty factors of 0.5 multiply to 0.5³⁰ ≈ 9×10⁻¹⁰: about a billionth. Thirty factors of 1.5 multiply to 1.5³⁰ ≈ 191,751: nearly two hundred thousand. Call the typical per-layer factor the gain. A gain of exactly 1 preserves the signal; anything else, applied at every layer, is exponential in depth. The chart shows how quickly it gets out of hand: over 100 layers, a gain of 0.8 loses ten orders of magnitude and a gain of 1.2 gains eight.

The analogy breaks in one useful place: the people in a real line are all the same, but a network's layers are matrices, which scale different directions by different amounts. A layer can shrink the signal along one direction and stretch it along another, and the product of many such layers stretches some directions enormously while crushing others. We will need that for the full picture, but the scalar case already contains the essential idea.

Two failures, two sets of symptoms

  • Vanishing gradients: the loss stalls early; gradient norms in early layers are orders of magnitude below those in late layers; early-layer weights barely change. The network is effectively a shallow network sitting on top of layers that stayed random.
  • Exploding gradients: the loss spikes or turns NaN/inf; a step is far too large and throws the weights somewhere terrible; the global gradient norm shoots up before the loss does.

The fixes, in one line each

  • Better activations (ReLU family) remove the shrinking slope factor (dl2).
  • Careful initialisation starts each layer's gain at about 1 (Xavier, He).
  • Normalisation layers re-centre and re-scale activations at every layer, so the scale cannot drift (dl4).
  • Residual (skip) connections: each block computes x + f(x), so the gradient always has a direct path.
  • Gates (LSTM, GRU) give recurrent networks the same kind of additive path across time (dl7).
  • Gradient clipping caps the size of any single gradient, protecting against explosion but not vanishing.

The residual trick in one line: the derivative of x + f(x) with respect to x is 1 + f′(x). Even if f′ is tiny, the 1 remains, so gradients reach early layers through the chain of "+x" paths undiminished. The rest of the lesson explains each fix well enough to say what it changes and where it stops helping.

2. From scalars to layers: Jacobians and singular values

Real layers map vectors to vectors, and the "factor" for a layer is its Jacobian J, the matrix of partial derivatives of the layer's outputs with respect to its inputs (dl1). The backward pass multiplies the gradient vector by one Jacobian per layer, and what matters at each step is how much that matrix stretches the vector's length. A matrix's stretching is described by its singular values: the largest is the most it can stretch any vector, the smallest the least. A worked case with a 2×2 layer W = [[0.6, 0.2], [−0.2, 0.6]] (a rotation combined with a shrink): both singular values equal √(0.6² + 0.2²) = 0.632. Push a gradient (1, 0) back through the layer repeatedly using Wᵀ: after one layer it is (0.6, 0.2) with length 0.632; after two, (0.32, 0.24), length 0.400; after three, (0.144, 0.208), length 0.253. The length is multiplied by 0.632 each time — after ten such layers, 0.01. The gradient is not merely rotating; it is vanishing, because every direction is shrunk.

For a general matrix the picture is richer: some directions grow while others shrink, and a product of many matrices can stretch a few directions to enormous size while crushing the rest. To have every direction preserved through depth, all singular values of the product must stay near 1 — the condition called dynamical isometry (deep section). For this lesson, the useful shortcut is the behaviour of random matrices, which is what an initialised network has.

Take a layer with n inputs whose weights are independent random numbers of variance σ². Feed it a vector of length 1; the output is n·(something) sums of random terms, and its expected squared length is n·σ², so its length is about σ√n. The gain of a random layer is σ√n, and it equals 1 when σ² = 1/n. An experiment confirms the prediction: 30 random 100×100 layers with σ√n = 0.8, 1.0 and 1.2 shrank a unit-length vector to 0.001, 0.56 and 134 (prediction 0.8³⁰ = 0.0012, 1.0³⁰ = 1, 1.2³⁰ = 237, close given the randomness). There is a critical scale, σ² = 1/n, and being 20% off it on either side is fatal at depth 30.

3. Forward signals and backward gradients follow the same rule

The same product structure governs the forward pass: the size of the activations at layer l is the input's size times the product of gains of the layers below. And the backward gradient is the loss gradient times the product of gains of the layers above (transposed). So a badly scaled initialisation damages both at once: too small, and activations shrink toward zero going up while gradients shrink toward zero going down; too large, and both explode. This is why the classic fixes are stated as "keep the variance of activations and of gradients constant across layers".

Glorot & Bengio (2010) worked out the conditions for a linear-ish layer with n_in inputs and n_out outputs. Forward variance stays constant if n_in·Var(w) = 1; backward variance stays constant if n_out·Var(w) = 1. When n_in ≠ n_out both cannot hold, so they compromise: Xavier (Glorot) initialisation, Var(w) = 2/(n_in + n_out). This assumes the activation is approximately the identity near zero with slope 1, as tanh is. For ReLU it is wrong, by a factor of two, for the reason from dl2: ReLU outputs zero for negative inputs, so the mean square of its output is half that of its input. Each layer therefore loses half the second moment, a gain of √½ ≈ 0.71 in length. He initialisation (He et al., 2015), Var(w) = 2/n_in, doubles the weight variance to compensate. With width n = 100, Xavier gives σ = 0.1 (1/√n), He gives σ = 0.141 (√(2/n)); with n = 1,000 the values are 0.032 and 0.045.

5. Fix 1: initialisation

  • Xavier/Glorot, Var(w) = 2/(n_in + n_out): for tanh, sigmoid, linear layers.
  • He/Kaiming, Var(w) = 2/n_in: for ReLU and its relatives.
  • Orthogonal: all singular values exactly 1, so lengths are preserved exactly; used for recurrent weights and in theory of very deep networks.
  • Biases to zero, usually, and for residual networks the last layer of each residual branch to zero or a small value, so every block starts as the identity.
  • Transformers: normal with standard deviation 0.02 for most weights, and the output projection of each residual branch scaled by about 1/√N, where N is the number of residual layers, as in GPT-2 — for 96 residual layers (48 blocks with two branches each), 1/√96 ≈ 0.10.

Initialisation only guarantees a good start. Once training moves the weights, the gains drift, and nothing keeps them in the right range. That is why normalisation exists.

6. Fix 2: normalisation layers

A normalisation layer (dl4 covers them properly) re-centres and re-scales its inputs so that they have a fixed mean and variance at every forward pass — for example, LayerNorm makes each token's vector have zero mean and unit variance across its features, and RMSNorm just divides by the root-mean-square. Whatever the previous layer's weights have drifted to, the scale entering the next layer is reset. Two things follow for gradients: the forward activations are kept in a healthy range (so saturation and blow-up are avoided), and the backward pass through a normaliser is automatically adjusted by the inverse of that scale, so gradient sizes do not depend on the weight scale of the layer beneath it. This is the reason BatchNorm allowed larger learning rates and made init less critical. A caution from the ResNet story below: normalisation alone did not fully solve depth.

7. Fix 3: residual connections

A residual block computes x_{l+1} = x_l + F_l(x_l), where F is a small stack of layers. Its Jacobian is I + J_F: the identity plus whatever F contributes. Backward, the gradient arriving at x_{l+1} splits: one copy goes straight through the "+" with gain exactly 1, the other goes through F and is multiplied by J_F. At x_l the two are added: ∂L/∂x_l = ∂L/∂x_{l+1} · (I + J_F). Unroll L blocks and the product (I + J_L)…(I + J_1) expands into a sum: the identity term (a path of gain 1 through every skip), plus each J_i alone (a path through one branch), plus pairs, triples, and so on — 2^L paths of many lengths.

Veit et al. (2016) read this expansion as an ensemble of paths of different lengths and measured how much of the gradient each path length carries. Their finding: in a 110-layer ResNet, most of the gradient comes from paths only 10–34 layers deep; the very long paths, which would suffer vanishing, contribute almost nothing. Deleting a single block from a trained ResNet at test time hurts modestly, whereas deleting one layer from a plain VGG-style network is catastrophic — so residual networks do not depend on every layer in series. Residual connections do not remove the product problem; they add short paths that bypass it.

Residual sums can still explode. A residual stream adds each branch's output: if the branches have output variance v and are roughly independent, the variance after L blocks grows like 1 + L·v — for v = 1 and L = 100 the standard deviation is ≈ 10, and the gradient similarly accumulates. The lab shows it: a 30-layer residual net with He-initialised branches had a first-layer gradient 8 million times that of the last layer. The fixes are to scale the branch initialisation by about 1/√L (variance L·(v/L) = v, so the total stays constant; the lab's scaled version gives 17), to zero-initialise each branch's final layer, or to add a learnable scalar per branch (ReZero, LayerScale), and to use normalisation in the branch (pre-norm) so the stream's scale does not feed back into the branch's input.

What the ResNet paper actually claimed. The paper asks a sharper question than "do gradients vanish?": if a shallow network can do a job, a deeper one could in principle copy it and make the extra layers identity, so it should never be worse on the training set. Empirically it was: on ImageNet the plain 34-layer network had higher training error than the plain 18-layer one, even though it used batch normalisation. The authors argue "this optimization difficulty is unlikely to be caused by vanishing gradients": with BN forward signals have non-zero variance, and they "verify that the backward propagated gradients exhibit healthy norms". They called it the degradation problem and proposed learning residuals F(x) = H(x) − x, which makes the identity (F = 0) trivially reachable. The gradient-flow view above (a clean gain-1 path) is one plausible reason it works, but the paper's own claim is about optimisation difficulty in general, and saying so precisely is worth points.

8. Fix 4: gates for sequences

A recurrent network applies the same weights at every time step, so a gradient travelling back T steps is multiplied by the recurrent Jacobian T times, and its size scales like the spectral radius (largest eigenvalue magnitude) of that matrix, times the activation slope, to the power T. A radius below 1 gives vanishing (the network cannot learn dependencies more than a few steps long); above 1, exploding. The LSTM (dl7) rewrites the state update as c_t = f_t ⊙ c_{t−1} + i_t ⊙ g_t: an additive update, gated by a forget gate f_t that the network can hold near 1. When f_t ≈ 1 the derivative of c_t with respect to c_{t−1} is about 1, the same identity path as a residual connection but through time. Truncated backpropagation through time, unrolling only the last k steps, limits how long the product can grow.

9. Exploding gradients: clipping

Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio (2013) described why recurrent networks explode with a picture: the loss surface has narrow high-slope "cliffs". Far from a cliff the gradient is modest; at its edge it becomes enormous, and a normal step multiplied by that enormous gradient throws the parameters far across the landscape, undoing progress. The remedy is gradient norm clipping: if the norm of the gradient exceeds a threshold, rescale the gradient to have exactly that norm, keeping its direction.

The mechanics, with numbers. Suppose the gradients of a model, concatenated across all parameters, have components (3, 4, 12). The global norm is √(9 + 16 + 144) = 13. With a threshold of 1.0 every gradient is multiplied by 1/13, giving (0.23, 0.31, 0.92), whose norm is 1 and whose direction is unchanged. If the norm had been 0.7, nothing would happen: the scale factor is min(1, threshold/norm). This is global-norm clipping and it is what torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_ does. Clipping each value separately (clip_grad_value_) distorts the direction (a gradient (0.1, 100) would become (0.1, 1), a different direction) and is rarely used.

scaler.scale(loss).backward()                 # mixed precision with fp16: scaled loss
scaler.unscale_(optimizer)                    # bring gradients back to true scale FIRST
gnorm = torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), max_norm=1.0)
scaler.step(optimizer); scaler.update()       # (with bf16 there is no scaler; just backward + clip + step)
log({"grad_norm": gnorm.item(), "clipped": gnorm.item() > 1.0})

The order matters: clipping scaled gradients would compare a scaled norm with an unscaled threshold. clip_grad_norm_ returns the pre-clip norm, which is the number to log: it is one of the best training-health signals there is. A typical threshold is 1.0 (LLaMA and PaLM both use global-norm clipping at 1.0); pick it from a healthy run's typical norm so that clipping fires rarely, and track the fraction of steps clipped — a rising fraction says training is drifting toward instability.

Dynamical isometry and orthogonal initialisation

The scalar-gain picture hides a matrix subtlety. Even when a random layer's average gain is exactly 1, its singular values are spread over a range (for a square Gaussian matrix, from near 0 up to about 2 times the average). A product of L such matrices has singular values spread over an exponentially wider range: a few directions are stretched enormously and most are crushed. The gradient's average length can be constant while a typical direction still vanishes. Saxe, McClelland and Ganguli (2013) named the desirable state dynamical isometry — all singular values of the network's input–output Jacobian concentrated near 1 — and Pennington, Schoenholz and Ganguli (2017) extended the analysis to non-linear networks. Orthogonal matrices have all singular values exactly 1, so a stack of them preserves lengths; with a suitably scaled activation the property can be kept through a non-linear network. Xiao et al. (2018) used a mean-field analysis and an orthogonal "delta" initialisation to train vanilla convolutional networks with 10,000 layers, without residual connections or normalisation, which demonstrates that depth alone was never the fundamental obstacle. The same line of work identifies an "edge of chaos", the boundary between the regime where perturbations die out (vanishing) and the one where they blow up (exploding), and shows that initialisation should sit at that edge. In practice residual connections and normalisation reach the same goal with far less fuss, but this theory explains why they work and is why orthogonal initialisation persists for recurrent weights.

Pre-norm versus post-norm, and very deep transformers

In the original transformer the layer norm sits after the residual addition (post-norm), so the normaliser is on the main path and every block's output passes through one. At initialisation this makes the gradient near the output layers large relative to the rest, which is why post-norm transformers need a careful learning-rate warmup and are fragile as depth grows. Moving the normaliser inside the branch (pre-norm: x + F(Norm(x))) leaves the residual stream a clean, unmodified identity path from the loss to every block, so gradients stay better behaved and warmup becomes less critical (Xiong et al., 2020). The price is that the stream's magnitude grows with depth (each block adds to it), so late-layer branches matter less, a known weakness of pre-norm for very deep models. DeepNorm (Wang et al., 2022) keeps post-norm but scales the skip path by a constant α > 1 and initialises the branch weights with a matching factor β < 1, both chosen from theory so the update size per layer is bounded; it allowed training transformers 1,000 layers deep. All of these are the same idea: keep the accumulated gain across depth near 1 by controlling the ratio between the identity path and the branches.

Adam changes which symptom hurts

With plain SGD, a parameter's step is proportional to its gradient, so a layer whose gradients are 10⁴ times smaller than another's moves 10⁴ times more slowly at the same learning rate — vanishing gradients translate directly into non-learning. Adam (dl5) divides each parameter's gradient by the running root-mean-square of its recent gradients, so the step is about η regardless of the gradient's absolute size, as long as the gradient stays well above the stabiliser ε (10⁻⁸ by default). Consequently, a well-scaled but low-magnitude gradient — early layers of a deep network — still produces normal-sized updates. Two caveats keep this from being a cure. If the forward signal has vanished, the early layers see almost no information from the input, and the normalised gradient is mostly noise. And ε is a floor: gradients around 10⁻²⁰, as in the sigmoid row of the lab, are ignored in either optimiser. This is also why adaptive optimisers made bad initialisations and un-normalised networks survivable in practice — and why the underlying causes still deserve fixing.

Loss spikes in large-model training

At LLM scale, the failure is usually not a permanent explosion but recurring spikes. PaLM's authors report that their largest model spiked about 20 times "despite the fact that gradient clipping was enabled", at irregular intervals, sometimes late in training, and never in the smaller models. They did not find a principled cure; the mitigation was to restart from a checkpoint about 100 steps before the spike and skip roughly 200–500 batches, after which the loss did not spike again at that point. Their ablation is the interesting part: training on the same batches from a different earlier checkpoint did not spike, so it was not "bad data" alone but the combination of specific batches and a particular parameter state. Wortsman et al. (2023) reproduced two well-known sources of instability at small scale: growth of attention logits (the query–key dot products become large so softmax saturates and its gradient vanishes or spikes) and divergence of the output logits from the log-probabilities. They showed that both appear at high learning rates and are cured by the same stabilisers used at scale: QK-layernorm (normalise queries and keys before the dot product) and an auxiliary z-loss on the softmax normaliser (PaLM used 10⁻⁴·log²Z). Other levers: a lower peak learning rate or longer warmup, a lower Adam β₂ (0.95 rather than 0.999 makes the second-moment estimate react faster after a distribution shift), and a bigger ε to avoid dividing by near-zero in rarely updated parameters.

Adaptive gradient clipping and scale invariance

Global-norm clipping uses one absolute threshold for the whole model, which is awkward if layers differ in scale or if the batch size changes. Adaptive gradient clipping (Brock et al., NFNets) clips a unit's gradient when the ratio of its gradient norm to its weight norm exceeds a threshold, so the allowed step is proportional to the weights being changed — the same idea as the update-to-weight ratio. It made possible training large-batch networks without batch normalisation. Relatedly, a layer followed by a normaliser is scale-invariant: multiplying its weights by c does not change the output, so the gradient scales as 1/c and the effective learning rate of a layer depends on its weight norm; weight decay and growing weight norms therefore interact with the gradient scale in ways that show up as slowly drifting gradient norms.

Precision limits

Vanishing has a numerical twin: a gradient smaller than the smallest representable number is exactly zero. In float16 the smallest normal number is about 6×10⁻⁵ (subnormals reach 6×10⁻⁸), so small gradients underflow even in networks that are fine in float32; loss scaling multiplies the loss so gradients sit in the representable range. bfloat16 has float32's exponent range and normally needs none (gpu4). Explosion has an overflow twin: float16 tops out at 65,504, so a gradient that is merely large in float32 becomes inf in float16. The mixed-precision scaler treats non-finite gradients as a signal to skip the step and reduce the scale, which is why a few skipped steps at the start of an fp16 run are normal and a continuing stream of them is not.

The public case: ResNet and the degradation problem

In 2015 Kaiming He, Xiangyu Zhang, Shaoqing Ren and Jian Sun at Microsoft Research wanted to know whether deeper image classifiers were simply better if they could be trained, and found they could not, quite. "Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition" (arXiv:1512.03385) opens with the observation that as networks get deeper, accuracy saturates and then degrades, and that "such degradation is not caused by overfitting": the deeper plain network has higher training error. Their fix was to let each block learn a residual on top of an identity shortcut. The numbers (all from the paper): on ImageNet, an 18-layer plain network had 27.94% top-1 validation error and a 34-layer plain network 28.54% — worse. The same two architectures with shortcuts (ResNet-18 and ResNet-34) reached 27.88% and 25.03%, and the deeper one now won. Going deeper still with bottleneck blocks, a single 152-layer ResNet reached 4.49% top-5 validation error, and an ensemble of residual nets achieved 3.57% top-5 error on the ImageNet test set, winning the ILSVRC 2015 classification task. The 152-layer network is 8× deeper than VGG-19 but has lower complexity: 11.3 billion FLOPs versus 19.6 billion.

On CIFAR-10 (small 32×32 images) they trained 20-, 32-, 44- and 56-layer plain networks and residual ones: the plain networks got worse with depth, the residual ones better. A 110-layer ResNet with 1.7 million parameters reached 6.43% test error (best of five runs; mean 6.61%), and a 1,202-layer network with 19.4 million parameters trained "with no optimization difficulty" to a training error below 0.1% but a test error of 7.93%, worse than the 110-layer model, which the authors attribute to overfitting: even that record-setting depth was not free. One footnote is a nice illustration of this lesson: the 110-layer network needed a warm-up phase at learning rate 0.01, because an initial 0.1 was "slightly too large to start converging".

The section most relevant to this topic is what they say about vanishing gradients: they note that vanishing/exploding gradients had been "largely addressed by normalized initialization and intermediate normalization layers", tested the plain nets with batch normalisation, verified healthy forward and backward norms, and concluded the optimisation difficulty is "unlikely to be caused by vanishing gradients". For an interview, this is the key nuance: residual connections made very deep networks trainable, the mechanism is a better-conditioned optimisation problem with a clean gain-1 path, and the paper itself does not claim it is about vanishing gradients in the narrow sense.

Loss spikes in production LLM runs

PaLM's account (Chowdhery et al., 2022, section 5.1) is the best-documented public example of gradient instability at scale: about 20 spikes in the largest model despite global-norm clipping at 1.0 and a z-loss, handled by rewinding roughly 100 steps and skipping 200–500 batches. The operational lessons: keep checkpoints frequent enough to rewind (the cost of a rewind is the time since the last checkpoint plus the data skipped); log the batch identifiers seen at each step so a spike can be replayed; and automate the skip-and-restart procedure since large runs run for weeks and spikes at 3 a.m. should not need a human.

What to monitor and how

At minimum, log every step: the training loss, the pre-clip global gradient norm, the fraction of steps where clipping engaged, the learning rate, and (for fp16) the loss-scale value and the count of skipped steps. Every few hundred steps, log per-layer gradient norms, per-layer weight norms and the update-to-weight ratio lr × ‖grad‖ / ‖weight‖, typically about 10⁻³ in a healthy run: a layer at 10⁻⁶ is barely learning; one at 10⁻¹ is unstable. Plot them as a heat map (layer on one axis, step on the other) to see drift and spikes at a glance. A gradient-norm spike that precedes a loss spike by a few steps is the classic signature and can trigger an automatic alert or a batch skip.

def layer_stats(model):
    for name, p in model.named_parameters():
        if p.grad is not None:
            ratio = (p.grad.norm() * lr) / (p.norm() + 1e-12)
            yield name, p.grad.norm().item(), ratio.item()

Failure modes in practice

NaN from one bad batch. A single example with an extreme value, an empty target sequence (division by zero in a mean) or a corrupted image can produce a non-finite gradient. Use clip_grad_norm_(..., error_if_nonfinite=True) or check torch.isfinite(gnorm) and skip the optimiser step when it fails; a run that silently applies a NaN update is unrecoverable, while a skipped step costs nothing. Log the batch identifiers of skipped steps.

Clipping that hides a problem. If the pre-clip norm is above the threshold on most steps, clipping has become part of the optimiser, effectively rescaling the learning rate: the true diagnosis is that the threshold is too low or the model is unstable. Track the clipped fraction and treat a sustained value above a few percent as a warning.

Warm restarts that forget optimiser state. Resuming from a checkpoint without Adam's moments makes the first steps huge (the second-moment estimate is empty), which looks exactly like a gradient explosion at the resume step. Restore optimiser state and the step counter (dl1, dl5).

Depth added without re-tuning. Adding layers to a working residual model changes the accumulated branch variance; the learning rate that worked at 12 layers may spike at 48. Scale branch initialisation with depth (the 1/√N rule) and re-run a short learning-rate sweep rather than assuming transfer.

Goal: measure vanishing and exploding gradients directly. The script builds a plain 30-layer, width-100 network with hand-written forward and backward passes (the same rules as dl1), records how large the gradient is when it reaches each layer, and compares initialisations, activations and residual connections; it then demonstrates global-norm clipping. NumPy only, Python 3.12, runs in a few seconds.

import numpy as np

DEPTH, WIDTH, BATCH = 30, 100, 64
ACT = {
    "sigmoid": (lambda z: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z)), lambda z, a: a * (1 - a)),
    "tanh":    (np.tanh,                        lambda z, a: 1 - a ** 2),
    "relu":    (lambda z: np.maximum(z, 0),     lambda z, a: (z > 0).astype(float)),
}

def run(act, std, residual=False, seed=0):
    """Forward + backward through DEPTH layers.
    Returns (backward signal size at each layer, weight-gradient norm of each layer), input side first."""
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    f, df = ACT[act]
    Ws = [r.normal(0, std, (WIDTH, WIDTH)) for _ in range(DEPTH)]
    h, cache = r.normal(size=(BATCH, WIDTH)), []
    for W in Ws:                                     # forward: keep inputs, pre-activations, outputs
        z = h @ W; a = f(z)
        cache.append((h, z, a))
        h = h + a if residual else a
    d = np.ones_like(h) / h.size                     # dL/dh at the top (loss = mean of the output)
    sig, wg = [], []
    for W, (hin, z, a) in zip(reversed(Ws), reversed(cache)):
        dz = d * df(z, a)
        wg.append(np.linalg.norm(hin.T @ dz))        # weight gradient: (layer input)ᵀ · dz
        d = dz @ W.T + (d if residual else 0)        # gradient handed to the layer below (+ skip path)
        sig.append(np.linalg.norm(d))
    return sig[::-1], wg[::-1]

print("depth 30, width 100.  'signal' = size of the gradient reaching a layer's input, relative to the top layer")
print(f"{'setup':34} {'signal @L1':>11} {'signal @L15':>12} {'weight-grad @L1':>16}")
setups = [("sigmoid, N(0, 0.1)", "sigmoid", 0.1, False),
          ("tanh, std 0.05 (too small)", "tanh", 0.05, False),
          ("tanh, Xavier 1/sqrt(n)", "tanh", 1 / np.sqrt(WIDTH), False),
          ("tanh, std 0.25 (too big)", "tanh", 0.25, False),
          ("relu, 1/sqrt(n) (too small)", "relu", 1 / np.sqrt(WIDTH), False),
          ("relu, He sqrt(2/n)", "relu", np.sqrt(2 / WIDTH), False),
          ("relu, 2/sqrt(n) (too big)", "relu", 2 / np.sqrt(WIDTH), False),
          ("relu, He + residual (unscaled)", "relu", np.sqrt(2 / WIDTH), True),
          ("relu, He/sqrt(L) + residual", "relu", np.sqrt(2 / WIDTH / DEPTH), True)]
for label, act, std, res in setups:
    sig, wg = run(act, std, res)
    top = sig[-1]
    print(f"{label:34} {sig[0]/top:11.1e} {sig[14]/top:12.1e} {wg[0]:16.1e}")

# ---- gradient clipping by global norm: direction is kept, length is capped
def clip(grads, max_norm=1.0):
    total = np.sqrt(sum((g ** 2).sum() for g in grads))
    return [g * min(1.0, max_norm / (total + 1e-6)) for g in grads], total
g = [np.array([3.0, 4.0]), np.array([12.0])]            # global norm = sqrt(9 + 16 + 144) = 13
clipped, total = clip(g)
print("\nglobal norm before clipping:", round(float(total), 2),
      " after:", round(float(np.sqrt(sum((c ** 2).sum() for c in clipped))), 3))
print("clipped grads:", [c.round(3).tolist() for c in clipped])

What to look for.

  1. The table's "signal @L1" column is the size of the gradient reaching layer 1 relative to the top layer. Sigmoid with a modest init loses about 19 orders of magnitude; tanh with σ = 0.05 loses 9 (0.5³⁰ ≈ 9×10⁻¹⁰, exactly the per-layer gain of 0.5 the theory predicts); tanh with Xavier σ = 1/√n stays near 0.1; a too-large tanh init grows by about a thousand.
  2. ReLU rows. He initialisation (σ = √(2/n)) keeps the ratio within a factor of a few of 1; the "Xavier-like" 1/√n loses a factor of about 4,000 and 2/√n gains about 100,000. The difference between the last two is a factor of two in the standard deviation of the weights.
  3. Weight-gradient column. For the too-small tanh network the first layer's weight gradient is about 10⁻¹⁰ in absolute terms: it is the product of a tiny backward signal and a tiny layer input (activations shrink too, going up), which is also why comparing weight-gradient norms across layers can look deceptively flat.
  4. Residual rows. Adding skip connections without scaling the branches does not help: the gradient at layer 1 is 8 million times that at layer 30. Scaling the branch weights by 1/√L (30) brings it back to about 17. Try replacing 1/√L with 1/L and watch what happens to the ratio.
  5. The clipping demo prints a global norm of 13.0 before clipping and 1.0 after, with gradients (3, 4, 12) → (0.231, 0.308, 0.923) — direction preserved, as in the lesson.

Try next. Sweep the width (50, 100, 400) at a fixed σ: the critical σ scales like 1/√n, so what is stable at one width explodes at another. Add batch normalisation to each layer's output and see how much less the ratio depends on σ.

Work it out

A 50-layer network where every layer's Jacobian has typical gain s. Compute the gradient scale reaching layer 1 for s = 0.8 and s = 1.2, then say what a residual connection changes.

Answer

The gradient at layer 1 passes through 49 Jacobians, so it is scaled by s⁴⁹.

s = 0.8: 0.8⁴⁹ ≈ 1.8 × 10⁻⁵. Layer 1 receives a gradient about 55,000 times smaller than layer 50, so with a shared learning rate it effectively never trains.

s = 1.2: 1.2⁴⁹ ≈ 7.6 × 10³. The gradient is thousands of times larger at layer 1; with a learning rate that suits the last layers, the first ones take enormous steps, and in float16 (maximum 65,504) intermediate values can overflow to infinity within a few steps.

The survivable band is narrow: a 20% deviation from 1.0 in either direction is fatal at 50 layers, because depth turns a small multiplicative bias into an exponential one.

What residuals change: with x + f(x), each layer's Jacobian becomes I + J_f. The identity term guarantees a path on which the product stays at 1 no matter how small J_f is, so gradients can flow straight to the input. If the branches are small (say J_f has norm 0.01) the total gain over 50 blocks is about 1.01⁵⁰ ≈ 1.6: harmless. The caution: if the branches are not small, their contributions add up and can explode (1.2⁴⁹ again), which is why residual branches are initialised small or zero and why pre-norm and depth-dependent scaling exist.

Clipping does not help here. It caps explosion; it cannot lift a vanished gradient. A different problem needs a different fix.

Diagnose it

You log the global gradient norm every step for a 24-layer transformer. It sits around 0.4, then over 30 steps climbs to 3 with the loss still falling, and at step 8,200 the norm jumps to 60 and the loss to 12 before slowly recovering. Your clip threshold is 1.0. What is likely happening, and what do you do?

Answer

Reading the log. The slow climb from 0.4 to 3 suggests a growing instability (growing weights or attention logits) rather than a one-off. The jump to 60 is a spike: at that step every gradient was scaled by 1/60, so the update was safely small in size but was still in the direction of one enormous gradient, which is why the loss went to 12. Clipping bounded the step's length; it did not remove the cause, and once the norm exceeded 1.0 for many steps the clipped fraction had already been high (the whole "0.4 to 3" period), meaning the optimiser was being throttled.

What to do, in order: (1) find the batch at step 8,200 — replay it from a checkpoint before the spike; if it spikes only from that parameter state, it is the combination effect seen in PaLM. (2) Check the trend in per-layer norms and attention-logit magnitudes to see which layer grows. (3) Apply the stabilisers: QK-normalisation, a z-loss on the output logits, a lower peak learning rate or longer warmup, a slightly lower Adam β₂, bf16 if the run is fp16. (4) Operationally: rewind to a checkpoint before the climb began and skip the offending batches, as PaLM did. (5) Re-tune the clip threshold using the healthy 0.4 as the typical scale, e.g. 1.0 is fine but 0.5 would have engaged earlier during the climb.

The habit to state in an interview: alert on the pre-clip gradient norm's trend, not only on the loss.
