In 2014 Google's best image classifier, Inception, took about 31 million training steps to reach 72.2% top-1 accuracy on ImageNet. Its authors could not simply turn up the learning rate to speed things up: a five-fold increase made the model's parameters "reach machine infinity" — training exploded. They could not use sigmoid activations either; the network never got better than one-in-a-thousand chance accuracy. Everything about that network was delicate: the initial weights, the learning rate, the choice of activation. Then Sergey Ioffe and Christian Szegedy added one small operation between layers, and the same network matched its old accuracy in 2.1 million steps with a five-times-higher learning rate — fourteen times fewer steps — and even trained with sigmoids.

The operation is normalisation: after a layer computes its outputs, subtract their mean and divide by their standard deviation, so the next layer always receives numbers on a standard scale, then let the network learn its preferred scale and shift. It sounds too simple to matter. But recall dl3: the gradient reaching the early layers is a product of one factor per layer, and each factor depends on the scale of that layer's weights and activations. A normalisation layer resets the scale at every depth, so the product cannot drift away from 1 however the weights below it change. That single change removed much of the fragility of deep networks, and it is now in almost every architecture you will meet.

The subtlety — and the reason this is a favourite interview topic — is that there are several versions and they are not interchangeable. BatchNorm, the original, normalises across the examples in a batch; it works wonderfully for image networks but has a training mode and an evaluation mode, fails with tiny batches, and is awkward with variable-length text. Transformers use LayerNorm or, in modern large language models, RMSNorm, which normalise across the features of each example on its own. And where you put the normalisation inside a transformer block (before or after the sub-layer) decides whether a deep model trains stably. Getting these details right is what separates "I've heard of BatchNorm" from being able to debug a model that behaves differently in evaluation than in training.

This lesson builds all of it from the ground up. We start from why standardising inputs helped in the first place (ml4) and extend it to hidden layers, then work every variant by hand on a 2×3 tensor with real numbers so the axis difference is unmistakable. We cover BatchNorm's running statistics and the train/eval trap, derive the gradient of a normalisation layer and what it implies, discuss why normalisation helps (with an honest account of the debate), compare LayerNorm and RMSNorm, examine GroupNorm and the small-batch failure, and place the norm inside a transformer block. The public case is the original BatchNorm paper, whose numbers we check against the source, and the lab shows a 30-layer network becoming insensitive to its initialisation.

Grading on a curve — two ways

Imagine a school where 200 students each take the same exam of 10 questions, and you want to put everyone's scores on a common scale. There are two natural ways to "grade on a curve". You can standardise each question across all the students: for question 3, compute the average score and the spread over the 200 students, and convert every student's score on that question to "how many spreads above or below the average". Or you can standardise each student across all their questions: for student 17, compute the average and spread over their 10 answers and convert each to a standardised score.

These two are BatchNorm and LayerNorm. Picture a table of activations in which each row is one example in the batch (a student) and each column is one feature (a question). BatchNorm computes a mean and variance down each column, pooling over the batch. LayerNorm computes them along each row, pooling over the features of a single example. RMSNorm is LayerNorm that skips the subtraction of the mean and only divides by a measure of size. The analogy holds well; where it needs care is that in a real network the "questions" are learned features whose meaning changes as training proceeds, which is why the layer keeps adapting.

The recipe

Every variant applies the same two steps to the numbers in one pooled set:

normalised = (x − mean) / √(variance + ε)        step 1: standardise (ε ≈ 1e-5 avoids dividing by zero)
output     = γ × normalised + β                   step 2: learned scale γ and shift β, one pair per feature

Step 2 matters. If the layer only standardised, every layer's output would be forced to mean 0 and variance 1, which would restrict what the next layer sees. γ (gain) and β (bias) are learned like any weights, so the network can restore any scale and offset it prefers — including exactly undoing the standardisation. What normalisation adds is that the output scale is now controlled by two dedicated numbers instead of by every weight in every layer below.

A first worked example

One example's features are (2, 4, 6). LayerNorm: mean = 4; the squared deviations are 4, 0, 4, so variance = 8/3 ≈ 2.67 and standard deviation ≈ 1.63; the normalised values are (2−4)/1.63, 0, (6−4)/1.63 = (−1.22, 0, 1.22). RMSNorm skips the mean: the root-mean-square is √((4 + 16 + 36)/3) = √18.67 ≈ 4.32, giving (2, 4, 6)/4.32 = (0.46, 0.93, 1.39). Same idea, slightly different result: LayerNorm centres the numbers on zero, RMSNorm only rescales them.

Which to use, briefly

Convolutional networks for images: BatchNorm (or GroupNorm when batches are small). Transformers and recurrent networks: LayerNorm or RMSNorm, because they do not depend on other examples in the batch. Modern LLMs: RMSNorm, applied before each sub-layer. The rest of the lesson explains why, and the traps that come with each.

1. Why normalise at all

Recall from ml4 that gradient descent on a loss whose features have wildly different scales converges slowly: the loss surface is a long narrow valley, the stable learning rate is set by the steep direction, and progress along the shallow one crawls. Centring and scaling the input features fixes this, which is why we standardise data before training. Inside a deep network the same problem reappears at every layer: the input to layer 7 is the output of layer 6, and its scale and offset depend on layer 6's weights, which keep changing. If layer 6's outputs drift to values around 100 (or 0.001), layer 7 sees a differently scaled problem, a saturating activation may be pushed into its flat region, and ReLU units may all switch off. Initialisation (dl3) sets the scales right at step 0 but cannot keep them right afterwards. Normalisation is the mechanism that maintains the scale throughout training: standardise each layer's inputs inside the network, on every forward pass.

2. BatchNorm, step by step

Take a mini-batch of m examples and one feature (one column of the table). BatchNorm (Ioffe & Szegedy, 2015) computes, for that feature:

  1. the batch mean μ = (1/m) Σ xᵢ;
  2. the batch variance σ² = (1/m) Σ (xᵢ − μ)²;
  3. the normalised values x̂ᵢ = (xᵢ − μ)/√(σ² + ε);
  4. the output yᵢ = γ x̂ᵢ + β, where γ and β are learned parameters for this feature.

In a convolutional layer the "feature" is a channel, and the statistics pool over the batch and all spatial positions (m × H × W values per channel), with one γ and β per channel. Worked on a single column: the four values (2, 4, 6, 8) have mean 5 and variance ((−3)² + (−1)² + 1² + 3²)/4 = 5, so σ = 2.236 and the normalised values are (−1.342, −0.447, 0.447, 1.342): mean 0, variance 1. With γ = 1 and β = 0 that is the output; with γ = 2 and β = 1 it would be (−1.68, 0.11, 1.89, 3.68).

The full 2×3 example. Now the smallest table that shows both axes: two examples, three features, X = [[2, 4, 6], [0, 3, 9]].

row 1row 2how it is computed
BatchNorm (per column)(1, 1, −1)(−1, −1, 1)column means (1, 3.5, 7.5), column variances (1, 0.25, 2.25)
LayerNorm (per row)(−1.225, 0, 1.225)(−1.069, −0.267, 1.336)row means (4, 4), row variances (2.67, 14)
RMSNorm (per row)(0.463, 0.926, 1.389)(0, 0.548, 1.643)row RMS (4.32, 5.48)

Look at what each does. LayerNorm's output for row 1 depends only on row 1: change the other example and nothing happens. BatchNorm's output for row 1 is (1, 1, −1) only because row 2 exists, and with a batch of just two examples every output is exactly ±1 — the layer can say only "larger or smaller than my batch-mate" for each feature, throwing away magnitude. That is an extreme case, but it explains the general behaviour: BatchNorm's output for an example depends on its batch-mates, and the estimate of the statistics gets noisier as the batch shrinks (section 8). RMSNorm preserves the sign pattern of its input (row 2 stays non-negative, since the inputs were), while LayerNorm forces zero mean. The lab prints all three so you can check them.

3. BatchNorm at inference: running statistics and the train/eval trap

At inference you may have one example, or a batch of unrelated requests, and you want each prediction to be deterministic and independent of the others. Batch statistics are then wrong for the job. BatchNorm therefore keeps running averages of the mean and variance during training, updated every step with a momentum of (by default in PyTorch) 0.1: running = 0.9 × running + 0.1 × batch_statistic. Starting from running mean 0 and variance 1, after one batch (2, 4, 6, 8) the running mean is 0.9 × 0 + 0.1 × 5 = 0.5 and the running variance is 0.9 × 1 + 0.1 × 6.67 = 1.57. (PyTorch stores the unbiased variance, dividing by m − 1 = 3, i.e. 6.67, in the running estimate, but uses the biased m-divisor for the normalisation itself.) After enough batches the running estimates converge to the population mean and variance. In evaluation mode the layer uses these stored numbers as constants, so the whole layer becomes a fixed per-channel affine map: y = γ (x − running_mean) / √(running_var + ε) + β.

This design produces a set of classic bugs, each worth being able to explain:

  • Forgetting model.eval() before validation or inference: BatchNorm keeps using (and updating) batch statistics, so predictions depend on batch composition and are noisier; a model that trained well appears to have poor accuracy.
  • Batch size 1 in training mode: the batch mean equals the single value, the variance is 0, so x − μ = 0 and the output is exactly β for every input. All information is destroyed. (In eval mode, batch size 1 is perfectly fine.)
  • Domain shift: running statistics reflect the training data. If serving data looks different (new camera, different resolution), they are wrong; re-estimating them on target data (AdaBN) is a cheap fix.
  • Small-batch fine-tuning: fine-tuning a pretrained model with a batch of 2–4 makes the running statistics drift to noisy estimates within a few dozen steps (0.9ⁿ decays quickly), so evaluation, which uses them, degrades. Standard remedy: freeze BatchNorm (keep it in eval mode) during fine-tuning of detection and segmentation models.
  • Data-parallel training: each GPU sees only its own slice of the batch, so statistics are per-GPU. SyncBatchNorm computes them across all GPUs at the price of an all-reduce per layer (gpu8).

4. What normalisation does to gradients

Since normalisation is inside the network, backprop (dl1) must go through it. Write the layer for one set of activations as x̂ = (x − μ)/σ, y = γ x̂ + β. The gradient with respect to the input, for upstream gradient dy and taking γ = 1 for clarity, is:

dx = (1/σ) · ( dy − mean(dy) − x̂ · mean(dy ⊙ x̂) )

Read it in words. The gradient is divided by σ, so if the input is large the gradient is correspondingly small (that is the automatic scale adjustment: gradients no longer depend on how large the activations below happen to be). And two terms are subtracted: mean(dy) removes the component of the gradient along the constant direction (adding a constant to every input changes nothing, since the mean is subtracted), and x̂ · mean(dy ⊙ x̂) removes the component along x̂ itself (multiplying the input by a constant changes nothing, since the variance is divided out). Worked with LayerNorm on (2, 4, 6): x̂ = (−1.225, 0, 1.225), σ = 1.633. For upstream gradient dy = (1, 0, 0): mean(dy) = 0.333; mean(dy ⊙ x̂) = −1.225/3 = −0.408; then dy − 0.333 − x̂ · (−0.408) = (0.667, −0.333, −0.333) − (0.5, 0, −0.5) = (0.167, −0.333, 0.167), and dividing by σ gives (0.102, −0.204, 0.102). Note that it sums to zero. Now try dy = −x̂ = (1.225, 0, −1.225), a gradient that would just make the input larger along its own direction: the formula returns exactly (0, 0, 0). Scaling the input has no effect on the output, so the gradient correctly has no such component. (The lab verifies both numbers with finite differences.)

This has a very useful consequence for training. A normalised layer is scale-invariant: multiplying the weights of the layer below by any positive constant c does not change the output. Two things follow (deep section): the gradient with respect to those weights scales as 1/c and is orthogonal to the weights, so weights tend to grow during training and the effective learning rate (step size relative to weight size, roughly η/‖w‖²) shrinks automatically. That built-in learning-rate adjustment is one reason normalised networks tolerate large learning rates.

5. Why does it help? An honest account

Ioffe and Szegedy motivated BatchNorm by internal covariate shift: as earlier layers change, the distribution of each layer's inputs changes, so each layer has to keep re-adapting; fixing the mean and variance of each layer's inputs should remove this. This is intuitive and was the accepted story for three years. Santurkar et al. (2018) tested it. They trained networks with BatchNorm and then added random noise (with non-zero mean and non-unit variance, different at every step) after each BatchNorm layer, deliberately introducing severe covariate shift; training was barely worse than plain BatchNorm and far better than no BatchNorm. They concluded that "such distributional stability of layer inputs has little to do with the success of BatchNorm" and measured instead that BatchNorm "makes the optimization landscape significantly smoother": the loss and its gradient change less between nearby points, so gradients are more predictive and larger steps are safe. Three accounts are now usually given together:

  • Smoother loss landscape (Santurkar et al.): the loss is better behaved, allowing higher learning rates and faster convergence.
  • Scale invariance and self-tuning learning rate: weights below a normalised layer can grow without changing the function, and their gradients shrink as they do, so the relative step size adapts on its own.
  • Regularisation from batch noise: each example's normalisation depends on which other examples share its batch, which injects noise similar in spirit to dropout; Ioffe and Szegedy found that BatchNorm sometimes made dropout unnecessary.

The honest interview answer is that the exact mechanism is still debated, that the original internal-covariate-shift explanation has been seriously challenged, and that the empirical benefits (higher learning rates, less sensitivity to initialisation, faster and more stable training) are not in doubt.

6. LayerNorm: the batch-independent version

Ba, Kiros and Hinton (2016) introduced LayerNorm for recurrent networks, where BatchNorm is awkward: statistics would have to be kept per time step, and sequences vary in length. LayerNorm normalises over the features of a single example, so for a transformer token vector x of width d:

μ = (1/d) Σⱼ xⱼ          σ² = (1/d) Σⱼ (xⱼ − μ)²          y = γ ⊙ (x − μ)/√(σ² + ε) + β

with γ and β vectors of length d (2d parameters). In a transformer the activations have shape (batch, tokens, d) and LayerNorm computes one mean and variance for each (batch, token) pair, over the d features. Nothing is shared between tokens or examples. That gives it all the properties transformers need:

Geometrically, LayerNorm does two projections: it subtracts the mean (projecting the vector onto the hyperplane of zero-sum vectors), then divides by the length (projecting onto a sphere of radius √d), then applies γ and β. Every token vector entering the next layer therefore has the same norm (before γ), which is why attention and feed-forward layers downstream see a stable scale.

7. RMSNorm: drop the mean

Zhang and Sennrich (2019) hypothesised that of LayerNorm's two invariances, re-centring (shift invariance) and re-scaling (scale invariance), only the second matters. RMSNorm removes re-centring:

rms = √( (1/d) Σⱼ xⱼ² + ε )          y = γ ⊙ x / rms          (no mean subtraction, no bias β)

One reduction (a sum of squares) instead of two, no subtraction, and half the parameters (d instead of 2d). Their reported result: comparable quality to LayerNorm with 7–64% less running time depending on the model. On our example, row 1 becomes (0.463, 0.926, 1.389) instead of LayerNorm's (−1.225, 0, 1.225): the direction of the vector is preserved and only its length is set. RMSNorm is the norm in LLaMA (which also uses it in pre-norm position), Gemma and OLMo 2, usually computed in float32 even when the model runs in bfloat16 because the sum of squares of many values loses precision otherwise. In FLOPs the saving is negligible next to the matmuls (LLaMA-7B has only 65 RMSNorm weight vectors — 2 per layer for 32 layers plus one final — about 266,000 parameters in total, 0.004% of the model), but norm layers are memory-bandwidth-bound and run at every layer for every token, so removing a pass over the data and fusing what remains into one kernel is a measurable speed-up (gpu6).

8. GroupNorm and the small-batch failure

BatchNorm's Achilles heel is small batches. Object detection and segmentation models process high-resolution images and often fit only 1–2 images per GPU. With so few samples the batch mean and variance are noisy estimates, and the noise hurts. Wu and He (2018) measured it on ResNet-50 for ImageNet classification: BatchNorm's validation error was 23.6% at batch size 32 but 24.8% at 8, 27.3% at 4 and 34.7% at 2. Their alternative, GroupNorm, divides the channels of a single example into G groups (G = 32 by default) and normalises over each group and all spatial positions, so it never uses other examples: its error was 24.1%, 24.2%, 24.0%, 24.2% and 24.1% at batch sizes 32, 16, 8, 4 and 2 — flat. At the regular batch size of 32 it was 0.5 points worse than BatchNorm, at batch size 2 it was 10.6 points better. With G = 1 GroupNorm is LayerNorm over channels and space (the paper measured 25.3%); with G = number of channels it is InstanceNorm (28.4% in their test), which normalises each channel of each image separately and is favoured for style transfer, where an image's contrast should not affect its style.

9. Where the norm goes in a transformer block

A transformer block has two sub-layers (attention, then a feed-forward network) each wrapped in a residual connection. The original 2017 transformer applied LayerNorm after the addition (post-norm); almost all current models apply it before the sub-layer, inside the branch (pre-norm).

Xiong et al. (2020) proved with a mean-field analysis that at initialisation, for post-norm, "the expected gradients of the parameters near the output layer are large", so a large learning rate is unstable and warmup is needed, whereas for pre-norm "the gradients are well-behaved at initialization", and they trained pre-norm transformers without the warm-up stage with comparable results. Two refinements matter in current models. First, pre-norm needs a final norm after the last block (before the output projection), because the residual stream's scale has grown through depth. Second, the extra stability of putting norms after sub-layers has been revived: Gemma 2 applies RMSNorm to "the input and output of each transformer sub-layer", and OLMo 2 uses "reordered norm", h = x + RMSNorm(Attention(x)), together with QK-norm (RMSNorm on the queries and keys before their dot product, "to avoid attention logits being too large, which can lead to training loss divergence") and a z-loss (dl3). These stabilise very long or high-learning-rate runs at little cost.

10. A normaliser makes the network forgiving: a measurement

Recall the dl3 experiment: a plain 30-layer ReLU network's first-layer gradient, relative to the last layer's, was 6 with the right (He) initialisation but 10⁵ with a too-large one and 3×10⁻⁴ with a too-small one. The lab of this lesson repeats it with a LayerNorm after every linear layer. Results (ratio of gradient at layer 1 to gradient at layer 30): with weights at half the He scale, 1.2×10⁻⁸ without normalisation and 0.24 with it; at He scale, 6.2 without and 0.24 with; at four times the He scale, 1.8×10¹⁸ without and 0.24 with. The same number for an initialisation range of a factor of eight — the LayerNorm has made the gradient independent of the weight scale, exactly what the scale-invariance argument of section 4 predicts. (The exact 0.24 depends on the random draw and the depth, but the independence of the initial scale is exact.) This is why normalised networks train from careless initialisations and tolerate large learning rates.

11. Practical rules

  • ε and precision. ε ≈ 10⁻⁵ (10⁻⁶ in some models) guards the division. Compute the statistics and the division in float32 even if the rest of the model runs in bfloat16 or float16; low-precision sums of squares over thousands of features lose accuracy.
  • Weight decay. Do not apply weight decay to γ and β (and biases): decaying γ towards zero fights the normalisation. Optimiser parameter groups (dl5) handle this.
  • Initialisation. γ = 1, β = 0 at the start, so the layer initially is pure standardisation. (Gemma-style norms parameterise the scale as 1 + weight with weight initialised to 0; the checkpoint convention must be respected when porting.)
  • Fold BatchNorm for inference. Since eval-mode BatchNorm is an affine map, it merges into the preceding convolution: w′ = w·γ/√(σ² + ε), b′ = β + γ(b − μ)/√(σ² + ε). Worked with w = 2, b = 0.5, running mean 1, variance 4, γ = 1.5, β = 0.2: w′ = 1.5 and b′ = 0.2 + 1.5 × (0.5 − 1)/2 = −0.175, and (1.5x − 0.175) equals the unfused output for every x.
  • Train/eval discipline. Call model.train() and model.eval() deliberately; in fine-tuning with small batches keep BatchNorm layers in eval mode.

Scale invariance, weight norms and the effective learning rate

Suppose a layer's output goes through a normaliser, so the loss L is unchanged when the layer's weight vector w is multiplied by c > 0: L(cw) = L(w). Differentiate with respect to c at c = 1: ∇L(w) · w = 0, so the gradient is orthogonal to the weights. A gradient step therefore moves the weights along a tangent and, by Pythagoras, increases the norm: ‖w − η∇L‖² = ‖w‖² + η²‖∇L‖². So weight norms grow during training with plain gradient descent. Differentiating again shows the gradient scales inversely with the weights: ∇L(cw) = ∇L(w)/c. Together: as ‖w‖ grows, the gradient shrinks like 1/‖w‖ and the change in the direction of w per step shrinks like η/‖w‖². This is an automatic learning-rate decay, controlled by the weight norm. It explains why BatchNorm networks tolerate a large initial learning rate (the weights grow until the effective rate is stable), why it makes a huge difference whether weight decay is used (weight decay shrinks ‖w‖ and so raises the effective rate; the balance between growth from gradients and shrinkage from decay sets an equilibrium effective learning rate), and why changing weight decay changes results in normalised networks in ways it would not in a plain one. It also warns against reading a weight-norm curve naively: a growing norm in a normalised layer is expected and does not by itself indicate instability.

BatchNorm leaks information between examples

Because one example's output depends on its batch-mates, BatchNorm can leak. In contrastive self-supervised learning (dl11), the positive pair sits inside the same batch as the negatives, and the batch statistics can let a model infer which examples belong together without learning good features — the "shortcut" that MoCo avoided with shuffled BatchNorm (statistics computed on a different device split for query and key encoders) and SimCLR with a global BatchNorm across devices. At serving time, using batch statistics would make one user's response depend on other users' requests batched with theirs, which is unacceptable for privacy and reproducibility, another reason transformer inference avoids it.

Norm layers as memory-bound kernels

A LayerNorm or RMSNorm does about 5–10 arithmetic operations per element but must read the whole activation and write it back: its arithmetic intensity is far below the GPU's ridge point (gpu2), so it is bounded by memory bandwidth, and an unfused implementation that computes the mean, the variance, the normalisation and the scale as separate kernels reads and writes the tensor several times. Production stacks fuse the whole norm into one kernel (and often fuse it with the residual add and the following quantisation), computing in float32 registers but reading and writing bfloat16. torch.compile generates such fusions automatically (gpu6). RMSNorm's advantage over LayerNorm is exactly here: one fewer reduction and no bias, so a leaner kernel.

Normalisation-free approaches

If normalisation only serves to keep scales controlled and gradients well-conditioned, similar effects may be obtained differently. NFNets (Brock et al., 2021) train large-batch ResNets without BatchNorm by scaling weights (scaled weight standardisation) and using adaptive gradient clipping — clipping each unit's gradient by the ratio of its norm to its weight's norm — and matched or surpassed BatchNorm models on ImageNet. Zhu et al. (2025) observed that trained LayerNorms in transformers often produce a tanh-like S-shaped input–output mapping and proposed Dynamic Tanh, tanh(αx) with a learnable scalar α, as a drop-in replacement; they report comparable results across vision and language models. Neither has displaced normalisation as the default, but they clarify what the layer is doing: bounding and re-scaling activations elementwise in a way that keeps optimisation well-behaved.

Conditioning: adaptive normalisation

The learned γ and β need not be fixed parameters; they can be predicted from other data. In conditional image generation (dl10), adaptive layer norm (adaLN, used in diffusion transformers) computes γ and β from the conditioning signal (the diffusion timestep, a class label), so a single network can behave differently per condition, and in style transfer AdaIN sets the per-channel statistics from a style image. This shows that after standardisation, γ and β are the natural place to inject information: the standardised activations are "content", and the affine parameters set their scale and offset.

Batch size, BatchNorm and the effective batch

With data parallelism, per-GPU batch size determines BatchNorm's statistics quality, so scaling out a fixed global batch over more GPUs shrinks the per-GPU batch and can degrade BatchNorm models even though the global batch is unchanged — a subtle scale-out bug. Goyal et al.'s ImageNet-in-an-hour work (ml4) kept the per-GPU batch at 32 and computed BatchNorm statistics per GPU, so that the definition of the loss did not depend on the number of GPUs. The alternatives are SyncBatchNorm (exact global statistics, one all-reduce of sums per norm layer per pass), 'ghost' batch normalisation (deliberately computing statistics over small virtual sub-batches as a regulariser) or replacing the norm with GroupNorm. The same reasoning is why gradient accumulation does not reproduce a large batch for BatchNorm networks: each micro-batch's statistics are computed alone (dl1).

Failure at the edge: ε, zeros and outliers

If a feature is (nearly) constant across the pooled set, its variance is close to zero and the output is dominated by ε: with ε = 10⁻⁵ and variance 10⁻⁶, the standard deviation is 0.0033 rather than 0.001, and tiny noise can be amplified by a factor of 300. In float16, x² can overflow above 256 in magnitude (256² = 65,536 > 65,504) if the sum of squares is computed in half precision, another reason for float32 accumulation. In LLMs a few hidden dimensions develop very large magnitudes ("massive activations" or outlier features); RMSNorm divides everything by an RMS dominated by those dimensions, which shrinks the informative ones — an interaction that motivates some of the quantisation and architecture work around outliers (dl2, gpu4).

The public case: Batch Normalization on Inception

Ioffe and Szegedy's paper (arXiv:1502.03167, Google, 2015) is short and dense with checkable numbers. They took the Inception network (batch size 32, learning rate 0.0015, momentum SGD), which reached 72.2% top-1 accuracy on the ImageNet validation set after 31 × 10⁶ steps, and inserted BatchNorm before each non-linearity. Their results, from the paper's Figure 3: BN-Baseline (BatchNorm only) reached 72.2% in 13.3 million steps — less than half; BN-x5 (also with a 5× higher learning rate, 0.0075, plus modifications such as removing dropout and reducing L2 weight regularisation by 5×) reached it in 2.1 million steps, the "14 times fewer steps" of the abstract, with a maximum accuracy of 73.0%; BN-x30 (learning rate 0.045, thirty times the original) trained "somewhat slower initially" but reached 74.8%, the best single-network accuracy, in about 6 million steps. Applying the same 5× learning rate to the original Inception "caused the model parameters to reach machine infinity". BN-x5-Sigmoid, the same network with sigmoid non-linearities, reached 69.8%, while "without Batch Normalization, Inception with sigmoid never achieves better than 1/1000 accuracy" — chance. An ensemble of six BN-x30-based networks achieved 4.9% top-5 validation error and 4.82% test error, ahead of the previous best of 4.94% (He et al.'s PReLU ensemble, dl2) and, the authors note, better than the estimated accuracy of human raters. The lessons are the ones from this lesson: normalisation buys a much larger usable learning rate (5× to 30×), makes training insensitive to activation choice (sigmoid works), and acts as a regulariser (dropout could be reduced or removed).

The counterpart case is Wu and He's Group Normalization paper (arXiv:1803.08494, Facebook AI Research, 2018), which quantified BatchNorm's weakness: ResNet-50's ImageNet error rose from 23.6% (batch 32) to 34.7% (batch 2), while GroupNorm with 32 groups was flat at 24.0–24.2%. For detection and segmentation (Mask R-CNN on COCO), where per-GPU batches are 1–2 images, they found that GroupNorm improved over the standard workaround of freezing BatchNorm into a fixed affine layer (their BN* baseline) by 1.1 box AP and 0.8 mask AP.

Real-world incident classes

The model that is great in training and poor in production. The number-one cause is a missing model.eval(), or running inference under model.train() with a small batch. Symptoms: accuracy that changes with batch size or with the order of requests, and predictions that are not deterministic. A one-line assertion at service start-up (assert not model.training) prevents it; a test that feeds the same example alone and inside a batch and checks that outputs match to within numerical noise detects any remaining batch dependence.

Silent drift of running statistics in fine-tuning. After a few hundred steps of fine-tuning with batch size 2–4, evaluation accuracy is far worse than at start although the training loss falls. The running mean and variance have been overwritten by noisy small-batch estimates. Fix by freezing BatchNorm (eval mode for those layers), or by using GroupNorm, and always compare against the pretrained statistics.

Porting bugs in norm layers. The Gemma port story in dl2 is also a norm story: the community fix (Hugging Face transformers PR #29402) listed, alongside the approximate GELU, that the RMSNorm which multiplies by (1 + weight) "must be upcasted to float32 and not bfloat16 or float16 halfway", unlike LLaMA's version, which downcasts before multiplying by the weights. Differences of this kind (ε, upcasting order, weight convention, whether the mean is subtracted) produce small numerical drift that grows with depth. A layer-by-layer comparison against a reference implementation on fixed inputs finds the first layer that diverges.

Instability that norm placement can cure. Loss spikes in long high-learning-rate runs are often attention logits growing (dl3). OLMo 2's technical report lists RMSNorm, reordered norm (normalising sub-layer outputs), QK-norm and z-loss among the stability measures it adopted; Gemma 2 normalises the input and output of every sub-layer. If a run is spiking, adding QK-norm or output norms is a cheaper experiment than changing the optimiser.

What to monitor

For BatchNorm networks: the running mean and variance of each layer over time (a running variance collapsing towards zero or a running mean far from the training data's indicates a problem), the difference in accuracy between train and eval modes on the same held-out data, and per-GPU batch size. For LayerNorm and RMSNorm models: activation RMS at the input of each block (it should grow smoothly with depth in pre-norm models, and a sudden jump points at a layer), the γ statistics (a γ growing without bound or collapsing to zero in one layer), and gradient norms per layer (dl3). During porting or quantisation: layer-wise output error against a float32 reference.

Goal: implement the three normalisers on the lesson's 2×3 tensor, show their different dependence on the batch, verify the LayerNorm gradient formula against finite differences, see BatchNorm's running statistics, and repeat the dl3 experiment to watch a normaliser make a 30-layer network insensitive to initialisation. NumPy only, Python 3.12, a few seconds.

import numpy as np
EPS = 1e-5

# ---- three normalisers; x has shape (batch, features)
def batchnorm(x):  m, v = x.mean(0), x.var(0);                 return (x - m) / np.sqrt(v + EPS)
def layernorm(x):  m, v = x.mean(1, keepdims=True), x.var(1, keepdims=True); return (x - m) / np.sqrt(v + EPS)
def rmsnorm(x):    return x / np.sqrt((x ** 2).mean(1, keepdims=True) + EPS)

X = np.array([[2., 4., 6.], [0., 3., 9.]])
np.set_printoptions(precision=3, suppress=True)
print("BatchNorm (per column):\n", batchnorm(X))
print("LayerNorm (per row):\n", layernorm(X))
print("RMSNorm (per row):\n", rmsnorm(X))

# ---- batch dependence: change the OTHER example, look at example 0's output
X2 = X.copy(); X2[1] = [100., 200., 300.]
print("\nexample 0 after swapping its batch-mate:  BN", batchnorm(X2)[0], " LN", layernorm(X2)[0])

# ---- train-mode BatchNorm with a batch of one destroys the input (mean = the value, variance = 0)
print("BN, batch of 1:", batchnorm(np.array([[2., 4., 6.]])))

# ---- LayerNorm backward by formula vs finite differences
def ln_backward(dy, x):
    xh, s = layernorm(x), np.sqrt(x.var(1, keepdims=True) + EPS)
    return (dy - dy.mean(1, keepdims=True) - xh * (dy * xh).mean(1, keepdims=True)) / s
x = np.array([[2., 4., 6.]]); dy = np.array([[1., 0., 0.]])
num = np.zeros_like(x)
for j in range(3):
    e = np.zeros_like(x); e[0, j] = 1e-6
    num[0, j] = ((layernorm(x + e) - layernorm(x - e)) * dy).sum() / 2e-6
print("\nLN backward  formula:", ln_backward(dy, x), " finite differences:", num)
print("gradient along the normalised direction is removed:", ln_backward(-layernorm(x), x))

# ---- running statistics for BatchNorm (momentum 0.1): what eval mode would use
batch = np.array([2., 4., 6., 8.]); rm, rv = 0.0, 1.0
rm, rv = 0.9 * rm + 0.1 * batch.mean(), 0.9 * rv + 0.1 * batch.var(ddof=1)
print("\nafter one batch: running mean", rm, " running var", round(rv, 3))

# ---- does a normaliser rescue a badly scaled 30-layer network? (compare dl3)
DEPTH, WIDTH = 30, 100
def signal(std, norm, seed=0):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    Ws = [r.normal(0, std, (WIDTH, WIDTH)) for _ in range(DEPTH)]
    h, cache = r.normal(size=(64, WIDTH)), []
    for W in Ws:
        z = h @ W
        y = layernorm(z) if norm else z
        a = np.maximum(y, 0)
        cache.append((z, y)); h = a
    d = np.ones_like(h) / h.size; out = []
    for W, (z, y) in zip(reversed(Ws), reversed(cache)):
        dy = d * (y > 0)
        dz = ln_backward(dy, z) if norm else dy
        d = dz @ W.T; out.append(np.linalg.norm(d))
    return out[-1] / out[0]                          # gradient at layer 1 relative to the top
print("\ngradient at layer 1 / gradient at layer 30, ReLU, 30 layers")
for label, std in [("too small (0.5x He)", 0.5 * np.sqrt(2 / WIDTH)), ("He", np.sqrt(2 / WIDTH)), ("too big (4x He)", 4 * np.sqrt(2 / WIDTH))]:
    print(f"{label:20} no norm {signal(std, False):9.1e}   with LayerNorm {signal(std, True):9.1e}")

What to look for.

  1. The three tables match section 2: BatchNorm gives [[1, 1, −1], [−1, −1, 1]] (a batch of two can only produce ±1); LayerNorm gives row 1 = (−1.225, 0, 1.225) and row 2 = (−1.069, −0.267, 1.336); RMSNorm gives (0.463, 0.926, 1.389) and (0, 0.548, 1.643).

Work it out

A BatchNorm layer sees the activations [2, 4, 6, 8] for one feature across a batch of 4, with γ = 1, β = 0. Compute the output. Then say what happens at batch size 1 in training mode, and at batch size 1 in evaluation mode. Finally, apply LayerNorm to the single example (2, 4, 6, 8) and compare.

Answer

Mean = 5, variance = ((−3)² + (−1)² + 1² + 3²)/4 = 5, so σ = 2.236 (ignoring ε).

Output = (x − 5)/2.236 → (−1.342, −0.447, 0.447, 1.342): zero mean, unit variance, then scaled by γ and shifted by β.

Batch size 1, training mode: the batch mean is the single value, so x − μ = 0 and the variance is 0. The output is 0/√ε = 0, then γ·0 + β = β: every input produces the same output and the layer has destroyed all information. Batch size 1, evaluation mode: perfectly fine, because BatchNorm uses the stored running mean and variance and no longer depends on the batch.

LayerNorm on one example (2, 4, 6, 8): here the four numbers are four features of a single example. The mean is 5, the variance 5 and the result is the same (−1.342, −0.447, 0.447, 1.342), because the arithmetic is identical; what differs is which numbers are pooled. In BatchNorm these were four examples' values of one feature. In LayerNorm they are one example's values of four features, and LayerNorm works for any batch size, including 1, and in training and inference alike.

Why transformers therefore use LayerNorm or RMSNorm: statistics that come from a single token's features do not depend on the batch, behave identically in training and inference, and are unaffected by padded positions. RMSNorm goes one step further and drops the mean subtraction, which is cheaper and works as well.

Choose a normaliser

You are fine-tuning a Mask R-CNN detector whose backbone is a ResNet-50 with BatchNorm. Memory limits you to 2 images per GPU on 8 GPUs. What do you do with the BatchNorm layers, and what do you change if you were training the same backbone from scratch?

Answer

Fine-tuning: two images per GPU give very noisy batch statistics, and updating the running statistics from them would drift them away from the pretrained values. The standard approach is to freeze BatchNorm: keep those layers in evaluation mode (running statistics fixed, so each layer is a fixed per-channel affine map) and either freeze γ and β too or train them with a small learning rate. That keeps fine-tuning consistent with the pretrained features and costs nothing at inference.

From scratch: options in order of preference. (1) GroupNorm (G = 32) in the backbone and heads, whose accuracy is independent of the per-GPU batch (Wu & He: 24.1% ImageNet error at batch 2 versus 34.7% for BatchNorm). (2) SyncBatchNorm across the 8 GPUs, which gives statistics from 16 images at the cost of an all-reduce per layer (gpu8); better than nothing but still small. (3) Gradient accumulation does not fix BatchNorm, because each micro-batch's statistics are computed separately. Either GroupNorm or synchronised BatchNorm is a legitimate answer; say what the trade-off is (accuracy at large batches versus independence from the batch).
