Suppose you have a correct gradient for every one of a model's billions of weights. Backpropagation (dl1) gave it to you, and the gradient of each weight says which way to nudge that weight to reduce the loss. You still have not trained anything. Someone has to decide how far to nudge each weight, whether to trust this step's gradient or the average of the last hundred, and what to do when one weight's gradient is a million times larger than another's. That decision procedure is the optimiser, and choosing it well is a large part of what separates a training run that converges in a week from one that diverges on day two.

The stakes are not academic. In a modern large-model run the optimiser is responsible for a surprising share of both the memory and the risk. With the standard choice, Adam in mixed precision, it stores four extra numbers per weight next to the weight itself — sixteen bytes per parameter in total — so a 7-billion-parameter model needs 112 GB of training state before a single activation is stored, more than one GPU holds. It also carries hyperparameters (two averaging rates, a stabiliser, a weight decay) that decide whether the loss curve is smooth or full of spikes, and a well-known implementation subtlety, the way weight decay is applied, that changed the default choice for transformers. Every LLM technical report lists its optimiser settings for exactly this reason.

This topic gives you the mechanism first and the practice second. We start from plain SGD and momentum (recalled from ml4), build up RMSProp's per-parameter scaling and then Adam, and work one complete Adam update by hand with real numbers, so that the rule every parameter moves by about the learning rate is something you have computed, not memorised. We derive why bias correction exists and why L2 regularisation and weight decay stop being the same thing under Adam, count the memory of each optimiser, compare Adam and SGD honestly, and look at what the recipes of LLaMA, Llama 3 and PaLM actually use and where newer optimisers such as Lion, LAMB and Muon fit. The public case is the pair of papers behind today's default, Adam and AdamW, checked against their own numbers. The lab reproduces the hand computation, compares optimisers on a badly scaled quadratic, measures the weight-decay difference and shows why weights must be kept in float32.

What an optimiser does

After backpropagation, every weight has a gradient g. The simplest thing you can do is the update rule from ml4: w ← w − lr × g. That is stochastic gradient descent (SGD) — "stochastic" because g comes from a random mini-batch, so it is a noisy estimate. Everything else in this topic is a refinement of one question: instead of using only the current gradient scaled by one global number, what can we remember from the past to take better steps?

Think of walking down a mountain in fog again (ml4). SGD looks only at the slope under your feet right now. Momentum makes you a heavy ball rolling downhill: you keep some of your previous velocity, so you smooth out zig-zags and build speed on consistent slopes. Adaptive methods (RMSProp, Adam) add a sense of scale: each direction you can move in gets its own stride length, scaled by how steep that direction has typically been — cautious in directions where the slope is wild, bold where it is gentle and steady. Adam, the combination of both ideas, is the default for training almost everything modern. The analogy fails on one point: in a real network the "directions" are individual parameters, billions of them, each with its own gradient history, so the bookkeeping has a real memory cost.

Three ideas, one sentence each

  • Momentum: keep a decaying running sum of past gradients and move along it. With β = 0.9, four gradients of +1, −1, +1, −1 (a zig-zag) leave a small velocity because the signs cancel, while +1, +1, +1, +1 builds it towards 10 (which is 1/(1 − β)) — the optimiser speeds up where progress is consistent and damps oscillation where it is not.
  • Adaptive step sizes: divide each parameter's step by a running measure of how large its gradients have been, so tiny-gradient parameters are not left behind and huge-gradient ones do not overshoot.
  • Adam: momentum on the numerator, adaptive scaling on the denominator, plus a correction for the fact that both averages start at zero.

The price of remembering

Remembering is not free. Momentum stores one extra number for every weight, and Adam stores two. A model with 7 billion weights therefore carries 7 billion extra momentum values and 7 billion extra squared-gradient values, in 32-bit precision, next to the weights themselves. That bookkeeping, not the arithmetic, is why optimiser choice shows up in GPU memory budgets, and why techniques such as sharding the optimiser state across GPUs exist. The trade is usually worth it: the extra state buys much faster and more reliable convergence than plain SGD, especially for transformers.

The settings you choose

  • Learning rate — the most important. Typical Adam values: 10⁻³ for small models, 3×10⁻⁴ or lower for transformers (lower for bigger ones), 10⁻⁵ to 5×10⁻⁵ for fine-tuning.
  • Weight decay — shrinks weights every step to regularise; 0.01–0.1 with AdamW.
  • Betas (Adam's two averaging rates) — 0.9 and 0.999 by default; 0.9 and 0.95 for most LLM training.
  • Schedule — warm up, then decay the learning rate (ml4).

2. SGD and momentum

Plain SGD with mini-batches is what ml4 developed: one global learning rate, noise from the mini-batch. It has two weaknesses. In a valley with steep walls and a shallow floor (an ill-conditioned loss), the learning rate must be small enough for the steep direction, so progress along the floor crawls. And every parameter shares one learning rate, although different parameters may deserve very different ones. Momentum addresses the first. PyTorch's form keeps a velocity buffer v ← μ·v + g and steps w ← w − lr·v, with μ = 0.9. On the quadratic loss L = ½(w₁² + 100w₂²), whose curvatures are 1 and 100, plain SGD's largest stable learning rate is 2/100 = 0.02 (ml4), so w₁ shrinks by only 1 − 0.019 = 0.981 per step and it takes 246 steps to settle below a loss of 10⁻³. With momentum 0.9 and learning rate 0.015 the same problem settles in 107 steps in the lab: the oscillation in the steep direction cancels in the velocity and the shallow direction accumulates up to 1/(1 − μ) = 10 times its raw gradient. Nesterov momentum evaluates the gradient at the look-ahead point w − lr·μ·v, which corrects overshoot slightly earlier.

3. Adaptive step sizes: AdaGrad and RMSProp

Momentum still uses one learning rate for all parameters. In many models parameters differ enormously in gradient scale: in a language model an embedding row for a rare word gets a gradient only when that word appears, while a layer-norm scale gets a gradient every step; the last layer's gradients differ in size from the first layer's (dl3). A single learning rate cannot suit both. AdaGrad (Duchi et al., 2011) divides each parameter's step by the square root of the sum of all its past squared gradients: parameters with small or rare gradients get bigger steps. Its weakness is that the sum only grows, so every learning rate decays toward zero and training eventually stalls. RMSProp (Tieleman & Hinton, 2012) fixes this by using an exponential moving average of the squared gradient instead of the sum, v ← β v + (1 − β) g², and stepping by g / (√v + ε): the denominator tracks the recent gradient size. The consequence worth internalising: after dividing by the RMS gradient, the step is about the size of the learning rate for every parameter, whatever its gradient's scale. A gradient of 100 and a gradient of 0.001 both lead to steps of about lr.

4. Adam: momentum plus scaling, with a correction

Kingma and Ba (2014) combined the two: keep m, an EMA of the gradient (momentum), and v, an EMA of the squared gradient (RMSProp), and step by m/(√v + ε). Their only subtlety is a fix for the start of training. Both averages are initialised at zero, so early in training they are biased toward zero. After one step, m = (1 − β₁)g = 0.1g and v = (1 − β₂)g² = 0.001g². In general after t steps m is missing a fraction β₁ᵗ of its weight on the (imaginary) initial zero, so the corrected estimates divide by what has accumulated:

m_t = β₁ m_{t−1} + (1 − β₁) g_t                 β₁ = 0.9
v_t = β₂ v_{t−1} + (1 − β₂) g_t²                β₂ = 0.999
m̂_t = m_t / (1 − β₁ᵗ)        v̂_t = v_t / (1 − β₂ᵗ)      (bias correction)
w_t = w_{t−1} − lr · m̂_t / ( √v̂_t + ε )         ε = 10⁻⁸

One full Adam update, by hand. Two parameters A and B, both starting at w = 1, learning rate 0.1. A always has gradient 100; B has gradient 0.001, then −0.001, then +0.001 (a noisy sign). Step 1: for A, m = 0.1 × 100 = 10 and v = 0.001 × 10,000 = 10; correction divides by 1 − 0.9 = 0.1 and 1 − 0.999 = 0.001, so m̂ = 100 and v̂ = 10,000, √v̂ = 100; the step is 0.1 × 100/100 = 0.1. For B: m = 0.0001, v = 10⁻⁹, m̂ = 0.001, v̂ = 10⁻⁶, √v̂ = 0.001; the step is 0.1 × 0.001/0.001 = 0.1. Two gradients differing by a factor of 100,000 produce identical steps. Continuing:

A, whose gradient is consistent, moves 0.1 every step: the step size is the learning rate, independent of the gradient's magnitude. B, whose gradient flips sign, moves 0.1 on the first step (Adam has no history to doubt it), then only −0.005 and +0.034 as the momentum average m̂ shrinks toward zero while √v̂ stays put — the disagreement between consecutive gradients damps its steps. (The lab and PyTorch's torch.optim.Adam reproduce these numbers.) The two behaviours are the whole personality of Adam: steps of about lr where gradients are consistent, much smaller steps where they are noisy.

Why the correction matters, in numbers. Without it, step 1 for parameter A would use m = 10 and √v = √10 = 3.16, giving 0.1 × 10/3.16 = 0.316: three times larger than intended. The reason is that with the defaults v starts out more biased (a factor 0.001) than m (a factor 0.1), so the ratio is too large by (1 − β₁)/√(1 − β₂) = 0.1/0.0316 = 3.16. Kingma and Ba show that without bias correction and with β₂ near 1, training is unstable in the first epochs; RMSProp, which has no correction, suffers from exactly this. (The intuition "the estimates start too small, so steps are too small" holds for each average separately but not for their ratio.)

Step size and signal-to-noise. A useful reading of Adam's update: if the gradient of one parameter has mean μ and standard deviation σ (from mini-batch noise), then m̂ ≈ μ and √v̂ ≈ √(μ² + σ²), so the step is about lr · μ/√(μ² + σ²). If the gradient is consistent (σ ≪ μ) the step is ±lr; if it is mostly noise (σ ≫ μ) the step shrinks toward zero. Kingma and Ba note this gives "a form of automatic annealing": as training converges and the true gradient μ shrinks relative to noise, steps shrink on their own. They also derive that the step is bounded: |step| ≤ lr in the ordinary case, a trust region around the current weight. The consequence for tuning is that lr in Adam is a distance per step per parameter, not something multiplied by a gradient — which is why Adam is insensitive to the scale of the loss: multiplying the loss by 1,000 leaves Adam's trajectory unchanged, while plain SGD at the same learning rate diverges in two steps (lab).

5. Weight decay: L2 versus decoupled

Weight decay means shrinking each weight a little every step, w ← w − lr·λ·w, which pulls weights toward zero and regularises the model (ml3). For plain SGD this is the same as adding the penalty (λ/2)‖w‖² to the loss: the penalty's gradient is λw, and the SGD step on it is exactly −lr·λ·w. Because the two are identical for SGD, libraries and papers long used the terms interchangeably. Under Adam they are not.

If the L2 term is put in the loss, its gradient λw is added to g before Adam's machinery, so it is divided by √v̂ along with everything else: the decay applied to a parameter is roughly lr·λ·w/√v̂. Loshchilov and Hutter (2017) pointed out the consequence: weights with large gradients (large √v̂) are decayed less, and weights with tiny gradients are decayed more — the reverse of what one wants, since the large-gradient weights are usually the important ones. With numbers, lr = 10⁻³, λ = 0.01 and w = 1 for two parameters, A with typical gradient size 10 and B with 0.01:

gradient scaleAdam + L2 shrink per stepAdamW shrink per step
A10≈ 10⁻³ × 0.01 / 10 = 10⁻⁶10⁻³ × 0.01 × 1 = 10⁻⁵
B0.01≈ 10⁻³ × 0.01 / 0.01 = 10⁻³ (7×10⁻⁴ measured)10⁻⁵

B is decayed about 700 to 1,000 times harder than A under Adam + L2, and identically under AdamW. The lab measures these numbers with simulated noisy gradients (1.0×10⁻⁶ and 7.05×10⁻⁴ against 1.0×10⁻⁵ for both). AdamW therefore applies the decay directly to the weight, outside the adaptive step: w ← w − lr·(m̂/(√v̂ + ε)) − lr·λ·w (in PyTorch, w ← w(1 − lr·λ) then the Adam update). Loshchilov and Hutter report two effects: it decouples the best weight decay from the best learning rate (the two hyperparameters can be tuned nearly independently), and it "substantially improves Adam's generalization performance", with a 15% relative improvement in test error on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet32x32 and the ability to compete with SGD with momentum on image classification. Practical rules: use AdamW, not Adam, for transformers; apply decay to weight matrices but not to biases and normalisation scales (dl4); LLM values are around 0.1 (LLaMA; Llama 3 sets the decay at each step to 0.1 times that step's learning rate, so it follows the schedule).

6. Adam versus SGD: what the evidence says

It is not settled that one dominates. On the side of SGD: Wilson et al. (2017) built a simple problem where SGD generalises perfectly while AdaGrad, RMSProp and Adam reach test error "arbitrarily close to half", and found in experiments that adaptive methods often reach better training loss but worse test accuracy than tuned SGD on several models. On the side of Adam: for transformers, SGD performs much worse. Zhang et al. (2024) explain this with a Hessian analysis: transformers are heterogeneous, meaning the curvature (Hessian spectrum) differs dramatically between parameter blocks such as embeddings, attention matrices and MLP layers, so one global learning rate cannot suit all blocks, while Adam's coordinate-wise rates can; on problems without such heterogeneity SGD matches Adam. AdamW then closed much of the generalisation gap on vision tasks. A defensible rule of thumb: transformers and NLP → AdamW; CNNs where the best final accuracy matters and you can tune a schedule → SGD with momentum is still competitive; when unsure → AdamW, which is more forgiving of learning rates and scales.

7. Hyperparameters in practice

  • Learning rate. Sets stability and quality. Recipes lower the peak as models grow: LLaMA used 3.0×10⁻⁴ for 7B and 1.5×10⁻⁴ for 65B; Llama 3 uses 3×10⁻⁴, 1.5×10⁻⁴ and 8×10⁻⁵ for 8B, 70B and 405B. Always with warmup (ml4).
  • β₁ = 0.9. Rarely changed.
  • β₂ = 0.95 versus 0.999. β₂ sets how long v remembers. At 0.999 it averages over about 1,000 steps and reacts slowly when gradients suddenly grow (a bad batch, a shift in the data), so steps can be far too large for a while: loss spikes. At 0.95 it averages over about 20 steps and tracks recent gradient size closely. LLaMA and most LLM recipes use 0.95. PaLM used a schedule β₂ = 1 − k^(−0.8) (about 0.84 at step 10, 0.99 at step ≈ 300 and 0.999 at step ≈ 10⁴), reporting it "more stable than the standard β₂ = 0.99" because rare embedding tokens can have poorly estimated second moments over short windows.
  • ε = 10⁻⁸. It bounds the step when √v̂ is tiny: a parameter whose gradient is far smaller than ε cannot get a full-sized step. Larger values (10⁻⁶ or more) are sometimes used for stability in low precision.
  • Weight decay. 0.1 for LLM pretraining, 0.01–0.05 for fine-tuning; 0 for biases and norm parameters.
  • Gradient clipping. Global norm 1.0 in nearly every LLM recipe (dl3).
  • Batch size. A larger batch lowers gradient noise so a higher learning rate is safe. For SGD the linear scaling rule holds up to a limit (ml4); for Adam, a square-root scaling is a common starting point. Always re-tune the learning rate and warmup when the batch size changes.

A concrete recipe (LLaMA, Touvron et al., 2023): AdamW with β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.95; cosine schedule with the final learning rate 10% of the peak; weight decay 0.1; gradient clipping 1.0; 2,000 warmup steps; peak 3.0×10⁻⁴ for 7B, batches of 4M tokens. Llama 3 405B: peak 8×10⁻⁵, linear warmup over 8,000 steps, cosine decay to 8×10⁻⁷ over 1.2 million steps, and batch size raised from 4M tokens to 8M and later to 16M ("we use a lower batch size early in training to improve training stability"), with few loss spikes.

8. What each optimiser costs in memory

Training state per parameter in mixed precision (gpu3): 2 bytes bf16 weights, 2 bytes bf16 gradients, 4 bytes fp32 master weights, and the optimiser's own state. Adam adds two fp32 numbers, m and v: 2 + 2 + 4 + 4 + 4 = 16 bytes per parameter. For a 7B model: 14 GB of weights, 14 GB of gradients, 28 GB of master weights, 28 GB for m, 28 GB for v — 112 GB, before activations, which exceeds one 80 GB GPU. Options in the same accounting:

  • ZeRO / FSDP (gpu9) shards the 12 optimiser-side bytes (and optionally gradients and weights) across data-parallel GPUs; Adam's update is elementwise, so each GPU updates its own slice and the result is exact.
  • 8-bit optimisers (Dettmers et al., 2022) store m and v in 8 bits with block-wise dynamic quantisation, cutting the moments from 8 to 2 bytes per parameter (a 75% reduction of optimiser state) with 32-bit-quality results on tasks including 1.5B-parameter language modelling.
  • Adafactor (Shazeer & Stern, 2018) replaces the full second-moment matrix of an n×m weight with row and column statistics, O(n + m) numbers instead of O(nm), and can drop the first moment; used for T5 and for PaLM.
  • Lion stores one momentum buffer; Muon stores one momentum buffer per matrix; SGD with momentum stores one.
  • LoRA (dl11) trains small adapter matrices so only they have gradients and optimiser state.

Precision, and why the master copy exists. An Adam step is of order lr — 3×10⁻⁴, say — but a bfloat16 number has 8 bits of precision, so near 1.0 the spacing between neighbouring representable values is 2⁻⁷ = 0.0078. An update smaller than half the spacing is rounded away. The lab emulates bfloat16: applying 1,000 updates of +3×10⁻⁴ to a bf16 weight of 1.0 leaves it at exactly 1.0, while an fp32 copy correctly reaches 1.3. So the optimiser keeps float32 master weights and float32 moments, updates those, and casts the result down for the forward and backward passes (gpu4). (For the same reason ε = 10⁻⁸ must not live in float16: its smallest positive number is about 6×10⁻⁸, so ε would flush to zero.) The step itself is memory-bound: for 7B parameters an unsharded update reads and writes roughly 28 bytes per parameter, 196 GB, which at H100 memory bandwidth (3.35 TB/s) is about 60 ms per step, one reason PyTorch offers fused (fused=True) optimiser kernels.

9. Using them in code

# Decay weight matrices, but not biases or normalisation scales (1-D parameters)
decay    = [p for p in model.parameters() if p.requires_grad and p.ndim >= 2]
no_decay = [p for p in model.parameters() if p.requires_grad and p.ndim < 2]
opt = torch.optim.AdamW([{"params": decay,    "weight_decay": 0.1},
                         {"params": no_decay, "weight_decay": 0.0}],
                        lr=3e-4, betas=(0.9, 0.95), eps=1e-8, fused=True)

def lr_at(step, warmup=2000, total=100_000, peak=3e-4, floor=0.1):
    if step < warmup: return peak * step / warmup                            # linear warmup
    t = (step - warmup) / (total - warmup)
    return peak * (floor + (1 - floor) * 0.5 * (1 + math.cos(math.pi * t)))  # cosine to 10%

for step, batch in enumerate(loader):
    for g in opt.param_groups: g["lr"] = lr_at(step)
    loss = model(batch).loss; loss.backward()
    torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)
    opt.step(); opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)

Two habits: save the optimiser state in every checkpoint (m, v and the step counter; restarting with empty moments makes the first steps huge), and log the update-to-weight ratio lr·‖update‖/‖w‖ per layer (about 10⁻³ is typical; dl3).

10. Beyond Adam: what the newer optimisers change

  • LARS / LAMB (You et al.) add a layer-wise trust ratio, scaling each layer's update by ‖weights‖/‖update‖, so layers with small weights are not blown away by a large-batch step. LAMB trained BERT with batches of about 32,000 and reduced training time "from 3 days to just 76 minutes" on a TPU pod.
  • Lion (Chen et al., 2023) was found by program search: step along the sign of an interpolated momentum, so every parameter's update has the same magnitude; it stores only one moment ("more memory-efficient than Adam") and the authors report up to 2% higher ImageNet accuracy for ViT and up to 5× less pre-training compute on JFT.
  • Shampoo / SOAP keep a Kronecker-factored preconditioner per matrix (an approximation to curvature), the second-order end of the spectrum.
  • Muon orthogonalises the momentum of each 2-D weight matrix (a matrix-shaped analogue of Adam's normalisation) and uses Adam for embeddings, norms and the output head. The Moonshot report "Muon is Scalable for LLM Training" adds weight decay and a per-matrix update-scale correction, finds about 2× compute efficiency over AdamW, and trains a 16B-parameter mixture-of-experts model on 5.7 trillion tokens.

AdamW remains the default baseline; a new optimiser has to beat a tuned AdamW at equal compute to justify its complexity and its extra implementation risk in distributed settings.

Adam as sign descent, and the geometry of its steps

When a parameter's gradient is constant, Adam's update is exactly lr · sign(g) (m̂ = g and √v̂ = |g|), and for noisy gradients it interpolates between sign descent and a normalised SGD step. Balles and Hennig (2018) made this explicit: the sign of the gradient carries the direction, and the variance-based scaling decides how much to trust it. This explains several practical facts. Adam's updates have nearly the same size for every parameter (an RMS of at most about lr, usually somewhat less because β₁ < β₂ and ε > 0), so the update RMS is a controlled quantity: a weight matrix with n parameters moves by about lr·√n per step regardless of the size of its gradient. Optimisers that change the geometry — Lion (exact sign), Muon (orthogonalised matrix step) — are best understood by what update norm they produce. The Muon report notes that AdamW keeps an update RMS of roughly a constant, while Muon's raw update RMS is 1/√max(A, B) for an A×B matrix, so they rescale it to match AdamW's: only then can the same learning rate and weight decay be reused, which they describe as making Muon work "out-of-the-box" at scale.

Adam as an approximation of second-order preconditioning

Newton-style methods scale the gradient by the inverse curvature. Adam's 1/√v, with v ≈ E[g²], is a diagonal approximation: E[g²] is the diagonal of the empirical Fisher information matrix, which approximates the Gauss–Newton curvature for likelihood losses. Taking the square root rather than the full inverse is a heuristic; practitioners find the exponent ½ works better than the exponent 1 of a true diagonal natural-gradient step, and it keeps the update norm bounded. This viewpoint explains why Adam helps with heterogeneous scales (parameters with large curvature have larger gradients and so get smaller steps) and why the more complete preconditioners (Shampoo's Kronecker factors, K-FAC) can do better at higher cost. It also explains the learning-rate scaling with width: the update per parameter is fixed at about lr, but a layer with more inputs sums more contributions, so the layer's output changes more; parameterisations such as μP scale the learning rate of each layer with width so that the optimum transfers from small to large models (ml4).

Convergence: what is proved and what is folklore

The original Adam paper gave a regret bound of order √T for convex problems; Reddi, Kale and Kumar (2018) found an error in that proof and constructed a simple convex problem on which Adam does not converge, because the exponential moving average forgets rare, large gradients and so lets the effective learning rate grow at exactly the wrong moment. Their fix, AMSGrad, takes the running maximum of v. In deep-learning practice the counter-example rarely bites, AMSGrad seldom beats Adam, and the more relevant failure is the interaction of a long-memory v (β₂ = 0.999) with sudden gradient growth, which produces the loss spikes that a smaller β₂ or clipping cures. The takeaway for an interview: know that Adam has no general convergence guarantee, that this is a known and studied issue, and that it rarely determines practical choices.

Weight decay, normalisation and the effective learning rate

In a network whose layers are followed by normalisation (dl4), the function is invariant to the scale of each weight matrix, so weight decay cannot be reducing capacity; it controls the weight norm, and through it the effective learning rate (step size relative to weight size, roughly lr/‖w‖²). Gradient noise pushes weight norms up, decay pulls them down, and the balance sets an equilibrium in which each step rotates the weight direction by an angle on the order of √(2·lr·λ) (results from analyses of "rotational equilibrium" for scale-invariant weights). With lr = 3×10⁻⁴ and λ = 0.1 that is about 0.008 radians per step. The practical corollary is that lr and λ should be thought of together through the product lr·λ: changing the learning rate along a schedule changes the effective decay, which is why some recipes (Llama 3) define the decay at each step as a fixed multiple of the current learning rate. It also means that AdamW's lr·λ is the natural quantity to hold constant when comparing runs of different lengths, and that decay timescales are 1/(lr·λ) steps: at lr·λ = 3×10⁻⁵, about 33,000 steps.

Optimiser state precision and sharding

Eight-bit optimisers (Dettmers et al., 2022) work because m and v tolerate coarse quantisation if it is done carefully: values are split into small blocks, each quantised with its own scale, and a non-linear (dynamic) 8-bit code gives resolution to both small and large magnitudes; a "stable embedding layer" (with layer norm and Xavier initialisation) removes the extreme second-moment values of rarely updated embedding rows that broke naive schemes. Sharding is even easier: because Adam's update is a purely elementwise function of (w, g, m, v), ZeRO-1 can give each data-parallel rank a slice of the state and all-gather the updated weights, with mathematically identical results (gpu9). Optimisers that need matrix-level operations, Shampoo's matrix roots or Muon's orthogonalisation (a few iterations of matrix multiplications), need the whole matrix on one device or extra communication, which is why distributed implementations of them are non-trivial engineering.

Schedules, EMA and schedule-free training

A cosine schedule commits to a total number of steps in advance, which is awkward for continued pretraining or scaling-law sweeps. Two alternatives are common: warmup-stable-decay (hold the peak learning rate, then decay quickly at the end, so any point of the stable phase can be branched into a final decay), and schedule-free optimisers (Defazio et al., 2024), which replace the decay with a carefully chosen averaging of iterates and need no total-steps hyperparameter. Related is keeping an exponential moving average of the weights and evaluating that: it smooths the noise a high learning rate leaves in the weights, giving some of the benefit of a decayed schedule at any time. All three exploit the same fact: near the end of training the noise in the iterates, not the bias, limits quality, and averaging in time or decaying the step removes it.

Sparse gradients and embeddings

Rare tokens produce non-zero embedding gradients only occasionally. With a dense Adam implementation, the row's m and v still decay every step even though the gradient is zero, and when a rare token finally appears its gradient is divided by a v that has shrunk toward zero: the step is huge (the large first-step effect again). Remedies are a lower β₂ in short-window runs, a larger ε, the "stable embedding" of the 8-bit paper, or sparse optimisers that update only the rows touched. PaLM's schedule β₂ = 1 − k^(−0.8) is motivated by precisely "rare embedding tokens [having] poorly estimated second moments over shorter windows".

The public case: Adam and AdamW

Diederik Kingma and Jimmy Ba's "Adam: A Method for Stochastic Optimization" (arXiv:1412.6980, ICLR 2015) proposed exactly the algorithm above with default settings α = 0.001, β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999 and ε = 10⁻⁸, argued that its step sizes are "approximately bounded by the stepsize hyperparameter", and showed in experiments on logistic regression, multilayer networks and convolutional networks that it matched or beat AdaGrad, RMSProp and SGD with Nesterov momentum. Section 6.4 of the paper is the empirical case for bias correction: training a variational autoencoder without the correction became unstable in the first epochs for β₂ close to 1, while with it Adam performed equal or better than RMSProp across settings. The paper's convergence proof was later shown to be flawed (Reddi et al., 2018), but the algorithm became the default optimiser of deep learning.

Ilya Loshchilov and Frank Hutter's "Decoupled Weight Decay Regularization" (arXiv:1711.05101, ICLR 2019) is the second half of the story. Their abstract states the inequivalence plainly: "L2 regularization and weight decay regularization are equivalent for standard stochastic gradient descent (when rescaled by the learning rate), but ... this is not the case for adaptive gradient algorithms, such as Adam". Their experiments used a 26-layer 2×64d ResNet on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet32x32 with fixed, step-drop and cosine-annealed schedules over budgets from 100 to 1,800 epochs. Findings: Adam with decoupled weight decay achieved a 15% relative improvement in test error over Adam with L2; the optimal weight decay became nearly independent of the learning rate (so the two-dimensional search collapses), and the result competed with SGD with momentum on image classification, where Adam had usually lost. PyTorch's AdamW and every major LLM codebase followed.

The recipes that came out of this are documented in public. LLaMA (Touvron et al., 2023): AdamW, β = (0.9, 0.95), cosine to 10%, weight decay 0.1, clipping 1.0, 2,000 warmup steps. Llama 3 (2024): AdamW with peak learning rates of 3×10⁻⁴, 1.5×10⁻⁴ and 8×10⁻⁵ for the 8B, 70B and 405B models, and for 405B a warmup of 8,000 steps, a cosine schedule decaying to 8×10⁻⁷ over 1.2 million steps, and a batch size doubled twice from 4M to 16M tokens, reporting "few loss spikes". PaLM (2022) is the notable non-AdamW example: Adafactor with a learning rate of 10⁻² for 10,000 steps and 1/√k decay after, β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 1 − k^(−0.8), clipping 1.0 and a weight decay proportional to the square of the learning rate. Muon's public case is Moonshot's Moonlight model.

Production failure modes

Resuming without optimiser state. Loading only the model weights from a checkpoint and starting a fresh optimiser sets m = v = 0 and, with bias correction, makes the first steps ±lr in every coordinate, at whatever learning rate the schedule is now at. The loss jumps at the resume step. Fix: checkpoint and restore the optimiser state and the step counter (distributed checkpoints shard them: gpu9), and validate that resumed loss continues the previous curve.

Weight decay on everything. Applying λ to biases, layer-norm scales and embeddings shrinks parameters that should not shrink (a norm scale decayed toward zero fights the normalisation). Symptom: slow degradation, norm scales trending toward zero. Fix: parameter groups as in the code above, and log per-group weight norms.

Learning-rate schedule bugs. Calling scheduler.step() once per micro-batch instead of once per optimiser step under gradient accumulation stretches the schedule by the accumulation factor; forgetting warmup after a batch-size change spikes the loss; a schedule that depends on total steps, then a run extended without adjusting it, decays too early. Log the learning rate every step.

Low-precision optimiser state. float16 moments with ε = 10⁻⁸ produce division by zero or garbage; bf16 master weights lose small updates (the lab). Keep master weights and moments in float32 unless using a validated 8-bit scheme.

Mixed loss scales and clipping order. Clip gradients after unscaling (dl3); with fp16 loss scaling, non-finite gradients must skip the optimiser step, and the moments must not be updated with infinities, or every later step is poisoned.

Loss spikes from a long-memory v. If spikes follow a rare large batch, check the ratio of the pre-clip gradient norm to its recent average and consider β₂ = 0.95, longer warmup, or clipping; the fix is usually cheaper than rewinding (dl3).

What to monitor

Per step: loss, learning rate, pre-clip global gradient norm. Periodically: per-layer update-to-weight ratio, the RMS of m̂/√v̂ (should be a stable fraction of 1), the second-moment statistics (very small v for embeddings is normal; v collapsing to ε everywhere is not), weight norms per group, the fraction of steps clipped, and for fp16 the loss scale and the skipped-step count. For cost planning, log optimiser-step time (the fused kernel should be a small percentage of the step) and peak memory by phase (gpu11).

Goal: implement SGD, momentum and Adam(W) from scratch in NumPy, reproduce the hand-computed Adam steps, compare optimisers on a badly conditioned quadratic (and see Adam's invariance to loss scale), measure how L2 and decoupled weight decay treat small- and large-gradient weights, and see why bfloat16 weights lose small updates. Python 3.12, NumPy only, runs in a few seconds.

import numpy as np

# ---- optimisers as tiny classes: state + step(w, g) -> new w
class SGD:
    def __init__(s, lr, beta=0.0): s.lr, s.beta, s.v = lr, beta, 0.0
    def step(s, w, g): s.v = s.beta * s.v + g; return w - s.lr * s.v
class Adam:
    def __init__(s, lr, b1=0.9, b2=0.999, eps=1e-8, wd=0.0, decoupled=True):
        s.lr, s.b1, s.b2, s.eps, s.wd, s.dec, s.m, s.v, s.t = lr, b1, b2, eps, wd, decoupled, 0.0, 0.0, 0
    def step(s, w, g):
        if s.wd and not s.dec: g = g + s.wd * w                 # L2 inside the gradient (goes through /sqrt(v))
        s.t += 1
        s.m = s.b1 * s.m + (1 - s.b1) * g
        s.v = s.b2 * s.v + (1 - s.b2) * g * g
        mh, vh = s.m / (1 - s.b1 ** s.t), s.v / (1 - s.b2 ** s.t)   # bias correction
        w = w - s.lr * mh / (np.sqrt(vh) + s.eps)
        if s.wd and s.dec: w = w - s.lr * s.wd * w                # AdamW: decay applied directly to the weight
        return w

# ---- 1. one Adam step by hand: gradients differ by a factor of 100,000, steps are identical
wA = wB = 1.0; A, B = Adam(0.1), Adam(0.1)
for t, (gA, gB) in enumerate([(100, 0.001), (100, -0.001), (100, 0.001)], 1):
    wA, wB = A.step(wA, gA), B.step(wB, gB)
    print(f"t={t}  A: w={wA:.4f}   B: w={wB:.4f}")

# ---- 2. an ill-conditioned quadratic  L = 0.5 * (w1^2 + 100 * w2^2)
H = np.array([1.0, 100.0])
def run(opt_factory, scale=1.0, steps=400):
    opts = [opt_factory(), opt_factory()]; w = np.array([5.0, 1.0]); hist = []
    for _ in range(steps):
        g = scale * H * w                                        # gradient of scale * L
        w = np.array([opts[i].step(w[i], g[i]) for i in range(2)])
        hist.append(0.5 * (H * w ** 2).sum())
        if not np.isfinite(w).all() or hist[-1] > 1e12: break
    return np.array(hist)
def settled(h, thr):                                             # first step after which the loss stays below thr
    above = np.where(h >= thr)[0]
    return 1 if len(above) == 0 else (int(above[-1]) + 2 if above[-1] + 1 < len(h) else None)
print("\nsteps until the loss is below 1e-3 and stays there (None = not within 400 steps)")
for name, f in [("SGD lr 0.019", lambda: SGD(0.019)), ("SGD+momentum lr 0.015", lambda: SGD(0.015, 0.9)),
                ("Adam lr 0.3", lambda: Adam(0.3)), ("Adam lr 0.05", lambda: Adam(0.05))]:
    print(f"  {name:24}", settled(run(f), 1e-3))
print("same problem with the loss multiplied by 1000 (gradients 1000x larger):")
for name, f in [("SGD lr 0.019", lambda: SGD(0.019)), ("Adam lr 0.3", lambda: Adam(0.3))]:
    h = run(f, scale=1000.0)
    print(f"  {name:24}", "diverged after %d steps" % len(h) if len(h) < 400 and h[-1] > 1e3 else f"steps until loss < 1e-3 for good: {settled(h, 1e-3)}")

# ---- 3. weight decay: L2 inside Adam vs decoupled AdamW, for a small-gradient and a large-gradient weight
def mean_drift(sigma, wd, decoupled, n=20000, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)                            # same gradient noise for every run
    opt = Adam(1e-3, wd=wd, decoupled=decoupled); w0 = 1.0; total = 0.0
    for _ in range(n):
        total += opt.step(w0, rng.normal(0, sigma)) - w0         # weight change in one step, weight held at 1.0
    return total / n
print("\naverage per-step shrinkage caused by weight decay 0.01 on a weight equal to 1.0 (lr 1e-3)")
for sigma in (10.0, 0.01):
    base = mean_drift(sigma, 0.0, True)                          # no decay: pure gradient-noise drift
    l2 = base - mean_drift(sigma, 0.01, False); adamw = base - mean_drift(sigma, 0.01, True)
    print(f"  gradient scale {sigma:5}:  Adam+L2 {l2:.2e}   AdamW {adamw:.2e}")

# ---- 4. why fp32 master weights: emulate bfloat16 by keeping only the top 16 bits of a float32
def to_bf16(x): return (np.float32(x).view(np.uint32) & 0xFFFF0000).view(np.float32)
w = np.float32(1.0); step = np.float32(3e-4)
print("\nbfloat16 weight 1.0, applying 1000 updates of +3e-4:")
wb = w
for _ in range(1000): wb = to_bf16(wb + step)
print("  pure bf16:", wb, "  fp32 master copy:", np.float32(1.0) + np.float32(3e-4) * 1000)

What to look for.

  1. Section 1 prints the hand-computed table: A moves 0.1 per step (w = 0.9, 0.8, 0.7) although its gradient is 100; B, with gradient ±0.001 flipping sign, goes 0.9000, 0.9053, 0.8717. Compare with torch.optim.Adam(lr=0.1) if PyTorch is installed: identical.
  2. Section 2 reports steps until the loss on ½(w₁² + 100w₂²) drops below 10⁻³ and stays there: SGD at its largest stable rate 246 steps, momentum (0.9, lr 0.015) 107, Adam (lr 0.3) 99, Adam with a smaller learning rate 216. Multiplying the loss by 1,000 makes SGD at lr 0.019 diverge within two steps, while Adam's trajectory is unchanged (99 steps). Change the learning rates to explore each optimiser's stability limit; Adam with a constant learning rate keeps a small residual oscillation of size about lr around the minimum, which is why schedules decay it.
  3. Section 3 holds a weight at 1.0 and feeds it zero-mean noisy gradients of scale 10 or 0.01, with lr 10⁻³ and λ = 0.01. Under Adam+L2 the decay per step is 1.0×10⁻⁶ for the large-gradient weight and 7.05×10⁻⁴ for the small-gradient one (about 700× more); under AdamW it is 1.0×10⁻⁵ for both, exactly lr·λ·w.
  4. Section 4 emulates bfloat16 by keeping only the top 16 bits of a float32. 1,000 updates of +3×10⁻⁴ leave the bf16 weight at exactly 1.0 (spacing 0.0078 is more than twice the update), while the float32 master copy reaches 1.3.

Work it out — weight decay

Two parameters are both currently 1.0. Parameter A has consistently large gradients (√v ≈ 10); parameter B has tiny ones (√v ≈ 0.01). Weight decay λ = 0.01, learning rate η = 0.001. Compare the decay each receives under Adam with an L2 penalty versus AdamW.

Answer

Adam + L2 in the loss. The penalty adds λw to the gradient, so it passes through the same division by √v as everything else. Effective shrinkage ≈ η·λw/√v:

  • A: 0.001 × 0.01 × 1.0 / 10 = 1 × 10⁻⁶
  • B: 0.001 × 0.01 × 1.0 / 0.01 = 1 × 10⁻³ (about 7×10⁻⁴ in a simulation, because the decay term itself contributes to √v)

B is decayed roughly 1,000 times harder than A, and A is the parameter doing the most work: the regulariser is strongest exactly where you wanted it weakest.

AdamW. Decay is applied straight to the weight, outside the adaptive step: η·λ·w = 1 × 10⁻⁵ for both. Uniform, predictable, and what "weight decay" was always meant to mean. This is why AdamW is the transformer default and why λ transfers across architectures under AdamW but has to be re-tuned under Adam + L2.

Size it up — optimiser memory

You want to fully fine-tune a 13B-parameter model with AdamW in bf16 mixed precision on 8 × 80 GB GPUs. Compute the training state per GPU with plain data parallelism, with ZeRO-1, and with full sharding (ZeRO-3/FSDP), ignoring activations. Then say what changes if you switch to an 8-bit optimiser.

Answer

Per parameter: 2 (bf16 weights) + 2 (bf16 gradients) + 4 (fp32 master) + 4 (m) + 4 (v) = 16 bytes, so 13B × 16 = 208 GB of state.

  • Plain data parallelism: every GPU holds all 208 GB. Impossible on 80 GB.
  • ZeRO-1 shards the 12 optimiser-side bytes: 26 GB weights + 26 GB gradients + 156 GB/8 = 19.5 GB → 71.5 GB per GPU. It fits on paper, but leaves under 9 GB for activations and buffers, so it is impractical.
  • ZeRO-3 / FSDP: everything sharded, 208/8 = 26 GB per GPU, leaving room for activations (gpu9).

8-bit optimiser: m and v drop from 8 to 2 bytes per parameter, so the per-parameter total is 10 bytes and the state 130 GB; under ZeRO-1 the sharded part is 13 B × (4 + 2) bytes / 8 GPUs = 9.75 GB, so 26 + 26 + 9.75 ≈ 62 GB, and under full sharding 16 GB. The master weights (4 bytes) and gradients remain, which is why 8-bit optimisers help but do not remove the need for sharding or LoRA at this scale.
