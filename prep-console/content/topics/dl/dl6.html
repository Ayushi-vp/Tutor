In 2011 the best image-recognition systems in the world did not learn from pixels. Teams of researchers hand-designed features — descriptors with names like SIFT and HOG that summarise edges and textures in small patches — and fed them to classifiers such as support vector machines. In the 2010 ImageNet competition, where systems must sort 1.2 million photographs into 1,000 categories, the best entry was wrong on the correct label for 47.1% of test images and had the right label outside its five best guesses for 28.2%. Progress was steady and slow, measured in fractions of a percentage point.

Then in 2012 three researchers at the University of Toronto entered a neural network that looked at raw pixels. On the ImageNet test set it made a wrong top-five prediction 15.3% of the time; the runner-up, a carefully engineered system, managed 26.2%. A gap of almost eleven points, in a competition where the year-to-year improvements had been a point or two, changed the field in a season. The network was a convolutional neural network, and its central idea was not new (convolutional networks trained by backpropagation had been reading handwritten zip codes and bank cheques since the late 1980s and 1990s). What had changed was scale: a big enough dataset, graphics cards to train on, and a handful of tricks — ReLU, dropout, data augmentation — that made a 60-million-parameter model trainable.

Underneath the success is one idea worth understanding thoroughly, because it recurs across all of machine learning: inductive bias, the assumptions an architecture makes about its data so that it need not learn them. A photograph is not a bag of a hundred and fifty thousand unrelated numbers. Pixels close together are related, and an edge or an eye looks the same whether it is at the top left or the bottom right. A convolutional layer builds those two facts into its structure, and in doing so it needs a thousand times fewer parameters than a fully connected layer for the same job and generalises from far fewer examples. That trade — assumptions instead of data — is exactly what the recent contest between convolutional networks and Vision Transformers is about, and a strong answer to "why do ViTs need more data?" is one sentence about inductive bias.

This lesson builds convolutional networks from scratch. We define the operation and work one 3×3 convolution by hand with real numbers, derive the output-size, parameter and compute formulas and check them on ResNet's first layer, explain what weight sharing and translation equivariance mean, work through pooling and receptive fields, look at 1×1 and depthwise convolutions, trace the landmark architectures from LeNet to ConvNeXt, and close with how convolutions run on GPUs and how they compare with ViTs. The public case is the AlexNet paper, whose numbers we checked against the source, including a striking one: 96% of its parameters sit in its three dense layers, while 92% of its arithmetic is in the convolutions.

A stencil sliding over a photograph

Imagine laying a small transparent stencil on a photograph, with a few numbers written on it. At each position you multiply each number on the stencil by the brightness of the pixel beneath it, add up the products, and write the total down. Then you slide the stencil one pixel to the right and repeat, then down a row, until you have covered the whole photo. The grid of totals is a new "image": bright where the photograph looks like the pattern the stencil encodes, dark where it does not. That is a convolution. The stencil is the kernel (or filter) and the new image is a feature map.

A stencil with the numbers [−1 0 1] in each of three rows responds strongly where dark pixels on the left of the window meet bright pixels on the right: a vertical edge. Other stencils find horizontal edges, diagonal edges, colour blobs. The important step is that the network learns its stencils. Early layers in a trained network discover edge and colour detectors on their own; later layers, whose stencils look at the feature maps of earlier ones, combine them into corners, textures, wheels and faces. The analogy is imperfect in one way: real layers apply many stencils at once (64 or 256) and each stencil covers all the input channels, so one layer's output is a whole stack of feature maps.

Images as numbers, and why a dense layer is wrong for them

A colour image is a grid of pixels with three channels (red, green, blue): a 224 × 224 photo is 224 × 224 × 3 = 150,528 numbers. A fully connected layer with just 1,000 neurons on that input would have 150 million weights, and it would treat pixel (10, 10) as having nothing special to do with its neighbour (10, 11) — rearrange the pixels consistently and it would learn just as well. That ignores the structure that makes images images. A convolutional layer keeps the structure: each output looks only at a small window of neighbouring pixels, and the same weights are reused at every position.

Key terms

  • Stride: how far the kernel moves each step (stride 2 halves the output size).
  • Padding: adding a border of zeros so the output keeps the input's size.
  • Channels: each layer has many kernels, producing many feature maps, called channels.
  • Pooling: downsample by taking the maximum or average over small windows, making the representation smaller and slightly shift-tolerant.
  • Receptive field: how much of the original image one output value "sees"; it grows with depth.

A typical CNN

[convolution → batch normalisation → ReLU → (pooling)] repeated several times, gradually shrinking the spatial size and increasing the number of channels; then global average pooling (average each channel to one number) and a fully connected layer that outputs class scores. ResNet (dl3) adds skip connections so that very deep versions train. Everything in the rest of the lesson is about what each of those pieces costs, what it assumes, and why it works.

1. The three assumptions built into a convolution

A convolutional layer makes three assumptions about its inputs, each of which is approximately true of natural images.

  • Locality. Useful patterns (an edge, a corner, an eye) are small, so each output need only look at a small neighbourhood of its input. This removes most of the connections a dense layer would have.
  • Stationarity / weight sharing. A pattern means the same thing wherever it appears, so the same detector should be applied at every position. This makes the layer translation-equivariant (shift the input and the feature map shifts with it) and cuts the number of parameters by orders of magnitude.
  • Hierarchy. Complex patterns are composed of simpler ones: edges into corners into shapes into objects. Stacking layers, each looking at the previous one's feature maps, builds that composition, and the region of the image seen by each unit grows with depth.

These are exactly the "strong and mostly correct assumptions about the nature of images (namely, stationarity of statistics and locality of pixel dependencies)" that the AlexNet authors give as the reason CNNs "have much fewer connections and parameters and so they are easier to train, while their theoretically-best performance is likely to be only slightly worse" than a dense network of the same size. A dense network could in principle learn the same solution, but it would have to discover locality and weight sharing from data, and it would need vastly more data and parameters to do so.

2. The convolution, worked by hand

For a single-channel image X and a k×k kernel K, with stride 1 and no padding, the output at row i, column j is the sum of the elementwise products of K with the window of X starting at (i, j):

out[i, j] = Σ_u Σ_v  K[u, v] · X[i + u, j + v]  + b          (u, v = 0 … k−1)

(Strictly this is cross-correlation, because a mathematical convolution flips the kernel first; deep-learning libraries and this lesson use the unflipped form and call it convolution, and since the kernel is learned the flip makes no difference.) Take the 5×5 image and vertical-edge kernel of the figure:

X = [[1, 2, 0, 1, 3],        K = [[-1, 0, 1],
     [4, 1, 0, 2, 1],             [-1, 0, 1],
     [2, 3, 1, 0, 2],             [-1, 0, 1]]
     [0, 1, 3, 2, 1],
     [1, 0, 2, 4, 0]]

Top-left window [[1,2,0],[4,1,0],[2,3,1]]: the middle column of K is zero, so only the left and right columns matter: left column (1, 4, 2) is multiplied by −1 to give −7, and the right column (0, 0, 1) by +1 to give +1; the sum is −6. Sliding right, the window [[2,0,1],[1,0,2],[3,1,0]] gives −(2+1+3) + (1+2+0) = −3, and the next gives −(0+0+1) + (3+1+2) = 5. Doing all nine positions gives the 3×3 feature map:

[[-6, -3,  5],
 [-2, -1,  0],
 [ 3,  2, -3]]

The output is smaller than the input (3×3 from 5×5) because the window cannot hang over the edge. With padding of 1 (a border of zeros around X) the window can be centred on every pixel and the output is 5×5: [[3, −5, 0, 4, −3], [6, −6, −3, 5, −3], [5, −2, −1, 0, −4], [4, 3, 2, −3, −6], [1, 4, 5, −4, −6]]. Both are checked in the lab. On a synthetic image with a dark left half and a bright right half, this kernel outputs zero over the flat regions and a strong response (30 for brightness 0 and 10) at the boundary: an edge detector, made of nothing but nine numbers and a sliding window.

3. Many channels, many filters: shapes, parameters and cost

A real layer takes a stack of C_in feature maps and produces C_out of them. Each output channel has its own kernel, and that kernel spans all input channels: it is a k×k×C_in block. The output at one position is the sum over the whole block. The tensor shapes and costs follow directly:

input   C_in  × H × W
kernels C_out × C_in × k × k                         (plus a bias per output channel)
output  C_out × H_out × W_out

H_out = ⌊(H + 2p − k) / s⌋ + 1        (same for W; p = padding, s = stride)
parameters   = (k · k · C_in + 1) · C_out
multiply-adds = H_out · W_out · C_out · k · k · C_in

Worked on the first layer of ResNet: input 224 × 224 × 3, a 7×7 convolution with 64 filters, stride 2, padding 3. Output size: ⌊(224 + 6 − 7)/2⌋ + 1 = ⌊223/2⌋ + 1 = 112, so 112 × 112 × 64. Parameters: 7·7·3·64 + 64 = 9,408 + 64 = 9,472. Multiply-adds: 112·112·64·(7·7·3 = 147) = 118 million. Now the counterfactual: a dense layer mapping the same 224·224·3 inputs to the same 112·112·64 outputs would need 150,528 × 802,816 ≈ 1.2 × 10¹¹ weights — about 12.8 million times more. That is what locality and weight sharing buy: the same input–output shape with 9,472 numbers to learn instead of 120 billion. Also note how the parameter count and the compute differ: the layer has few weights but each is used at 12,544 positions (112 × 112), so the compute is about 12,500 times the parameter count. Convolutions are parameter-light and compute-heavy.

4. Stride, padding and the size formula

The formula ⌊(n + 2p − k)/s⌋ + 1 is applied separately along each axis. Common patterns worth recognising: "same" padding for a 3×3 stride-1 layer is p = 1 and keeps the size (n + 2 − 3 + 1 = n); a 3×3 stride-2 layer with p = 1 halves it (224 → ⌊(224 + 2 − 3)/2⌋ + 1 = 112); a 7×7 stride-2 layer with p = 3 also gives 112. "Valid" convolution uses p = 0 and shrinks the map by k − 1. A dilated convolution spaces the kernel taps d apart, so its effective size is d(k − 1) + 1 with no extra weights: a 3×3 kernel with dilation 2 covers 5×5. Stride and pooling are the main tools for shrinking the spatial size while the channel count grows, so the total number of activations shrinks slowly while the receptive field grows quickly. AlexNet's sequence of spatial sizes is a good example: 224 → 55 (conv 11×11, stride 4, padding 2) → 27 (max pool 3×3, stride 2) → 27 → 13 → 13 → 13 → 13 → 6 (final pool), and 6 × 6 × 256 = 9,216 is the input width of its first dense layer.

5. Weight sharing and translation equivariance

Because every position uses the same kernel, shifting the input shifts the output: if a cat's eye detector fires at (30, 40) in one photo and the cat moves 20 pixels to the right in the next, the detector fires at (30, 60). This is translation equivariance, and the lab verifies it numerically. It has two practical consequences. First, the parameters are shared, so a pattern learned in one place is useful everywhere: with far fewer parameters the network sees effectively more training signal per weight. Second, the gradients (dl1) of a shared kernel are the sum of the gradients at all positions where it was applied, so a kernel used 12,544 times receives a well-averaged gradient, which is part of why CNNs train stably. The other side of the coin is that convolution is not a good fit for data where position matters absolutely (a chessboard, a fixed-layout form): there a dense or attention layer, or added coordinate channels, is more natural.

Equivariance is not invariance. A classifier should output "cat" wherever the cat is, and that comes from the layers on top: pooling takes a maximum over a window, so a small shift of the pattern does not change the pooled output, and global average pooling at the end averages each channel over all positions, discarding where the pattern occurred and keeping how strongly it occurred.

6. Pooling, stride and going down the pyramid

Max pooling with a 2×2 window and stride 2 replaces each 2×2 block by its maximum, halving both dimensions. For the block [[1, 3], [2, 8]] the output is 8; if the bright pixel moves to another cell of the same block the output is still 8. It has no parameters, reduces computation for later layers and adds slight shift tolerance. Average pooling takes the mean. Many modern networks replace pooling with a strided convolution (stride 2), which learns its downsampling. At the end, global average pooling turns a C×H×W map into a C-vector (each channel averaged over all positions), which replaced the enormous dense layers of AlexNet and VGG: it has no parameters, works for any input size and, since each channel then acts like a class evidence map, is easier to interpret.

7. The receptive field

The receptive field of a unit is the region of the input that can influence it. A 3×3 convolution unit sees 3×3 pixels of its input. A second 3×3 layer on top sees a 3×3 block of first-layer units, each of which sees 3 pixels of the input, but neighbouring ones overlap, so together they cover 5×5. A third covers 7×7. The figure shows the one-dimensional version: each extra layer of width 3 adds k − 1 = 2.

The general recurrence tracks the receptive field r and the jump j, the distance in input pixels between adjacent units of the current layer. Start with r = 1, j = 1. For a layer with kernel k and stride s: r ← r + (k − 1)·j, then j ← j·s. Worked for conv 3×3 (s 1), conv 3×3 (s 1), max pool 2×2 (s 2), conv 3×3, conv 3×3: r goes 3, 5, then pooling gives 6 with jump 2, then 6 + 2·2 = 10, then 14. So the fourth convolution unit sees a 14×14 patch. For ResNet's opening (7×7/2, 3×3/2 pool, then two 3×3) it is 27. Strides and pooling are why deep CNNs see large regions with few layers: after a stride-2 layer, every extra 3×3 layer adds 4 input pixels rather than 2.

Why stacked small kernels win (the VGG insight). Two stacked 3×3 layers have the same 5×5 receptive field as one 5×5 layer. With C channels in and out, they cost 2 · 9 · C² = 18C² weights instead of 25C² — 28% fewer (at C = 64: 73,728 versus 102,400) — and have two non-linearities instead of one, so they can represent more. Three 3×3 layers replace a 7×7 (27C² versus 49C², 45% fewer). Simonyan and Zisserman built VGG on exactly this, using only 3×3 kernels: "3×3 (which is the smallest size to capture the notion of left/right, up/down, center)". It is why almost every CNN since uses 3×3 kernels.

8. 1×1 convolutions, bottlenecks and depthwise-separable convolutions

A 1×1 convolution has a single-pixel kernel: at each position it computes a weighted sum across the channels only, a small dense layer applied at every position. It cannot see neighbouring pixels, but it can change the number of channels cheaply. That is the basis of the bottleneck block used by ResNet-50 and deeper: a 1×1 convolution reduces 256 channels to 64, a 3×3 convolution works at 64 channels, and a 1×1 restores 256. Weights: 256·64 + 64·64·9 + 64·256 = 16,384 + 36,864 + 16,384 = 69,632, against 2 · 9 · 256² = 1,179,648 for two plain 3×3 convolutions at 256 channels — 17 times fewer for a similar receptive field. The expensive spatial operation runs where there are few channels.

A depthwise-separable convolution (MobileNet, EfficientNet) splits a standard convolution into two cheap steps: a depthwise k×k convolution that filters each channel separately (k²·C weights), and a pointwise 1×1 convolution that mixes channels (C·C_out weights). With k = 3 and 128 channels in and out: standard 9·128·128 = 147,456 weights; separable 9·128 + 128·128 = 1,152 + 16,384 = 17,536 — 8.4 times fewer, matching the ratio 1/C_out + 1/k² = 0.0078 + 0.111 = 0.119. The MobileNet paper reports "between 8 to 9 times less computation" for 3×3 separable convolutions at "only a small reduction in accuracy". A caution for the interview: FLOP counts overstate the speed-up on GPUs, because depthwise layers do very little arithmetic per byte of memory they move (the roofline model of gpu2) and run memory-bound. AlexNet itself used grouped convolutions (each output channel sees only half of the input channels, in two groups) — but as a workaround for splitting the network over two GPUs, each with 3 GB of memory.

9. How convolutions run on hardware

GPUs are built for large matrix multiplications, so convolution libraries turn convolution into one. The classic method is im2col: unroll every k×k×C_in window of the input into a row of a matrix, giving (H_out·W_out) × (k²·C_in); the kernels form a (k²·C_in) × C_out matrix; their product is the whole output. For ResNet's stem: 12,544 × 147 times 147 × 64. The lab implements it and checks it equals the four-loop definition. Because windows overlap, a naive im2col copy inflates the input's memory by roughly k²; production libraries (cuDNN) use implicit GEMM, which forms the windows on the fly, and choose Winograd or FFT-based algorithms for particular kernel sizes (Winograd reduces the multiplications for 3×3 kernels). Practical consequences: use half-precision and a channels-last memory layout so convolutions map to tensor cores (gpu1), fold BatchNorm into the preceding convolution for inference (dl4), and remember that the arithmetic intensity of a layer (multiply-adds per byte moved) — high for dense 3×3 convolutions with many channels, low for depthwise ones and for the very first layer with 3 input channels — decides whether it is compute- or memory-bound.

The step from ResNet-50 to ConvNeXt is instructive. Liu et al. found that simply improving the training recipe (longer training, AdamW, stronger augmentation, regularisation) raised ResNet-50 from 76.1% to 78.8% top-1 before changing any architecture; then a "patchify" stem (4×4 kernel, stride 4, like ViT), depthwise 7×7 kernels, an inverted bottleneck (the hidden layer wider than the input), fewer activation and normalisation layers, GELU and LayerNorm brought the tiny model to 82.0%, above Swin-T's 81.3%. A lot of what was credited to "transformers" in 2020 was training recipe and design choices, and the lesson generalises: when comparing architectures, hold the training recipe fixed.

11. Convolutional networks versus Vision Transformers

A Vision Transformer cuts the image into 16×16 patches (a 224×224 image gives 14 × 14 = 196 patches), projects each patch linearly to a vector (this patch embedding is exactly a convolution with kernel 16, stride 16), adds position embeddings and runs a standard transformer over the 196 tokens. There is no locality or weight sharing after that first layer: attention connects every patch to every other. Dosovitskiy et al. state the trade-off directly: transformers "lack some of the inductive biases inherent to CNNs, such as translation equivariance and locality, and therefore do not generalize well when trained on insufficient amounts of data. However, the picture changes if the models are trained on larger datasets (14M–300M images). We find that large scale training trumps inductive bias." On ImageNet alone, ViT-Large underperformed ViT-Base; only with pretraining on JFT-300M (300 million images) was the full benefit of size seen, and the best model reached 88.55% top-1 on ImageNet. Touvron et al.'s DeiT reached 83.1% top-1 (86M-parameter ViT) using ImageNet only, in under three days on one machine, by adding heavy augmentation and regularisation, and up to 85.2% with distillation from a convolutional teacher — the inductive bias transferred through the teacher's outputs rather than through the architecture. "Inductive bias substitutes for data" is the one-sentence summary; hierarchical transformers such as Swin restore locality by restricting attention to windows.

12. Beyond classification

Convolutional backbones produce feature maps at several resolutions, which is what other tasks need. Object detection predicts boxes and classes (Faster R-CNN, YOLO, DETR); semantic segmentation assigns a class to every pixel (U-Net, DeepLab), decoding with upsampling layers (transposed convolutions, dl10); instance segmentation separates individual objects (Mask R-CNN). In almost all of them the default starting point is a backbone pretrained on ImageNet (or a larger set) and fine-tuned (dl11). Convolutions are not only for images: 1-D convolutions process audio and time series (WaveNet's dilated causal convolutions), text (early sentence classifiers) and long-sequence models, where FFT-based long convolutions reappear in recent architectures; 3-D convolutions process video and volumetric medical scans.

A convolution is a constrained matrix multiply

Flatten the input image into a vector of length n. The convolution's output is a linear function of it, so it equals multiplying by a matrix: an (H_out·W_out·C_out) × (H·W·C_in) matrix W. That matrix is sparse (each output row has only k²·C_in non-zero entries, because of locality) and structured (the non-zero blocks are the same kernel repeated at shifted positions, a block-Toeplitz structure, because of weight sharing). A dense layer would be a general matrix. The inductive bias is precisely the restriction of the matrix to that family; the hypothesis space is smaller, so it contains fewer functions, but the functions that matter for images are still in it. This is also why convolutions can be implemented with any fast linear-algebra method: FFT-based convolution multiplies in the frequency domain, with cost O(n log n) regardless of kernel size, which is why very long 1-D convolutions are practical in sequence models.

Equivariance, aliasing and what pooling breaks

A convolution with stride 1 is exactly translation-equivariant on an infinite domain. Two things spoil this in practice. First, boundaries: zero padding lets the network detect absolute position near the edges (Islam et al., 2020, showed CNNs can encode absolute position from padding), so equivariance is approximate. Second, downsampling: striding or pooling by 2 samples the feature map at half the rate, and if the map has fine detail (high frequencies) shifting the input by one pixel can change which samples are kept, so outputs change discontinuously. This is aliasing, in the signal-processing sense. Zhang (2019), "Making Convolutional Networks Shift-Invariant Again", showed that modern CNNs' predictions can change substantially under one-pixel shifts and that low-pass filtering (blurring) before subsampling restores much of the lost consistency and improves accuracy and robustness. CNNs are also not equivariant to rotation or scale; group-equivariant CNNs build those symmetries in, at the cost of more computation, while augmentation approximates them cheaply.

Effective receptive field and large kernels

The theoretical receptive field bounds where information could come from, but the influence of an input pixel on an output falls off with distance: Luo et al. (2016) showed the effective receptive field is roughly Gaussian and occupies only a fraction of the theoretical one, and that it grows only like the square root of depth for standard stacks. Practical responses: dilated convolutions (widen without parameters; used in WaveNet and segmentation networks), feature pyramids (combine scales), large depthwise kernels (ConvNeXt's 7×7, RepLKNet's up to 31×31, cheap because depthwise) and attention, which has a global receptive field from the first layer. ViTs' promise is exactly that no information is more than one layer away; their cost is the quadratic attention and the loss of the locality prior.

Texture bias, shape bias and robustness

Networks trained on ImageNet turn out to rely heavily on local texture: Geirhos et al. (2019) showed that ImageNet-trained CNNs classify an image with a cat's shape and an elephant's skin texture as an elephant, contrary to the human preference for shape, and that training on stylised images that break texture cues increases shape bias, accuracy under distortion and robustness. Convolutions' locality plausibly encourages texture reliance, and it partly explains sensitivity to noise and adversarial perturbations that leave shape intact. Practical consequence: expect distribution shift (a new camera, weather, compression) to hurt more than the benchmark suggests, evaluate on shifted data, and use augmentation, pretraining at scale or robust architectures where it matters.

Scaling CNNs: compound scaling and the training recipe

Tan and Le (EfficientNet, 2019) observed that scaling depth, width or input resolution alone saturates quickly, and proposed compound scaling: increase all three together by fixed ratios found by a small grid search. Their EfficientNet-B7 reached 84.3% top-1 on ImageNet while being 8.4 times smaller and 6.1 times faster on inference than the best previous ConvNet. Later work (FixRes, Touvron et al., 2019) showed a train–test resolution discrepancy: objects appear larger in random-crop training images than in centre-crop test images, so testing at a higher resolution than training improves accuracy without retraining — a reminder that preprocessing is part of the model. The ConvNeXt ablation adds that most of the recent gains attributed to architecture are shared with the training recipe.

Vision Transformers, hybrids and self-supervision

ViT's data-hunger is mitigated in several ways: stronger augmentation and distillation (DeiT); self-supervised pretraining on unlabeled images (masked autoencoders, DINO, dl11); hierarchical designs with windowed attention (Swin), which reintroduce locality and a multi-scale pyramid so that the model works for dense tasks; and hybrids that use a convolutional stem or convolutional blocks early (where locality is most useful) and attention later. In practice the best choice is often decided by data and deployment: CNNs and hybrids are efficient and strong at moderate data scales and on edge devices; ViTs scale well with large pretraining, are the vision encoders inside multimodal systems (CLIP-style encoders, vision-language models) and share tooling with language transformers.

Quantisation, fusion and deployment

CNNs quantise well: their activations after ReLU are non-negative with moderate ranges, and int8 inference typically loses well under one point of accuracy with calibration on a small dataset. Deployment pipelines fuse convolution, batch normalisation and ReLU into a single kernel (the fold from dl4, then an activation epilogue), choose per-channel weight scales, and use channels-last layout. TensorRT and similar compilers do this automatically; the engineering work is validating that the fused, quantised model matches the reference on a held-out set and that the preprocessing (channel order, mean/std normalisation, resize interpolation) is identical in training and serving.

The public case: AlexNet, 2012

Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton's "ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks" (NeurIPS 2012) is the paper that started the deep-learning era in vision. The facts below are from the paper. The network takes a 224×224×3 image (the paper's own figure says 224; implementations use padding to make the arithmetic work) and has five convolutional layers followed by three fully connected layers and a 1,000-way softmax: conv1 with 96 kernels of 11×11×3 at stride 4; conv2 with 256 kernels of 5×5×48; conv3 with 384 kernels of 3×3×256; conv4 with 384 kernels of 3×3×192; conv5 with 256 kernels of 3×3×192; then two dense layers of 4,096 neurons and the output layer. It has "60 million parameters and 650,000 neurons". My recount of the layer definitions gives 60,965,224 parameters: 2.33 million in the five convolutions and 58.6 million in the three dense layers, so 96% of the weights are in the classifier, while the convolutions account for about 92% of the roughly 724 million multiply-adds (the lab prints both).

The ingredients they list as responsible for the result are a checklist of this course. ReLU (dl2): a four-layer CNN on CIFAR-10 reached 25% training error six times faster than with tanh, and the authors say they "would not have been able to experiment with such large neural networks" otherwise. GPUs: the network was too big for one card, so it was split over two GTX 580 GPUs with 3 GB each, communicating only at certain layers (the cause of the grouped convolutions), and trained for roughly 90 passes over the 1.2 million training images in five to six days. Regularisation (dl8): dropout with probability 0.5 in the first two dense layers, which they found roughly doubled the iterations to converge but prevented substantial overfitting, and data augmentation — random 224×224 crops of 256×256 images plus horizontal flips (a 2,048-fold enlargement of the set) and PCA-based colour jitter. Optimisation (dl5): SGD with batch size 128, momentum 0.9 and weight decay 0.0005, a learning rate of 0.01 divided by 10 whenever validation error stopped improving (three times in all), with the remark that "weight decay here is not merely a regularizer: it reduces the model's training error". Also local response normalisation and overlapping pooling (3×3 windows with stride 2), which lowered error by about 1.4/1.2 and 0.4/0.3 points respectively (top-1/top-5) — details later dropped in favour of BatchNorm.

Results: on ILSVRC-2010, top-1 and top-5 test error of 37.5% and 17.0%, against 47.1% and 28.2% for the best competition entry, an average of six sparse-coding models. On ILSVRC-2012, a single CNN had 18.2% top-5 validation error, an average of five CNNs 16.4%, and the winning entry, which averaged seven CNNs including two pre-trained on the full 15-million-image ImageNet and fine-tuned, achieved 15.3% top-5 test error against 26.2% for the second-best entry. The same year's field of competitors used engineered features. The following years' winners (VGG, GoogLeNet, ResNet) were all deeper CNNs, and GoogLeNet's 6.67% top-5 error came with "12× fewer parameters than the winning architecture of Krizhevsky et al." — the dense layers were the first thing to go.

Failure modes in production vision systems

Preprocessing mismatch (the number one bug). A model trained on RGB images normalised with the ImageNet mean and standard deviation, served with BGR images (OpenCV's default), unnormalised pixels, or a different resize interpolation (bilinear versus bicubic, PIL versus OpenCV, with or without anti-aliasing), degrades silently, often by several points, without any error. Put the exact preprocessing in the model artefact and test it: run the same image through the training pipeline and the serving pipeline and compare the tensors.

Train–test resolution and crop mismatch. Training with random-resized crops and testing with centre crops at the same size makes object sizes differ; FixRes showed that a higher test resolution helps (and that fine-tuning at test resolution helps more). If accuracy is off after a change to image size, suspect object scale.

BatchNorm in the wrong mode. A detector fine-tuned with batches of 2 with BatchNorm in training mode corrupts its running statistics (dl4); freeze BatchNorm or use GroupNorm.

Distribution shift. A new camera, lighting, compression level or seasonal change shifts the input statistics; because CNNs are texture-sensitive (deep section), the drop can be large. Monitor input statistics (mean brightness, blur, resolution), track confidence and prediction distribution over time, and keep a labelled sample from production for periodic evaluation.

Latency surprises. FLOPs are a poor guide to speed: depthwise networks are memory-bound on GPUs, small batches leave tensor cores idle, and the first layers (few channels, huge spatial size) and the last (post-processing such as non-maximum suppression in detectors) can dominate. Profile on the target hardware with the real batch size (gpu11), use channels-last and mixed precision, and consider TensorRT/ONNX runtime with int8 calibration.

Small objects lost to downsampling. A backbone with total stride 32 turns a 16-pixel object into half a feature-map cell; detectors add feature pyramids or run at higher input resolution. If small objects are missed, increase resolution or add a higher-resolution feature level before making the model bigger.

Label noise and shortcuts. Because CNNs pick up whatever cue is predictive (backgrounds, watermarks, scanner artefacts in medical images), a high validation score can mean the model learned the shortcut; inspect saliency maps and evaluate on data from a different source.

What to monitor

Input: image size, brightness and contrast histograms, blur, file-format mix. Model: prediction class distribution, mean confidence, the fraction of low-confidence predictions, and per-slice accuracy on the labelled production sample. System: per-stage latency (decode, resize, model, post-process), GPU utilisation and memory, batch-size distribution.

import numpy as np

def conv2d_naive(x, k, stride=1, pad=0):
    """x: (C_in, H, W), k: (C_out, C_in, kh, kw) -> (C_out, H_out, W_out). Four loops, exactly the definition."""
    x = np.pad(x, ((0, 0), (pad, pad), (pad, pad)))
    C_out, C_in, kh, kw = k.shape
    H_out, W_out = (x.shape[1] - kh) // stride + 1, (x.shape[2] - kw) // stride + 1
    out = np.zeros((C_out, H_out, W_out))
    for o in range(C_out):
        for i in range(H_out):
            for j in range(W_out):
                window = x[:, i * stride:i * stride + kh, j * stride:j * stride + kw]
                out[o, i, j] = (window * k[o]).sum()
    return out

def conv2d_im2col(x, k, stride=1, pad=0):
    """The same convolution as ONE matrix multiply: unroll every window into a row (this is what GPUs do)."""
    x = np.pad(x, ((0, 0), (pad, pad), (pad, pad)))
    C_out, C_in, kh, kw = k.shape
    H_out, W_out = (x.shape[1] - kh) // stride + 1, (x.shape[2] - kw) // stride + 1
    cols = np.stack([x[:, i * stride:i * stride + kh, j * stride:j * stride + kw].ravel()
                     for i in range(H_out) for j in range(W_out)])          # (H_out*W_out, C_in*kh*kw)
    return (cols @ k.reshape(C_out, -1).T).T.reshape(C_out, H_out, W_out)

# ---- 5. receptive field of a stack: r += (k-1) * jump; jump *= stride
def receptive_field(stack):
    r, jump = 1, 1
    for k, s in stack: r += (k - 1) * jump; jump *= s
    return r

What to look for.

  1. The valid convolution prints [[-6, -3, 5], [-2, -1, 0], [3, 2, -3]], the numbers of section 2; with padding 1 the output is 5×5 (first row 3, −5, 0, 4, −3). im2col gives identical values; the last line of the block confirms a match with torch.nn.functional.conv2d when PyTorch is available.
  2. Equivariance: rolling the image 2 pixels to the right moves the feature map 2 columns to the right. The check compares interior columns, avoiding the border where the wrap-around of np.roll and zero padding matter.
  3. Edge response: on an image with a dark left half (0) and bright right half (10), one row of output is [0, 0, 30, 30, 0, 0]: zero on flat regions, 30 = 3 rows × 10 at the boundary, two positions wide because the window overlaps the edge at two offsets.
  4. Counts: ResNet's stem has 9,472 parameters and 118.0 million multiply-adds and would need 1.21×10¹¹ weights as a dense layer; AlexNet's conv layers hold 3.8% of the weights but 91.9% of the arithmetic, its fully connected layers 96.2% of the weights.
  5. Receptive fields: 7, 14 and 27, matching the worked examples.

Work it out

Input 224×224×3. First layer: 64 filters, 7×7, stride 2, padding 3. Compute the output shape and the parameter count. Then compare two stacked 3×3 convolutions with one 5×5, at 64 channels in and out, and say how the numbers change with a bottleneck.

Answer

Output: ⌊(224 + 2·3 − 7)/2⌋ + 1 = ⌊223/2⌋ + 1 = 111 + 1 = 112, so 112 × 112 × 64.

Parameters: 7 × 7 × 3 × 64 + 64 = 9,408 + 64 = 9,472. Tiny, which is the point of weight sharing: the same map as a dense layer would need 224·224·3 × 112·112·64 ≈ 1.2 × 10¹¹ weights. The layer does 112·112·64·147 ≈ 118 million multiply-adds, because each weight is reused at 12,544 positions.

Two 3×3 versus one 5×5 at 64 channels:

  • One 5×5: 5 × 5 × 64 × 64 = 102,400 parameters, receptive field 5×5, one non-linearity.
  • Two 3×3: 2 × (3 × 3 × 64 × 64) = 73,728 parameters, receptive field also 5×5, two non-linearities.

28% fewer parameters, the same receptive field and more expressive power: the VGG insight, and why nothing modern uses large dense kernels. With a bottleneck at 256 channels: 1×1 (256→64) + 3×3 (64→64) + 1×1 (64→256) = 16,384 + 36,864 + 16,384 = 69,632 weights, versus 2 × 9 × 256² = 1,179,648 for two plain 3×3 layers: 17 times fewer.

The transferable idea: locality and weight sharing are a prior, an assumption about the data baked into the architecture. A Vision Transformer discards that prior and has to learn the relationships from data, which is why it needs far more of it to win.

Trace a receptive field

A network stacks: conv 3×3 (stride 1), conv 3×3 (stride 1), max-pool 2×2 (stride 2), conv 3×3 (stride 1), conv 3×3 (stride 1). What is the receptive field of a unit in the last layer, and what is it if the third layer is a stride-2 convolution with a 3×3 kernel instead of the pool?

Answer

Use r ← r + (k − 1)·j, then j ← j·s, starting at r = 1, j = 1.

  • conv 3×3 s1: r = 1 + 2·1 = 3, j = 1.
  • conv 3×3 s1: r = 3 + 2·1 = 5, j = 1.
  • pool 2×2 s2: r = 5 + 1·1 = 6, j = 2.
  • conv 3×3 s1: r = 6 + 2·2 = 10, j = 2.
  • conv 3×3 s1: r = 10 + 2·2 = 14, j = 2.

With a stride-2 3×3 convolution instead of the pool: after the second layer r = 5, j = 1; the strided conv gives r = 5 + 2·1 = 7 and j = 2; then r = 7 + 4 = 11 and 11 + 4 = 15. The difference (15 versus 14) is that the strided convolution's kernel is 3 wide rather than 2. The point to state: after a stride-2 layer every additional 3×3 layer adds 4 input pixels rather than 2, which is how deep CNNs cover large regions with few layers, and why the effective receptive field (which is smaller) still limits how much context they actually use.
