Read the sentence "The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too big." What does "it" refer to? You know instantly that it is the trophy, because you have been carrying the sentence's meaning along in your head while you read. Now imagine asking a computer to do the same, or to translate the sentence into French, to transcribe a spoken one, to predict the next word in a text message, or to forecast tomorrow's electricity demand from the last month of readings. In every one of these the input is a sequence: its elements arrive in order, meaning depends on that order and on everything that came before ("not good" is not "good"), and different examples have different lengths. A feed-forward network wants a fixed number of inputs and has no notion of "before"; feeding it a sentence as a fixed-size bag of numbers throws away exactly what matters.

The natural fix, invented in the 1980s, is to let a network read the sequence one element at a time while carrying a small vector of memory, its hidden state, from each step to the next. That is a recurrent neural network (RNN). It is elegant — the same few weight matrices are reused at every step, so the model has the same size for a ten-word sentence and a ten-thousand-word document — and it worked for short-range tasks. It broke on long ones, and the reason is by now a familiar one. To learn that a word fifty steps back matters, the error signal must travel back through fifty steps, each multiplying it by a factor (dl3). Multiply fifty numbers slightly below 1 and nothing is left: the network cannot learn what it cannot feel. Sepp Hochreiter analysed this in his 1991 diploma thesis, and in 1997 he and Jürgen Schmidhuber proposed the long short-term memory (LSTM), a memory cell protected by learned gates, so that gradients could travel a thousand steps.

From roughly 2014 to 2018 LSTMs and their cousin the GRU were the standard tool for speech, handwriting, language modelling and machine translation. A four-layer LSTM encoder–decoder matched the best phrase-based translation systems in 2014, and in 2016 an eight-layer LSTM system with attention went into production at Google Translate. Then the transformer replaced them almost everywhere, and a whole generation of engineers stopped learning them. That is a mistake for interviews, for two reasons. First, the transformer is defined against its predecessor: attention was invented to fix a specific flaw of recurrent encoder–decoders, and you cannot explain why transformers scale, why they need a KV cache, or what state-space models such as Mamba are trying to recover without understanding what recurrence gave up. Second, recurrent models are quietly back — constant memory per token, linear-time inference, on-device speech recognition — so "what comes after the transformer?" is a live question with a recurrent-flavoured answer.

This lesson builds all of it from the ground up. We start with the plain RNN and work a forward pass by hand, then derive why the gradient vanishes with real numbers, and build the LSTM from that diagnosis: the design principle first, then the equations, then one complete cell step with every value computed. We cover the GRU, the standard architectures (stacked, bidirectional, encoder–decoder), teacher forcing and beam search, the fixed-vector bottleneck and attention with a worked example, and finally why recurrent models lost on throughput and how state-space models and linear attention try to get the best of both. The public case is the original 1997 LSTM paper and the 2014 sequence-to-sequence system it enabled, checked against their numbers, and the lab measures the vanishing gradient directly.

Reading with an index card

Imagine reading a long book with a single index card on which you may write a short summary. After each page you look at the card and at the page, then rewrite the whole card. That is an RNN: the card is the hidden state, and each step's update mixes the old summary with the new input, overwriting the card. It has an obvious weakness. If you rewrite the entire card every page, something you noted 200 pages ago survives only if it happens to be re-copied, unchanged, hundreds of times in a row. Information fades, and with it the ability to learn from what happened long ago.

Now change the rules: you have a notebook (the LSTM's cell state) that you do not rewrite. On each page you make three decisions: what to erase from the notebook (the forget gate), what new note to write (the input gate), and how much of the notebook to read out onto your index card (the output gate). Most of the notebook is carried along untouched, so old notes persist for as long as you choose not to erase them. Where the analogy breaks: the decisions are not made by a person but by small learned functions of the current page and the card, and each decision is a smooth number between 0 (none) and 1 (all) rather than yes/no, so the whole thing can be trained by gradient descent.

One step of a plain RNN

h_t = tanh( W · x_t + U · h_(t−1) + b )          new state = squash( input term + previous-state term + bias )
y_t = V · h_t                                     optional output at each step

x_t is the input at step t (a word embedding, say), h_(t−1) the previous hidden state, and W, U, V, b are learned weights, the same at every step. Reading "the cat sat": h₁ summarises "the", h₂ summarises "the cat" and h₃ "the cat sat". The final state can feed a classifier (sentiment), or every step's output can predict the next word (a language model).

The memory problem, in one paragraph

Training unrolls the network over time and backpropagates through every step (backpropagation through time, BPTT). The gradient that reaches step 1 from step 50 has been multiplied by the recurrent Jacobian 49 times, so it vanishes or explodes (dl3). A plain RNN struggles to connect things more than about 10–20 steps apart. The LSTM's answer is the notebook above: a memory updated by addition, not by repeated overwriting, so information and gradients can survive hundreds of steps. A GRU is a streamlined version with two gates. Everything that follows is the detail: first the plain RNN and its failure with numbers, then the LSTM that was designed to fix that failure, then the encoder–decoder systems built from it and the bottleneck that led to attention.

1. Sequence tasks and their shapes

Recurrent networks are used in four common configurations, which differ in when inputs are read and outputs produced.

  • Many-to-one: read the whole sequence, output once (sentiment of a review, the class of a time series). Use the final hidden state.
  • Many-to-many, aligned: an output at every step (tagging each word with its part of speech, frame-by-frame speech labels).
  • Language modelling: an aligned case where the target is the input shifted by one: at each step predict the next token. This is self-supervised (ml1): the text supplies its own labels, and the loss is cross-entropy on the next token, the same objective GPT-style models train on (dl1's softmax and p − y).
  • Sequence-to-sequence: an encoder RNN reads the input sequence into a state, a decoder RNN generates a different-length output from it (translation, summarisation).

In code a batch is a tensor of shape (batch, time, features). Sequences in a batch have different lengths, so the short ones are padded and a mask records which positions are real: the loss must ignore padded positions, and the recurrence must not let padding update the state of a sequence that has ended (frameworks provide packed sequences for this).

2. The plain RNN, worked by hand

The cell is h_t = tanh(w·x_t + u·h_(t−1) + b). Take the smallest case: scalar input and state, input weight w = 0.5, recurrent weight u = 0.8, bias 0, start state h₀ = 0, and the input sequence (1, 0, 0), a single "signal" followed by silence, like a word that the network should remember.

h₁ = tanh(0.5·1 + 0.8·0)      = tanh(0.500) = 0.462
h₂ = tanh(0.5·0 + 0.8·0.462)  = tanh(0.370) = 0.354
h₃ = tanh(0.5·0 + 0.8·0.354)  = tanh(0.283) = 0.276

Without further input the state decays: 0.462 → 0.354 → 0.276 → 0.217. The network is forgetting the signal at about 20–25% per step. With vectors the same happens along each direction of the recurrent matrix U, with the rate set by U's eigenvalues and the tanh's slope. The number of parameters of an RNN layer with input size m and hidden size n is n(m + n) + n (input matrix, recurrent matrix and bias); for m = 300, n = 512 that is 416,256, independent of how long the sequences are.

3. Backpropagation through time and the vanishing gradient, with numbers

Unroll the network (figure above) and it is an ordinary, very deep network with shared weights. Backprop (dl1) then works exactly as before, except that a shared weight's gradient is the sum over all the steps at which it was used. To see how a loss at step T depends on the state at step 1 we need ∂h_T/∂h_1, and by the chain rule it is a product of one Jacobian per step:

∂h_T/∂h_1 = ∏_{t=2..T}  diag(1 − h_t²) · U          (the tanh slope is 1 − h², then the recurrent weights)

In our scalar example each factor is u·(1 − h_t²). Step 2 to 3: 0.8 × (1 − 0.276²) = 0.8 × 0.924 = 0.739; step 1 to 2: 0.8 × (1 − 0.354²) = 0.700. Multiplying: ∂h₃/∂h₁ = 0.739 × 0.700 = 0.517. Continuing the recurrence, the gradient from step 1 to step T is 0.517 at T = 3, 0.306 at T = 5, 0.096 at T = 10, 0.010 at T = 20 and 1.3×10⁻⁵ at T = 50. The pure weight factor 0.8ᵀ⁻¹ is 1.8×10⁻⁵ at T = 50; the tanh slope trims a little more. A word 50 steps back has a millionth of a say in the gradient, so training cannot discover that it matters.

For a vector state the story is the same with matrices: the gain is set by how U (and the tanh slopes) stretch vectors, roughly the spectral radius (largest eigenvalue magnitude) of U to the power T. The lab measures this for a 16-unit tanh RNN: the largest singular value of the product of Jacobians is 1.0 after one step, 2×10⁻⁴ after 10 and 2×10⁻²² after 50 for a recurrent matrix scaled to spectral radius 0.9. Even with radius 1.2, above 1, the tanh saturates and the product still vanishes (6.9×10⁻²⁹ at step 100), a reminder from dl3 that saturating activations turn large weights into vanishing gradients; genuine explosions in RNNs happen when the units stay in the sensitive region. Two standard remedies for the explosive side (both from dl3): gradient clipping by global norm, and truncated BPTT: process the sequence in chunks of k steps, carry the hidden state across chunks but detach it so gradients flow back at most k steps. Truncation bounds cost and memory (BPTT stores the activations of every step, about 6n numbers per example per step for an LSTM: 3.2 GB per layer for n = 1,024, batch 64 and 2,048 steps) at the price of never learning dependencies longer than k.

4. The LSTM: design principle

Hochreiter's 1991 analysis (reviewed in the 1997 paper) says that in a recurrent unit with a single self-connection the error signal is multiplied at each step by f′(net)·w. For a logistic sigmoid f′ is at most 0.25, so unless the weight exceeds 4 the error decays exponentially (the paper's "if |w| < 4.0, error flow tends to vanish"). The remedy they propose is almost a tautology: to get error flow that neither vanishes nor explodes, make that factor exactly 1. A unit with a linear activation and a self-connection of weight 1 does that; they named it the constant error carousel (CEC). A unit that only ever remembers is useless, so they wrapped it in multiplicative gates that learn when to write to it and when to read from it: "multiplicative gate units learn to open and close access to the constant error flow". That is the whole invention: an additive memory path with gain 1, guarded by learned gates. The gradient that would have been forced through repeated squashing now travels along the carousel.

5. The LSTM cell, equation by equation

At each step the LSTM computes four quantities from the same inputs, the current input x_t and the previous hidden state h_(t−1): three gates (sigmoids, so values in (0, 1)) and one candidate (a tanh, values in (−1, 1)).

f_t = σ( W_f x_t + U_f h_(t−1) + b_f )       forget gate    — how much of the old cell to keep
i_t = σ( W_i x_t + U_i h_(t−1) + b_i )       input gate     — how much of the candidate to write
g_t = tanh( W_g x_t + U_g h_(t−1) + b_g )    candidate      — the new content
o_t = σ( W_o x_t + U_o h_(t−1) + b_o )       output gate    — how much of the cell to expose
c_t = f_t ⊙ c_(t−1) + i_t ⊙ g_t              cell state     — additive memory update  (⊙ = elementwise product)
h_t = o_t ⊙ tanh(c_t)                        hidden state   — what the rest of the network sees

One LSTM step, every number. One input and one hidden unit so every value can be followed. Weights (input, recurrent, bias) for the gates: forget (0.5, 0.5, +1), input (0.6, 0.4, 0), candidate (1.0, 0.5, 0), output (0.7, 0.3, 0). State before the step: h_(t−1) = 0.5, c_(t−1) = 1.0; input x_t = 2.0.

Read it: the cell kept 90% of its old memory (f = 0.905) and wrote 0.785 of new content (i·g). The backward pass through this step shows the design at work. The derivative of the new cell with respect to the old one is exactly ∂c_t/∂c_(t−1) = f = 0.905, a single gate value — no weight matrix, no squashing slope. The gradient over the next T steps along the cell path is the product of the forget gates: with f ≈ 0.95 constant it is 0.95⁵⁰ = 0.077 after 50 steps, with f ≈ 0.99 it is 0.605, with f ≈ 0.999 it is 0.951, whereas a plain RNN with per-step factor 0.7 has 10⁻⁸ after 50 steps. The network can learn to push f toward 1 for information it needs to keep. The lab verifies the gate values, and PyTorch's LSTMCell computes the same thing (its gate order is input, forget, candidate, output).

What this buys, and what it does not. The lab's measurement is the honest picture: an LSTM with a forget-gate bias of 1 still lost a factor of 10⁻⁸ over 50 steps, only better than the plain RNN's 10⁻²². The architecture makes long memory possible and learnable: gate biases can be pushed high, the network can learn f near 1 where memory is needed, and the gradient along the cell path survives. It does not make memory automatic. Initialising the forget-gate bias to a positive value (1 or more; Gers et al. 2000 and Jozefowicz et al. 2015 report it matters) starts the cell in "remember by default" mode, since a bias of 0 gives f = 0.5 and halves the memory each step. Tallec and Ollivier's "chrono initialisation" goes further and sets forget biases so that units cover a spread of time scales matching the expected dependency lengths.

History and variants. The 1997 LSTM had input and output gates but no forget gate: the carousel simply kept everything, which makes it hard to reset. Gers, Schmidhuber and Cummins (2000) added the forget gate. Peephole connections let gates see the cell state; coupled input–forget gates set i = 1 − f. Greff et al. ("LSTM: A Search Space Odyssey") ablated the variants and found none clearly better than the standard cell, with the forget gate and output activation function the most important parts.

Size. Each of the four blocks has an input matrix, a recurrent matrix and a bias, so an LSTM layer has 4·(n(m + n) + n) parameters: for m = 300 and n = 512, 4 × 416,256 = 1,665,024 (PyTorch, which stores two bias vectors per block, reports 1,667,072 — the lab checks against the library when it is installed). It is four times a plain RNN's parameter count and roughly four times its computation per step.

6. The GRU

Cho et al. (2014) proposed the gated recurrent unit, a simplification of the LSTM with no separate cell state and two gates:

z_t = σ( W_z x_t + U_z h_(t−1) + b_z )              update gate — how much to move toward the new candidate
r_t = σ( W_r x_t + U_r h_(t−1) + b_r )              reset gate  — how much of the old state feeds the candidate
h̃_t = tanh( W x_t + U (r_t ⊙ h_(t−1)) + b )         candidate state
h_t = (1 − z_t) ⊙ h_(t−1) + z_t ⊙ h̃_t              interpolate between old state and candidate

(Conventions differ: Cho et al. and PyTorch define z as the keep gate, h_t = z ⊙ h_(t−1) + (1 − z) ⊙ h̃_t; the two are the same model with z ↔ 1 − z.) The last line is the GRU's additive path: when z_t is near 0 the state is copied unchanged, so gradients pass with gain 1 − z_t, the same idea as the LSTM's forget gate (here the forget and input gates are tied: keep the fraction 1 − z of the old, write z of the new). It has three weight blocks instead of four (1,248,768 parameters at m = 300, n = 512 versus 1,665,024) and one state vector instead of two. Chung et al. (2014) compared LSTM and GRU on music and speech modelling and found both clearly better than plain tanh units and roughly comparable to each other. In practice LSTMs are the default when in doubt and GRUs are chosen for speed and size.

7. Architectures built from recurrent cells

  • Stacked RNNs: the hidden-state sequence of layer 1 is the input sequence of layer 2. Depth adds capacity; Sutskever et al. found four-layer LSTMs clearly better than shallow ones. Residual connections between layers (GNMT) allow eight or more.
  • Bidirectional RNNs: one RNN reads left to right, another right to left, and their states are concatenated at each position, so every position sees its whole context. Right for tagging, classification and encoders; impossible for generation (the future does not exist).
  • Encoder–decoder (seq2seq): the encoder's final state initialises the decoder, which generates the output one token at a time, feeding back its own previous output.
  • Teacher forcing. During training the decoder is fed the true previous token, not its own prediction; this stabilises and parallelises training but creates exposure bias: at inference the decoder consumes its own (possibly wrong) tokens, a situation it never saw. Scheduled sampling mixes the two.
  • Decoding. Greedy decoding picks the top token at each step; beam search keeps the k best partial sequences (Sutskever et al. found a beam of just 2 captured most of the benefit; they used 12 for their headline result), typically with a length normalisation so that short outputs are not favoured.

8. The fixed-vector bottleneck and attention

In the basic encoder–decoder the encoder must compress the entire source sentence into one vector, the final hidden state, from which the decoder generates everything. A fixed-size vector has fixed capacity, so quality degrades on long sentences. Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio (2014) "conjecture that the use of a fixed-length vector is a bottleneck" and propose to let the decoder look back at all of the encoder's states and choose. At each output step i the decoder computes a score between its current state and each encoder state h_j, turns the scores into weights with a softmax, and takes the weighted sum as that step's context:

e_ij = score(s_(i−1), h_j)          α_ij = softmax_j(e_ij)          c_i = Σ_j α_ij · h_j

Worked example. Three encoder states h₁ = (1, 0), h₂ = (0, 1), h₃ = (1, 1). At some decoder step the scores are (2.0, 1.0, 0.1). Exponentials: 7.389, 2.718, 1.105, summing to 11.213, so the weights are α = (0.659, 0.242, 0.099) (the same numbers as the softmax in dl1). The context is c = 0.659·(1, 0) + 0.242·(0, 1) + 0.099·(1, 1) = (0.758, 0.341): dominated by the encoder state the decoder found most relevant. Nothing is compressed: at every step the decoder can pick a different mixture.

9. Why recurrent models lost, and what remains

Two structural limits, both visible in the numbers, ended the RNN era for large models.

  1. No parallelism over time. The state at step t needs the state at step t−1, in both the forward and the backward pass. A 2,048-token sequence is a chain of 2,048 dependent matrix multiplications, each small (batch × n by n × 4n), so a GPU with tens of thousands of cores sits mostly idle. A transformer computes all 2,048 positions in a handful of large matrix multiplies, with a causal mask enforcing order. That difference in hardware utilisation, more than any difference in modelling power, is why transformers scaled to trillions of tokens.
  2. Limited effective memory. Even a well-trained LSTM does not use its whole history. Khandelwal et al. (2018), "Sharp Nearby, Fuzzy Far Away", measured LSTM language models and found they use "about 200 tokens of context on average", with word order mattering only for the nearest ~50 tokens. And a fixed-size state cannot store an arbitrary earlier token exactly: exact copying and recall are hard, while attention can look back at any position directly.

The attention-versus-recurrence trade at inference is easy to state with numbers. A 7-billion-parameter transformer (32 layers, width 4,096, bf16) keeps a KV cache of 2 (keys and values) × 32 × 4,096 × 2 bytes = 0.5 MB per token (gpu3); at 4,096 tokens that is 2 GB per sequence. A recurrent model of the same width and depth keeps an LSTM state of 2 (h and c) × 32 × 4,096 × 2 bytes = 0.5 MB in total, whatever the context length: one token of transformer cache equals the entire recurrent state. That constant memory and constant per-token cost is why recurrent ideas keep coming back.

Why the LSTM's gradient is only partly protected

Differentiate the LSTM step and the state Jacobian has two parts. Along the cell state, ∂c_t/∂c_(t−1) = diag(f_t), plus terms that arrive through the gates' dependence on h_(t−1) (each gate is a function of h_(t−1), and h_(t−1) depends on c_(t−1) through o ⊙ tanh(c)). The first term is the protected carousel: it has no weight matrix and no squashing slope. The second contains the usual products of weight matrices and slopes and can vanish or explode like a plain RNN's. Hochreiter and Schmidhuber's paper acknowledged this by truncating the gradient at the gates in the original algorithm ("truncating the gradient where this does not do harm"), so that error flows through the carousel only and not around the recurrent connections. Modern implementations use full BPTT, but the picture stands: the guaranteed part of the gradient is the cell-state path, whose gain is the product of forget gates, and long-range learning succeeds when the network drives those gates to 1. Corollaries: gate saturation near 1 is desired for memory cells and a symptom of a problem for forgetting ones; initial forget-gate biases set the initial time scale of memory (a gate value f corresponds to a time constant of 1/(1 − f) steps: 0.73 gives about 4 steps, 0.99 gives 100); and layer normalisation applied inside the cell stabilises the gate pre-activations.

Regularisation and optimisation of LSTM language models

Before transformers, the best language models were heavily regularised LSTMs. Merity, Keskar and Socher's AWD-LSTM (2017) is the canonical recipe: weight-dropped recurrent connections (DropConnect on the hidden-to-hidden matrices), variational dropout (the same dropout mask at every time step within a sequence, rather than fresh noise each step, which would erase memory), embedding dropout, weight tying between input embedding and output softmax, ASGD optimisation, and truncated BPTT with randomised sequence lengths. The same architecture was the base of ULMFiT (dl11), the first widely used pretrain-then-fine-tune recipe for text. The lesson in variational dropout is worth remembering for interviews: ordinary dropout applied independently at each step destroys the recurrent signal, because a memory unit dropped at step 7 loses what it carried from step 6; tying the mask across steps preserves it.

Parallelising a linear recurrence: the scan and the convolution views

The LSTM and GRU are non-linear in h: the gates depend on h_(t−1), so step t genuinely needs step t−1. Take that dependency away and something remarkable happens. A linear recurrence h_t = a_t·h_(t−1) + b_t is a composition of affine maps, and composing two of them gives another: applying (a₁, b₁) then (a₂, b₂) equals the single map (a₂·a₁, a₂·b₁ + b₂). Because that composition is associative, all T states can be computed with a parallel prefix scan in about log₂ T rounds of fully parallel work, instead of T dependent steps. The lab does this for T = 8: 3 rounds instead of 8, with identical results. When a and b do not depend on the position (a fixed linear system), the whole recurrence is a convolution: y_t = Σ_k c·a^k·b·u_(t−k) is convolution of the input with the kernel (c·b, c·a·b, c·a²·b, …). For a = 0.9, b = c = 1 the kernel is (1, 0.9, 0.81, 0.729, …), exactly a geometrically decaying memory, and it can be applied with an FFT in O(T log T), or by the sliding windows of dl6 (a long convolution). So a linear recurrent model has three equivalent forms: a recurrence (O(1) memory per step at inference), a convolution (parallel training), and a scan (parallel training with input-dependent parameters).

State-space models. S4 (Gu et al., 2021) parameterises a continuous-time linear system x′(t) = A x(t) + B u(t), y(t) = C x(t), discretises it to h_t = Ā h_(t−1) + B̄ u_t, and uses a special structured initialisation of A (HiPPO) so the state approximates a running summary of the whole history; it trains as a long convolution and runs as a recurrence. Its weakness is that A, B, C are fixed: the model cannot choose what to remember based on content, which hurts language. Mamba (Gu and Dao, 2023) makes B, C and the step size input-dependent ("selective"), which gives content-based forgetting and writing like an LSTM's gates but in a linear-in-state form, and computes it with a hardware-aware parallel scan (fusing the recurrence into GPU registers and shared memory rather than materialising the expanded state, gpu6). The paper reports 5× higher inference throughput than transformers, linear scaling in sequence length, and Mamba-3B matching transformers twice its size on language modelling. RWKV is a related architecture, trained in parallel like a transformer and run as an RNN. Linear attention (Katharopoulos et al., 2020, "Transformers are RNNs") replaces the softmax with a feature-map kernel so that causal attention becomes S_t = S_(t−1) + φ(k_t) v_tᵀ, y_t = S_tᵀ φ(q_t): an RNN whose state is a matrix. Hybrids interleaving a few attention layers with state-space layers try to keep exact recall where it matters while keeping most layers at constant memory.

The fixed-size state and what it cannot do

A recurrent state of n numbers can carry at most about n·(bits per number) of information about the past, however long the context. Tasks that require exact reproduction of an arbitrary earlier span (copying a long string, looking up a key–value pair mentioned 10,000 tokens ago) need capacity proportional to the context, which an attention layer gets for free because it stores the entire history in the KV cache. Empirical work on recurrent and state-space models finds them weaker than transformers on such recall and copying tasks at matched size, and stronger on cost. That is the trade behind hybrids (a small fraction of attention layers to a majority of recurrent ones) and the reason "transformers are RNNs with a state that grows with the sequence" is a useful way of seeing the whole family.

Training tricks worth knowing

Truncated BPTT with state carry-over: keep hidden states across consecutive chunks of a long document (stateful training) but detach them (h = h.detach()) so the graph does not grow; reset at document boundaries. Gradient clipping at a global norm around 0.25–5 is standard for recurrent nets (Pascanu et al.). Layer normalisation inside the cell (dl4) stabilises training and is used in many recurrent architectures. cuDNN fuses the four gate matmuls into one and pipelines layers and time steps (the wavefront of layer l at step t can overlap with layer l+1 at step t−1), recovering some parallelism from a stacked recurrence. Packed sequences avoid computing on padding. Connectionist temporal classification (CTC) and RNN transducers extend recurrent models to speech and handwriting where input and output lengths differ and alignment is unknown, and streaming RNN-transducer models remain common for on-device speech recognition because their state is small and their latency constant.

The public case: LSTM (1997), sequence-to-sequence (2014), GNMT (2016)

Sepp Hochreiter and Jürgen Schmidhuber's "Long Short-Term Memory" (Neural Computation 9(8), 1997) is a long paper (46 pages) whose abstract makes its claims plain: recurrent learning "takes a very long time, mostly due to insufficient, decaying error backflow"; LSTM, by "enforcing constant error flow through constant error carrousels within special units" with multiplicative gate units that "learn to open and close access to the constant error flow", "can learn to bridge minimal time lags in excess of 1000 discrete time steps"; it is "local in space and time; its computational complexity per time step and weight is O(1)"; and, on artificial long-time-lag tasks, it "leads to many more successful runs, and learns much faster" than real-time recurrent learning, BPTT and other methods, and "solves complex, artificial long time lag tasks that have never been solved by previous recurrent network algorithms". Note what it is: a 1997 result on synthetic benchmarks. It took a further 17 years for hardware and data to make LSTMs a mainstream tool.

The moment it became one is Sutskever, Vinyals and Le's "Sequence to Sequence Learning with Neural Networks" (2014). A four-layer LSTM encoder read the English sentence into a vector; a four-layer LSTM decoder produced the French. Vocabularies were 160,000 source and 80,000 target words, the state dimension was 1,000 per layer, and each model had about 384 million parameters (as a check: eight LSTM layers of 8 million each is 64M, the two embedding tables 160M + 80M, and the output softmax 80M, summing to 384M); the headline result is an ensemble of five such models. On the WMT'14 English–French test set the ensemble reached a BLEU score of 34.81, "by far the best result achieved by direct translation with large neural networks", compared with 33.30 for the phrase-based statistical baseline. A striking detail: reversing the order of the words in the source sentences (not the targets) "improved the LSTM's performance markedly", because it put the first source words next to the first target words and created many short-range dependencies that made optimisation easier; the authors call it "one of the key technical contributions of this work". The same fixed-vector design also exposed the bottleneck: Bahdanau et al. (2014) replaced it with attention and on English–French with sentences of up to 50 words measured 26.75 BLEU (RNNsearch-50) against 17.82 for the fixed-vector RNNencdec-50 on the full test set, and, unlike the fixed-vector model, RNNsearch did not degrade with sentence length.

Two years later Google's Neural Machine Translation system (Wu et al., 2016) put the design into production: an LSTM encoder–decoder with 8 encoder layers (the first bidirectional) and 8 decoder layers, residual connections between layers and attention from decoder to encoder. In a human side-by-side evaluation on simple sentences it "reduces translation errors by an average of 60% compared to Google's phrase-based production system" across several language pairs. It is the high-water mark of LSTM machine translation; the transformer paper appeared the following year and the recurrent encoder–decoder was displaced.

Failure modes and debugging

State leaking between sequences. A stateful LSTM that carries its hidden state across batches (correct for consecutive chunks of one long document) but is fed unrelated sequences, or is not reset at document boundaries, conditions each example on the previous one: validation loss looks fine within a document and evaluation on shuffled data is worse. Reset explicitly, and for independent sequences start from zero states.

Padding and masking errors. Averaging the loss over padded positions dilutes it and teaches the model to predict padding; letting padding tokens update the state of a finished sequence changes its final state (which is then used for classification). Use packed sequences or masks in both the loss and the recurrence, and test with a batch where one sequence is padded versus alone: outputs must match.

Memory blow-up from BPTT. Not detaching the carried state makes the autograd graph span the whole dataset: memory grows every batch until out of memory (dl1). BPTT also stores activations at every step: for a layer of width 1,024, batch 64 and 2,048 steps that is about 3 GB per layer. Truncate, checkpoint, or shrink.

Exploding gradients and NaNs. Loss spikes and NaNs in recurrent nets are usually exploding gradients; clip the global norm (values of 0.25–5 are typical), lower the learning rate, and check that forget-gate biases are not so large that the cell saturates and blows up.

Exposure bias and decoding surprises. A model that is excellent under teacher forcing may produce repetitive or derailed text under free running; evaluate with the same decoding you will deploy, and try beam search with length normalisation (beam search without normalisation prefers short outputs) or scheduled sampling.

A bidirectional layer in a generator. Sees the future during training; validation loss is impossibly good and inference collapses. Bidirectionality belongs only in encoders and taggers.

Slow training. RNN training is latency-bound: profile before scaling up. Use cuDNN-backed implementations (in PyTorch nn.LSTM, not a Python loop over LSTMCell), larger batches, mixed precision, and a channels of parallelism from batch and layers, and consider a transformer or convolutional alternative if throughput dominates.

What to monitor

Gradient norm per step (clipping engaged?), the mean and saturation fraction of each gate (a forget gate stuck near 0 means the cell never remembers; near 1 for every unit means it never forgets), per-time-step loss (does the loss on late tokens of a long sequence keep improving with context?), the loss as a function of context length (the Khandelwal shuffle test), and for encoder–decoders the alignment map: attention weights should look like a mostly diagonal band for translation. Sudden loss spikes on a specific length bucket usually point to a batching or masking bug.

Goal: reproduce the lesson's one-step LSTM computation, measure how much gradient survives T steps in a plain RNN versus LSTMs with different forget-gate biases, see a linear recurrence computed in parallel with a prefix scan and as a convolution, and compute an attention context vector. NumPy only, Python 3.12; the parameter-count check against PyTorch runs only if torch is installed. The gradient experiment uses numerical Jacobians and takes a few seconds.

import numpy as np
sig = lambda z: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))

# ---- 1. one LSTM step by hand: one input, one hidden unit (gate order i, f, g, o as in PyTorch)
def lstm_step(x, h, c, W, U, b):
    n = h.size
    z = W @ x + U @ h + b
    i, f, g, o = sig(z[:n]), sig(z[n:2 * n]), np.tanh(z[2 * n:3 * n]), sig(z[3 * n:])
    c2 = f * c + i * g                      # additive memory update: the constant-error path
    h2 = o * np.tanh(c2)
    return h2, c2, (i, f, g, o)
W = np.array([[0.6], [0.5], [1.0], [0.7]]); U = np.array([[0.4], [0.5], [0.5], [0.3]]); b = np.array([0.0, 1.0, 0.0, 0.0])
h, c, gates = lstm_step(np.array([2.0]), np.array([0.5]), np.array([1.0]), W, U, b)
print("gates i, f, g, o =", np.round([float(v[0]) for v in gates], 4), " new c =", np.round(c, 4), " new h =", np.round(h, 4))

# ---- 2. how much gradient survives T steps? product of Jacobians dh_t/dh_(t-1), for a plain RNN and an LSTM
rng = np.random.default_rng(0); n = 16
def rnn_norms(rho, T=100):
    U = rng.normal(size=(n, n)); U *= rho / max(abs(np.linalg.eigvals(U)))      # scale to spectral radius rho
    Wx = rng.normal(0, 0.5, (n, n)); h = np.zeros(n); J = np.eye(n); out = []
    for t in range(T):
        h = np.tanh(U @ h + Wx @ rng.normal(size=n))
        J = (np.diag(1 - h ** 2) @ U) @ J                                       # chain rule, one factor per step
        out.append(np.linalg.norm(J, 2))
    return np.array(out)
def lstm_norms(T=100, forget_bias=1.0):
    W = rng.normal(0, 0.5, (4 * n, n)); U = rng.normal(0, 0.5 / np.sqrt(n), (4 * n, n)); b = np.zeros(4 * n); b[n:2 * n] = forget_bias
    h, c = np.zeros(n), np.zeros(n); J = np.eye(2 * n); out = []
    def step(state, x):
        return np.concatenate(lstm_step(x, state[:n], state[n:], W, U, b)[:2])
    for t in range(T):
        x = rng.normal(size=n); s = np.concatenate([h, c]); s2 = step(s, x)
        Jt = np.zeros((2 * n, 2 * n))
        for k in range(2 * n):                                                    # numerical Jacobian of one step
            e = np.zeros(2 * n); e[k] = 1e-6
            Jt[:, k] = (step(s + e, x) - step(s - e, x)) / 2e-6
        J = Jt @ J; h, c = s2[:n], s2[n:]; out.append(np.linalg.norm(J, 2))
    return np.array(out)
print("\nlargest singular value of the product of state Jacobians (d h_T / d h_1 for the RNN, d(h,c)_T / d(h,c)_1 for the LSTM)")
print("   T   RNN rho=0.9  RNN rho=1.2   LSTM b_f=1   LSTM b_f=5")
r09, r12, l1, l5 = rnn_norms(0.9), rnn_norms(1.2), lstm_norms(forget_bias=1.0), lstm_norms(forget_bias=5.0)
for T in (1, 10, 25, 50, 100): print(f"{T:4d}   {r09[T-1]:11.2e} {r12[T-1]:12.2e} {l1[T-1]:12.2e} {l5[T-1]:12.2e}")
print("cell-state path only: product of forget gates 0.95^50 =", round(0.95 ** 50, 4), " 0.99^50 =", round(0.99 ** 50, 3), " 0.999^50 =", round(0.999 ** 50, 3))

# ---- 3. a linear recurrence h_t = a_t*h_(t-1) + b_t can be computed in parallel: the pairs compose associatively
T = 8; a = rng.uniform(0.5, 1.0, T); bb = rng.normal(size=T)
seq = np.zeros(T); h = 0.0
for t in range(T): h = a[t] * h + bb[t]; seq[t] = h                              # sequential: T dependent steps
A, B = a.copy(), bb.copy(); step = 1
while step < T:                                                                   # scan: log2(T) rounds, each fully parallel
    A2, B2 = A.copy(), B.copy()
    A2[step:] = A[step:] * A[:-step]; B2[step:] = A[step:] * B[:-step] + B[step:]
    A, B = A2, B2; step *= 2
print("\nparallel scan equals sequential recurrence:", np.allclose(seq, B), " (rounds needed:", int(np.ceil(np.log2(T))), "instead of", T, ")")
k = 0.9 ** np.arange(6); u = rng.normal(size=6); y = np.zeros(6); h = 0.0
for t in range(6): h = 0.9 * h + u[t]; y[t] = h
print("linear state-space model = convolution with kernel", np.round(k[:4], 3), "...:", np.allclose(y, np.convolve(u, k)[:6]))

# ---- 4. attention: weights over three encoder states
scores = np.array([2.0, 1.0, 0.1]); alpha = np.exp(scores) / np.exp(scores).sum()
H = np.array([[1.0, 0.0], [0.0, 1.0], [1.0, 1.0]])
print("\nattention weights", np.round(alpha, 3), " context vector", np.round(alpha @ H, 3))

# ---- 5. parameter counts (PyTorch stores two bias vectors per gate group)
m_in, n_h = 300, 512
print("LSTM:", 4 * (n_h * (m_in + n_h) + 2 * n_h), " GRU:", 3 * (n_h * (m_in + n_h) + 2 * n_h), " plain RNN:", n_h * (m_in + n_h) + 2 * n_h)
try:
    import torch
    print("torch LSTM params:", sum(p.numel() for p in torch.nn.LSTM(m_in, n_h).parameters()), " GRU:", sum(p.numel() for p in torch.nn.GRU(m_in, n_h).parameters()))
except ImportError:
    print("(torch not installed - skipped cross-check)")

Work it out — size and steps

An LSTM layer with input size 300 and hidden size 512. Count its parameters and compare with a GRU. Then compute how many sequential steps a 2,048-token sequence needs, and contrast with a transformer.

Answer

Parameters. An LSTM has four blocks (forget, input, candidate, output), each a linear map over [x; h] with a bias: 4 × ((300 + 512) × 512 + 512) = 4 × (415,744 + 512) = 1,665,024 ≈ 1.67 M (PyTorch keeps two biases per block and reports 1,667,072). A GRU has three blocks: 3 × 416,256 = 1,248,768 ≈ 1.25 M, about 25% fewer. A plain RNN has one block: 416,256.

Sequential steps. 2,048: step t cannot begin until step t − 1 finishes, in both the forward and backward pass. A GPU with tens of thousands of cores sits almost idle, because the work is a chain of small matrix multiplications, not one big batch.

A transformer computes all 2,048 positions in parallel during training, because attention sees the whole sequence at once and causality is enforced by a mask rather than by ordering. That is the practical reason transformers scaled and RNNs did not: throughput, not expressiveness.

The nuance to add: at inference a transformer is also sequential, one token at a time, and must attend over the whole KV cache, about 0.5 MB per token for a 7B model. An LSTM of the same width and depth needs only 0.5 MB of state in total. State-space models (Mamba, S4) revive the recurrent formulation with a scan to try to get both.

Trace the gradient

A plain scalar RNN has recurrent weight u = 0.8 and, at the relevant steps, tanh slope factors of about 0.92 (states near 0.28). An LSTM cell-state path has forget gates of 0.95. Roughly how much gradient reaches 20 steps back in each, and what would you change to make the RNN remember?

Answer

RNN: the per-step factor is about 0.8 × 0.92 ≈ 0.74 (it varies with the state, with the exact recurrence in the lesson giving 0.010 at 20 steps). 0.74¹⁹ ≈ 0.003; the exact calculation gives about 0.01. Either way the gradient from 20 steps back is about 1% or less of what it would be with no decay.

LSTM cell path: 0.95¹⁹ ≈ 0.38 (about 0.36 for 20 factors): a third of the gradient survives, and a forget gate of 0.99 would keep 82%.

To make the plain RNN remember: you cannot fix it by initialisation alone. Options: initialise U near the identity or orthogonal so the gain is 1 (with ReLU units, the "IRNN" trick); use gradient clipping to control the explosive side; but the robust answer is to add the gate: switch to an LSTM or GRU and initialise the forget-gate bias to at least 1 so the cell remembers by default, or use a chrono initialisation matched to the dependency lengths. If the dependency is genuinely far (hundreds of steps) consider attention or a state-space layer.
