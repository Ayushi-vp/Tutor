You train a network on 30 noisy measurements of a smooth curve. After a few hundred steps its predictions track the true curve beautifully, and the error against it is the lowest it will ever be. You leave it running, because the training loss is still falling and loss falling is what training is for. By the end the training error is a sixth of the noise you know is in the data — the network has learned not just the curve but every random wobble in your 30 points — and its error against the true curve is six times worse than it was at step 450. Nothing went wrong in the code. The network did precisely what you asked: it minimised the training loss. The trouble is that minimising the training loss and predicting well on new data are different goals, and a deep network has enough capacity to pursue the first at the expense of the second.

This is overfitting, and the whole family of techniques for handling it is called regularisation: anything that makes it harder for a model to memorise the training set, or less rewarding, so that what it learns generalises. Modern networks make the problem sharp. They have millions or billions of parameters — often more than training examples — and in 2016 Zhang and colleagues showed that a standard image classifier can fit a dataset whose labels have been replaced by random noise perfectly. So capacity alone cannot explain why these networks generalise at all when the labels are real; a great deal of the answer is in how we train them, and regularisation is the part we control directly.

Regularisation is also where a lot of interview questions hide small, sharp bugs. What exactly does dropout do at test time, and why is the network scaled during training rather than after? What happens if you forget model.eval()? When does data augmentation hurt? Why has dropout largely disappeared from LLM pretraining but reappeared in fine-tuning? Answering well needs the mechanism, not the list of names, and the numbers: what dropout does to the sum of 100 activations, what a mixed image looks like, why early stopping and weight decay are close cousins.

This lesson builds the toolkit from the problem outward. We start with what overfitting looks like and what a training curve reveals, then set up a map of regularisers by what each one assumes. We work dropout by hand (masks, inverted scaling, backprop, the ensemble picture, the test-time weight-scaling rule with an exact worked example, and its equivalence to a data-dependent L2 penalty), including the inference-time bug. Then data augmentation and mixup, label smoothing, early stopping (with its link to weight decay) and weight averaging, and finally what regularisation looks like in the era of LLMs. The public case is the dropout paper itself, whose numbers we checked against the source, and the lab overfits a small network on purpose so that you can watch each regulariser change the curves.

Why deep networks overfit

A network with millions of parameters can memorise a training set of thousands of examples, including the mistakes in its labels and the random noise in its measurements. Memorising is not learning: on new data the memorised details are useless or harmful. Think of a student who prepares for an exam by memorising last year's answers word for word. They score perfectly on last year's paper (training error 0) and badly on this year's (test error high), because they learned the answers instead of the subject. Regularisation is the teacher who changes the questions each time, hides some of the notes, or stops the revision session before the cramming starts: all ways of forcing the student to learn what generalises.

The techniques, in one line each

  • Dropout: during training, randomly switch off a fraction of the units at each step (10–50%); no unit can rely on a particular other unit, so features must be redundant and robust. At test time all units are used.
  • Weight decay: shrink weights slightly every step, preferring simpler functions (ml3, dl5).
  • Data augmentation: create new training examples by transforming existing ones in ways that do not change the label: flip or crop a cat photo and it is still a cat.
  • Label smoothing: soften the one-hot targets so the model is never pushed to be infinitely confident.
  • Early stopping: keep checking validation loss and stop (or keep the best checkpoint) when it stops improving.
  • Averaging: average predictions (ensembles) or weights (EMA, SWA) to cancel out noise.
  • More data: the most reliable regulariser of all, and the one everything else imitates.

Reading the curves

Plot training loss and validation loss against training steps. Early on both fall. When validation loss starts rising while training loss keeps falling, the model has begun to overfit; that turning point is where early stopping saves the best model. A second sign is a training error below the noise floor: if the labels have irreducible noise of variance 0.09, no honest model can score below 0.09 on the training set, and any that does has fitted the noise.

Two rules of thumb

First, regularisation is a dial, not a switch: too little leaves the model memorising, too much starves it so it cannot even fit the training data (underfitting), and the right setting depends on how much data you have relative to the model. Second, every regulariser rests on an assumption about your data (small weights are simpler, flipped images are still the same object, labels are slightly noisy), so it helps only when that assumption is true. Keep both in mind as we go through the tools.

A worked example

An image classifier with 5,000 training images reaches 99% training accuracy and 72% validation accuracy after 30 epochs. Adding random crops, flips and colour jitter, plus dropout 0.3, gives 91% training and 84% validation: lower training accuracy, higher real accuracy. The gap shrinking (27 points to 7) is the sign that regularisation is working, even though the training number went down.

1. What overfitting is, with numbers

Recall from ml2 that expected test error splits into bias, variance and irreducible noise. Overfitting is high variance: the fitted function changes a lot if the training sample changes, because it is bending to fit the noise in this particular sample. The tell-tale gap between training and validation error is the generalisation gap. The lab makes it concrete. The training data are 30 points from y = sin(3x) + noise with noise standard deviation 0.3, so the noise variance is 0.3² = 0.09: no model can honestly get a mean squared training error below about 0.09. A network with two hidden layers of 64 tanh units, trained with Adam for 3,000 steps, reaches a training error of 0.016, more than five times below the noise floor, which is only possible by fitting noise. Its error against the true curve tells the rest of the story: 0.014 at step 450 (the best it ever does), 0.093 at step 3,000: overfitting made it more than six times worse.

The surprise of deep learning is that overfitting is not automatic in proportion to size. Zhang et al. (2017) trained standard architectures on CIFAR-10 with the labels replaced by random ones, and the networks still reached zero training error: "Deep neural networks easily fit random labels." So the networks could memorise everything, yet when the labels are real they generalise. Something in training (the optimiser's implicit preference for simple solutions, the structure of real data, architectural priors) does much of the regularising, and explicit regularisation adds to it. The consequence for practice is to treat regularisation as something you tune against validation curves rather than something you apply by reflex.

3. Dropout, step by step

The operation. For each unit in a layer, independently, with probability p set its output to zero ("drop" it); otherwise keep it. In inverted dropout, the standard implementation, the surviving outputs are also multiplied by 1/(1 − p) during training. Worked example with p = 0.5: activations (2, 4, 1, 3, 5) and a random mask (1, 1, 0, 1, 0) become (2, 4, 0, 3, 0) × 2 = (4, 8, 0, 6, 0). Dropped units are zero, survivors are doubled. Why double? Each unit is kept with probability 0.5, so its expected output is 0.5 × (2x) + 0.5 × 0 = x: the expected activation equals the original. With 100 units each equal to 1.0 the expected sum during training is 100 × 0.5 × 2 = 100, exactly the sum at inference when dropout is off and nothing is scaled. The lab confirms it (mean 100.04) and shows the price: the sum has a standard deviation of about 10. That noise is the regulariser; the matching mean is the reason inference needs no special code.

Backward. Dropout is a multiplication by a fixed (per-step) mask, so by the rules of dl1 the gradient flowing back is multiplied by the same mask and scale: dropped units receive gradient zero for this step, and survivors receive twice the usual gradient (for p = 0.5). Each step updates a different sub-network.

Why it works: three views.

  • Preventing co-adaptation. Without dropout, a hidden unit can learn to fix the mistakes of a specific neighbour, a fragile joint arrangement that fits the training data but breaks on new inputs. With dropout the neighbour is missing half the time, so each unit must be useful on its own. The dropout paper offers an evolutionary analogy: sexual reproduction mixes half of each parent's genes, so genes must work with many random partners rather than as a co-adapted set, and "ten conspiracies each involving five people is probably a better way to create havoc than one big conspiracy that requires fifty people to all play their parts correctly".
  • Implicit ensemble. A network with n units has 2ⁿ ways to keep or drop them (for 100 units, about 1.3 × 10³⁰), all sharing weights. Training with random masks trains an exponentially large ensemble of "thinned" networks, each getting occasional updates. Ensembles reduce variance; this one is essentially free.
  • Noise as a penalty. Injecting noise into a model is equivalent to adding a regularisation penalty; for a linear model dropout is exactly an L2 penalty with per-feature strengths (below).

The test-time rule and why it is right. The ensemble would need 2ⁿ forward passes; instead one runs the full network once with the weights scaled by the keep probability (in the original formulation; inverted dropout builds the scaling into training). This is a good approximation to averaging the sub-networks' predictions. For a single logistic unit it is exact in a precise sense. Take weights (2, 2), input (1, 1) and p = 0.5, with no train-time scaling. The four masks give pre-activations 0, 2, 2, 4, i.e. predictions σ(0) = 0.5, σ(2) = 0.881, σ(2) = 0.881, σ(4) = 0.982. Their arithmetic mean is 0.811. Their normalised geometric mean (the geometric mean of the probabilities, divided by the sum of the geometric means for the two classes) is 0.881, and the weight-scaled network gives σ(0.5 · 4) = σ(2) = 0.881: identical. (The geometric mean of sigmoids corresponds to averaging the logits, which is what scaling the weights does.) For deep non-linear networks the correspondence is approximate, but it works well in practice, and averaging many stochastic passes (MC dropout) is the more faithful version, used to estimate uncertainty.

Dropout as an adaptive L2 penalty. For linear regression with dropout on the inputs (each input kept with probability q and scaled by 1/q), the expected squared loss over masks is exactly

E[ ‖y − X̃w‖² ] = ‖y − Xw‖²  +  ((1 − q)/q) · Σⱼ wⱼ² · Σᵢ xᵢⱼ²

The second term is an L2 penalty on the weights, with a separate strength for each feature proportional to that feature's sum of squares (its variance, for centred data). So dropout shrinks weights attached to high-variance features more. The lab checks this numerically: with q = 0.8 and three features of scales 1, 3 and 0.3, the Monte Carlo average of the dropout loss was 103.81 and the formula gives 103.85. The penalty terms are 3.02, 3.28 and 0.85 for weights 0.5, −0.2 and 1.0: the small weight on the high-variance feature is penalised as heavily as the big weight on the low-variance one. That is a data-dependent ridge, which is what makes dropout different from plain weight decay.

Where dropout is used, and at what rate. Typical rates: 0.5 for fully connected hidden layers in the original paper (p = 0.5 for hidden units, 0.8 retention for input units), lower (0.1–0.3) for convolutional layers, where neighbouring activations are strongly correlated and dropping single pixels barely removes information. Variants adapted to structure: spatial dropout or DropBlock drop whole channels or contiguous regions; DropConnect drops weights rather than activations; variational dropout reuses one mask across all time steps of a sequence in RNNs (dl7) so the memory is not erased by fresh noise every step; stochastic depth (Huang et al., 2016) drops entire residual blocks with a survival probability that decays with depth — it took a 110-layer ResNet on CIFAR-10 from 6.41% to 5.25% error, with a theoretical saving of about 25% of training time, and let a 1,202-layer network reach 4.91%. In transformers dropout of about 0.1 is applied to attention weights, to each sub-layer's output before the residual addition, and to embeddings (the original transformer's base model used 0.1).

Interaction with BatchNorm. Dropout changes the variance of a unit's output between training (some units zeroed, others scaled up) and inference (no dropout). BatchNorm's running statistics (dl4) were collected under the training variance, so at test time a layer after dropout sees a different variance than BatchNorm expects. Li et al. (2018) call this "variance shift" and show it degrades accuracy when dropout comes before BatchNorm layers. Practical fixes: put dropout after the last BatchNorm, before the classifier, or use small rates. LayerNorm-based transformers do not have this problem.

4. The inference-time bug

Dropout and BatchNorm both behave differently in training and evaluation, and PyTorch switches them with model.train() and model.eval(). Forgetting eval() at validation or inference time is the most common evaluation bug in deep-learning code, and it has several distinct symptoms.

  1. Random predictions. Dropout stays on, so the same input gives different outputs on each call. The mean is right, the variance is not: 100 units of value 1.0 had a standard deviation of about 10 in their sum, and a classifier's top prediction can flip between calls.
  2. Batch-dependent normalisation. BatchNorm keeps using batch statistics instead of running averages (and updates the running averages with the validation batches, silently contaminating the model, dl4). Predictions depend on which other examples are in the batch, and a batch of one gives garbage.
  3. Metrics that are noisy and slightly off, easy to misread as a regularisation problem.

Related variants: applying the train-time scaling twice, or forgetting the test-time weight scaling in a hand-written non-inverted dropout, gives outputs of the wrong magnitude (twice too large or too small); leaving MC dropout switched on in a production endpoint gives non-deterministic results; and a library wrapper that calls train() on a sub-module after eval() on the parent re-enables dropout in part of the model. Defences: call model.eval() explicitly in the inference entry point, assert not model.training in serving code, and write a test that runs the same input twice and checks the outputs are identical. Finally, torch.no_grad() is an independent switch: it stops autograd recording (saving memory and time) and does nothing about dropout or BatchNorm; you need both, and forgetting eval() is a correctness bug while forgetting no_grad() is only a resource one.

5. Weight decay, briefly (see ml3 and dl5)

Weight decay shrinks every weight by a small factor each step, w ← (1 − lr·λ)w − lr·∇L; for plain gradient descent this equals an L2 penalty on the loss, and under Adam the decoupled form (AdamW) must be used (dl5). In the lab, decoupled weight decay of 1 (against a learning rate of 3×10⁻³) held the test error at 0.017 all the way to step 3,000, where the unregularised network had reached 0.093 — while a value of 10 was too strong (training error 0.176, well above the noise floor 0.09, test error 0.115): underfitting. With normalised layers weight decay controls the effective learning rate rather than capacity (dl4, dl5). LLM pretraining uses about 0.1 with AdamW.

6. Data augmentation

Data augmentation is regularisation by manufacturing training examples: apply transformations that do not change the label, so the model sees many variants of each example and must learn what is invariant. It is the strongest regulariser when the invariance is genuine, and it is the reason AlexNet could train a 60-million-parameter network on 1.2 million images: random 224×224 crops of 256×256 images plus horizontal flips "increases the size of our training set by a factor of 2048" (dl6), and PCA-based colour jitter added more. A second use is test-time augmentation (TTA): average predictions over several augmented copies of a test image (AlexNet used ten crops), a cheap ensemble.

Mixup. Zhang et al. (2017) train on convex combinations of pairs of examples and of their labels: x̃ = λx₁ + (1 − λ)x₂, ỹ = λy₁ + (1 − λ)y₂, with λ drawn from a Beta(α, α) distribution. The figure shows λ = 0.7 blending two 3×3 "images" (a cat and a dog) into a new image and the soft label (0.7, 0.3). It encourages the network to behave linearly between training examples, which smooths decision boundaries, and it improves calibration and robustness to label noise. It is an instance of vicinal risk minimisation: train on samples from the neighbourhood of each training point rather than the points alone. The paper's numbers: CIFAR-10 test error 5.6% → 4.2% (PreAct ResNet-18), 3.8% → 2.7% (WideResNet-28-10), 3.7% → 2.7% (DenseNet-BC-190); CIFAR-100 25.6% → 21.1%, 19.4% → 17.5%, 19.0% → 16.8%. Cutout (mask a random square) and CutMix (paste a patch from another image and mix labels in proportion to the area) are related. RandAugment replaces a search over augmentation policies by two hyperparameters (how many transformations, how strong).

Pitfalls. Wrong invariances (above). Augmenting before splitting: if you create augmented copies and then split randomly into train and validation, near-duplicates of training images land in validation and inflate the score; always split first, then augment only the training part. Distribution mismatch: heavy augmentation makes training inputs look unlike test inputs; the model can be poor at clean test data (fixes: lighter augmentation at the end of training, or fine-tuning on clean data, as in the FixRes line of work).

7. Label smoothing

With one-hot targets and cross-entropy (dl1), the loss keeps decreasing as the correct-class probability approaches 1, which requires the correct logit to exceed the others by an ever larger margin: the model is pushed to be infinitely confident, and becomes over-confident and over-fitted. Szegedy et al. (2015) replace the target with a softened version: for K classes and smoothing ε, the true class gets 1 − ε + ε/K and every other class ε/K. With ε = 0.1 and K = 4 the target for class 0 is (0.925, 0.025, 0.025, 0.025) instead of (1, 0, 0, 0). Now the loss is minimised at a finite logit gap and confidence is capped (for K = 1,000 and ε = 0.1 the optimal probability on the true class is 0.9001, a logit gap of about ln(0.9001/0.0001) ≈ 9.1). They report "a consistent improvement of about 0.2% absolute" in both top-1 and top-5 error on ImageNet (Inception-v3). Label smoothing also improves calibration, and it makes the outputs less informative for distillation; it is standard in image classification and translation and rarely used in LLM pretraining.

8. Early stopping

Train while monitoring validation loss; keep the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss and stop after patience evaluations without improvement. It costs nothing, and the lab shows what it buys: the unregularised network's test error was 0.0139 at step 450 and 0.0929 at step 3,000, a gap of nearly 7×. Why it regularises: gradient descent from small weights fits the directions of high curvature (the signal, well determined by the data) first and the low-curvature directions (mostly noise) later. For a quadratic loss with curvature h along one direction, after τ steps at learning rate η the weight has reached the fraction 1 − (1 − ηh)^τ of its optimum, while ridge regression with strength λ reaches h/(h + λ). Matching them gives λ ≈ 1/(ητ). With η = 0.1 and τ = 100 (λ ≈ 0.1): for h = 1 the two fractions are 1.00 and 0.91; for h = 0.1, 0.63 and 0.50; for h = 0.05, 0.39 and 0.33; for h = 0.01, 0.095 and 0.091. The high-curvature directions are essentially fully learned, the low-curvature ones barely at all, exactly the shape of an L2 penalty. So early stopping is approximately weight decay in disguise, with the number of steps standing in for the penalty strength.

Practice and caveats. Evaluate on a validation set large enough to make its standard error small compared with the differences you care about (otherwise you overfit the validation set through the stopping rule); use patience to ride out noise; save the best checkpoint to disk. With a fixed learning-rate schedule such as cosine decay the last checkpoint is usually best, and early stopping is less used. And there are regimes where early stopping misleads: epoch-wise double descent (Nakkiran et al., 2019), where test error falls, rises, then falls again with longer training, and grokking (Power et al., 2022), where validation accuracy on small algorithmic tasks jumps from chance to perfect long after training accuracy has saturated, with weight decay driving the transition.

9. Averaging: EMA, SWA, ensembles

Noise in the final weights (from a high learning rate and mini-batch noise) hurts generalisation, and averaging removes it. Exponential moving average (EMA) of the weights, w_ema ← 0.999·w_ema + 0.001·w after each step, evaluated in place of the raw weights, is nearly free and standard in detection and diffusion models (dl10). Stochastic weight averaging (Izmailov et al., 2018) averages weights collected along the SGD trajectory with a constant or cyclical learning rate, giving flatter minima and better generalisation than the final iterate. Checkpoint averaging does the same across saved checkpoints. Ensembles of independently trained networks average predictions and are among the most reliable ways to improve accuracy and calibration, at N times the inference cost; distillation compresses an ensemble into one student.

11. Regularisation in the era of LLMs and fine-tuning

The regime decides. In LLM pretraining the dataset is so large that each token is seen about once, so there is little to memorise and the variance term is small; regularisation buys nothing and dropout costs capacity and compute, so modern recipes use dropout 0 (the original transformer's 0.1 is gone) and AdamW weight decay of about 0.1, whose role is mainly to control weight norms and the effective learning rate (dl4, dl5). When data is repeated, Muennighoff et al. (2023) measured the cost: "training with up to 4 epochs of repeated data yields negligible changes to loss compared to having unique data", with "meaningful gains from repeating data ... up to around 16 epochs beyond which returns diminish extremely fast". Fine-tuning on a small dataset is the opposite regime: overfitting is real, and the tools are small learning rates (10⁻⁵ to 5×10⁻⁵), one to three epochs, early stopping on held-out data, freezing lower layers or parameter-efficient methods (LoRA, whose adapters carry their own dropout of 0.05–0.1), mixing in general data or regularising toward the pretrained weights to prevent catastrophic forgetting, and, for instruction tuning, noise on the embeddings (NEFTune reports raising LLaMA-2-7B's AlpacaEval score from 29.79% to about 64.7% in the authors' Alpaca setting; treat as a result to reproduce). A modern twist: Liu et al. (2023), "Dropout Reduces Underfitting", show that dropout applied only during the early phase of training lowers the variance of mini-batch gradients and helps underfit models, and late-phase dropout helps overfit ones.

Why generalisation is more than capacity: implicit regularisation and double descent

Classical theory says a model that can fit anything should generalise poorly, so the finding that networks fit random labels and still generalise on real data (Zhang et al.) forced a rethink. Part of the answer is that gradient-based optimisation is itself a regulariser: from small initial weights it tends to find low-norm, smooth, "simple" interpolating solutions first, larger learning rates and smaller batches add noise that biases towards flatter minima (ml4, dl5), and architectures such as CNNs restrict the functions that are easy to represent (dl6). The second part is that the shape of test error against model size is not U-shaped: Nakkiran et al. (2019) show double descent, where test error first falls, then rises as the model becomes just large enough to fit the training set exactly (the interpolation threshold), then falls again as models grow further, and the same happens as a function of training epochs. Label noise makes the peak more pronounced, and regularisation flattens it. The practical consequences: do not assume that a bigger model overfits more, that stopping earlier is always safer, or that a training error of zero is a warning by itself; measure.

Dropout as approximate Bayesian inference, and its limits

Gal and Ghahramani (2016) showed that a network trained with dropout can be interpreted as an approximate variational posterior over weights, so that keeping dropout active at test time and averaging T stochastic passes (MC dropout) gives an estimate of predictive uncertainty: the spread across passes is the epistemic uncertainty. It is attractive because it needs no change to training and only T forward passes, and it is used in medical imaging and active learning. The caveats matter: the uncertainty depends on the dropout rate and placement (a hyperparameter, not something learned), it tends to be under-dispersed compared with deep ensembles, and the Bayesian justification relies on approximations that are debated. State it as "a cheap uncertainty heuristic", not a calibrated posterior.

Noise injection and Tikhonov regularisation

Adding small Gaussian noise ε to the inputs during training approximately equals adding a penalty on the squared gradient of the network with respect to its inputs: E‖f(x + ε) − y‖² ≈ ‖f(x) − y‖² + σ²‖∇ₓf‖² for noise variance σ² (Bishop, 1995). So noise injection is Tikhonov regularisation: it asks the function to be smooth around each training point. Mixup extends this, using structured "noise" — the difference to another example — and asks the function to be linear in between. Adversarial training uses the worst-case perturbation instead of a random one. All are forms of smoothness prior. This unifies data augmentation, dropout and input noise as choices of which neighbourhood of each training point to demand consistency over.

Sharpness, flat minima and sharpness-aware minimisation

Empirically, solutions in wide, flat regions of the loss landscape generalise better than sharp ones, since small perturbations in the weights (or the shift between training and test distributions) change the loss less. Large-batch training, small learning rates and no weight averaging tend to find sharp minima; SGD noise, large learning rates, SWA and EMA find flat ones. Sharpness-aware minimisation (SAM; Foret et al., 2020) minimises the worst-case loss within a small ball around the weights, taking a gradient step at the perturbed point; it improves generalisation on several benchmarks at about twice the compute per step. Care is needed with the flatness story: sharpness is not invariant to reparameterisation (scale-invariant layers make sharpness arbitrary; dl4), so it is a heuristic, not a law.

When and why weight decay works with normalisation

In a network with normalised layers, scaling a layer's weights by c does not change the output, so weight decay cannot be reducing capacity. It controls the weight norm and therefore the effective learning rate, which sets the noise level of SGD, and that noise is the regulariser (dl4, dl5). This is one reason decay values that look strong (0.1 in AdamW) are standard in LLMs, and why the interaction of learning rate and decay is tuned as a product. It also explains grokking-type transitions, where decay slowly pushes the network from a memorising solution toward a lower-norm, generalising one.

Consistency, distillation and self-training

A broad family of regularisers penalises differences between predictions on perturbed versions of the same input: consistency regularisation (Π-model, mean teacher, FixMatch) uses unlabelled data by demanding the model give the same prediction on a weak and a strong augmentation of an unlabelled image. Distillation trains a student on the soft outputs of a teacher or ensemble, whose non-zero probabilities on wrong classes are a richer target than one-hot labels (much like label smoothing but informed). Self-training and noisy-student methods iterate this. In each, augmentation, noise and averaging are combined to turn unlabelled data into a regularising signal, and they are the main way large-scale vision models exploited unlabelled images before self-supervised pretraining.

Regularisation and calibration

Modern networks are often over-confident: their predicted probabilities exceed their accuracy (Guo et al., 2017). Regularisers change calibration in different ways: label smoothing and mixup typically improve it (they cap confidence), weight decay and ensembles help, and dropout's effect depends on whether it is used at test time. Label smoothing has a side effect worth knowing for distillation: it discards information about which wrong classes are similar to the right one, so students distilled from label-smoothed teachers can be worse. Temperature scaling on a validation set (ml15) is the standard post-hoc fix and interacts with all of these.

Scaling laws, repetition and the data wall

Scaling-law results treat data as a resource to be spent once. When the supply of unique tokens is limited, Muennighoff et al. fitted a law for the diminishing value of repeated epochs and found that repetition up to about four epochs is nearly as good as fresh data and that returns vanish somewhere around sixteen; they also examined augmenting with code data or relaxing quality filters as substitutes for fresh text. The general lesson for regularisation is that the dividing line between "memorising" and "learning" in a large model depends on how many times each example is seen and how much capacity is spent on it: rare, repeated sequences are memorised (a privacy and copyright issue, as well as an overfitting one), while common patterns are generalised. Deduplication of training data, which removes repeated documents, is therefore a regulariser too.

The public case: dropout and the 2012 ImageNet result

Dropout was introduced by Hinton, Srivastava, Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Salakhutdinov in a 2012 arXiv preprint ("Improving neural networks by preventing co-adaptation of feature detectors"), and described fully in Srivastava et al., "Dropout: A Simple Way to Prevent Neural Networks from Overfitting" (JMLR 15, 2014). Its abstract states the idea in three sentences: drop units "randomly ... during training. This prevents units from co-adapting too much. During training, dropout samples from an exponential number of different 'thinned' networks. At test time, it is easy to approximate the effect of averaging the predictions of all these thinned networks by simply using a single unthinned network that has smaller weights." The paper's motivation is a theory of the role of sex in evolution and the analogy of conspiracies; its evidence is broad. On MNIST (permutation-invariant, no distortions), networks without dropout or pretraining reached about 1.60% test error; dropout gave 1.35%; ReLU units 1.25%; adding max-norm weight constraints 1.06%; and a network with two hidden layers of 8,192 units — over 65 million parameters trained on 60,000 images — reached 0.95%, a network the authors note is "very hard" to train well with standard regularisation and early stopping but where dropout "does not even need early stopping". On CIFAR-10 a convolutional network improved from 15.60% to 12.61% with dropout in all layers. On ImageNet (ILSVRC-2010) the convolutional network with dropout got 37.5% top-1 and 17.0% top-5 error against 47.1% and 28.2% for sparse coding; and in ILSVRC-2012 "convolutional nets with dropout achieve a test error of about 16% which is a staggering difference" from the "about 26%" of methods using standard vision features. All of those dropout networks used p = 0.5 for hidden units and retention 0.8 for inputs. That last result is AlexNet (dl6), which used dropout with probability 0.5 in its first two fully connected layers, the layers holding 96% of its parameters, and which the authors found roughly doubled the number of iterations needed to converge while preventing "substantial overfitting". Dropout thus sits at the root of the deep-learning boom, and its modern fate is instructive: it remains a first-line tool for small and medium datasets, and has been removed from large-scale LLM pretraining.

Production failure modes

Eval mode forgotten. Symptom: predictions differ between two calls on the same input; accuracy varies with batch size; a serving endpoint gives different answers to identical requests. Test: call the model twice on the same input and assert equality; assert not model.training at service start.

Validation set contaminated. Augmented copies split across train and validation; duplicated records across splits (common in web-scraped data); patient or session identifiers not grouped in splits; the validation set reused so many times to choose hyperparameters that it is effectively training data. Symptom: validation score far better than production. Fix: split by group, deduplicate before splitting, keep a final untouched test set.

Augmentation mismatch between train and serve. A model trained on heavily augmented crops served on full-frame images (or the reverse) loses accuracy; the same applies to normalisation and resizing. Put the deterministic part of the pipeline in the model artefact and version it (dl6).

Regularisation that hides underfitting. A model with dropout 0.5 and weight decay 10⁻¹ on a small network may never fit; the training curve plateaus high and validation looks "healthy" because it is close to training. Always compare training error (measured in eval mode) with the noise floor or with a known baseline: if it is far above, back off the regularisation.

Early stopping on a noisy validation set. With a validation set of 500 examples and accuracy differences of 0.5%, the best epoch is chosen by noise, and the reported validation score is optimistically biased. Use a larger set, smooth the metric, or select the checkpoint on one set and report on another.

Fine-tuning collapse. Fine-tuning a large pretrained model on a few thousand examples for many epochs at a high learning rate produces a model that reproduces the training set and has lost general abilities. Fixes: lower the learning rate, one to three epochs, early stopping, LoRA, replay of general data.

Memorisation of training data by large models. Large models trained on repeated or duplicated text can reproduce it verbatim, which is an overfitting failure with legal and privacy consequences. Deduplicate training data, limit epochs on any one document, and test for extraction.

What to monitor

Training and validation loss on the same axes, with the training loss measured in eval mode as well as the running training loss (dropout inflates the latter); the generalisation gap over time; validation metrics per data slice; the effective noise floor if labels are noisy; weight norms per layer; and, in fine-tuning, a held-out set of general-capability prompts to detect forgetting. Log the exact regulariser settings (dropout p, decay, augmentation policy, seed) with every run, because most regularisation results are not reproducible without them.

Goal: implement dropout forward and backward, verify its expectation, its test-time weight-scaling rule and its equivalence to an adaptive L2 penalty, then overfit a small network on purpose and compare early stopping, weight decay and dropout on it, with hand-written backprop and Adam. NumPy only, Python 3.12, about 15 seconds (six 3,000-step trainings of a 64-unit network).

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# ---- 1. inverted dropout: forward, backward, and its statistics
def dropout(x, p, rng):
    mask = (rng.random(x.shape) >= p) / (1 - p)          # keep with prob 1-p, scale survivors by 1/(1-p)
    return x * mask, mask                                # backward multiplies the gradient by the same mask
x = np.array([2.0, 4.0, 1.0, 3.0, 5.0])
y, m = dropout(x, 0.5, np.random.default_rng(1))
print("one draw:", x, "->", y, " (survivors doubled, dropped units zero)")
ys = np.stack([dropout(np.full(100, 1.0), 0.5, rng)[0] for _ in range(20000)])
print("100 units of value 1, p=0.5: mean sum %.2f (eval sum = 100), std of the sum %.2f" % (ys.sum(1).mean(), ys.sum(1).std()))

# ---- 2. test-time weight scaling versus averaging over all masks, for one sigmoid unit (x=(1,1), w=(2,2))
sig = lambda z: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z)); w = np.array([2.0, 2.0]); xx = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
masks = [(a, b) for a in (0, 1) for b in (0, 1)]
probs = np.array([sig(w @ (xx * np.array(mk))) for mk in masks])              # plain dropout p = 0.5, no train-time scaling
arith = probs.mean(); g1 = np.exp(np.log(probs).mean()); g0 = np.exp(np.log(1 - probs).mean())
print("\n4 masks:", np.round(probs, 3), " arithmetic mean %.3f, normalised geometric mean %.3f, weight-scaled network sigma(0.5*w.x) = %.3f" % (arith, g1 / (g1 + g0), sig(0.5 * (w @ xx))))

# ---- 3. dropout on the inputs of linear regression = a data-dependent L2 penalty
n, d, q = 40, 3, 0.8                                                            # keep-probability q on the inputs
X = rng.normal(size=(n, d)) * np.array([1.0, 3.0, 0.3]); yv = rng.normal(size=n); wv = np.array([0.5, -0.2, 1.0])
loss_mc = np.mean([((yv - ((rng.random(X.shape) < q) * X / q) @ wv) ** 2).sum() for _ in range(40000)])
loss_formula = ((yv - X @ wv) ** 2).sum() + (1 - q) / q * (wv ** 2 * (X ** 2).sum(0)).sum()
print("\nexpected dropout loss: Monte Carlo %.2f   formula ||y-Xw||^2 + (1-q)/q * sum_j w_j^2 sum_i x_ij^2 = %.2f" % (loss_mc, loss_formula))
print("penalty per feature (weights 0.5, -0.2, 1.0; feature scales 1, 3, 0.3):", np.round((1 - q) / q * wv ** 2 * (X ** 2).sum(0), 2))

# ---- 4. overfitting a small regression problem: none vs early stopping vs weight decay vs dropout (hand-written backprop, Adam)
def make_data(seed=3):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    xt = r.uniform(-1, 1, (30, 1)); yt = np.sin(3 * xt) + r.normal(0, 0.3, xt.shape)
    xv = np.linspace(-1, 1, 400)[:, None]; return xt, yt, xv, np.sin(3 * xv)          # validation = the true noiseless function
def train(p=0.0, wd=0.0, steps=3000, lr=3e-3, seed=0):
    xt, yt, xv, yv = make_data(); r = np.random.default_rng(seed); H = 64
    P = {"W1": r.normal(0, 1, (1, H)), "b1": np.zeros(H), "W2": r.normal(0, 1 / np.sqrt(H), (H, H)), "b2": np.zeros(H), "W3": r.normal(0, 1 / np.sqrt(H), (H, 1)), "b3": np.zeros(1)}
    M = {k: np.zeros_like(v) for k, v in P.items()}; V = {k: np.zeros_like(v) for k, v in P.items()}
    def fwd(x, train_mode):
        h1 = np.tanh(x @ P["W1"] + P["b1"]); m1 = (r.random(h1.shape) >= p) / (1 - p) if train_mode and p else np.ones_like(h1)
        h2 = np.tanh((h1 * m1) @ P["W2"] + P["b2"]); m2 = (r.random(h2.shape) >= p) / (1 - p) if train_mode and p else np.ones_like(h2)
        return (h2 * m2) @ P["W3"] + P["b3"], (x, h1, m1, h2, m2)
    hist = []
    for t in range(1, steps + 1):
        out, (x, h1, m1, h2, m2) = fwd(xt, True); d = 2 * (out - yt) / len(xt)
        G = {"W3": (h2 * m2).T @ d, "b3": d.sum(0)}
        d2 = (d @ P["W3"].T) * m2 * (1 - h2 ** 2); G["W2"], G["b2"] = (h1 * m1).T @ d2, d2.sum(0)
        d1 = (d2 @ P["W2"].T) * m1 * (1 - h1 ** 2); G["W1"], G["b1"] = x.T @ d1, d1.sum(0)
        for k in P:                                                             # Adam, decoupled weight decay on the matrices
            M[k] = 0.9 * M[k] + 0.1 * G[k]; V[k] = 0.999 * V[k] + 0.001 * G[k] ** 2
            P[k] -= lr * (M[k] / (1 - 0.9 ** t)) / (np.sqrt(V[k] / (1 - 0.999 ** t)) + 1e-8)
            if wd and k.startswith("W"): P[k] -= lr * wd * P[k]
        if t % 10 == 0:
            tr = ((fwd(xt, False)[0] - yt) ** 2).mean(); va = ((fwd(xv, False)[0] - yv) ** 2).mean(); hist.append((t, tr, va))
    return np.array(hist)
print("\nmean squared error against the TRUE function (30 noisy training points, noise std 0.3):")
print(f"{'setup':32}{'train MSE':>11}{'test MSE at end':>17}{'best test MSE (step)':>24}")
runs = {"no regularisation": train(), "weight decay 1": train(wd=1.0), "weight decay 10 (too strong)": train(wd=10.0),
        "dropout 0.1": train(p=0.1), "dropout 0.5 (too strong)": train(p=0.5), "dropout 0.1 + weight decay 1": train(p=0.1, wd=1.0)}
for name, h in runs.items():
    b = h[:, 2].argmin(); print(f"{name:32}{h[-1, 1]:11.4f}{h[-1, 2]:17.4f}{h[b, 2]:14.4f} ({int(h[b, 0]):4d})")

# ---- 5. mixup and label smoothing arithmetic
lam = 0.7; a, b = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0]), np.array([0.0, 1.0, 0.0])
print("\nmixup lam=0.7: image pixels (10, 0) and (2, 8) ->", lam * np.array([10, 0]) + (1 - lam) * np.array([2, 8]), " label ->", lam * a + (1 - lam) * b)
K, eps = 4, 0.1; print("label smoothing eps=0.1, K=4, true class 0 ->", np.round((1 - eps) * np.array([1, 0, 0, 0]) + eps / K, 4))

What to look for.

  1. Dropout mechanics. One draw turns (2, 4, 1, 3, 5) into a vector with dropped units at zero and survivors doubled. For 100 units of value 1 with p = 0.5 the mean sum is 100.04 (matching evaluation) and its standard deviation is about 10: the noise that regularises.
  2. Weight scaling. The four masks give predictions 0.5, 0.881, 0.881, 0.982; the arithmetic mean is 0.811 but the normalised geometric mean equals the weight-scaled network's 0.881.
  3. Adaptive L2. The Monte Carlo expected dropout loss (103.81) matches the formula ‖y − Xw‖² + ((1 − q)/q)Σⱼ wⱼ²Σᵢxᵢⱼ² (103.85); the three per-feature penalties are 3.02, 3.28 and 0.85.
  4. The overfitting table. With no regularisation the training MSE is 0.016 (below the 0.09 noise floor), the test MSE at step 3,000 is 0.093 and the best is 0.014 at step 450. Weight decay 1 and dropout 0.1 end at 0.017 and 0.018; decay 10 and dropout 0.5 underfit (0.115 and 0.052); combining dropout 0.1 with decay 1 is worse (0.024) than either alone. Try other values and seeds: which regulariser wins depends on the problem and the amount of noise.
  5. Mixup and label smoothing arithmetic: blending pixel pairs (10, 0) and (2, 8) at λ = 0.7 gives (7.6, 2.4) with label (0.7, 0.3); label smoothing with ε = 0.1 and K = 4 gives the target (0.925, 0.025, 0.025, 0.025).

Work it out — dropout scaling and the eval bug

A layer has 100 units all outputting 1.0, with dropout p = 0.5. Compute the expected output sum during training (with inverted dropout) and at evaluation. Then say what breaks if you forget model.eval().

Answer

Training, inverted dropout: each unit survives with probability 0.5, and survivors are scaled by 1/(1 − p) = 2. Expected sum = 100 × 0.5 × 1.0 × 2 = 100. Its standard deviation: each unit contributes 2 with probability 0.5 and 0 otherwise, a variance of 1 per unit, so 100 units give variance 100 and a standard deviation of 10.

Evaluation: dropout is off and nothing is scaled: sum = 100 × 1.0 = 100, with no variance. The two means match by construction, which is the point of scaling during training rather than at test time.

Forgetting model.eval(): two things break at once, and the second is worse. Dropout stays on: predictions are stochastic (the same input gives a sum of 100 ± 10 and a classifier's top class can flip). BatchNorm keeps using batch statistics and updating its running averages from validation batches: outputs depend on which examples share the batch, and the model is silently altered by validation data. Symptoms: validation metrics that are noisy, batch-size dependent and slightly worse than they should be, easily misread as a regularisation problem. torch.no_grad() is a separate concern (memory, not correctness).

Diagnose it — a small-data fine-tune

You fine-tune a pretrained model on 5,000 examples. Training loss is 0.05 and falling; validation loss bottomed at epoch 2 at 0.61 and is now 0.95 at epoch 10. Validation accuracy has fallen from 81% to 74%. The learning rate is 2×10⁻⁴ and there is no regularisation. What is happening and what do you change?

Answer

Diagnosis: classic overfitting of a small dataset. Training loss is tiny (memorising), validation loss turned up after epoch 2, and the accuracy loss suggests the pretrained representation is being overwritten (catastrophic forgetting). The learning rate of 2×10⁻⁴ is high for full fine-tuning of a pretrained model (10⁻⁵ to 5×10⁻⁵ is typical).

Changes, in order: (1) keep the epoch-2 checkpoint now (early stopping with the best-validation checkpoint and a patience of one or two evaluations), and re-run with a learning rate of about 2×10⁻⁵ and 2–3 epochs; (2) use a parameter-efficient method (LoRA with adapter dropout 0.05–0.1) or freeze lower layers so far fewer weights can move; (3) weight decay of 0.01–0.1, label smoothing 0.1 for classification, and light augmentation valid for the domain; (4) mix in a slice of general or pretraining-like data or regularise toward the pretrained weights if general ability matters; (5) confirm validation is a clean, group-split set of enough size (a few thousand examples for 1% effects) so that the epoch-2 choice is not noise. Then compare against the linear-probe baseline (frozen encoder plus a linear layer, dl11): if full fine-tuning cannot beat it on validation, the data are too few for it.
