A team is about to launch an assistant for a new insurance product. There is no production traffic yet, so they ask a strong LLM to generate 2,000 customer questions across the product's features, along with expected answers drawn from the policy documents. Their assistant scores 94% on this set. Two weeks into the beta, an engineer labels 300 real customer questions and the assistant scores 71%. The real questions look nothing like the generated ones. They are fragments ("still no money??"), they combine three issues in one message, they refer to phone calls the assistant knows nothing about, and a fifth of them ask things the policy documents simply don't cover. The synthetic set measured how well the assistant answered questions an LLM imagines customers ask.

Meanwhile, a second team fine-tunes a smaller model on 50,000 synthetic support conversations generated by a larger model, and it works well, until they generate the next round of training data from the fine-tuned model and train on that. Over a few rounds, the model's answers grow more uniform and lose the unusual but correct responses that edge cases need.

Synthetic data, meaning data generated by models rather than collected from people, has become one of the most important tools in AI engineering. It bootstraps eval sets before launch, fills rare slices random sampling never reaches, creates adversarial and perturbed cases, protects privacy where real records can't be used, and supplies training data at a scale no labelling team could. It has also become one of the most common ways teams fool themselves, because synthetic data inherits the biases and blind spots of the model that generated it, and those biases are invisible unless you measure them.

This lesson covers both halves. On the generation side: attribute grids, seeds and perturbations for diverse inputs, verification for generated labels, filtering for validity, duplicates, quality and answerability, and measuring diversity instead of assuming it. On the trust side: the specific ways synthetic data lies (the realism gap, generator bias, label errors, leakage) and how to detect each, with a real anchor set as the cure. Then training data: what Self-Instruct and phi-1 showed about synthetic data done well, and what model collapse shows about synthetic data done recursively, with the replace-versus-accumulate result that says how to avoid it.

Think of a teacher writing practice exams for their own students. The questions will be useful, covering the syllabus and testing the concepts the teacher emphasised. They will also be shaped by the teacher's mental model: phrased the way the teacher phrases things, focused on what the teacher finds important, and free of the confusions the teacher doesn't know students have. Students who ace the practice exams may still struggle on an exam written by someone else, or on the problems they meet in real life. The practice exam is real practice. It just isn't an unbiased sample of what's coming.

LLM-generated data is a practice exam written by a model. It covers what you ask it to cover, quickly and cheaply, and it reflects the generator's view of the world: its style, its sense of what a typical question looks like, its knowledge and its gaps. The analogy also suggests the remedy. A good teacher checks practice exams against real past papers, and asks colleagues to write some questions. A good team checks synthetic data against real data and uses more than one generator, so that no single model's habits define the whole test.

The synthetic data pipeline

Generation starts from seeds, real examples or hand-written ones, and an attribute grid that spreads generation across the situations you need. Generated items are filtered: invalid ones, duplicates, low-quality ones and unanswerable ones are removed. Generated labels, such as expected answers, are verified, by running code or computing results where possible and by human review of a sample otherwise. The diversity of what survives is measured against real data, because generators repeat themselves. Everything is stored with provenance, marked synthetic, with the generator and prompt recorded. A real anchor set checks the most important property of all: do conclusions drawn from the synthetic data hold on real data?

A rule of thumb helps decide how much of this pipeline a given use needs: the closer synthetic data gets to a decision, the more verification it needs. Synthetic adversarial prompts used to look for weaknesses need little: a strange attack is still a useful test, and a human reviews whatever it finds. Synthetic regression cases that gate a release need verified labels, because a wrong label blocks good releases or passes bad ones. Synthetic data that produces a headline quality number needs to be shown to agree with real data, because executives and customers will act on that number. And synthetic training data needs verification at scale, because errors there are not just mis-scored but learned, and then repeated in every output the trained model produces.

The same pipeline serves training data, with two additions covered later: verifying at much larger scale, usually by rejection sampling against automatic checks, and avoiding the recursive trap that leads to model collapse.

1. Why and when to use synthetic data

Synthetic data earns its place in five situations. Naming which one you're in helps decide how much to trust it.

  • Cold start: no real traffic yet. Synthetic questions let you build a first eval suite before launch. They should be replaced by real traffic as soon as it exists (ev2).
  • Coverage of rare slices: a slice that is 1% of traffic gets few random samples, and generation can produce dozens of cases for it. These belong in targeted suites, not the representative set.
  • Adversarial and edge cases: attacks, boundary conditions, tricky phrasings (ev9). Here synthetic data is the natural source, because real users rarely supply these on demand.
  • Perturbations for robustness: paraphrases, typos, reordered information, irrelevant distractors and changed numbers, generated from real examples. rs8's GSM-Symbolic case showed how templated variants of a benchmark exposed fragility and contamination.
  • Privacy and scale: domains where real records can't be used, such as health or finance, and training data at volumes no human team could label.

In each case synthetic data is a supplement to real data with a specific job. The failure in the opening story was using it as a substitute for the representative set that estimates real quality.

2. Generating inputs that cover the space

Ask an LLM for "1,000 customer questions about returns" and you will get 1,000 variations on a few dozen well-formed questions, because the generator gravitates to its most typical outputs. Diversity has to be engineered. Three techniques work well together.

Attribute grids. Define the dimensions that matter and generate for combinations. For a support assistant: intent (return, delivery, billing, complaint, account, out of scope), persona (first-time buyer, frustrated repeat customer, small-business owner, non-native speaker, elderly user), difficulty (single clear question, multi-issue, ambiguous, unanswerable), style (terse, rambling, typo-heavy, formal), and channel (chat, email, voice transcript). A prompt per combination ("Write a message from a frustrated repeat customer, writing tersely with typos, about a delivery problem that also involves a billing error") produces far more varied inputs than one general prompt. With 6 × 5 × 4 × 4 × 3 = 1,440 combinations, you won't fill every cell. Sample the grid, oversampling cells that matter.

Seeds from real data. Show the generator a few real examples (anonymised) and ask for new ones in the same spirit. This pulls the output toward real phrasing and real concerns, and away from the generator's idea of a typical question. Rotate seeds so the output doesn't cluster around a handful of them.

Perturbing real inputs. Take real examples and transform them. Paraphrase, inject typos, remove punctuation, add irrelevant context ("my daughter's birthday is next week, anyway…"), change numbers and names, switch language, split one message into three. Each perturbation keeps the real example's substance and tests robustness to one kind of variation. For eval purposes, perturbations also give paired comparisons. If a system passes the original and fails the paraphrase, the item is measuring luck (lm14).

For RAG evals there is a specific trap. Generating questions from a document produces questions that reuse the document's words, so retrieval looks unrealistically easy (ev6). Ask the generator to write the question as a user who hasn't read the document would, without using its distinctive terms. Then check lexical overlap between question and source and reject high-overlap items.

3. Generating labels, and verifying them

Generating inputs is relatively safe: a strange input is still a test. Generating expected outputs is riskier, because a wrong label silently grades correct behaviour as wrong, or wrong behaviour as right. And generator errors aren't random. They concentrate in the hard cases, which are exactly where an eval needs to be right.

Verify labels by the strongest available means. For verifiable tasks, check mechanically: run generated code against tests, execute generated SQL, recompute arithmetic, validate extracted fields against a structured source. Generate many candidate answers and keep only those that pass. This is rejection sampling with a verifier, the same idea rs6 described for distilling reasoning. For unverifiable tasks, sample 100-200 items for expert review and measure the label error rate overall and by slice. If 8% of generated labels are wrong, you know scores on this set carry an 8% noise floor (ev5). You can fix labels in risky slices or exclude them. Agreement between two generators from different model families, or across several samples from one, is a cheap flag: items where they disagree go to humans first.

A round-trip check catches a common failure in generated question-answer pairs: questions the context doesn't actually answer. Generate a question from a passage, then have a separate model answer the question from the passage alone, and check the answer matches. Questions that fail are ambiguous, unanswerable or mislabelled. Some should be kept deliberately as unanswerable cases, labelled as such.

4. Filtering

Raw generator output contains a lot of junk, and filtering usually removes a large share of it. A practical filter stack, in order of cost:

  1. Validity: parses, matches the schema, within length limits, in the requested language, not a refusal ("As an AI, I can't…").
  2. Exact duplicates: normalise and hash.
  3. Near-duplicates: items that are trivially different. Use n-gram similarity, MinHash (ev11 works it through), or embedding cosine above a threshold.
  4. Novelty against existing data: remove items too similar to what you already have, including the seeds. Self-Instruct, below, did exactly this for each new instruction.
  5. Quality: a judge scoring realism, correctness and difficulty, validated against human ratings on a sample (ev4). Keep items above a threshold.
  6. Answerability and label checks: round-trip and verification from section 3.
  7. Difficulty: for capability evals, remove items every current system gets right. They add cost without information.
  8. Decontamination: remove anything overlapping your held-out eval sets, so synthetic training data doesn't leak test items (ev2, ev11).

Filters shape the data, so audit them. An LLM quality judge that prefers longer, more formal items will remove exactly the terse, messy inputs real users send. Look at a sample of what each filter removes, not just what it keeps.

5. Self-Instruct: a generation pipeline, worked

Self-Instruct (Wang et al., 2022) is a clear public example of a generate-filter-train pipeline. It started from 175 human-written seed tasks, each an instruction with an example. In each round, it sampled some existing tasks into a prompt and asked GPT-3 to write new instructions. It decided whether each new instruction was a classification task, which affects how instances should be generated, and generated input-output instances for it. Then it filtered: new instructions too similar to existing ones (by ROUGE-L overlap) were discarded, as were invalid or duplicate instances. Survivors joined the pool and seeded later rounds. The result was a dataset of tens of thousands of instructions. Fine-tuning GPT-3 on it gave a 33-point absolute improvement on the Super-NaturalInstructions benchmark, roughly matching InstructGPT-001 there. In human evaluation, the authors report it beat models tuned on existing public instruction datasets by a large margin and came within 5 points of InstructGPT-001.

Three lessons generalise. First, a small, high-quality seed set can bootstrap a large dataset. Second, the novelty filter is what stops the pool collapsing onto a few task types, since without it generation drifts towards its favourites. Third, the result inherits the generator's ceiling. The fine-tuned model approached, but did not exceed, the instruction-following of a model trained with human data. Synthetic data transfers capability; it rarely creates more than the generator has.

6. Measuring diversity

Diversity has to be measured, because generated data that looks varied at a glance often isn't. Four measures, from simplest to most informative:

Distinct-n is the number of unique n-grams across the dataset divided by the total number of n-grams. Worked with four synthetic questions:

  • "how do i return an item i bought"
  • "how do i return a jacket i bought"
  • "how do i return an item i ordered"
  • "how can i return an item i bought"

Each has 8 words and so 7 bigrams, 28 in total. Most repeat ("how do", "do i", "i return", "return an", "an item", "item i", "i bought"), and only 13 are distinct, so distinct-2 = 13/28 = 0.46. Four real messages from the same intent:

  • "jacket doesnt fit can i send it back"
  • "return label never arrived??"
  • "wrong size again, refund pls"
  • "how do i return an item i bought"

These have 7 + 3 + 4 + 7 = 21 bigrams, all distinct, so distinct-2 = 21/21 = 1.0. Distinct-n is crude. It depends on length and ignores meaning, but it catches lazy generation instantly.

Embedding spread: the mean pairwise cosine similarity between items' embeddings, lower meaning more spread. If synthetic items average 0.62 similarity and real items 0.41, the synthetic set is more homogeneous in meaning, not just wording.

Grid coverage: what fraction of attribute-grid cells have at least k surviving items after filtering? Filtering often empties exactly the difficult cells.

Distributional comparison with real data: embed real and synthetic items together, cluster them, and compare the share of each in every cluster. Use PSI or KL divergence from ae3 as a single number, but mainly look for clusters that are real-only (situations the generator never produces) and synthetic-only (situations users never create).

7. When synthetic data lies to you

Synthetic data misleads in recognisable ways. Each has a detection method.

The realism gap. Generated inputs are cleaner, more grammatical, more self-contained, more answerable and more polite than real ones. Systems score higher on them. The opening story's 94% against 71% is typical in direction, if not in size. Detect it by comparing per-slice results on a real anchor set and by the cluster comparison above. In that story, the cluster comparison would show synthetic data almost devoid of multi-issue, context-dependent and unanswerable messages, and the per-slice comparison would show those slices carrying most of the real failures.

Generator bias. The generator writes questions it finds natural and, often, questions it can answer. Systems built on similar models then do unusually well, because the questions sit in their comfort zone. If a judge from the same family grades the outputs, the bias compounds through self-preference (ev4). Detect it by generating with two model families and checking whether system rankings change with the generator, and by cross-family judging.

Label errors. Generated expected answers are wrong at some rate, concentrated in hard cases. Detect it by expert review of a sample (section 3).

Leakage. A generator trained on public benchmarks can reproduce their items or close paraphrases. Generated "fresh" test questions may be contaminated for any model that saw the same benchmarks. Detect it with n-gram and embedding overlap checks against known benchmarks and your own eval sets.

Circularity. Generating data, training on it, and evaluating on more data from the same generator measures agreement with the generator, not quality. Detect it by evaluating on data the generator didn't produce.

The common remedy is a real anchor set: a real-data subset that every synthetic-based evaluation must agree with. The test of a synthetic eval isn't whether its absolute scores match real scores. They usually won't. The test is whether it ranks system versions the same way and shows the same direction of change. Concretely, over the last several system changes, compare the deltas on the synthetic set with the deltas on the anchor. If they agree in sign and roughly in size, the synthetic set is a useful instrument. If they don't, it is measuring something else.

8. Synthetic training data

Synthetic data for training follows the same principles at larger scale, with verification even more important, because errors are learned rather than just mis-scored.

Distillation trains a model on a stronger model's outputs: instructions and responses, reasoning traces, or preferences. rs6 covered distilling reasoning, where generated solutions are filtered by checking final answers, which is rejection sampling with a verifier. Instruction data bootstrapped à la Self-Instruct taught early open models to follow instructions. Curated synthetic corpora can teach specific skills efficiently. The phi-1 paper ("Textbooks Are All You Need", Gunasekar et al., 2023) trained a 1.3B-parameter code model, in four days on eight A100 GPUs, on about 6B tokens of web data filtered for "textbook quality" plus about 1B tokens of synthetic textbooks and exercises generated with GPT-3.5. It reached 50.6% pass@1 on HumanEval and 55.5% on MBPP, competitive at the time with open models many times its size. The lesson is not that synthetic data beats real data. It is that carefully targeted and filtered data, synthetic and real, can be far more valuable per token than raw scale.

Two cautions specific to training. First, check the generator's terms of use. Some providers restrict using their outputs to train competing models. Second, beware the recursion in the next section.

9. Model collapse: replace versus accumulate

Model collapse (Shumailov et al., published in Nature in 2024 under the title "AI models collapse when trained on recursively generated data") is what happens when models are trained, generation after generation, on data produced by earlier models. Each generation learns the distribution from a finite sample of the previous model's outputs. Rare events are under-represented in any finite sample, so the next model assigns them less probability, the next sample contains even fewer, and so on. The tails of the distribution, the unusual but valid cases, disappear first. Eventually outputs become narrow and repetitive. The authors demonstrated this in Gaussian mixture models, variational autoencoders and language models, and warned that as the web fills with model-generated text, genuine human data will become increasingly valuable.

A toy simulation shows the mechanism. Start with 100 samples from a standard normal distribution (mean 0, standard deviation 1). Each generation, fit a normal distribution to the current data and draw 100 new samples from the fit.

  • Replace: each generation trains only on the previous generation's samples. In three runs, the fitted standard deviation after 50 generations was about 0.64, 0.33 and 0.45, and after 200 generations about 0.02-0.07. The distribution collapsed to a near-point. The probability of a value beyond ±2, which is 4.6% for the original distribution, falls to about 1.2% at standard deviation 0.8 and to essentially zero by 0.5. The tails go first.
  • Accumulate: each generation's samples are added to a pool that still contains the original 100 real samples, and the fit uses the whole pool. The standard deviation stayed near 1.0 (about 0.91-1.04) for all 200 generations.

That second result matches the theory. Gerstgrasser et al. (2024) showed that replacing real data with each generation's synthetic data tends towards collapse, but accumulating successive generations of synthetic data alongside the original real data avoids it. In the accumulate setting, test error has a finite upper bound independent of the number of iterations. They confirmed this across language models, diffusion models for molecules and variational autoencoders.

The practical rules follow directly. Keep real data in every training mixture and every eval set. Track provenance so you know what is synthetic. Never build a pipeline where a model's outputs become the main training data for its successor without real-data anchoring. And value fresh human data, such as real user interactions (with consent) and expert-written examples, as the scarce resource it is becoming.

Precision and recall for generated distributions

"Is this synthetic data good?" splits into two questions. Is each generated item realistic, meaning it lies within the support of the real distribution? That is precision. Does the generated set cover all the kinds of real items, including the rare ones? That is recall. Researchers measure these for generative models by checking, in an embedding space, whether each generated item falls near some real item and whether each real item has some generated item nearby. A generator with high precision and low recall produces realistic but narrow data, the typical LLM failure. One with low precision produces varied nonsense. The cluster comparison in the body is a coarse, practical version of the same idea.

Active generation targeted at failures

The most valuable synthetic data is often generated around failures. Take each production failure, characterise what made it hard (a negation, a mixed intent, an unusual unit), and generate variants that share that property. This turns one failure into a family of regression cases and probes whether the fix generalises. It is the eval equivalent of hard-negative mining, and it is how synthetic data helps most at the boundary of what a system can do, which is where random generation is weakest.

Persona prompting has limits

Asking a model to write "as a 70-year-old non-native speaker" produces the generator's stereotype of that person, not a sample of real people. Persona-conditioned data can exaggerate some traits, sanitise others, and encode biases about groups. It is useful for spreading coverage. It is not a substitute for real users from those groups, and evaluation conclusions about how a system serves a group should rest on real data from that group.

Privacy: synthetic is not automatically anonymous

If a generator is fine-tuned on private records, or prompted with them, it can reproduce identifying fragments. A synthetic record that closely mirrors a real one can re-identify the person. Privacy-preserving synthesis needs either generation without access to the records, formal guarantees such as differential privacy in the generator's training, or explicit checks for memorised content (nearest-neighbour distance between synthetic and real records). Treat "it's synthetic, so it's safe" as a claim to verify.

Synthetic preference data and AI feedback

Preference data for alignment can also be synthesised: a model compares two responses against written principles and the preferences train a reward model. This is the idea behind reinforcement learning from AI feedback and constitution-guided approaches. It scales cheaply, and it inherits every judge bias from ev4, including verbosity and self-preference. As with any judge, validate AI preferences against human preferences on a sample, and watch for the trained model drifting towards what the AI judge likes rather than what people do.

Difficulty is a moving target

Synthetic capability evals have a short shelf life. A generator asked for "hard" questions produces questions hard for models like itself at the time; within a model generation or two they are easy, and the eval saturates (ev1). Some teams generate difficulty adaptively: keep the items current systems fail, generate variants of those, and discard items everything passes, so the set tracks the frontier of what the system can do. This keeps the eval informative but makes scores incomparable over time, because the set keeps changing; pair it with a fixed anchor subset for trend lines, as ev2 recommends for any evolving golden set.

Mixing synthetic and real data in one metric

When a single number must combine real and synthetic items, treat them as strata (ev2) and weight each by what it represents, rather than pooling raw counts. A suite of 300 real items and 2,000 synthetic ones, pooled, is 87% synthetic and reports mostly on the generator's world. Reporting the two separately, with the real result as the headline and the synthetic result as coverage, is almost always clearer, and it makes divergence between them visible instead of averaging it away.

Verifiers make synthetic data trustworthy

The domains where synthetic training data works best (maths, code, formal reasoning, structured extraction) share one property: answers can be checked automatically. A verifier turns an untrustworthy generator into a trustworthy data source by rejecting wrong outputs. Where no verifier exists, the quality of synthetic data is bounded by the quality of whatever judges it. So when planning synthetic data, a senior engineer's first question is "what checks each item?", and if the answer is "nothing", a sample-and-measure plan is required before the data is used.

The public cases: Self-Instruct, phi-1 and model collapse

Three published results together mark out the useful territory for synthetic data. Self-Instruct (Wang et al., 2022) showed that a model could bootstrap its own instruction-following data from 175 seed tasks, with filtering for validity and novelty. Fine-tuning GPT-3 on it gave a 33-point absolute gain on Super-NaturalInstructions, roughly matching InstructGPT-001 there and coming within 5 points of it in human evaluation. The recipe, seed, generate, filter, train, became the template for many open instruction datasets. Its ceiling was the generator's own ability.

phi-1 (Gunasekar et al., 2023) showed that targeted, filtered data, including about 1B tokens of synthetic textbooks and exercises from GPT-3.5 alongside about 6B tokens of filtered web data, could train a 1.3B-parameter model to 50.6% on HumanEval. It is evidence that data quality, of which synthetic generation is one lever, can substitute for a great deal of scale.

Model collapse (Shumailov et al., Nature, 2024; Gerstgrasser et al., 2024) marked the boundary. Recursive training that replaces real data with generated data loses the tails and degrades. Accumulation with real data keeps error bounded. Synthetic data is a powerful complement to real data and a dangerous replacement for it.

Failure modes in practice

The synthetic headline. A team reported "94% accuracy" from a synthetic eval in a launch review. Real-traffic accuracy turned out to be 71%. Fix: headline numbers only from real or realistic representative sets. Synthetic suites are reported separately as coverage and regression results.

The homogeneous set. A synthetic RAG eval of 5,000 questions had a distinct-2 of 0.18 and mean pairwise embedding similarity of 0.7. In effect it held a few hundred questions in many phrasings, all using the documents' own terms. Fix: attribute grids, real seeds, overlap rejection and diversity metrics as acceptance criteria for the set.

The label drift. A model generated expected answers for 3,000 policy questions. A later audit found 11% wrong, concentrated in edge cases, and the team had spent a month "fixing" correct system behaviour to match wrong labels. Fix: verify a sample before use, measure the error rate, and keep provenance so the generated labels can be found and corrected.

The self-referential loop. Training data generated by model A, evaluated on synthetic questions from model A, judged by model A. Every number looked great, and real users disagreed. Fix: different generators and judges for training and evaluation, plus a real anchor.

Synthetic data in the index. A team generated FAQ articles with an LLM to fill gaps in a help centre, and the RAG assistant began answering from them. Some contained confident errors, which the assistant then repeated faithfully. Fix: synthetic content intended for retrieval goes through the same human review as any published documentation, and its provenance is visible to the eval.

What to track

For every dataset with synthetic content, record and display: the synthetic share; the generator models and prompts; filter pass rates per stage; the measured label error rate from the last audit; diversity metrics (distinct-n, embedding spread, grid coverage); the cluster comparison against recent real traffic; and the agreement between synthetic and real anchor results on recent system changes.

Design and diagnose: a synthetic eval for a new feature

Your team is launching a "chat with your bank statements" feature in six weeks. You can't use real customer statements for evaluation before launch. A colleague proposes generating 3,000 synthetic statements with an LLM, generating 5 questions per statement with the same LLM, generating expected answers with it too, and gating launch at 90% accuracy. Critique the plan and propose a better one.

Answer

Problems with the plan. (1) Circularity: the same model generates the data, the questions and the answers, so the eval measures agreement with that model. (2) Label errors: statement questions are numeric ("how much did I spend on groceries in March?"), and LLMs make arithmetic and aggregation errors, which concentrate in exactly the multi-step questions that matter. (3) Realism gap: generated statements will be cleaner than real ones (consistent merchant names, no pending transactions, no refunds, no currency conversions), and generated questions cleaner than users' ("why is my money gone", "that coffee place thing"). (4) Clustering: 5 questions per statement means effective sample size well below 15,000 (ev7). (5) A 90% gate on synthetic data says little about real accuracy.

Better plan. Generate statements programmatically, not with an LLM. Use a simulator with realistic merchant-name variation, pending and reversed transactions, refunds, foreign currency, recurring payments, and statistics drawn from published or internal aggregate spending patterns. Then the ground truth is computed exactly by code: answers are verified by construction.

Questions. Write 100 by hand with the product team and support staff, in real users' voice. Collect phrasing from existing support tickets about statements, without personal data. Then generate variants with two different model families using an attribute grid: question type (sum, find, compare, explain, unanswerable), time reference style, vagueness, typos. Expected answers come from code over the simulated statement, never from the LLM.

Filtering and diversity. Deduplicate. Check that each question's computed answer is unique, flagging ambiguous ones. Measure distinct-n and the grid coverage. Include 15% unanswerable or ambiguous questions where the expected behaviour is to ask or decline.

Anchor. Run an internal dogfood: employees use the feature on their own statements with consent and rate answers. That gives a small real anchor. Check that system changes move dogfood and synthetic results in the same direction.

Gate. Set separate thresholds per question type, with intervals computed at the statement level. Ship to a small beta, and replace synthetic headline numbers with real-traffic labels as soon as possible.
