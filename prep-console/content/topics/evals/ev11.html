A company fine-tunes a model on two years of its customer-support conversations, about 400,000 of them, to make an assistant that writes like its best agents. The first version has strange habits. It ends a third of its replies with the same sign-off paragraph, word for word. It recommends a discontinued product. On one internal test, it reproduces a real customer's phone number. Its evaluation scores are excellent, suspiciously so on a few questions it answers with uncanny precision.

Every one of these problems was in the data. The repeated sign-off came from a support macro pasted into 60,000 conversations, so the model learned it as the most important thing to say. The discontinued product came from old conversations nobody filtered by date. The phone number appeared in dozens of conversations with one persistent customer and was memorised. The uncanny eval answers were memorised too: some eval questions had come from the same support logs and were sitting in the training data.

Fine-tuning (lm11) is often described as "train on your data". The hard part is deciding what your data should be. That job is data curation: sourcing, cleaning, deduplicating, filtering for quality, deciding the mixture, removing anything that overlaps the evals, handling personal data, and knowing where every example came from and whether you're allowed to use it. It is unglamorous work that decides more about the resulting model than most modelling choices. It is also an evaluation problem in two senses. Every curation step should be justified by evals of the trained model, and curation done wrong, through contamination, makes those evals meaningless.

This lesson walks the pipeline. It covers cleaning and normalisation, exact deduplication, and near-duplicate detection with MinHash, worked by hand on two sentences, with the banding trick that makes it scale, using the configuration from the FineWeb web dataset. It covers heuristic and model-based quality filters, mixtures and epochs, decontamination against eval sets, PII detection and masking, and licensing and provenance. The case is the 2021 paper that showed how much duplication standard training sets contain and what removing it does.

Curating training data is like editing an anthology. You have a mountain of submissions. Many are near-copies of each other: the same story submitted twice with a new title, three versions with minor edits. Some are badly written, some are off-topic, some infringe someone's copyright, and some contain a real person's private details. A few are the very stories the critics will use to review the anthology, and including them would be cheating. The editor's job is to choose, from the mountain, a collection that teaches readers what the anthology is meant to teach, in the right proportions, with each piece included once, legally and safely.

The analogy breaks in scale. An editor reads every submission, and can tell at a glance that two stories are the same one retitled. A training pipeline may handle millions of documents and can't compare every pair for near-duplication, since that would be trillions of comparisons. Much of the technical content of curation is about doing an editor's judgement automatically, cheaply and measurably at that scale, and about checking afterwards, with evals of the trained model, that the automated judgement was right.

The curation funnel

Each stage of the funnel targets one problem. Cleaning removes what isn't usable at all: broken records, the wrong language, data outside the relevant period. Exact deduplication removes identical copies. Near-duplicate removal collapses templated and lightly edited copies, which is where most real duplication lives. Quality filtering keeps examples worth imitating. Decontamination removes anything overlapping the eval sets. PII masking and licence checks make the data safe and legal to use. The output is a versioned dataset with a datasheet describing all of the above.

Why does each stage matter enough to justify its cost? Because a model learns whatever is frequent and consistent in its data, whether you meant it to or not. Duplicates make a few examples frequent, so the model over-learns them, and that is how a support macro becomes a verbal tic or a phone number becomes a memorised string. Low-quality examples are consistent in their flaws, so the model learns the flaws. Eval items in training make the model look like it generalises when it remembers. Personal data that appears often enough can be reproduced word for word. Every stage of the funnel removes one way the data could teach the wrong lesson, and the order matters too: cheap, high-volume stages first, so expensive ones such as model-based filters and human review run on less data.

Two further decisions sit across the funnel. The first is the mixture: how much of each source goes into training, and how many times small sources are repeated. The second is validation: every step is a hypothesis ("removing these makes the model better"), and the way to test it is to train with and without the step and compare on the eval suite.

1. Sourcing and cleaning

Fine-tuning data usually comes from four kinds of source, each with characteristic problems. Production logs, such as past support conversations, user-accepted drafts and resolved tickets, are realistic and abundant. They are also full of personal data, templated text and behaviour you don't want to imitate (rude agents, wrong answers, outdated policy), and using them needs a lawful basis and attention to retention rules (ae5). Expert-written examples are high quality and expensive, so there are always few of them. Public datasets are convenient and come with licences and unknown contamination. Synthetic data (ev10) is cheap and scalable, and carries the generator's biases and label errors.

Cleaning makes the data usable. Parse records into the training format (instruction and response, or multi-turn conversations), dropping those that fail to parse. Normalise text: consistent Unicode, whitespace and line endings, and remove markup, signatures and quoted reply chains in emails. Filter by language if the model targets specific languages. Filter by date where content goes stale, such as policies and product names. Remove records whose outcome shows they're bad targets: escalated conversations, replies later corrected by a supervisor, tickets reopened by the customer. Each filter should be logged with how many records it removed, because a filter that removes 40% when you expected 5% is usually a bug.

2. Exact deduplication

The simplest duplicates are identical after normalisation. Normalise (lower-case, collapse whitespace, strip punctuation if appropriate), hash each record with a fast hash, and keep one record per hash. It is cheap, linear in the data size, and catches a surprising amount: repeated submissions, logging bugs that wrote records twice, data merged from overlapping exports.

Duplication can also live at the substring level: a long boilerplate paragraph inside otherwise different documents. Lee et al. (2021) used a suffix array, a sorted list of every suffix of the concatenated corpus, to find repeated substrings of 50 tokens or more and remove them. That is how they found a 61-word English sentence repeated over 60,000 times in C4 (the Colossal Clean Crawled Corpus), a widely used web corpus. For fine-tuning data, the equivalent is the repeated sign-off macro from the opening story. Removing it from the training targets, while keeping the conversations, solves the problem.

3. Near-duplicates and Jaccard similarity

Most duplication is near-duplication: the same support macro with a different customer name, the same article with different navigation text, the same template with a changed number. Exact hashing misses all of it, because one changed character changes the hash completely. To find near-duplicates we need a similarity measure and a way to compute it at scale.

The standard measure is Jaccard similarity between sets of shingles. A shingle is an overlapping sequence of n words (or characters), and a document is represented as the set of its shingles. Jaccard similarity is J(A, B) = |A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B|.

Worked, with word bigrams (n = 2) on two support sentences that differ by one word:

  • A: "refunds are processed within five business days of approval"
  • B: "refunds are processed within seven business days of approval"

Each has 9 words and so 8 bigrams. Number the distinct bigrams across both: 0 "refunds are", 1 "are processed", 2 "processed within", 3 "within five", 4 "five business", 5 "business days", 6 "days of", 7 "of approval", 8 "within seven", 9 "seven business". Then A = {0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7} and B = {0, 1, 2, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9}. They share 6 bigrams (0, 1, 2, 5, 6, 7), and their union has 10. So J = 6/10 = 0.6.

One changed word removed two shared bigrams from each side, because every shingle containing that word differs. In a 300-word document, the same one-word change would leave Jaccard near 0.99. Shingle size matters too: larger n makes the measure stricter, since more shingles touch each change. FineWeb used 5-word shingles for web documents.

Computing exact Jaccard for every pair is the problem. With a million documents there are about 5 × 1011 pairs, and each comparison handles sets of hundreds of shingles. MinHash shrinks each document to a short signature, and banding avoids comparing most pairs at all.

4. MinHash, worked by hand

MinHash (Broder, 1997) rests on one observation. Take a random hash function h that assigns each shingle a number, effectively putting all shingles in a random order. Look at the smallest hash value in A and the smallest in B. Consider the union A ∪ B: under a random ordering, its smallest element is equally likely to be any of its members. The two minima are equal exactly when that smallest element is in both sets, which happens with probability |A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B|. So:

P(min h(A) = min h(B)) = J(A, B)

One hash function gives a yes/no observation with probability J. Use k independent hash functions and the fraction of matching minima estimates J. Each document is represented by its signature, the k minima, and comparing two documents means comparing two short vectors.

Worked with the two sentences and 8 toy hash functions of the form h(x) = (a·x + b) mod 11, applied to the bigram numbers 0-9. Because 11 is prime, each of these functions just reorders the numbers 0-10, which is exactly the random ordering MinHash needs.

Check one row by hand. For (a, b) = (1, 3), bigram 8 ("within seven", only in B) hashes to (8 + 3) mod 11 = 0, the smallest possible value, so B's minimum is 0. A doesn't contain bigram 8, and its smallest value is 3, from bigram 0. The minima differ because the "winner" of the union was a bigram unique to B. 4 of 8 minima match, so the MinHash estimate is 0.5, against a true Jaccard of 0.6. That isn't an error in the method; it's sampling noise. Each hash is a coin flip with probability 0.6, so with k hashes the standard error of the estimate is √(J(1 − J)/k). For k = 8 that is √(0.24/8) ≈ 0.17, so 0.5 is well within one standard error. With k = 128 it falls to about 0.043, and with FineWeb's 112 hashes (14 × 8) to about 0.046. Production systems use around 100 or more hash functions, and can confirm pairs near the threshold with exact Jaccard.

5. Banding: finding candidates without comparing every pair

Signatures make each comparison cheap, but comparing every pair is still quadratic. Locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) avoids most comparisons. Split each signature of k = b × r values into b bands of r consecutive values. For each band, hash its r values into a bucket. Two documents become a candidate pair if they land in the same bucket for at least one band, and only candidate pairs are compared (or simply treated as duplicates).

The probability that two documents with similarity s match on all r values of one band is sr. The probability that they match on at least one of b bands is

P(candidate) = 1 − (1 − sr)b

FineWeb's configuration, from the datatrove pipeline used to build it, is 5-word shingles with 14 bands of 8 hashes each (112 hash functions), applied separately to each Common Crawl dump. Plugging in b = 14 and r = 8:

The curve is S-shaped, with its steep part near (1/b)1/r = (1/14)1/8 ≈ 0.72. Pairs well above that are almost always caught, and pairs well below almost never become candidates. You tune the threshold with b and r: more rows per band make the curve steeper and push the threshold up, more bands push it down. The trade-off is between missed duplicates (false negatives, pairs above the intended threshold that escape) and wasted comparisons or wrong merges (false positives, pairs below it that get caught).

After candidates are found, duplicates are grouped, often by treating candidate pairs as edges and taking connected components, and one representative per group is kept. One finding from FineWeb's ablations is worth knowing: deduplicating each crawl dump separately produced better models than deduplicating across all dumps globally. Aggressive global deduplication had removed proportionally more of the higher-quality content that recurs across crawls, leaving a lower-quality remainder. Even deduplication is a choice to validate with ablations, not a step whose more aggressive version is automatically better.

6. Quality filters

After deduplication, the next question is which examples are worth imitating. There are two kinds of filter.

Heuristic rules are cheap and transparent. Examples are length bounds, the share of non-alphabetic characters, repeated lines or n-grams, language-identification confidence, presence of boilerplate phrases, and for code, whether it parses. Rules like these were used to build large pretraining corpora. DeepMind's Gopher work, for instance, filtered web documents by word count (keeping roughly 50 to 100,000 words) and mean word length, among other rules. For fine-tuning data, domain rules matter more: drop conversations that were escalated, replies later corrected, tickets reopened, and responses that don't follow the current policy.

Model-based filters score each example with a learned model. You can train a small classifier on a few hundred examples labelled good or bad by experts, or use a validated LLM judge (ev4) with a rubric for what makes a good training target. FineWeb-Edu is a public example at scale: an LLM rated a sample of web pages for educational value, a classifier trained on those ratings scored all of FineWeb, and the high-scoring 1.3 trillion tokens became FineWeb-Edu. For fine-tuning on support conversations, outcome signals are often the best "labels": conversations that resolved without follow-up contact and earned good ratings make better targets than ones that merely read fluently.

LIMA (Zhou et al., 2023) showed how far quality can substitute for quantity. A 65B-parameter LLaMA model fine-tuned on just 1,000 carefully curated prompt-response pairs, with no reinforcement learning, produced responses that people judged equivalent to or better than GPT-4's in 43% of comparisons, 58% against Bard and 65% against DaVinci003. The authors' interpretation, which they called the superficial alignment hypothesis, was that almost all knowledge is learned in pretraining and fine-tuning mainly teaches format and style, so a small, excellent dataset suffices for that.

Audit what filters remove, by slice. A classifier trained mostly on English may score other languages lower. A judge that likes long answers removes concise good ones. A rule that drops short conversations may remove all of a quick-resolution intent. Sample the rejected examples, not just the accepted ones.

7. Mixtures and epochs

Fine-tuning data usually comes from several sources of different sizes and value, and the mixture decides how much each contributes. Worked example: 40,000 filtered support conversations (realistic, medium quality), 2,000 expert-written examples (high quality, covering weak areas) and 20,000 verified synthetic examples (rare intents and edge cases). You plan a training set of 30,000 examples with target shares of 50/20/30.

Small, valuable sources are upsampled, repeated over several epochs (passes over that source). Large, noisier ones are down-sampled. Repetition has limits. Each extra pass gives less new signal and raises the risk of memorising those exact examples, so watch held-out performance on that source and test for verbatim regurgitation. The weights themselves are hypotheses to tune with evals per capability. If tone improves but policy accuracy drops, the mixture is over-weighting conversational style relative to policy knowledge. Keep a general-capability eval too, because a narrow mixture can erode skills the base model had, which is sometimes called forgetting.

8. Decontamination

Decontamination removes training examples that overlap evaluation data. It is the training-side half of the contamination problem ev2 raised for golden sets. In the opening story, eval questions taken from the same support logs ended up in the training data, and the model's "uncanny precision" on them was memory. Lee et al. found the same issue in public data: over 4% of the validation sets of some standard datasets overlapped their training sets.

A practical decontamination step:

  1. Normalise training and eval text identically.
  2. N-gram overlap: flag any training example sharing a long run of words with any eval item, such as 13 consecutive words, the threshold the GPT-3 paper used for its benchmark decontamination. Index eval n-grams in a hash set so each training example is checked in one pass.
  3. Semantic overlap: flag training examples whose embedding is very close to an eval item, to catch paraphrases and synthetic variants generated from eval items, which share meaning but few long n-grams.
  4. Review and remove flagged examples, recording what was removed and why. Sometimes the eval item is the one to retire, if it turns out to be a common template that legitimately appears in training.
  5. Re-run whenever the training data or any eval set changes.

Worked: checking 150,400 training examples against 1,200 eval items flags 600 training examples overlapping 41 eval items. Most are a support macro that appears in both. The training examples are removed, leaving 149,800. The 41 eval items are re-examined: 35 stay, since the overlap was a shared policy sentence now removed from training, and 6 are retired from the eval as too templated to test generalisation. Without this step, the evaluation of the fine-tuned model would have overstated quality on exactly those items.

9. PII detection and masking

Production data contains personal information, and fine-tuned models can memorise and reproduce it, especially data that repeats, like the persistent customer's phone number. PII handling combines several detectors:

  • Patterns for structured identifiers: email addresses, phone numbers in local formats, card numbers (validated with the Luhn checksum to cut false positives), national ID formats, IBANs, IP addresses.
  • Named-entity recognition for names, addresses and organisations, which patterns can't catch reliably.
  • Context rules, such as "my account number is …".

Detected PII is replaced with typed placeholders ("[EMAIL]", "[PHONE]"), or with realistic fakes that are consistent within a conversation, so the model still learns the conversational structure without the real values. Where a field isn't needed at all, drop it before detection. Removal by design beats detection.

Measure the detector. Seed a sample with known PII and count how much it finds. Suppose it catches 940 of 1,000 seeded instances, a recall of 94%. If about 3% of 150,000 conversations contain PII, roughly 4,500 conversations, then around 6% of those instances, a couple of hundred, may survive. Whether that is acceptable depends on sensitivity, and it usually argues for layering: dropping fields, deduplication (which removes repeated records, the ones most likely to be memorised), and a memorisation test before release. Prompt the model with prefixes of training records containing masked-then-reinserted canary values, and check whether it completes them verbatim.

10. Licensing and provenance

Every dataset raises the question "are we allowed to use this, for this purpose?" The answer depends on the source. Your own production data depends on your terms of service, privacy policy, consents and retention rules. Public datasets come with licences: permissive ones (MIT, Apache 2.0, CC BY), copyleft or share-alike ones (GPL, CC BY-SA), non-commercial ones (CC BY-NC), and custom terms. Scraped content raises terms-of-service questions, and some publishers signal opt-outs. Model-generated data depends on the provider's terms, some of which restrict using outputs to train competing models. Contractor-written data depends on the contract's ownership terms. This lesson isn't legal advice, and these questions need legal review, but the engineering requirement is clear. Record the source and licence of every dataset, decide deliberately whether each use is permitted, and keep enough lineage to remove a source and retrain if needed.

A datasheet (Gebru et al., 2018) is the standard way to record this. It covers motivation, sources, composition (sizes, languages, slices, synthetic share), collection process, every preprocessing step with its version and thresholds, known gaps and biases, licences, and permitted and prohibited uses. It turns the curation pipeline from tribal knowledge into an auditable artefact, and it is what lets someone answer a question about the model months later.

Validating curation with ablations

Every curation step is a hypothesis about what makes a better model, and the only real test is training with and without it. Dataset papers like FineWeb are largely collections of such ablations: train small models on data processed different ways, with equal compute, and compare on a fixed suite of evals. For a fine-tuning team, a reduced version is usually affordable: a smaller base model or fewer steps, a few variants (raw, deduplicated, deduplicated plus filtered), paired comparison per capability (ev7), plus memorisation and safety checks. Ablations often overturn intuitions. FineWeb's finding that per-dump deduplication beat global deduplication is a reminder that "more aggressive" is not "better" by default.

Choosing shingle size and threshold

Shingle size and similarity threshold define what counts as a duplicate. Small shingles (unigrams) make unrelated documents on the same topic look similar. Large shingles (10+ words) make lightly edited copies look different. Thresholds that are too low merge distinct documents. Too high, and templated variants survive. The right values depend on document length and on what kind of near-duplication you need to remove: boilerplate-heavy web pages differ from short support replies. Calibrate on a labelled sample. Have people mark a few hundred pairs as duplicate or not, then pick parameters whose LSH curve separates them, measuring precision and recall of the duplicate decision.

Semantic deduplication

MinHash finds lexical near-duplicates. Two support answers that say the same thing in different words are not caught. Semantic deduplication embeds examples and clusters them, keeping a representative subset of each tight cluster. This can remove redundancy that lexical methods miss and, in some reported pretraining experiments, has improved efficiency. It is more expensive and riskier, because embedding similarity also merges examples that differ in exactly the detail that matters (ev3's negation problem). Use it for redundancy reduction in large, homogeneous sources, with careful thresholds and spot checks, not as a replacement for lexical dedup.

Contamination you can't see by overlap

N-gram and embedding checks catch copies and paraphrases. They don't catch a training set that teaches the specific answers to eval questions in a different form, for example a policy explanation written by someone who was looking at the eval set. Nor do they catch data that makes the eval easier for systematic reasons, such as training heavily on the exact templates the eval uses. Organisational controls handle these: separate eval and training data owners, access controls on eval sets, and a rule that nobody writing training data looks at eval items. Fresh eval items written after the training data was frozen are the final check (ev2).

Data poisoning

If training data includes content an adversary can influence, such as scraped web pages, user-submitted feedback or public datasets, an attacker can plant examples that teach a backdoor or a bias. The OWASP (Open Worldwide Application Security Project) Top 10 for LLM applications lists data and model poisoning for this reason. Curation defences include provenance tracking (knowing which sources could be influenced), anomaly detection on new data, deduplication (poisoning often relies on repetition), and evaluating for specific triggered behaviours. ev9's red-teaming mindset applies to the data pipeline too.

Curation for preference data and evaluation data

The same pipeline applies, with twists, to other kinds of data. Preference data for alignment (pairs of responses with a preferred one) needs deduplication of prompts, so a few prompts don't dominate; filtering of pairs where labellers disagreed (ev5), which carry little signal; and decontamination against evals just like instruction data. Evaluation data needs curation too: golden sets should be deduplicated, because ten near-identical items inflate the apparent sample size and shrink intervals falsely (ev7), and checked for PII before they are shared with vendors or contractors (ev2). A team that builds one curation library and applies it to training, preference and evaluation data avoids maintaining three inconsistent ones.

Recording what was removed

Teams record what they keep and rarely what they discard. Keeping a sample of removed examples per filter, with the reason, makes three things possible. You can audit filter behaviour by slice. You can recover data if a filter turns out to be wrong. And you can answer "why doesn't the model handle X?" when X was filtered out. It costs little storage and saves a great deal of debugging.

The public case: "Deduplicating Training Data Makes Language Models Better" (Lee et al., 2021)

This paper, from researchers at Google, quantified how much duplication standard language-model training sets contain, and what removing it does. The authors built two tools. The first, exact substring deduplication, used a suffix array over the whole corpus to find and remove repeated runs of 50 or more tokens. The second, approximate deduplication, used MinHash with LSH to find near-duplicate documents. Applied to widely used datasets, including C4, the tools found extensive duplication. The most memorable example is a single 61-word English sentence repeated over 60,000 times in C4. Many documents were near-copies, such as templated pages that differed only in a product name or location.

They then trained language models on the original and deduplicated versions. Models trained on deduplicated data emitted memorised training text ten times less often, and reached the same or better accuracy with fewer training steps. They also measured train-test overlap. Over 4% of the validation sets of some standard datasets overlapped their training sets, which means evaluation numbers on those sets had been partly measuring memorisation. They released their code.

The production lessons map directly onto fine-tuning work. Duplication is everywhere, mostly in near-duplicate form, and exact hashing alone misses it. Duplicates cause memorisation, which is a quality problem (repeated macros) and a privacy problem (repeated personal data). And training-evaluation overlap silently inflates scores, so decontamination is part of evaluation, not an optional extra. Later dataset efforts, including FineWeb with its documented MinHash configuration, made these steps standard practice.

Failure modes in practice

The macro parrot. A fine-tuned support model ended a third of its replies with a sign-off pasted into 60,000 training conversations. Fix: substring-level deduplication of repeated spans in targets, plus near-duplicate collapsing of templated replies.

The stale policy. The model recommended a discontinued product, because two-year-old conversations were never filtered by date or checked against current policy. Fix: date filters, a policy-consistency check on targets, and a regression eval on current-policy questions.

The contaminated win. A fine-tuned model scored 12 points above the base model on the team's eval, and 3 points above on freshly written items. Eval questions had come from the same logs. Fix: decontamination before training, eval sets drawn from a separate period or source, and fresh-item checks.

The over-filter. A quality classifier removed 70% of Spanish conversations as "low quality" because it was trained on English examples, and the model's Spanish got worse. Fix: per-language thresholds or classifiers, and auditing removals by slice.

The unremovable source. A data provider later withdrew permission for its content, and the team had no record of which training examples came from it, so the only option was to rebuild the whole dataset from scratch. Fix: per-example provenance from ingestion onwards, so a source can be filtered out and the model retrained on a known subset.

What to track

For each dataset version, track counts after every stage; duplicate-cluster size distribution; filter removal rates by slice; the decontamination report (flagged examples and eval items); PII detector recall on the seeded sample and the residual estimate; source and licence breakdown; the synthetic share; and results of the ablations and the memorisation test that justified the version.

Diagnose and design: the fine-tune that looked too good

Your team fine-tuned a model on 300,000 support conversations. On your 1,000-item eval it scores 89% against the base model's 74%. A colleague writes 150 fresh questions in the same style, and on those the fine-tuned model scores 77% against the base model's 73%. Users report that it often ends replies with "Is there anything else I can help you with today? Have a wonderful day!" and occasionally mentions a promotion that ended last year. Explain what probably happened and how you'd rebuild the dataset.

Answer

Diagnosis. The large gap between the original eval (+15 points) and fresh questions (+4) points to contamination. The eval items, or near-copies of them, are probably in the training data. Check with a 13-gram overlap and embedding similarity between the 1,000 eval items and the training set, and see how much of the 15-point gain sits on overlapping items. The repeated sign-off means a templated closing appears in a huge share of training targets, overweighted by duplication. The expired promotion means the data wasn't filtered by date or checked against current policy.
