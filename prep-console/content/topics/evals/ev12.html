A team has done everything the earlier topics in this track recommend, once. They built a golden set from real traffic (ev2), validated an LLM judge against expert labels (ev4), computed paired comparisons with intervals (ev7) and ran an A/B test for their last model change (ev8). Six months later the practice has quietly decayed. The eval script lives on one engineer's laptop and runs when someone remembers. A prompt change shipped on a Friday with "looked fine on a few examples". The judge prompt was edited in March, so scores before and after aren't comparable, and nobody wrote that down. Production logs exist, but as unstructured text in a bucket that's deleted after 14 days, and nobody looks at them. There is a dashboard with 41 charts. When quality drops after the provider quietly updates the model, the first signal is a customer escalation two weeks later.

This is the normal fate of evaluation that isn't built into the engineering system. The methods in this track only protect a product if they run automatically, on every change and continuously in production. Their results must be stored so they can be compared, failures must reach a person who acts on them, and the loop must close back into the eval suite. ae7 covered the release side of LLMOps: manifests, continuous-integration (CI) tiers, canaries and rollback. ae3 covered production monitoring and drift. This lesson builds the evaluation system that connects them, the senior engineer's answer to "how do you know it still works next week?"

It covers seven things. Running eval suites in CI within a time and cost budget, including record/replay. Designing regression gates that catch what matters without flapping, worked through a gate's actual logic. Versioning eval sets and graders so results stay comparable. Tracing production requests in a standard, privacy-safe structure. Sampling production for judged and human review, with the sample sizes. Alerting on quality drift with control charts, worked by simulation to show why the obvious daily threshold is slow. And the one-page eval dashboard a team actually reads. The case is GitHub's description of how the Copilot team evaluates models: thousands of tests in CI, an audited judge, and canary rollouts to employees.

A factory that makes a precision part doesn't rely on an inspector glancing at a few parts at the end of the week. It has a quality system. Incoming material is checked before it enters the line, which is the CI gate. Every batch carries a record of which machine, which settings and which material lot produced it, the trace and the version keys. Inspectors pull parts from the line at regular intervals, at random and whenever a sensor flags something, which is production sampling. Measurements go on control charts pinned to the wall, with limits that tell the shift supervisor when the process has drifted, not just when a single part is bad. That is drift alerting. One board shows the handful of numbers that say whether the line is healthy, the dashboard. And every defect that reaches a customer is traced back to its cause, and a new check is added so it can't recur. That last step is the loop.

The analogy carries further than it might seem. Control charts were invented in manufacturing in the 1920s for exactly the problem LLM teams face: telling a real shift in a process from normal variation, using small samples taken over time. Where the analogy breaks is the parts. An LLM system's "process" includes components the team doesn't control, such as the provider's model, user behaviour and the documents in the index. So the quality system must watch inputs and dependencies, not just outputs.

The closed evaluation loop

Read the loop from left to right along the top. A change passes through CI eval tiers: a fast smoke suite on every pull request, a full suite before merge or release, and exhaustive runs nightly. A regression gate decides whether it may proceed. It goes through a canary and is released. Then along the bottom, from right to left. Production requests are traced, traces are sampled, and the sample is graded with the same graders as the offline suite: assertions, a validated judge and a human subset. Grades feed control charts that alert on drift and a dashboard. Flagged items are triaged, and confirmed failures become new eval cases that gate the next change. Underneath it all, every result is stored with its version keys, so any number can be reproduced and compared with the right baseline.

Notice that the same graders appear on both paths. The policy judge that gates a release in CI is the judge that grades the production sample, and the assertions that block a pull request are the assertions that run on live responses. That symmetry is deliberate: it means an offline score and a production score measure the same thing, so a gap between them points at a real difference in inputs or behaviour rather than at two inconsistent rulers.

None of the pieces is new to this track. What this lesson adds is the engineering that makes them run every day without heroics: budgets, gate logic, storage, sampling arithmetic, alert design and ownership.

1. Eval suites in CI: tiers, budgets and record/replay

ae7 introduced eval tiers. The engineering challenge is making them fast and cheap enough that nobody is tempted to skip them. A useful split, with typical budgets:

TierWhenContentsBudget
SmokeEvery pull request200-500 cases: assertions, must-pass cases, recorded responses where valid< 5-10 min, near-zero model cost
FullBefore merge of prompt/model/retrieval changes; release candidatesFull golden set, 3 samples/item, live model calls, validated judges, slices, safety suite30-60 min, tens of dollars
Nightly / scheduledDaily on main and on production configEverything, plus red-team runs, long-tail suites, probe sets against productionHours, budgeted monthly

Two techniques keep the smoke tier fast. Record/replay: when a change can't affect what goes into the model, such as a refactor of response formatting or a change to logging, replay model responses recorded from the last live run. Tests then run in seconds, cost nothing and are deterministic. When a change can affect the model's input (the prompt template, retrieved context, tool schemas, model parameters), replayed responses are invalid, and the affected suites must run live. Deciding which is which is the second technique, change-based suite selection. Map files and configuration to the suites they can affect. A change to the refund prompt triggers the refund feature's live suite. A change to the chunker triggers retrieval metrics and the RAG suite. A change to shared infrastructure triggers everything.

Cost controls matter for the full tier. Cache judge verdicts keyed by a hash of (judge version, rubric version, input, output), so unchanged outputs aren't re-judged. Run calls in parallel within provider rate limits. Track eval cost and runtime as metrics in their own right. An eval suite whose cost doubles every quarter will eventually be cut, usually at the worst moment.

2. Regression gates that catch what matters

A regression gate is code that reads eval results and returns pass or fail with reasons. ae7 worked a paired comparison that blocked a release because one slice collapsed. Here is the full structure of a gate, as a team would implement it:

def gate(candidate, baseline, cfg):
    reasons = []
    # 1. Must-pass cases: every trial must pass
    for case in cfg.must_pass:
        if not all(candidate.trials[case]):
            reasons.append(f"must-pass failed: {case}")
    # 2. Aggregate non-inferiority on paired per-item scores
    d = [candidate.score[i] - baseline.score[i] for i in cfg.items]
    lo, hi = paired_ci(d)                          # ev7: paired SE or bootstrap
    if lo < -cfg.margin:                           # e.g. margin = 0.015
        reasons.append(f"aggregate: lower bound {lo:+.3f} below -{cfg.margin}")
    # 3. Slice floors with Holm correction across gate slices
    for s, p_value, score in holm_adjusted(candidate, baseline, cfg.gate_slices):
        if score < cfg.floor[s] and p_value < 0.05:
            reasons.append(f"slice {s}: {score:.1%} below floor {cfg.floor[s]:.0%}")
    # 4. Guardrails
    if candidate.schema_valid_rate < 0.995: reasons.append("schema validity")
    if candidate.p95_latency_ms > baseline.p95_latency_ms + 300: reasons.append("latency")
    if candidate.cost_per_req > 1.10 * baseline.cost_per_req: reasons.append("cost")
    return (len(reasons) == 0, reasons)

Each component catches a different kind of regression. Must-pass cases are the non-negotiables: legal requirements, safety behaviours, money limits. They are run several times and must pass every time, because pass^k is what matters for them (ev1). The aggregate non-inferiority check uses a paired comparison with a margin (ev7). It asks "is the candidate not worse than baseline by more than 1.5 points, with 95% confidence?", rather than "is it not significantly worse?", which a noisy suite always passes. Slice floors catch the collapse of one slice that an average hides, with a correction across the gate slices so five slices don't create a 23% chance of a false block. Guardrails catch improvements bought with latency, cost or broken formatting.

Worked: a candidate prompt for the support assistant, run against baseline on the full tier.

The gate fails on latency. The new prompt is longer and pushes the model to write more. Quality is fine, so the fix is to trim the prompt or cap output length, not to abandon the change. The refund-disputes slice is below its floor but not significantly, given its noise, so the gate passes it and flags it for the next run. That is the right behaviour for a 120-item slice whose interval is about ±6 points.

Flakiness is the gate's main enemy. Measure the suite's A/A noise by running the same configuration ten times every month or so, and set margins above it (ev7). Replay where valid. Quarantine cases that flip without cause and fix them, rather than re-running the gate until it goes green. Make overrides possible but costly: a named approver and a written reason, recorded with the release. A gate that is overridden every week is telling you its thresholds or its cases are wrong.

How do you know the gate works? Test it. Seed known-bad candidates, such as a prompt with the refund-limit sentence deleted, a cheaper model known to be worse, or a retrieval config with half the index missing, and check that the gate blocks each. This is mutation testing for the eval system. It measures the gate's sensitivity, which is otherwise only discovered when a real regression gets through.

3. Versioning: eval sets, graders and results

An eval result is a function of four things: the system (the release manifest from ae7), the eval set, the grader (judge model, judge prompt, rubric, parsing code) and the harness (sampling settings, number of trials, aggregation). Change any one and the number changes. So every stored result is keyed by all four:

result_key = (manifest="support-2026.10.03-r2",
              eval_set="support-golden@v7 (sha256:9c1e…)",
              grader="policy-judge@v4 (model=judge-2026-08, rubric=r12)",
              harness="harness@1.8 (trials=3, temp=0.7)")

The rules follow. Eval sets are immutable once versioned. Changing a case creates a new version, identified by a content hash so silent edits are impossible. Each version has a changelog: cases added from production failures, cases retired, expected answers updated after a policy change. Graders are versioned the same way, and a new grader version is validated against human labels before use (ev4). Comparisons are only made between results that differ in exactly one key, usually the manifest. When the eval set or grader changes, re-run the baseline on the new version to create a bridge, so trend lines can continue. Some teams keep a small anchor set and anchor grader that never change, purely for long-run trends (ev2).

Store results at the item level, not just aggregates: per case, per trial, every grader's verdict and reasoning, with the trace of the system's run. Item-level storage is what makes paired comparisons, slice breakdowns and "show me what changed" diffs possible. Frameworks such as Inspect write structured logs with every sample, score and transcript for this reason.

4. Tracing production requests

Production evaluation starts with data, and the right shape for that data is a trace: one record per request, made of nested spans, one per step, each timed and annotated. For a RAG assistant with tools:

Useful attributes per span: for model calls, the model identifier as returned by the API, the prompt template version, sampling parameters, input and output token counts, latency, finish reason. For retrieval, the query, the returned document IDs and scores, and the index version. For tool calls, the name, arguments (redacted where sensitive), result status and latency. At the trace level, the release manifest ID, a pseudonymous user or session ID, the channel, and links to feedback events that arrive later. Feedback includes thumbs, edits and escalations (ev8).

Use a standard schema rather than inventing one. The OpenTelemetry project maintains semantic conventions for generative AI, standard names for these attributes and spans, now kept in a dedicated repository. Examples are gen_ai.operation.name, gen_ai.request.model and gen_ai.usage.input_tokens, with conventions for agents, tool execution and evaluation as well. Standard names let tracing tools, dashboards and eval pipelines interoperate. The conventions are still evolving, so pin the version you implement.

Two constraints shape what you store. Privacy: redact personal data at write time, not later, and store raw content only where policy allows (ae5). Retention: keep full content for a short window for debugging, keep structured metadata longer for trends, and copy sampled, consented and scrubbed traces into the eval store when they become cases. ae7 called this trade-off debuggability versus data retention. The design decision is made once, deliberately, rather than discovered during an incident.

Traces also enable replay. A production trace's inputs (message, retrieved documents, tool results) can be fed to a candidate system offline, to see how it would have answered the same request. That turns production traffic into a ready-made eval set. Traces that led to failures become regression cases, and random traces become fresh representative samples.

5. Sampling production for review

You can't have humans read every conversation, and you may not want to pay a judge for every one. Production sampling has two different jobs, and they need different samples.

Job 1: an unbiased estimate of quality. This needs a random sample, ideally stratified by slice (ev2), graded by a validated judge. The sample size comes from the precision you need. For a weekly estimate within ±3 points at around 85% quality: n = 1.96² × 0.85 × 0.15 / 0.03² ≈ 545 judged conversations a week. For ±3 points in each of five slices, about 545 per slice. A human-labelled subset of 100-150 a week measures the judge's ongoing agreement and specificity (ev4). If the judge's error rates are known, the estimate can be corrected for them (the Rogan-Gladen correction from ev4).

Job 2: finding failures fast. This needs a targeted sample, items selected because a signal suggests trouble. Candidates include thumbs-down and escalations, regenerations, low judge confidence (probability of PASS near 0.5), guardrail flags, long or looping agent traces, conversations in new intent clusters (ae3), and outputs that changed most between releases. Targeted samples are where reviewers find new failure modes several times faster than random reading. They are deliberately unrepresentative, so they are never mixed into the quality estimate unless reweighted.

Worked: 50,000 conversations a week. Draw a 1.2% random sample, 600 conversations stratified across five intents, and grade them with the validated judge. That gives about ±2.9 points on the weekly headline. Send 120 of those to human reviewers for judge calibration. Draw 200 targeted conversations from negative signals for human triage. Total human load is about 320 reviews a week, roughly 8-11 hours at 30-40 an hour, which fits within one person's part-time role. Judge cost for 600 conversations with three criteria is a few dollars at the prices assumed in ev1. Running the judge on 100% of traffic would also be affordable at this volume. That is a reasonable choice, removing sampling error, but the human subset remains essential.

6. Alerting on quality drift with control charts

With a daily judged sample, you have a time series of quality. When should it alert? The obvious design is a threshold: alert if today's pass rate falls more than three standard errors below normal. That is a Shewhart chart, the basic control chart. It is good at catching big, sudden drops and poor at catching small, persistent ones, which are exactly what silent provider updates and slow input drift produce.

Worked: 500 judged conversations a day, normal pass rate 85%. The daily standard error is √(0.85 × 0.15 / 500) = 0.016, or 1.6 points. A 3-sigma limit sits 4.8 points below the mean, at 80.2%. On a normal day the chance of a false alarm is about 0.13%, roughly one every couple of years of daily checks. Now suppose quality drops by 2 points, to 83%. A single day's pass rate falls below 80.2% only occasionally, because the drop is small relative to the daily noise.

An EWMA chart (exponentially weighted moving average) accumulates evidence. Each day it updates zt = λxt + (1 − λ)zt−1, where xt is today's pass rate and λ, often 0.2, sets how quickly old days are forgotten. The EWMA is much less noisy than a single day. Its long-run standard deviation is σ√(λ/(2 − λ)) = 1.6 × √(0.2/1.8) = 1.6 × 0.333 ≈ 0.53 points. So its 3-sigma limit sits only 1.6 points below the mean, at 83.4%. A persistent 2-point drop pulls the EWMA steadily towards 83%, and it crosses the limit within days.

A simulation makes the comparison concrete. 300 runs, 500 judged items a day, 10 normal days at 85%, then a persistent drop:

Drop3-sigma daily threshold: days to alarm (mean / median / 90th pct)EWMA λ = 0.2, 3-sigma limit
85% → 83% (2 points)17.3 / 12 / 357.0 / 6 / 11
85% → 82% (3 points)6.3 / 4.5 / 144.0 / 4 / 6
85% → 80% (5 points)1.8 / 1 / 32.3 / 2 / 3

The pattern is the one statistical process control has taught since the 1920s. Single-observation limits are best for large shifts. Accumulating charts, EWMA and its cousin CUSUM (cumulative sum, which adds up deviations from target and alarms when the sum crosses a threshold), are best for small, persistent ones. Run both: a Shewhart limit for outages and an EWMA or CUSUM for drift. The false-alarm rates are comparable. In the simulation, the EWMA chart with no real change ran for several hundred days on average before a false alarm, similar to the 3-sigma threshold's roughly 740-day average. The average run length (ARL) is the standard way to compare charts: long when nothing has changed, short after a real shift.

Quality is not the only thing to chart. Leading indicators often move first: input drift (the intent mix, language mix, input length, ae3), grader health (the judge's verdicts on a fixed set of stored outputs with known verdicts, which should never change, so any change is judge drift), the serving model identifier returned by the API (a change means the provider changed something), guardrail and refusal rates, implicit feedback rates (ev8), latency and cost. Each alert needs an owner and a runbook: what to check first, how to tell system drift from judge drift from input drift, and when to roll back. Too many alerts without owners produce alert fatigue, and then the important one is ignored.

7. The dashboard a team actually uses

Most eval dashboards fail by showing too much. The one teams use answers the questions they actually ask, in roughly this order, on one page:

  1. Is quality where it should be? Headline quality per product surface from the random production sample, with intervals, a trend line and alert state.
  2. Did the last change hurt anything? Candidate against current release from the full CI tier: aggregate with interval, gate slices against floors, guardrails, and gate verdict.
  3. Can we trust the graders? Judge-human agreement, kappa and specificity on the latest calibration sample, and the drift check on the fixed set (ev4).
  4. What is failing? Top failure categories from this week's triage, with counts and the share that now have eval cases.
  5. What does it cost? Cost and latency per request, and eval spend.
  6. Where are the examples? Every number links to example traces, an owner and a runbook.

What's left off matters as much: raw counts without intervals, metrics that no one owns or acts on, and diagnostic charts that only matter during an investigation, which belong in drill-down views. A good test of any chart is to ask what decision someone would make if it moved. If nobody can say, remove it.

8. Closing the loop: triage, ownership and the weekly review

The loop closes through people. Flagged and sampled failures go into a triage queue. A reviewer reads each one, assigns a cause from a maintained failure taxonomy (retrieval miss, stale document, policy misread, tool error, tone, injection attempt…) and a severity (ev9), and decides the action: a bug, a prompt change, a data fix, or "acceptable". Every confirmed failure becomes an eval case with provenance, generalised into a few variants so the fix addresses the cause rather than one phrasing, and added to a regression or targeted suite (ev2). Never add it to the representative set.

A short weekly quality review keeps the system alive. Look at the dashboard, discuss alerts and their causes, review the top failure categories, check the coverage metric (the share of failure categories with at least one eval case), and assign owners. It is the evaluation equivalent of an operations review, and teams that skip it find their dashboards drifting into wallpaper. Clear ownership underpins it. Product owners own the rubrics and cases, engineers own harness, gates and alerts, and someone owns the weekly review (ev1).

Evaluating the evaluation system

The eval system itself can fail silently: gates that never block, judges that drift, alerts that never fire. Test it like any system. Gate sensitivity: seed known-bad candidates and confirm they're blocked, and seed known-equivalent ones (an A/A) and confirm they pass. Alert sensitivity: inject a synthetic drop into a replayed quality series, or temporarily route a small share of traffic to a deliberately degraded variant in a safe slice, and measure time to alert. Coverage: track the share of production failure categories covered by eval cases, and how many production incidents were caught by an eval or alert before a user reported them. That last ratio is the single best measure of whether the evaluation system works.

Determinism and replay in practice

Record/replay needs care. Recorded responses are valid only while the model's inputs are identical, so the replay key must include everything that reaches the model: the rendered prompt, retrieved context, tool results and parameters, hashed. A mismatch must fall back to a live call, never silently reuse a stale response. Agent traces are harder, because each step depends on earlier ones. Replaying a trajectory step by step works only if every tool result is also recorded, and any divergence ends the replay. Teams often keep a replay mode for fast CI and treat live runs as the source of truth for gates.

Infrastructure changes deserve evals too

ae7's case, Anthropic's 2025 postmortem, showed quality bugs coming from serving infrastructure rather than models or prompts: routing errors, a compiler issue, a sampling bug. Such changes don't touch prompts, so naive suite selection skips them. The defence is to treat infrastructure changes (serving stack, tokeniser, quantisation, batching, hardware) as triggers for the full live suite, to run numerical-equivalence checks on fixed inputs where possible, and to keep continuous probe evals on production so a bug that slips through is visible within days.

Cost management at scale

Eval cost grows with features, suites, trials and judge criteria. Levers include caching judge verdicts, replay for non-model changes, distilling a large judge into a cheaper fine-tuned one validated against the original and humans (ev4), sampling rather than grading everything in nightly suites, retiring stale cases to slower tiers, and budgeting eval spend per team with visibility on the dashboard. The goal isn't minimal spend. It is spend proportional to the cost of regressions the evals prevent.

Multi-tenant and per-customer evaluation

Enterprise products often serve customers with different documents, policies and expectations. A global quality number can hide one large customer's collapse. Per-tenant golden sets for the largest customers, per-tenant slices in production sampling, and per-tenant floors in gates catch this. Privacy constraints often mean per-tenant data can't leave that tenant's boundary, so the harness must run where the data lives.

When evals and incidents disagree

Sometimes users report problems that every eval and alert says aren't happening. Treat this as an eval failure until proven otherwise. The sample may not cover the affected slice, the judge may be blind to that failure, or the issue may be in a channel or locale the traces don't capture. Pull the reported cases, see why the evaluation system missed them, and fix that, as well as the product bug. Each such miss should make the evaluation system strictly better.

Evaluating agents in the loop

Agents stretch every part of this system. Traces are long and branching, so spans must capture each step's tool call, arguments and result, and sampling must consider whole trajectories. Graders check outcomes in sandboxed environments (ev1), which CI must be able to create and reset quickly. Costs per eval run are higher, so tiers matter more. And drift can appear as longer trajectories, more tool errors or more retries before quality visibly drops, so steps per task and tool-error rates belong on the control charts alongside success rate. The next track, on agentic systems, builds on this foundation.

Proportionality

Not every feature needs every piece of this lesson. A low-traffic internal tool may need tracing, a 50-case CI suite and a monthly look at sampled traces. A customer-facing assistant handling money needs all of it. The senior skill is matching the evaluation system's weight to the stakes and the rate of change, and growing it as the product grows, rather than building a platform no one uses or shipping a product no one can measure.

The public case: how GitHub evaluates models for Copilot (GitHub Blog, January 2025)

GitHub's engineering post "How we evaluate AI models and LLMs for GitHub Copilot" describes an evaluation system with the shape this lesson builds. The team runs more than 4,000 offline tests, most of them as part of an automated CI pipeline, before any change reaches production. For code changes, they keep around 100 containerised repositories whose CI tests pass. They deliberately modify the code so tests fail, then measure whether a candidate model can modify the codebase to make the tests pass again. That is a functional-correctness eval (ev3) built from real repositories, with outcomes graded by execution. For completions they track the percentage of unit tests passed, similarity to the original known-good code and token usage. For chat, question-answering accuracy and token efficiency. For both, latency and acceptance rates.

For chat, where responses are open-ended, they use an LLM judge, "a model with known good performance", and they routinely audit its outputs to make sure it stays aligned with human reviewers and consistent across requests. That is ev4's validated, monitored judge. Models are also tested for toxic content, prompt-injection resistance and relevance filtering for non-code queries (ev9). And before production, major changes go through live internal evaluations, where some GitHub employees are switched to the new model, a canary on internal users (ev8, ae7).

What makes the post useful as a case is the integration: thousands of automated tests in CI, outcome-graded tasks built from real code, an audited judge for the open-ended parts, safety suites, internal canaries, and online metrics such as acceptance rate. No single piece is exotic. Together they are an evaluation system that runs on every change, which is the difference between knowing a product works and hoping it does.

Failure modes in practice

The overridden gate. A gate blocked three releases in a month on a flaky slice, so the team added a "skip evals" label. Within a quarter it was used on a third of releases, including one that shipped a regression. Fix: measure A/A noise and fix the thresholds, quarantine flaky cases, and make overrides require a named approver and a written reason that appears in the release record.

The incomparable trend. The judge prompt was edited and the quality chart jumped 5 points. For two months nobody knew whether quality had changed. Fix: version graders, re-score a bridge set with old and new graders, and annotate the chart.

Alert fatigue. Thirty alerts across input drift, latency, cost and quality fired weekly with no owners. When a real quality drop came, its alert was ignored with the rest. Fix: fewer alerts, each with an owner and runbook, control charts tuned to known false-alarm rates, and a weekly review of alert outcomes.

Traces that can't be used. Prompts and outputs were logged as free text with personal data, so legal blocked any use of them for evaluation. Fix: structured spans, redaction at write time, and a documented retention and reuse policy agreed before launch.

A checklist for an evaluation system

Tiered CI suites with budgets and record/replay. Gates with must-pass cases, paired non-inferiority, slice floors and guardrails, tested with seeded regressions. Results keyed by manifest, eval-set, grader and harness versions, stored per item. Structured, redacted traces with standard attributes. Random and targeted production sampling with a human calibration subset. Control-chart alerts on quality, graders, inputs and dependencies, each with an owner. A one-page dashboard. A triage process that turns failures into eval cases, and a weekly quality review.

Goal: build a tiny eval harness with a stub model and a regression gate, then an EWMA drift detector on simulated production quality. Python 3.12, standard library only.

  1. In ev12_lab.py, write a stub model(version, case, rng) returning pass or fail with per-slice probabilities. For example, baseline "r2" passes refunds 0.92, legal 0.97, general 0.85. Candidate "r3" passes 0.89, 0.98, 0.88, and adds latency. Include 10 must-pass cases that r2 always passes and r3 fails with probability 0.05.
  2. Build 800 cases across slices, run each version with 3 trials (seeded), and store results keyed by (version, eval_set_version, grader_version, harness_version) in a dict.
  3. Implement gate(candidate, baseline) as in the body: must-pass (all trials), paired non-inferiority with a bootstrap CI and margin 0.015, slice floors with a Holm correction, and a latency guardrail. Print the verdict and reasons.
  4. Run an A/A comparison (r2 against r2 with different seeds) ten times and print how often the gate fails. Then seed a known-bad candidate (refunds at 0.80) and confirm it is blocked.
  5. Drift: simulate 60 days of production with 500 judged items a day at 85%, dropping to 83% from day 31. Plot as text, or print, the daily rate and an EWMA with λ = 0.2. Report the first day each of the daily 3-sigma rule and the EWMA rule alarms. Repeat over 200 seeds and print the mean delay of each.

What to look for. The gate should catch the must-pass failure in most r3 runs (with 10 cases × 3 trials at 5% failure each, P(all pass) = 0.9530 ≈ 21%), so about 79% of r3 runs are blocked on must-pass alone. That shows why must-pass behaviour needs pass^k thinking. The A/A gate should rarely fail. If it fails often, the margin is below the noise. In the drift simulation the EWMA should alarm about a week after the drop on average, and the daily threshold much later and more erratically, matching the body's table.

Design: evaluation operations for a new AI feature

You are the senior engineer on a new "AI answers" feature in a project-management product. It answers questions about a customer's own projects using retrieval over their tasks, documents and comments, with a few read-only tools. It launches to 10% of customers next month, about 30,000 questions a week, growing tenfold by year end. The team has an offline golden set of 400 questions, a validated judge for correctness and faithfulness, and nothing else. Design the evaluation system for launch and for the following six months.

Answer

Before launch (weeks 1-3). Instrument tracing with OpenTelemetry GenAI attribute names: request, retrieval (doc IDs, index version), model calls (model ID returned, prompt version, tokens, latency), tool calls, final answer, guardrail results, and linked feedback. Redact at write time, keep full content 14 days, keep metadata 13 months, and agree a reuse policy with legal. Build the harness. Version the 400-question set (v1) and the judge (v1). Store item-level results keyed by manifest, eval-set, grader and harness versions. Add CI tiers: a smoke suite (assertions plus 100 must-pass and representative cases, replayed for non-model changes) on every pull request, and the full suite (400 × 3 trials, live, judged) on prompt, model, retrieval and infrastructure changes. Add a gate: must-pass for tenant isolation (no cross-customer retrieval, which is an always-on assertion) and read-only tool use; paired non-inferiority on correctness and faithfulness with a 2-point margin set from A/A noise; floors on three gate slices; and latency and cost guardrails. Test the gate with seeded bad candidates.

Launch (10%). Sample about 600 questions a week at random, stratified by question type, and judge them for an estimate within roughly ±3 points. Send 120 a week to human reviewers to keep the judge calibrated (track kappa and specificity). Send 150 targeted questions a week from thumbs-down, low judge confidence and new clusters to human triage. Run control charts: daily judged quality with Shewhart and EWMA rules, a judge drift check on a fixed set, the returned model ID, input-cluster mix, refusal and guardrail rates. Each alert has an owner and runbook.

Months 2-6. Grow the golden set from triaged failures into regression and targeted suites, keeping the representative set refreshed monthly from random production samples. Add per-tenant slices for the largest customers. Raise sample sizes for per-slice precision as traffic grows, or judge 100% of traffic if cost allows. Add red-team and injection suites, especially for content in comments, which users other than the asker can write (ev9). Run online tests for major changes (ev8). Hold a one-page dashboard and a weekly quality review. Track coverage of failure categories by eval cases, and the share of incidents caught by evals or alerts before users reported them, as the measure of whether the system works.
