A team evaluates its document-question-answering assistant on 150 questions an engineer wrote in an afternoon. It scores 91%. In production, users complain constantly. When someone finally reads real traffic, the questions look nothing like the eval: half are about scanned PDFs with tables, a fifth are in German, many refer to "the contract I uploaded yesterday", and a steady trickle ask things no document could answer. The 91% was accurate. It was accurate about a product nobody used.

A second team does sample from real traffic, 400 conversations at random. Their legal team asks how the assistant handles questions about contract termination, which carry real liability. There are six such cases in the set. The assistant got five right. Is that 83%? With six cases, the honest interval runs from roughly 44% to 97%. The set cannot answer the one question that matters most to the business.

A third team built an excellent set a year ago. Since then the company changed its returns policy twice, launched a new product line and opened a market in Spain. Their expected answers now mark correct replies as wrong, they have no cases for the new products, and their engineers, who have tuned the prompt against those same 300 cases for a year, can recite them.

All three teams have a golden set, a set of cases with trusted expected outputs, and all three are measuring the wrong thing with it. The golden set is the foundation of everything in this track. Graders compare against it, LLM judges are calibrated on it, and release decisions are made on scores computed from it. Its flaws propagate into every number.

This lesson covers how to build one properly. You sample real traffic, keep representative and targeted sets apart, and stratify by slice and reweight. You size the set from the confidence interval you need, worked by hand for a 78% pass rate on 200 cases. You write labelling guidelines that make "correct" unambiguous, keep the set fresh and versioned, and protect it from contamination.

A golden set is like the reference weights in a standards laboratory. Every scale in the country is calibrated against weights that are themselves calibrated against a national standard. If the reference weight is off by a gram, every scale calibrated against it is off by a gram, and no amount of careful weighing downstream fixes it. If the reference weights are all one kilogram, nobody's scale is checked at five grams or fifty kilos. A golden set plays the same role for an AI system. It must be accurate (expected answers are right), representative (it covers the inputs that actually occur, in the right proportions) and precise enough (big enough that its scores can distinguish the differences you care about).

The analogy breaks in one important place. A reference weight doesn't change, but your users do. Traffic drifts, policies change, products launch. A golden set therefore has to be maintained, more like a living test suite than a museum piece.

The pipeline from traffic to a golden set

Read it left to right. You start from real traffic, because real inputs are the only honest source. You clean it: remove duplicates (the same question pasted fifty times would dominate the set) and personal data (ae5). You group it into slices, subsets sharing a property such as intent, language or customer tier, so you can see what is there and report results per slice. You sample in two ways: at random, so the set mirrors traffic, and by stratum, so rare slices get enough cases. People label each case following written guidelines, and some cases get two labellers to check the guidelines are clear. The result is stored as a versioned set with a record of where every case came from, and part of it is held out from day-to-day use.

It helps to see how each of the three teams in the opening story broke one property. The first team's set was accurate but not representative: engineer-written questions instead of real traffic. The second team's set was representative but not precise enough where it mattered: six legal cases cannot pin down a rate. The third team's set was once all three, and then decayed: expected answers went stale, traffic moved on, and tuning against the same cases turned the set into a training set. Each step in the pipeline above exists to defend one of these properties. Sampling from traffic defends representativeness. Stratifying and sizing defend precision. Guidelines, double-labelling, versioning and refreshes defend accuracy over time.

The rest of the lesson takes each step in turn and adds the arithmetic. The central formula appeared in lm14: a pass rate p measured on n cases has a standard error of √(p(1 − p)/n). That formula decides how big the set must be overall and, more importantly, how big each slice must be.

1. What goes into a case

A golden-set case has more parts than an input and an answer. A useful record looks like this:

{
  "id": "ret-0142",
  "input": {"message": "Can I return the blue jacket? Bought 2 March.",
            "context": {"order_id": "<masked>", "final_sale": false, "delivered": "2026-03-04"}},
  "expected": {"action": "answer",
               "must_include": ["return is possible", "deadline 3 April"],
               "must_not_include": ["restocking fee"]},
  "slice": {"intent": "returns", "language": "en", "tier": "standard"},
  "provenance": {"source": "prod-sample", "sampled": "2026-05-12", "method": "stratified",
                 "labelled_by": ["r07", "r12"], "guideline_version": "3.2"},
  "set_version": 7
}

Each field earns its place. The input includes all the context the system will see (account state, retrieved documents if they are fixed for the test, conversation history), because the same message with different context has a different right answer. The expected block describes correctness in a form graders can use. The slice tags allow per-slice reporting. Provenance, the record of where the case came from, lets you answer later questions: was this sampled at random or chosen because it failed? Which guideline version was it labelled under? Who labelled it?

Note that expected is not one reference reply. For open-ended outputs, a single reference text penalises correct answers that are worded differently. ev3 shows how badly word-overlap metrics behave for exactly this reason. Required facts, prohibited claims, an expected action and, where needed, a rubric are more robust.

2. Sampling real traffic

Real traffic is the best source because users do things nobody predicts. They paste whole emails, ask three questions in one message, switch languages mid-sentence, refer to things said ten turns ago, and ask questions the product was never meant to answer. Cases written by engineers are cleaner, shorter and more answerable than real ones, so they overestimate quality.

Before any traffic exists, use the closest substitute. That might be dogfooding (staff using the product), a beta, logs of the human-run process the AI will assist (support tickets answered by people), or domain experts writing questions in the voice of real users. Replace these with real traffic as soon as you have it.

Cleaning comes first. De-duplicate exact and near-exact repeats: if a broken integration sent "test" 4,000 times, random sampling would fill the set with it. (ev11 covers near-duplicate detection with MinHash; for now, normalising whitespace and case and hashing catches most repeats.) Scrub personal data: names, emails, phone numbers and account numbers are masked or replaced with realistic fakes, and the expected answers must be written against the masked version. Filter out traffic that is not in scope: health checks, internal testing, obvious spam.

Then slice. Some slice properties are metadata you already have: language, customer tier, platform, input length. Others need inference. Intent is usually found by embedding the messages and clustering them (ae3), then having a person name the clusters ("returns", "delivery status", "account access", "complaint", "out of scope"). This step is valuable on its own. The cluster sizes tell you what the product is actually used for, which is often not what the product team believed.

3. Representative sets and targeted sets

There are two legitimate reasons to include a case, and they must not be mixed.

A representative set is sampled at random from cleaned traffic. Its pass rate is an unbiased estimate of the pass rate users experience. This is the headline number: "the assistant handles 78% of conversations correctly". It is only meaningful if the sampling was random. The moment you add cases because they are interesting, the number stops estimating anything about users.

A targeted set is built on purpose to probe specific behaviour. Examples include every production failure turned into a regression case, adversarial prompts (ev9), rare but costly slices, and boundary cases experts write (a return requested on day 30 exactly). Its pass rate is a measure of how the system handles those situations. It says nothing about what fraction of users are served well, because the set is deliberately enriched with hard cases.

Here is how mixing them misleads. Suppose the representative set of 200 cases scores 78%. You add 100 failure-mined cases on which the system scores 40%. The combined score is (156 + 40)/300 = 65.3%. Next month, a change raises the representative score to 80% while the targeted score rises to 55%. The combined score is (160 + 55)/300 = 71.7%. A 6.4-point jump on the dashboard reflects mostly progress on hard cases, and only 2 points of change in what users experience. Report them as separate suites, each with its own interval.

4. Stratifying and reweighting

Random sampling has a weakness: rare slices get few cases. If a slice is 5% of traffic, a random sample of 200 contains about 10 cases from it, and an interval on 10 cases is so wide it is useless. The fix is stratified sampling: decide how many cases you want from each slice, and sample that many from within each.

Stratified sampling with equal or boosted counts per slice changes the composition of the set, so the raw pass rate no longer matches the user experience. You fix that by reweighting: compute each slice's pass rate, then combine them weighted by each slice's true share of traffic. Worked example with three slices:

SliceTraffic shareCases sampledPassedSlice pass rate
Order status70%806885.0%
Returns25%604575.0%
Legal complaints5%603355.0%
Total100%20014673.0% unweighted

The unweighted pass rate is 146/200 = 73.0%, but that over-represents the hard legal slice, which has 30% of the cases and only 5% of the traffic. The traffic-weighted estimate is 0.70 × 0.85 + 0.25 × 0.75 + 0.05 × 0.55 = 0.595 + 0.1875 + 0.0275 = 81.0%. That is the number that estimates what users experience. The 55% on legal complaints is now measured on 60 cases instead of the 10 random sampling would have given, so it is precise enough to act on.

Which slices deserve oversampling? The ones where errors are costly (legal, medical, financial, safety), the ones where the system is weak (low pass rate means more room for movement and more to learn), and the ones that matter strategically (a new market, a new enterprise customer). Slices where the system is reliably near 100% and errors are cheap can get fewer cases.

5. How precise is the set? The 78%-on-200 example

Every score computed from a golden set is an estimate. lm14 introduced the standard error of a proportion. Here is the full calculation for a pass rate of 78% on 200 representative cases, because this is the number you will be asked to defend.

The standard error (SE) is the typical amount the estimate would vary if you drew a different random 200 cases:

SE = √(p(1 − p)/n) = √(0.78 × 0.22 / 200) = √(0.1716 / 200) = √0.000858 = 0.0293, or 2.93 percentage points.

A 95% confidence interval is a range built so that, over many repetitions of the sampling, 95% of such ranges would contain the true pass rate. With the normal approximation it is p ± 1.96 × SE:

1.96 × 2.93 = 5.74 points, so the interval is 72.3% to 83.7%. The half-width, ±5.74 points, is the margin of error.

That simple interval is called the Wald interval, and it has known problems. It assumes the estimate's sampling distribution is symmetric and normal, which is roughly true for moderate p and large n and false otherwise. At 199 passes out of 200, Wald gives 99.5% ± 1.0%, an upper bound above 100%. The Wilson interval fixes this. Its formula looks heavier but is a few lines of code:

import math
def wilson(passes, n, z=1.96):
    p = passes / n
    denom = 1 + z*z/n
    centre = (p + z*z/(2*n)) / denom
    half = z * math.sqrt(p*(1-p)/n + z*z/(4*n*n)) / denom
    return centre - half, centre + half

wilson(156, 200)   # (0.7176, 0.8318)

For 156 of 200, Wilson gives 71.8% to 83.2%, slightly shifted toward 50% compared with Wald. Brown, Cai and DasGupta's 2001 study of binomial intervals showed that the Wald interval's actual coverage can fall well below its nominal 95%, erratically, even at sample sizes people consider large, and recommended Wilson-type intervals instead. Use Wilson by default for pass rates.

What does a ±5.7-point interval mean in practice? If a competitor or a new model version scores 81% on the same set, you cannot say, from the two intervals alone, that either is better. (A paired comparison on the same items is more sensitive than comparing two intervals, as lm14 showed and ev7 develops. Still, the set-level interval tells you the resolution of your instrument.)

A special case comes up constantly with safety and regression suites: zero failures. If 0 of 200 cases fail, the pass rate is 100% and the Wald interval has zero width, which is absurd. The rule of three gives an approximate 95% upper bound on the failure rate of 3/n = 3/200 = 1.5%. To claim a failure rate below 0.1% you would need about 3,000 clean cases. "We saw no failures" is a much weaker statement than it sounds.

6. Sizing the set from the precision you need

Turn the formula around. If you need a margin of error E at 95% confidence, you need

n ≈ 1.96² × p(1 − p) / E²

At p = 0.78:

Margin of error ECases needed (p = 0.78)Cases needed (worst case p = 0.5)
±5 points264385
±3 points7321,068
±2 points1,6482,401
±1 point6,5929,604

Two patterns matter. Precision costs quadratically: halving the margin quadruples the cases. And p(1 − p) is largest at 0.5, so if you don't know the pass rate in advance, plan with 0.5 and you will not be under-sized.

The bigger trap is slices. Precision is governed by the number of cases in the slice, not in the set. Split the 200 representative cases by intent:

Order status has 120 cases (98 pass, 81.7%, ±6.9 points). Returns has 50 (37 pass, 74%, ±12.2). Legal has 30 (21 pass, 70%, ±16.4). The headline looks respectable at ±5.7, but the legal slice could plausibly be anywhere from 54% to 86%. If someone asks "how good are we on legal questions?", the honest answer is "we don't know yet".

So the sizing question is really: which slices need which precision? If you need ±5 points on each of five important slices, you need around 264 cases in each, about 1,300 in total, plus the representative sample. That is a lot of labelling, which is why the cheapest precision gains come from elsewhere. Paired comparisons need far fewer cases to detect a difference between two versions than independent estimates need to pin down each one (ev7). Several trials per case remove the noise from sampling randomness, though not the noise from which cases happened to be chosen.

One more caution: the formula assumes cases are independent. If the set contains ten paraphrases of the same question, or five turns from the same conversation, those cases rise and fall together, and the effective sample size is smaller than the count. Either keep one case per underlying situation in the representative set, or compute intervals that account for the clusters (ev7 bootstraps whole clusters for this reason).

7. Labelling guidelines

The expected outputs are where the "golden" in golden set comes from, and they are only as good as the instructions labellers follow. Labelling guidelines are a document that tells a labeller, for each type of case, what a correct output is. Good guidelines contain:

  • The form of correctness for each case type. For a factual question, the exact answer and acceptable variants ("3 April", "April 3rd", "within 30 days of delivery, so by 3 April"). For an explanatory answer, the facts it must contain and claims it must not make. For a classification, the label set with definitions. For open-ended quality, a rubric with levels and an example of each.
  • The authoritative sources. Which policy document version, which knowledge base. Labellers must not answer from memory.
  • What the system should do when it can't succeed. When the question is ambiguous, out of scope or unanswerable from available information, the expected behaviour is usually to ask a clarifying question, decline, or escalate. These cases must be labelled as such, or the eval will reward confident guessing (lm15 showed how binary grading rewards guessing).
  • Worked borderline examples with the decision and the reasoning, because the borderline is where labellers diverge.

Test the guidelines before trusting the labels. Have two people independently label the same 50 items and compare. Where they disagree, either the guideline is ambiguous (fix the wording, add an example) or the case is genuinely ambiguous (either drop it or make the expected behaviour "ask for clarification"). ev5 covers how to measure this agreement properly with chance-corrected statistics. The rule of thumb from ev1 applies here too: a good case is one where two domain experts would independently reach the same verdict.

Who labels matters. Correctness in specialised domains (law, medicine, tax, a company's internal policies) needs people who know the domain. Crowd workers can judge fluency and obvious errors but will miss subtle domain mistakes, and the eval will then pass outputs that experts would fail.

8. Versioning and provenance

A golden set changes over time, so every score must say which version it was computed on. Treat the set like code. Store it in version control or a dataset registry, give each release a version number, and record in every eval report the set version alongside the system version (the release manifest from ae7).

The golden rule of comparison follows: compare system versions on the same set version. Suppose this month's report shows 85% against last month's 82%, but 30% of the set was refreshed in between. You have learned nothing about the system from those two numbers. The new cases might simply be easier. To separate the effects, run both system versions on the new set. Better still, change the set and the system in separate steps. Many teams also keep a small anchor subset that never changes, so that long-term trends remain comparable across set versions.

Provenance per case pays off when something looks wrong. If a slice's score drops, you can check whether its cases were recently added, labelled under a new guideline version, or labelled by one particular person whose labels disagree with others.

9. Keeping it fresh

Golden sets decay in three ways. Each needs its own countermeasure.

The product changes. A policy update makes expected answers wrong. The system, correctly following the new policy, now "fails" those cases. If nobody updates the set, engineers learn to ignore those failures, and then they ignore real ones too. The countermeasure is to link cases to the policy or document they depend on, so that a policy change triggers review of the dependent cases.

The traffic changes. New products, new markets and new user behaviours appear. The set no longer represents users and has no cases for what is new. The countermeasure is a scheduled refresh: every month, say, sample a fresh representative batch from recent traffic. Compare its intent mix with the set's. Add cases for new intents and retire cases for intents that have disappeared.

The team overfits to it. After months of tuning prompts against the same cases, the team has, in effect, trained on the test set. The score rises faster than real quality. The countermeasure is a held-out set: a portion, perhaps 20%, that is not used for day-to-day iteration and is consulted only for release decisions. If dev-set scores climb and held-out scores do not, you are overfitting. Rotating fresh cases into both portions slows the process.

10. Contamination

Contamination means eval items, or close variants of them, are present in data the system has seen: its training data, its prompt or its retrieval corpus. A contaminated system can recall answers instead of producing them, and the eval measures memory. lm14 and rs8 covered contamination of public benchmarks (GSM1k showed accuracy drops of up to 8% on fresh problems for some models). Here the concern is your own set, which leaks in more mundane ways:

  • Few-shot examples. Someone improving the prompt copies three eval cases, with their ideal answers, into the prompt as examples. Those three now pass trivially, and similar cases get easier.
  • Retrieval corpora. A support agent writes a help-centre article or a canned reply based on an eval case, and it gets indexed. The system retrieves the answer.
  • Fine-tuning data. The eval set, or production logs that include eval inputs, ends up in a fine-tuning dataset (ev11 covers decontaminating training data).
  • Third parties. Eval data sent to a model provider or tool vendor whose terms allow them to use it for training. Check the data-use terms of every service the eval data passes through.

Detection works by searching for overlap. Compare eval inputs and expected answers against prompts, retrieval documents and training sets, using exact n-gram overlap (shared runs of n consecutive words; the GPT-3 paper used 13-word runs for its benchmark decontamination) or embedding similarity for paraphrases. Embed a canary string in eval files. This is a unique random identifier that should never appear anywhere else, so finding it in a training corpus or a model's output proves leakage. The BIG-bench benchmark files carry such a canary. Be suspicious of any score that jumps without an obvious cause. The definitive check is to write fresh cases of the same kind and difficulty. If the system scores much lower on them than on the old ones, the old ones are compromised.

Prevention is mostly organisational. Store the set with access controls, separately from training data. Automatically de-duplicate prompts, retrieval corpora and training sets against it as part of their build pipelines. Treat any eval item found in a prompt as a bug.

Label noise biases scores, and sample size does not fix it

Suppose 5% of the golden set's expected answers are wrong. A perfect system will "fail" those 5%, so its score is capped at 95%. A system that happens to make the same mistakes as the labellers gets credit for them. Worse, label errors are rarely random. They cluster in the hardest, most ambiguous cases, which are exactly where systems differ. Increasing n shrinks the interval around a biased number. It does not move the number toward the truth. Audit labels by re-labelling a random sample with a second expert, and by reviewing every case that all system versions fail: a case nothing passes is often a labelling error.

Stratified estimates are more precise when strata differ

Stratification is not only about guaranteeing rare-slice coverage. When pass rates differ substantially between strata, a stratified estimate of the overall rate has lower variance than a simple random sample of the same size. That is because the variation between strata is removed from the error. The variance of the reweighted estimate is Σ wh² ph(1 − ph)/nh over strata h with traffic weights wh. Allocating cases in proportion to wh√(ph(1 − ph)) minimises it, a rule from survey sampling called Neyman allocation. In practice teams bend this rule toward costly slices, but knowing it lets you say why oversampling a tiny slice too hard makes the headline estimate noisier. Each case in a 5% slice gets a weight proportional to 0.05/nh, and heavily oversampled strata contribute little information to the overall rate.

Difficulty and discrimination

Not every case is equally informative. A case every system passes, or every system fails, cannot distinguish versions. Item response theory, the statistics behind standardised tests, models each item's difficulty and its discrimination (how sharply it separates strong from weak test-takers), and some benchmark builders use it to prune uninformative items. For a product golden set the practical version is simpler. Track each case's pass rate across recent system versions, and periodically review cases stuck at 0% (often label bugs or impossible tasks) or at 100% for months (fine for regression, wasted in a capability suite).

Time-based splits catch what random splits miss

If the held-out set is drawn at random from the same period as the dev set, it shares that period's topics and phrasing, so overfitting to the period goes undetected. A held-out set drawn from later traffic, a time-based split, tests whether improvements generalise to the future, which is the only thing a production system cares about. The same idea underlies contamination-resistant benchmarks that only use problems published after a model's training cutoff.

Coverage versus representativeness is a real trade-off

Product teams want coverage: a case for every feature and edge case. Statisticians want representativeness: a sample that mirrors traffic. A set built purely for coverage over-weights rare situations and misstates the user experience. A set built purely for representativeness under-tests rare, costly situations. The standard resolution is the one in this lesson: separate suites, each honest about what its number means, and reweighting when a single headline is required. Interviewers probe this because candidates who have only ever built one big mixed set cannot explain what their pass rate estimates.

Privacy and realism pull in opposite directions

The most realistic cases are verbatim user messages with their full context, and that context is often personal: names, addresses, health details, account histories. Masking can change the task. Replace a customer's name with "[NAME]" and the model may behave differently than it would with a real name, and a masked date breaks any case whose correct answer depends on that date. Good practice replaces personal values with realistic synthetic ones of the same type (a fake but plausible name, a shifted but consistent date) and writes the expected answer against the replaced version. Retention rules matter too: if your privacy policy says logs are deleted after 30 days, a golden set built from logs needs a documented legal basis to keep those cases longer, or it must be built only from scrubbed, consented or synthetic data.

The expected answer can be the wrong unit

For agents and multi-turn systems, a golden "answer" may not exist. The right unit is an end state (the booking exists with these properties) or a set of constraints on the trajectory (never refunds without confirmation). Golden sets for such systems store environment snapshots and outcome checks rather than texts. They also need careful reset logic, because a case that leaves state behind contaminates the next case.

The public case: SWE-bench Verified (OpenAI, August 2024)

SWE-bench is a benchmark built from real GitHub issues in popular Python repositories. A model is given a repository and an issue and must produce a code change; the change is graded by running unit tests that the real fix made pass. By mid-2024 it was the standard measure of coding agents, and it had exactly the golden-set problems this lesson describes. Some issue descriptions did not contain enough information to know what fix was wanted. Some tests checked details of the original fix (a specific error message, a function name) that a different, valid fix would not match. Those cases failed correct solutions, so scores understated what models could do.

OpenAI's response was a labelling operation. 93 software developers experienced in Python screened 1,699 randomly sampled cases from the test set, each case annotated independently by three developers. They followed guidelines asking whether the problem statement was well specified, whether the tests would unfairly reject valid solutions, and other issues, each rated on a severity scale from 0 to 3, with 2 and 3 counted as severe. 38.3% of cases were flagged for underspecified problem statements and 61.1% for unit tests that could unfairly reject valid solutions. In total 68.3% were filtered out. The remaining 500 cases became SWE-bench Verified. GPT-4o's best score went from 16% on the original set to 33.2% on the verified one.

Three lessons carry straight to product golden sets. First, label errors bias scores, here by half, and no amount of extra cases would have fixed that; only re-labelling did. Second, the work was done with written guidelines, a severity scale and three independent labellers per case, which is how you make labels trustworthy (ev5). Third, a verified set eventually saturates. Within about a year frontier systems were scoring above 80% on it, and harder sets were needed. Golden sets are maintained, not finished.

Failure modes in practice

The engineer-written set. Cases written by the people building the system are clean, well-formed and answerable, and they reflect what the builders think users do. Symptom: excellent offline scores and poor user feedback. Fix: sample real traffic, and compare the intent mix of the set against production clusters.

The set that silently became targeted. Every production failure was added to the main set "so we don't regress". After a year, half the set is failure-mined, and the headline pass rate is meaningless as an estimate of user experience. Fix: split the suites retroactively using provenance. If there is no provenance, start recording it now.

Stale expected answers. After a policy change, 12% of cases expect the old behaviour. The team "fixes" the regressions by reverting the system to the old policy, or learns to ignore red cases. Fix: link cases to the documents they depend on, and review them when those documents change.

Personal data in the eval set. Logged conversations copied into the set with names, emails and order numbers intact, then shared with vendors and contractors. Fix: scrub at ingestion, and audit the set periodically with a personal-data detector (ae5).

What to track about the set itself

A golden set is an asset, and its health is worth a dashboard section of its own. Track the number of cases per slice and the interval width that gives; the age distribution of cases and the date of the last refresh; the intent mix of the set against the last 30 days of traffic; the share of cases that every recent system version fails (probable label bugs); the double-labelled sample's agreement rate; and the dev-versus-held-out gap.

Design: a golden set for a contract-review assistant

A legal-tech company's assistant answers questions about uploaded contracts ("What's the termination notice period?", "Is there an auto-renewal clause?"). It handles about 8,000 questions a week. 60% are about dates and notice periods, 25% about obligations and liability, 10% are summaries, and 5% are questions the contract can't answer. Lawyers' time costs $150 an hour and they can label about 20 cases an hour. The head of legal wants to know, with a margin of ±5 points, how accurate the assistant is on liability questions specifically, and the CEO wants a single headline number. You have a budget of $15,000 for labelling. Design the set.

Answer

Budget to cases. $15,000 / $150 = 100 lawyer-hours × 20 = 2,000 labels. Reserve about 20% for double-labelling (checking guidelines and measuring agreement) and for refreshes, which leaves about 1,600 single-labelled cases to build the set.

Liability slice precision. If the pass rate is unknown, plan at p = 0.5: n = 1.96² × 0.25 / 0.05² ≈ 385. If a pilot suggests around 80%, n ≈ 246. Allocate about 300 liability cases. That gives ±5 points even at a pass rate somewhat below 80%.

Representative set for the headline. Draw about 400 questions at random from production, de-duplicated and with client names masked. This gives ±4-5 points on the headline and naturally includes about 20 unanswerable questions.

Other strata. Fill the rest (about 900) with stratified samples. About 300 for dates and notice periods (high volume, and errors here are costly). About 200 summaries, graded with a rubric of required clauses and prohibited invented ones. About 150 unanswerable questions, where the expected behaviour is "the contract does not say", to measure false confident answers (lm15). And a targeted suite of about 250 for known failures, such as scanned PDFs with tables and amended contracts. Keep that suite separate from the headline.

Headline. Compute it from the random 400 alone, or combine all strata reweighted by the 60/25/10/5 traffic shares. Report it with a Wilson interval. Report every slice with its own interval.

Guidelines and quality. Expected answers are written as the exact value plus the clause reference, and lawyers must cite the clause. Have two lawyers double-label 150 cases. Disagreements go to adjudication, and the guidelines are revised before full labelling begins.

Maintenance. Hold out 20%. Version the set. Refresh 10% quarterly from new traffic. Keep contracts used in the set out of any fine-tuning data and out of the few-shot examples.
