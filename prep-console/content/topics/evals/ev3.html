A team building a meeting-summary feature needs a number to track. They pick ROUGE, the standard summarisation metric, which measures how many words of a reference summary appear in the generated one. They write reference summaries for 300 meetings and start iterating. Over two months ROUGE climbs from 0.38 to 0.46, and they ship the latest model with a celebratory post. Within a week, users complain that summaries attribute decisions to the wrong people and include action items nobody agreed to. When someone reads the outputs, the pattern is obvious. The new model learned to write in the exact style of the reference summaries ("The team agreed that... Action item: ... will ...") and to fill that template with plausible details. ROUGE rewarded the style. It could not see the errors.

A second team built a coding assistant and measured it by comparing generated code to reference solutions with BLEU, a word-overlap metric from machine translation. The code that scored highest was code that looked like the reference, and a fair amount of it didn't run. When OpenAI introduced its Codex code models in 2021, the paper made this explicit: functionally wrong programs often had higher BLEU scores than correct ones. That is why they evaluated by running unit tests instead.

Both teams made the same mistake. They chose a metric because it was standard and easy to compute, not because it measured what made an output good. Every eval needs a number. Choosing which number to compute, for which kind of output, and knowing exactly how it can lie, is the subject of this lesson.

It goes from the most closed outputs to the most open. It starts with exact match and the normalisation that makes it work, then token F1 for partial credit, and field-level scores for structured output. Then BLEU and ROUGE, computed by hand so their failures are visible, and embedding similarity and BERTScore. Then task-specific checks such as execution, which are the best automated metrics when they exist, and the pass@k estimator for sampled outputs. Last, calibration: what it means for a score to mean what it says. Judgement-based grading by LLMs gets its own topic next (ev4).

Think of choosing a metric like choosing how to mark an exam. A multiple-choice question is marked by comparing the letter to the key: fast, objective, no judgement needed. A short-answer question ("name the capital of Australia") is marked against a list of acceptable answers, with some tolerance for spelling. A maths problem might get partial credit for method. An essay needs a marker with a rubric. Nobody would mark an essay by counting how many words it shares with a model essay. Yet that is what BLEU and ROUGE do when applied to open-ended LLM output.

The analogy breaks in one place, and it helps. With an exam, the question type is given. With an LLM feature, you design the output, and you can often turn an essay question into several short-answer ones. If the support assistant returns a JSON object with an action field and a refund_days field, and a prose reply, then the action and the number can be marked like multiple choice. Only the prose needs a rubric. Designing outputs so that more of them can be checked mechanically is the single most effective thing you can do for evaluation.

The spectrum of metrics

On the closed end, the output is a label, a number, an entity or an action, and exact match after normalisation is ideal. A little further along are text spans, such as an extracted quote or a name with a title, where token F1 gives partial credit. Structured outputs are graded field by field. Executable outputs, code and SQL, are graded by running them, which is the gold standard of automated evaluation because it checks behaviour rather than form. Then come translations and summaries, where the traditional metrics, BLEU and ROUGE, compare words against a reference, and embedding-based metrics compare meanings. These are cheap but weak, for reasons we will compute. On the open end, chat replies and explanations need a rubric applied by a human or an LLM judge.

Before trusting any metric, ask it three questions. What does it reward? ROUGE rewards sharing words with the reference, which is not the same as being right. How can an output score well while being bad, or badly while being good? Every metric in this lesson has a concrete answer, and the body works one example for each. Has anyone checked it against human judgement on this task? A metric validated for news translation in 2002 has not been validated for your insurance chatbot. Metrics that survive all three questions for your outputs are the ones to gate on. The others are diagnostics at best.

Two ideas recur throughout. First, a metric is a model of what good means, and every metric has a characteristic way of being fooled. Know it before you trust the number. Second, the number has to support a decision. A metric that moves when quality moves, and stays still when it doesn't, is valid. One that moves with style, length or wording is measuring something else.

1. Exact match and the normalisation that makes it work

Exact match (EM) scores 1 if the prediction equals an acceptable answer and 0 otherwise. Stated that baldly, it sounds uselessly strict: "Canberra." with a full stop would fail against "Canberra". So EM is always computed after normalisation, transforming both sides to a canonical form. The conventions popularised by the SQuAD reading-comprehension benchmark are a good base: lower-case everything, remove punctuation, remove the articles "a", "an" and "the", and collapse whitespace. For LLM features you usually add domain rules: numbers to a standard format ("1,000", "1000" and "one thousand" all become 1000), dates to ISO format (2026-04-03), units converted, known aliases mapped ("USA", "United States", "U.S." become one value).

import re, string
def normalise(s: str) -> str:
    s = s.lower()
    s = "".join(ch for ch in s if ch not in string.punctuation)
    s = re.sub(r"\b(a|an|the)\b", " ", s)
    return " ".join(s.split())

def exact_match(pred: str, golds: list[str]) -> int:
    return int(any(normalise(pred) == normalise(g) for g in golds))

Notice golds is a list. Many questions have several acceptable answers, and the golden set (ev2) should record them all.

With LLMs there is a step before normalisation: answer extraction. Models rarely reply with just the answer. They say "Based on the policy, you have 30 days, so the deadline is 3 April." To compare with the gold "3 April", you have to find the answer in the text. The options, from most to least robust, are these. You can ask for structured output with an answer field. You can ask the model to end with a fixed marker ("Final answer: ...") and parse after it. Or you can write a regex for the expected type, such as the last date in the text. Each fallback adds a way for a correct answer to score 0. Extraction failures look exactly like model errors in the aggregate. Teams have spent weeks "improving the model" when the real problem was that a new model version wrote "Final Answer:" with a capital A. The defence is to log extraction failures separately and to read a sample of zeros before acting on them.

2. Token F1: partial credit for spans

Sometimes a prediction is partly right. The gold answer is "Eiffel Tower" and the model says "the Eiffel Tower in Paris". EM gives 0, which is harsh. Token F1 treats the prediction and gold as bags of tokens and computes precision and recall over them (ml7), after normalisation.

Worked: normalised, the prediction is [eiffel, tower, in, paris] (the article is removed) and the gold is [eiffel, tower]. The overlap is 2 tokens.

  • Precision = overlap / prediction tokens = 2/4 = 0.50. Half of what the model said is in the gold.
  • Recall = overlap / gold tokens = 2/2 = 1.00. All of the gold is in what the model said.
  • F1 = 2PR/(P + R) = 2 × 0.5 × 1.0 / 1.5 = 0.67.

With multiple gold answers, take the maximum F1 over them. Token F1 is well suited to extractive tasks, where the answer is a span of a document. It is poorly suited to anything where word order or a single word changes the meaning. "Not eligible" and "eligible" have F1 0.67, and "5 to 7 days" and "7 to 5 days" have F1 1.0.

Long, chatty LLM answers also distort F1. A correct answer buried in a three-sentence explanation has terrible precision. So for LLM outputs, extract the answer first and compute F1 on the extracted span, or ask for a short answer field.

3. Structured output: schema validity and field-level scores

When the output is structured, such as JSON from an extraction model, evaluation splits into two layers that should be reported separately.

Schema validity asks whether the output parses and conforms to the schema: required fields present, types right, enums in range. This is a pass/fail assertion and its rate is a metric in itself. A model that produces invalid JSON 3% of the time has a 3% hard-failure rate in production, whatever its field accuracy.

Field-level scores compare each field with its gold value, after field-appropriate normalisation: dates to ISO, amounts to decimals with two places, names trimmed and case-folded. Worked, for one invoice:

FieldGoldPredictedOutcome

Counting a correct field as a true positive (TP), a predicted value that is wrong or invented as a false positive (FP), and a gold value not correctly predicted as a false negative (FN): TP = 3, FP = 2 (vendor, po_number), FN = 2 (vendor, tax). Precision = 3/5 = 0.60, recall = 3/5 = 0.60, F1 = 0.60. Over many invoices you sum the counts (micro-average) or average per field (macro-average). The macro view matters more, because the average hides per-field problems. A system can score 0.95 overall while getting the total amount right only 80% of the time, and the total is the field that costs money.

The invented PO number deserves its own counter. Hallucinated fields (lm15) are usually worse than missing ones, because downstream systems trust them.

4. BLEU, computed by hand

BLEU (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy, Papineni et al., 2002) was designed to rank machine-translation systems cheaply. It compares a candidate translation with one or more reference translations by counting shared n-grams, runs of n consecutive words.

The recipe, for n = 1 to 4: count the candidate's n-grams that appear in the reference, clipping each n-gram's count at the number of times it appears in the reference (so "the the the the" can't score by repeating a common word). Divide by the candidate's total number of n-grams. That gives four modified precisions, p1 to p4. BLEU is their geometric mean multiplied by a brevity penalty (BP), which punishes candidates shorter than the reference: BP = 1 if the candidate is longer than the reference, otherwise exp(1 − r/c), with r the reference length and c the candidate length.

BLEU = BP × exp((ln p1 + ln p2 + ln p3 + ln p4)/4)

Worked, reference "the cat is on the mat", candidate "the cat sat on the mat":

nCandidate n-gramsMatches (clipped)pn
1the, cat, sat, on, the, matthe ×2, cat, on, mat = 55/6
2the cat, cat sat, sat on, on the, the matthe cat, on the, the mat = 33/5
3the cat sat, cat sat on, sat on the, on the maton the mat = 11/4
4the cat sat on, cat sat on the, sat on the matnone0/3

Both sentences have 6 words, so BP = 1. But p4 = 0 and ln 0 is minus infinity, so BLEU = 0 for a sentence that differs from the reference by one word. That is why BLEU was defined at the corpus level, pooling n-gram counts across a whole test set before dividing, and why sentence-level BLEU needs smoothing. With a simple add-one smoothing on each precision, the candidate scores about 0.49.

Now the failure. Score two more candidates against the same reference, with the same smoothing:

  • "a cat was sitting on the rug", a reasonable paraphrase, though "rug" isn't quite "mat": about 0.26.
  • "the cat is not on the mat", the opposite meaning: about 0.50.

The candidate that contradicts the reference scores highest, because it contains the reference word for word plus one extra word. BLEU measures surface similarity to one particular wording. It knows nothing about meaning.

5. ROUGE, and the refund example

ROUGE (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation, Lin, 2004) was designed for summarisation and is recall-oriented: what fraction of the reference's content does the candidate cover? ROUGE-N is n-gram recall: matching n-grams divided by the reference's n-grams. ROUGE-L uses the longest common subsequence (LCS), the longest sequence of words that appears in both texts in the same order, not necessarily adjacent. From it, precision = LCS/candidate length, recall = LCS/reference length, and an F-score combines them.

Here is a case that matters for a support assistant. The reference answer is "refunds are processed within 5 to 7 business days" (9 words). Three candidates:

CandidateCorrect?LCSROUGE-L FROUGE-1 recall
"refunds are processed within 2 to 3 business days"No: wrong number70.787/9

Check the first row by hand. The longest common subsequence is "refunds are processed within to business days", 7 words, so precision is 7/9, recall is 7/9 and F = 0.78. The wrong answer wins by a wide margin. The one word that makes it wrong is the one word ROUGE doesn't care about. The colloquial correct answer scores almost nothing.

6. Why overlap metrics fail for LLM output, and where they still help

The examples show four failure mechanisms.

Paraphrase. A correct answer in different words scores low. Modern LLMs paraphrase freely, so the variance of BLEU and ROUGE across correct answers is huge.

Meaning-critical tokens are cheap. A negation, a number or a name is one token among many, so getting it wrong costs little overlap.

One reference is not the answer space. The metrics compare with the references you wrote. If there are many good answers, as there almost always are for open-ended output, every good answer that isn't close to a reference is punished.

They can be gamed by style. Optimising against ROUGE teaches a model the references' style, as the meeting-summary team discovered.

The Codex paper showed the code version of this: programs that were functionally wrong (they disagreed with the reference solution on some input) often had higher BLEU than functionally correct ones. Code makes the point vivid because correctness can be checked objectively. The same thing happens, less visibly, in prose.

Overlap metrics still have uses. They are cheap regression detectors when the output is meant to stay close to a fixed text. Examples are templated messages, translations of short UI strings, and a model that should reproduce a clause verbatim. A sudden change in average ROUGE between two versions says that something changed, worth a look. And for machine translation at corpus level, with several references, metrics in the BLEU family remain a standard sanity check. They should not be the decision metric for an LLM feature's quality.

7. Embedding similarity and BERTScore

The obvious fix for paraphrase is to compare meanings rather than words. An embedding model (lm10) maps a text to a vector such that texts with similar meanings land close together. Cosine similarity measures closeness: the dot product of two vectors divided by the product of their lengths. It is 1 for identical directions and around 0 for unrelated ones. Worked with toy 3-dimensional vectors a = (0.8, 0.5, 0.1) and b = (0.7, 0.6, 0.2): the dot product is 0.56 + 0.30 + 0.02 = 0.88; |a| = √0.90 = 0.949 and |b| = √0.89 = 0.943; cosine = 0.88 / (0.949 × 0.943) = 0.983. Real embeddings have hundreds or thousands of dimensions, but the arithmetic is the same.

Comparing the embedding of an answer with the embedding of the reference does credit paraphrases. "Your refund will arrive in 5 to 7 business days" and "refunds are processed within 5 to 7 business days" will be very close. But the wrong answer, "within 2 to 3 business days", is also very close, because it shares the topic, the structure and almost all the meaning. So does "refunds are not processed within 5 to 7 days". Embeddings capture what a text is about far better than whether it is right. That makes them excellent for retrieval and clustering, and weak as correctness metrics.

BERTScore (Zhang et al., 2019) refines the idea at the token level. Each token in the candidate and reference gets a contextual embedding from a BERT-style model. Each candidate token is matched to its most similar reference token (greedy matching by cosine). Averaging those best-match similarities gives precision; the same from the reference side gives recall; and F1 combines them. Optional idf weighting gives rare, content-bearing words more say. The authors also rescale scores against a baseline, because raw values bunch in a narrow high range. BERTScore correlates better with human judgements than BLEU on several benchmarks, because it credits synonyms and paraphrases. It shares the embedding blind spot: a single flipped number or added "not" barely changes the token matches.

If you use similarity metrics, two rules apply. First, raw values have no meaning on their own. A cosine of 0.83 might be "same answer" for one embedding model and "loosely related" for another. Calibrate a threshold by labelling a few hundred pairs as equivalent or not and choosing the threshold that best separates them, then report the precision and recall of the metric as a classifier. Second, pair them with checks for the things they miss: an exact-match check on extracted numbers and entities, or a negation-aware judge.

8. Task-specific checks: the best automated metrics

Whenever an output can be checked by doing something with it, that check beats any comparison with a reference. The principle is functional correctness: judge the output by whether it works.

  • Code: run it against unit tests in a sandbox. HumanEval, introduced in the Codex paper, has 164 hand-written problems with an average of 7.7 tests each. Hand-written, because the models were trained on so much public code that problems copied from the internet might already be memorised.
  • SQL: execute the generated query and the reference query on the same database and compare result sets (order-insensitive unless the question asks for an order). Use several database states, because a wrong query can return the right rows on a small test database by accident.
  • Maths and numeric answers: extract the final number and compare within a tolerance, or check symbolic equivalence.
  • Tool and API calls: check the function name and arguments against the expected call, with normalisation for argument order and formats, or execute against a mock and check the resulting state.
  • Citations: check that every cited document ID exists and was retrieved, and, with a judge or entailment model, that the cited passage supports the claim (ev6).
  • Format and constraints: length limits, required sections, allowed vocabulary, language detection, banned content patterns.

Task-specific checks have their own failure: weak tests. If a function has two thin unit tests, wrong code passes by accident. If tests encode one particular implementation (an exact error message), valid alternatives fail, which is what SWE-bench Verified found in 61% of the cases it screened (ev2). Test the tests: deliberately break the reference solution in small ways (mutation testing) and check the tests catch it, and review cases where every system fails.

9. pass@k: sampled outputs and the unbiased estimator

ev1 defined pass@k as the probability that at least one of k samples succeeds. For a single task with known per-sample success probability p, it is 1 − (1 − p)k. In practice you don't know p for each problem. You estimate it by drawing samples, and the obvious estimation shortcut is biased.

The Codex paper's estimator: for each problem, generate n ≥ k samples (they used n = 200 for k up to 100), count the number c that pass the tests, and compute

pass@k = 1 − C(n − c, k) / C(n, k)

where C(a, b) is the number of ways to choose b items from a. The fraction is the probability that a random subset of k of your n samples contains no correct sample. Subtracting from one gives the probability that it contains at least one. Average this over problems.

Worked, n = 10 samples, c = 3 correct:

  • k = 1: 1 − C(7,1)/C(10,1) = 1 − 7/10 = 0.30. For k = 1 it reduces to c/n, as it should.
  • k = 5: C(7,5) = 21 and C(10,5) = 252, so 1 − 21/252 = 1 − 0.083 = 0.917.
  • The plug-in formula 1 − (1 − 0.3)5 = 0.832 gives a different answer. The paper shows in an appendix that this plug-in is biased.

The bigger bias comes from computing pass@k from an average pass@1 across problems. Take two problems, one the model always solves (p = 1.0) and one it never solves (p = 0.0). Average pass@1 is 0.5. Plugging 0.5 into 1 − (1 − p)5 gives 0.97. But the true pass@5 is still 0.5: five tries at the impossible problem still fail. Problems differ in difficulty, so pass@k must be computed per problem and then averaged. This is an instance of Jensen's inequality, which says the average of a curved function is not the function of the average.

For a sense of scale, the Codex paper reports that its 12-billion-parameter Codex model solved 28.8% of HumanEval problems with one sample, and 70.2% when 100 samples were drawn per problem and a problem counted as solved if any passed. The paper's own framing is that pass@k with large k is meaningful only if something can pick the right sample. When they instead picked the sample with the highest mean log-probability, which needs no tests, results fell well short of the oracle. That is the pass@k against pass^k distinction from ev1 again. Report the k that matches how the product chooses among samples.

10. Calibrated scores

Many graders and systems produce a score rather than a verdict: a classifier's probability, a judge's 1-10 rating, a model's stated confidence. A score is calibrated if it means what it says: among outputs scored 0.8, about 80% are correct (ml15). Calibration matters for evaluation in two places. Scores used as thresholds ("send outputs with confidence below 0.7 to a human") only work if the threshold means what you think. And estimates computed from scores ("average judge probability of correctness is 0.85, so we're 85% correct") are only right if the scores are calibrated.

Checking calibration means binning items by score and comparing each bin's average score with its observed accuracy. Worked, 200 answers from a system that states its confidence, checked against gold labels:

Confidence binItemsAverage confidenceActually correctAccuracyGap
High1200.92960.800.12
Medium500.75360.720.03
Low300.55180.600.05

The expected calibration error (ECE) weights each bin's gap by its share of items: (120 × 0.12 + 50 × 0.03 + 30 × 0.05)/200 = (14.4 + 1.5 + 1.5)/200 = 17.4/200 = 0.087, about 9 points. Most of it comes from the high bin, where the system says 92% and delivers 80%: classic overconfidence. Plotting accuracy against confidence per bin gives a reliability diagram, and a calibrated system's points lie on the diagonal.

What do you do with a miscalibrated score? If the ranking is good (higher scores really are more often correct) but the values are off, fit a monotone mapping from score to observed accuracy on held-out labelled data. Platt scaling and isotonic regression (ml15) are the usual tools, and temperature scaling (Guo et al., 2017) is the one-parameter version for model probabilities. Then re-check on fresh data. If you use scores for selective answering (answer only when confident, otherwise abstain or escalate), report the trade-off as coverage against accuracy. In the table, answering only the medium and high bins covers 170/200 = 85% of questions at (96 + 36)/170 = 77.6% accuracy, against 75% when answering everything. That is a small gain, because the high bin is overconfident. Calibrating it first would let you pick a better threshold.

A judge's 1-10 score is not a probability at all. It is an ordinal label, and different judges and prompts use the scale differently. If you need "probability a human would accept this", fit that mapping from judge scores to human verdicts on labelled data, exactly as above. ev4 discusses why binary judge questions are often better than scales in the first place.

Validity at two levels: item and system

A metric can be useful for ranking systems and useless for grading individual outputs, or the reverse. At the system level you ask: across several model versions, does the metric's ranking match human rankings? Averaging over hundreds of items cancels much of the per-item noise, so even a crude metric can rank systems tolerably when they differ a lot. At the item level you ask: for individual outputs, does the metric agree with humans about which are good? BLEU was validated at the system level for translation and was never meant for single sentences. When you validate a metric for your task, report both: a rank correlation (Kendall's tau or Spearman's rho) between metric and human ratings over items, and agreement on the ordering of the versions you actually compare. Use item-level validity when the metric selects outputs for review or rejection, and system-level validity when it gates releases.

Micro and macro averages tell different stories

A micro-average pools all items, or all fields, before computing the metric. A macro-average computes the metric per group (per field, per slice, per class) and then averages the groups. With imbalanced groups they diverge. Micro is dominated by the common cases; macro gives a rare but important group equal say. The choice is a statement about what you value. Dashboards should show the per-group numbers, because both averages can hide a group that has collapsed.

Length is a confounder for almost every metric

Recall-oriented metrics (ROUGE, BERTScore recall) rise with length, because longer outputs contain more of the reference's words. Precision-oriented ones fall. Judges, as ev4 shows, often prefer longer answers. Users usually prefer shorter ones. So any change that alters output length, such as a new model, a prompt tweak or a different temperature, moves metrics for reasons unrelated to quality. Report the length distribution alongside every metric, and when comparing versions with different lengths, check whether the metric difference survives controlling for length, for example by comparing within length buckets.

Composite scores hide trade-offs

Teams like a single number, so they combine accuracy, faithfulness, tone and format into a weighted score. The weights are arbitrary, and a composite can stay flat while one component collapses and another improves. Prefer a small set of gate metrics with individual thresholds, each must-pass, and use composites only for coarse ranking of many candidates. If a composite is required, publish its components next to it.

Binary verdicts versus continuous scores

Continuous metrics carry more information per item and give smoother statistics. Binary verdicts (correct or not, acceptable or not) are easier to define, validate and act on. They are also easier for graders to agree on, and they map directly to "what share of users got a good answer". A practical pattern: keep continuous metrics as diagnostics, and gate on binary verdicts with clear definitions. When a continuous metric is used for a gate, convert it to a binary verdict at a threshold calibrated against human judgement.

Multiple references and reference-free metrics

One response to the single-reference problem is to collect several references per item: BLEU and ROUGE both support multiple references, taking the best match. This helps at the margin, but the space of good answers to an open question is far larger than any handful of references, and writing references is expensive. The other response is to drop references and grade the output against the input and the sources: is every claim supported by the retrieved documents (faithfulness, ev6), does it answer the question (relevance), does it follow the stated constraints? These reference-free checks need a judge or an entailment model rather than string arithmetic, which is why the open end of the metric spectrum belongs to ev4. The trade-off is that reference-free grading cannot tell you whether an answer is complete without some statement of what complete means, so the best rubrics combine both: required facts from the golden set, and faithfulness to sources.

Answer extraction is part of the system under test

If an LLM feature in production parses a "Final answer:" marker, extraction failures are real failures, and the eval should count them. If extraction exists only in the eval harness, its failures are eval bugs and should be counted separately. Mixing the two produces arguments where one engineer says the model got worse and another says the parser did. The clean design is to make extraction part of the product (structured output) and have the eval call the same code.

The public case: Codex and HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021)

OpenAI's Codex paper is the clearest public example of choosing a metric for validity rather than convenience. Code generation had mostly been evaluated by comparing generated code with reference solutions, by exact or fuzzy match such as BLEU. The authors argued that match-based metrics cannot account for the large space of programs functionally equivalent to a reference, and adopted functional correctness instead: a generated function counts as correct if it passes the problem's unit tests. They built HumanEval, 164 hand-written Python problems with an average of 7.7 tests each. The problems were hand-written because public problems were likely to be in the training data, which is contamination prevention (ev2). Generated code ran in a sandbox (gVisor) with network access blocked.

The paper made three contributions that matter here. It defined pass@k with the unbiased estimator 1 − C(n−c, k)/C(n, k), with n = 200 samples per problem, and showed the plug-in estimator was biased. It reported that the 12B Codex model solved 28.8% of problems with one sample, against 0% for GPT-3 and 11.4% for GPT-J, and 70.2% with 100 samples when any passing sample counted. And it provided the evidence against BLEU: functionally incorrect programs often had higher BLEU scores than functionally correct ones. HumanEval was later saturated by stronger models and is now considered easy and potentially contaminated, but its metric design became the template for code evaluation.

Failure modes in production metrics

The parser regression. A model upgrade changes output formatting, with markdown around numbers or "Final Answer" capitalised, and exact-match scores drop 8 points overnight. Engineers start a model investigation. Symptom: failures concentrated in a format pattern, with correct answers visible in the raw text. Fix: count extraction failures separately and alert on them; move to structured output.

The metric that rewarded verbosity. A team optimised token-F1 recall on answers, so answers got longer and users got annoyed. Symptom: metric up, average length up, thumbs-down up. Fix: report length with every metric and add a length-aware or precision component.

Thin tests. A code assistant's hidden tests had one assertion per function, so 15% of "passing" functions were wrong when reviewed. Fix: mutation testing of the test suite, more tests per task, property-based tests.

The uncalibrated threshold. A system routes answers with confidence below 0.8 to humans. After a model update the new model is more confident across the board, routing falls by half, and errors reaching customers double. Fix: recalibrate the confidence-to-accuracy map on each model version, and monitor the routed share and the accuracy of non-routed answers.

The benchmark that leaked. A public benchmark used as the main yardstick turns out to be in a model's training data, so scores jump without any real change in capability. This is not a metric bug but it looks like one. Fix: keep a private, hand-written set of the same kind, as the Codex authors did with HumanEval, and compare.

What to track

Estimate and decide: metrics for a policy-answering assistant

An insurance company's assistant answers customers' questions about their policy ("Am I covered for water damage from a burst pipe?", "What is my excess?"). Outputs are a short prose answer. The team currently tracks BERTScore F1 against an expert-written reference answer, which averages 0.89 and rose to 0.91 after a prompt change. A reviewer read 40 answers after the change and found 6 that stated the wrong excess amount or the wrong coverage decision. The head of product asks: "BERTScore went up, so why are we getting worse answers?" Explain, and propose a metric design.

Answer

Why the metric misled. BERTScore matches tokens by contextual similarity. An answer saying "your excess is £250" and one saying "your excess is £500" share almost every token and every bit of context, and the number differs in one token whose embedding is close to the other number's. The same is true of "you are covered" against "you are not covered". The prompt change probably made answers closer in style to the expert references (similar phrasing, structure, length), which BERTScore rewards, while correctness on the two facts that matter did not improve or got worse. 6 wrong in 40 is 15%, with a Wilson interval of about 7% to 29%: not precise, but clearly not acceptable for coverage decisions.

Redesign the output. Have the model return structured fields as well as prose: coverage_decision (covered, not covered, depends, or cannot determine), excess_amount, policy_clause_ids, plus the prose answer generated from them.

Metrics. Normalised exact match on coverage_decision and excess_amount against expert gold. These are the gate metrics, reported per policy type with intervals. Citation checks: cited clause IDs exist and were retrieved, and a judge checks that the cited clause supports the decision. A consistency assertion: the prose states the same decision and amount as the fields. Abstention quality: on questions the policy can't answer, the expected decision is "cannot determine". Measure both false confident answers and needless abstentions. Keep BERTScore as a diagnostic for drift in phrasing, not as a quality metric.

Validation. Have experts label 150 answers. Check that the new metrics flag the answers experts reject, measuring precision and recall of each metric as a detector, and re-check after every model or prompt change.
