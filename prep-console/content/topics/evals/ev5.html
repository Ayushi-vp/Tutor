A team wants to know whether their assistant's replies are "helpful". They ask two experienced support agents to label 200 replies as helpful or not. Agent A says 81% are helpful. Agent B says 64%. On the same 200 replies, the two agents agree on whether a reply is helpful only 70% of the time. Which number is the assistant's helpfulness? Neither. The team has not measured helpfulness. It has measured two people's private definitions of it.

A second team avoids that by using crowd workers through a labelling platform, three per item, with majority vote. Their labels look consistent, with high agreement, until a domain expert audits 50 of them. The crowd consistently marks as correct a common answer pattern that is actually wrong, because the answer sounds authoritative and the workers can't check it. Three labellers making the same mistake produced a confident, wrong majority.

Human judgement is the reference everything else in evaluation is calibrated against. It sits above the judge in the eval pyramid (ev1) and supplies the golden labels (ev2) and the judge's validation set (ev4). But "a human looked at it" is not, on its own, a measurement. Human labels become trustworthy only through a process. You write guidelines that pin down what each label means, and run calibration rounds that align labellers. You overlap labels to measure agreement and use statistics that correct for chance. You adjudicate disagreements, check quality with hidden gold items, and match labeller expertise to the task. That process has a cost, and deciding how much of it a decision needs is part of the job.

This lesson treats human labelling the way an engineer treats any measurement system. It covers how guidelines and calibration rounds work. It shows how to quantify agreement: Cohen's kappa worked from a 2×2 table, and why the same raw agreement can give very different kappas. It extends to many raters with Fleiss' kappa and to messy real-world data with Krippendorff's alpha, both computed by hand. It covers how to turn several labels into one by majority vote or adjudication, how to monitor labeller quality, and how to budget the whole operation. The case is the labelling programme behind InstructGPT, one of the most consequential human-labelling efforts in AI so far.

Think of a team of human labellers as a set of measuring instruments, like several thermometers placed in the same room. If they all read 21°C, you trust the reading. If one says 18 and another 24, you don't know the temperature. You have discovered that your thermometers disagree. You can respond in several ways. Calibrate them against a reference (gold items with known answers). Discover that one is broken (an unreliable labeller). Realise that they are measuring different things, one in the sun and one in the shade (ambiguous guidelines). Or accept that the room really is uneven (genuinely subjective judgements) and report the spread.

The analogy breaks in an important way. A thermometer measures a physical quantity that exists independently of it. Many labelling tasks measure something that only exists once the guideline defines it. "Helpful" is not a property of a reply in the way temperature is a property of a room. It becomes a measurable property when the guideline says, for example, "helpful means it resolves the customer's stated problem, using correct policy, without asking for information already given". That is why most of the work of human evaluation is definition, and why disagreement so often points back at the guideline.

The labelling operation at a glance

The flow has two loops. Before any real labelling, everyone labels the same small set in a calibration round. Agreement is measured, disagreements are discussed, and the guidelines are revised. This repeats until agreement is good enough for the purpose. Then production labelling begins. Most items get one label, but a share get two or more (overlap) so agreement can be measured continuously. Hidden gold items with known answers check each labeller's accuracy. Items where labellers disagree go to an adjudicator, an expert who decides and records why, and those reasons feed back into the guidelines.

Every labelling design answers three questions, and the rest of the lesson gives the tools to answer them deliberately rather than by habit. Who labels? Domain experts, trained generalists or a crowd, decided by what the label requires someone to know. How many labels per item? One, two, or more, decided by what the labels are for and what an error costs. How are disagreements resolved? By vote, by an expert adjudicator, or by a statistical model of each labeller's reliability. A golden set used to gate releases and a weekly sample used to estimate production quality deserve different answers, and the arithmetic of cost at the end of the body makes the trade-off concrete.

Underneath both loops is one question: how much do the labellers agree, beyond what chance alone would produce? Answering it properly needs a little statistics, and that is where the body begins.

1. Guidelines and calibration rounds

ev2 listed what labelling guidelines must contain: definitions of each label, authoritative sources, decision rules, borderline examples and what to do with broken or ambiguous items. Here the focus is on how guidelines are developed, because a first draft is never good enough.

A calibration round works like this. Pick 30-50 items that span the range, including obvious passes, obvious fails and the hard middle. Have every labeller label them independently, without discussing them. Compute agreement, overall and per item. Then meet and go through the items with the most disagreement. For each, ask: did people read the guideline differently (fix the wording), did the guideline not cover the situation (add a rule and an example), or is the item genuinely ambiguous (decide what the product wants, or add a "can't tell" option)? Revise the guideline, pick a fresh set of items, and repeat. Two or three rounds typically move agreement from mediocre to good. Re-using the same items would let labellers memorise the discussed answers and overstate the improvement.

Guidelines must be versioned, and every label should record the version it was made under. When a rule changes ("from now on, a reply that asks for an order number already given counts as unhelpful"), labels made under the old rule are inconsistent with new ones, and you need to know which is which. A changelog at the top of the guideline is the cheapest tool in the whole operation.

Calibration also reveals when a criterion is too broad. If labellers keep disagreeing because "helpful" means correctness to one and politeness to another, split it into separate criteria. This is the same decomposition principle as for judge rubrics (ev4), and it helps humans for the same reasons it helps models.

2. Why raw agreement is not enough

The simplest measure of agreement is observed agreement (po): the share of items on which two labellers gave the same label. It is intuitive and should always be reported. But it has a flaw. Some agreement happens by chance, and how much depends on how the labels are distributed.

Suppose 90% of replies are acceptable. Two labellers who don't read the replies at all and just say "acceptable" 90% of the time at random will agree whenever both say acceptable (0.9 × 0.9 = 0.81) or both say unacceptable (0.1 × 0.1 = 0.01), which is 82% of the time. An observed agreement of 85% on such a task is barely better than coin-flipping with a biased coin. The same 85% on a task where labels split 50/50, with chance agreement of 50%, would be impressive. Raw agreement alone cannot tell these apart.

3. Cohen's kappa, worked from a 2×2 table

Cohen's kappa (Cohen, 1960) corrects for chance. It compares observed agreement po with chance agreement pe, the agreement expected if each labeller labelled independently at their own rates:

κ = (po − pe) / (1 − pe)

Read it as: of the agreement that was possible beyond chance (1 − pe), what fraction was achieved (po − pe)? Kappa is 1 for perfect agreement, 0 for chance-level agreement, and negative if labellers agree less than chance.

lm14 computed kappa from marginal rates. Here is the full calculation from a table, which is how you will usually meet it. Two labellers, A and B, rate 100 support replies as acceptable or unacceptable:

B: acceptableB: unacceptableTotal (A)
A: acceptable70878
A: unacceptable71522
Total (B)7723100

Step 1, observed agreement: the diagonal. po = (70 + 15)/100 = 0.85.

Step 2, each labeller's rates: A says acceptable 78/100 = 0.78 of the time, B 77/100 = 0.77.

Step 3, chance agreement: if independent, both say acceptable with probability 0.78 × 0.77 = 0.6006, and both say unacceptable with probability 0.22 × 0.23 = 0.0506. pe = 0.6006 + 0.0506 = 0.651.

Step 4, kappa: κ = (0.85 − 0.651)/(1 − 0.651) = 0.199/0.349 = 0.57.

On the scale proposed by Landis and Koch (1977), which is widely used though somewhat arbitrary, 0.41-0.60 is "moderate" agreement. The other bands are 0.21-0.40 fair, 0.61-0.80 substantial and 0.81-1.00 almost perfect. So 85% agreement here is only moderate once chance is accounted for, because acceptable replies dominate.

4. The prevalence paradox

Now take a second pair of labellers on a different task, also with 85% observed agreement but balanced categories:

B: acceptableB: unacceptableTotal (A)
A: acceptable40747
A: unacceptable84553
Total (B)4852100

po = (40 + 45)/100 = 0.85, the same. pe = 0.47 × 0.48 + 0.53 × 0.52 = 0.2256 + 0.2756 = 0.501. κ = (0.85 − 0.501)/(1 − 0.501) = 0.349/0.499 = 0.70, "substantial".

This is the prevalence paradox: kappa falls when one category dominates, even if the labellers are just as consistent. It has practical consequences. Kappas are not comparable across tasks with different label distributions. Rare-event labelling, such as flagging harmful outputs that occur 2% of the time, can show 97% raw agreement and a low kappa. That low kappa is not wrong: most of the 97% is the easy agreement on the 98% of harmless items, and what you care about is whether labellers agree on the rare harmful ones.

Report three things together: raw agreement, the category distribution, and kappa (or alpha). For rare categories also report positive specific agreement: 2 × (both positive) / (2 × both positive + disagreements). It answers "when one labeller flags an item, how often does the other?" In the first table, for the unacceptable category, it is 2 × 15/(2 × 15 + 8 + 7) = 30/45 = 0.67. When one labeller calls a reply unacceptable, the other agrees two times in three.

5. Many raters: Fleiss' kappa

Cohen's kappa is for exactly two labellers who both label every item. Real operations often have three or more labels per item, from different people for different items. Fleiss' kappa handles a fixed number of raters per item, n, who need not be the same people.

For each item, it asks what share of rater pairs agree. For each category, it asks how common that category is overall, which gives the chance agreement. Worked: 6 items, 3 raters each, labels Pass or Fail.

Fleiss' κ = (0.667 − 0.525)/(1 − 0.525) = 0.142/0.475 = 0.30, "fair". Half the items have a dissenting rater, which in a three-rater setup means a lot of disagreement.

6. Missing labels and ordinal scales: Krippendorff's alpha

Production labelling is messier still. Not every labeller sees every item, so some labels are missing. Labels may be ordinal (a 1-5 scale, where 4 versus 5 is a smaller disagreement than 1 versus 5) or interval. Krippendorff's alpha handles all of this in one framework:

α = 1 − Do/De

where Do is the observed disagreement and De is the disagreement expected by chance. Like kappa, α = 1 is perfect agreement and 0 is chance. The difference is in how disagreement is measured. Alpha uses a distance function suited to the data type: for nominal labels any difference counts 1; for ordinal or interval labels, larger differences count more.

A small nominal example with missing data: 6 items, up to 3 raters, labels P or F, "–" for missing.

The computation builds a coincidence matrix. For every item, every ordered pair of labels from different raters is counted, each weighted by 1/(m − 1), where m is the number of labels on that item. Items with only one label are dropped, because there is nothing to compare. Working through it:

  • Item 1 (P, P, P; m = 3): 6 ordered pairs, all P-P, each weighted 1/2, adding 3 to the P-P cell.
  • Item 2 (P, F; m = 2): the pairs P-F and F-P, weight 1, adding 1 to each disagreement cell.
  • Item 3 (F, F, F): adds 3 to F-F.
  • Item 4 (P, P, F; m = 3): P-P twice (adding 1), P-F twice and F-P twice (adding 1 to each disagreement cell).
  • Item 5 (F, F; m = 2): adds 2 to F-F.
  • Item 6 (P, P; m = 2): adds 2 to P-P.

The totals are P-P 6, F-F 5, P-F 2, F-P 2, summing to 15, the number of pairable labels. The category totals are nP = 6 + 2 = 8 and nF = 5 + 2 = 7, with n = 15. For nominal data:

α = 1 − (n − 1) × (disagreeing cells)/(Σc≠k nc nk) = 1 − 14 × (2 + 2)/(8 × 7 + 7 × 8) = 1 − 56/112 = 0.50.

Krippendorff's own guidance is to rely on data with α ≥ 0.800 and to draw only tentative conclusions from data between 0.667 and 0.800. Those are conventions, not laws, but they are a useful anchor. By that standard, this toy data is not reliable. In practice, use a tested implementation for real data and compute it by hand once to understand it.

Which coefficient to use? Two raters labelling everything: Cohen's kappa. A fixed number of raters per item: Fleiss' kappa. Rotating raters, missing labels or ordinal scales, which describes most production operations: Krippendorff's alpha. Whichever you use, report raw agreement too.

7. From several labels to one: majority vote and adjudication

When items have several labels, you need one final label. There are two main approaches.

Majority vote takes the most common label. If labellers are independent and each is right with probability p, the majority of n labellers is right with the probability that more than half are right, a binomial sum:

Labeller accuracy p1 labeller3 (majority)5 (majority)7 (majority)
0.7070.0%78.4%83.7%87.4%
0.8080.0%89.6%94.2%96.7%
0.9090.0%97.2%99.1%99.7%

For p = 0.8 and three labellers: P(all three right) = 0.8³ = 0.512, and P(exactly two right) = 3 × 0.8² × 0.2 = 0.384, for a total of 0.896. Doubling the cost from one to three labels buys about 10 points of accuracy. Going from three to five buys under 5 more, and from five to seven under 3.

The independence assumption is the catch. Labellers who share a misleading guideline, a common misconception or a pull toward authoritative-sounding answers make the same mistakes, and voting confirms them. That was the second team's problem in the opening story. Their errors were systematic, and no number of extra crowd labels would have fixed them.

Adjudication sends disagreements to an expert, often a senior labeller or domain specialist, who makes the final call and records why. It costs more per item, but it's applied only to disagreements, and it can fix systematic errors because the adjudicator knows more. Their recorded reasons are the best source of guideline improvements. A widely used design is two labellers per item, agreements accepted automatically and disagreements adjudicated. Note one limitation: if both labellers agree on a wrong answer, adjudication never sees it. That is why gold items are needed as well.

A third approach, useful with large crowds, models each labeller's reliability. The Dawid-Skene model (1979) treats each item's true label as unknown, and each labeller as having their own probability of giving each label given the truth. The expectation-maximisation (EM) algorithm then estimates both at once from overlapping labels. Reliable labellers get more weight than unreliable ones without anyone being told which is which. Its limit is the same as majority vote's: it cannot detect errors that every labeller shares.

8. Annotator quality

Labeller quality varies between people and drifts over time with fatigue, boredom and shifting interpretations. An ongoing operation monitors it with several signals:

  • Gold items (also called honeypots): items with expert-decided labels mixed unannounced into each labeller's queue, typically 2-5% of items. Accuracy on gold is the most direct quality measure. Rotate gold items so they aren't recognised, and draw new ones from recently adjudicated cases.
  • Agreement with peers on overlap items, per labeller. Someone whose agreement with everyone else is low is either unreliable or seeing something others miss. Read their disagreements to tell which.
  • Label distribution. A labeller marking 98% of items acceptable when peers mark 80% is probably not reading carefully.
  • Time per item. Implausibly fast labelling is a red flag. So is a sudden slowdown, which may mean the queue changed.
  • Intra-annotator agreement: re-showing an item weeks later and checking the labeller gives the same label. Low self-consistency means the guideline leaves too much to interpretation.

Quality monitoring should lead to action. Give feedback with concrete examples, retrain, and remove labellers who stay below a threshold. Re-label their recent work if it fed a golden set. Selection matters as much as monitoring. The InstructGPT team, below, screened candidates on tasks resembling the real work before hiring them.

Match expertise to the label. Fluency, tone and obvious instruction-following can be judged by trained generalists. Correctness in law, medicine, tax, security or a company's internal policy needs people who know the domain. Crowd labels on expert content tend to fail in the worst way, confidently and consistently. Measure this directly by having experts label a sample of crowd-labelled items.

9. The cost of human labels

Labelling cost is simple arithmetic, and it is worth doing before committing to a design:

cost ≈ items × labels per item × (seconds per label / 3,600) × hourly rate, plus adjudication, plus gold-item overhead.

Worked: 2,000 support replies, three criteria each, about 90 seconds per reply (40 an hour), at $40 an hour.

A golden set used for release gates and judge validation (ev2, ev4) is worth double labelling with adjudication, because its errors propagate into every decision. A weekly production quality estimate can use single labels with an overlap sample, because you need an unbiased estimate with a known error rate, not perfect individual labels. Training data for fine-tuning (ev11) is often single-labelled at scale, with filtering of low-agreement items.

Calendar time matters as much as money. 100 labelling hours spread across five part-time experts is two to three weeks. Plan labelling ahead of the decisions that need it, and keep a standing labelling capacity for continuous production sampling rather than rushing it before each release.

Agreement is reliability, not validity

High agreement means labellers apply the guideline consistently. It does not mean the guideline captures what you care about. Two labellers trained to mark "contains an apology" as helpful will agree beautifully and measure the wrong thing. Validity is checked differently. Do the labels predict outcomes you care about (resolved tickets, user retention)? Do domain experts outside the labelling team endorse them? Do they agree with the people the product serves? This is the same reliability/validity distinction ev1 made for evals, applied to their foundation.

Subjective tasks: measure the distribution, not one label

For some criteria, such as offensiveness, humour, tone and political balance, people legitimately disagree, and the disagreement is a property of the content, not an error. Forcing a single label erases information: a joke that 50% of people find offensive is different from one that 5% do. For such tasks, collect several independent ratings per item from a pool that represents your users, and report the distribution: the share finding it offensive, with an interval. The "truth" for an item is then a probability, and a model or judge is evaluated on how well it predicts that probability, not on matching a majority label.

Labeller pools shape what "good" means

Whoever labels defines quality for the system. A pool that is young, English-speaking and from one country will encode its preferences, and a model tuned to those labels will reflect them. The InstructGPT authors were explicit that their roughly 40 labellers were "clearly not representative" of the full range of people affected. For products with diverse users, recruit labellers who resemble them, measure agreement across subgroups, and look for systematic differences rather than averaging them away.

Pairwise preference labelling has its own statistics

Many LLM labels are preferences between two responses (lm6), not absolute ratings. Agreement on preferences is usually computed on non-tied pairs, and ties need a policy: allowed, forbidden, or treated as half. The InstructGPT figures of about 73-77% agreement are on rankings of model outputs. Preferences can also be aggregated into a ranking with the Bradley-Terry model (ev4). Note that preference agreement in the 70s is typical of hard comparisons between good outputs: when both responses are decent, people split. So a reward model or judge reaching similar agreement is near the ceiling, not failing.

Label noise as a ceiling on measurable improvement

If gold labels are wrong on 8% of items, a perfect system scores at most 92%. Two systems that differ only on mislabelled items look identical, or reversed. As systems improve, the share of remaining "errors" that are really label errors grows, and evaluation stops being able to see progress. SWE-bench Verified (ev2) is the public example: re-labelling raised a model's measured score from 16% to 33%. The mature practice is to periodically adjudicate the items every top system fails, because those are disproportionately label errors.

Choosing which items to label

Labelling budgets are finite, so which items get labelled matters as much as how. A random sample gives unbiased estimates of overall quality, and every operation needs one. But for improving a judge or finding failures, labels are more valuable where the automated graders are uncertain or disagree: items where the judge's two orders disagree (ev4), where two judges disagree, where the judge's probability of PASS is near 0.5, or where the system's own confidence is low. This targeted selection, a form of active learning, finds disagreements and errors several times faster than random sampling. The catch is the same as ev2's representative-versus-targeted distinction. Labels chosen this way are enriched with hard cases, so they must be kept separate from, or reweighted against, the random sample when estimating overall rates.

Model-assisted labelling and anchoring

A tempting way to cut cost is to show labellers a pre-filled label from a model and ask them to confirm or correct it. Throughput rises sharply, but people anchor on the suggestion: they accept plausible pre-labels they would not have chosen themselves, especially under time pressure. The resulting labels agree with the model more than independent labels would, which makes the model look better than it is, and is a disaster if those labels are then used to validate that same model. Use pre-labels for training data where speed matters and some bias is tolerable; never for golden sets or judge validation. If you must use them, measure the anchoring by having a subset labelled blind and comparing.

Annotator incentives and wellbeing

Paying per item rewards speed over care. Paying hourly with quality checks rewards care but needs monitoring. Either way, labellers respond to what is measured, so measure accuracy on gold, not just throughput. Labelling harmful content (violence, abuse, self-harm) also takes a real psychological toll. Responsible operations limit exposure, rotate tasks, offer support and allow opting out. This is an ethical obligation, and it is also a quality issue: exhausted labellers produce worse labels.

The public case: InstructGPT's labelling programme (Ouyang et al., 2022)

InstructGPT, OpenAI's 2022 work that turned GPT-3 into an instruction-following model with human feedback, rested on a human labelling operation. That operation's design is unusually well documented in the paper's appendices. The team hired about 40 contractors through Upwork and Scale AI. They were selected by a screening test of performance on tasks resembling the real work: flagging sensitive content in agreement with researchers, ranking outputs in agreement with researchers, and writing appropriate responses to sensitive prompts. The team deliberately kept the group small to allow high-bandwidth communication: onboarding, detailed instructions, and a shared chat channel for questions.

Labellers wrote demonstrations and, crucially for evaluation, ranked model outputs. The paper reports agreement between labellers: training labellers agreed with each other 72.6 ± 1.5% of the time, and held-out labellers, who were not involved in producing training data, 77.3 ± 1.3%. The authors compare this with researcher-researcher agreement of 73 ± 4% in earlier summarisation work. They also report that the guidance differed by phase. During training data collection labellers were asked to prioritise helpfulness to the user. In final evaluations they were asked to prioritise truthfulness and harmlessness. That is a vivid example of how the guideline defines what is being measured.

Three lessons carry over to any labelling operation. First, screening on realistic tasks and keeping close contact with a small team produced consistent labels. Second, even so, roughly a quarter of pairwise judgements were contested. Human preference is noisy, and evaluations built on it must report that noise. Third, the authors were candid that their labellers were not representative of everyone affected, a limitation of any labeller pool.

Failure modes in practice

Guideline drift. The guideline changes in a chat thread, not in the document. New labellers follow the old version, old labellers the new one, and agreement quietly falls. Fix: one versioned document, a changelog, and every label stamped with its version.

The confident crowd. Crowd labellers marked authoritative-sounding wrong answers as correct, consistently, and majority vote locked it in. Fix: expert gold items on that slice, expert labellers for correctness, and auditing a sample of crowd labels with an expert.

Gold items everyone recognises. After three months the same 40 gold items had been seen dozens of times, and gold accuracy was 99% while peer agreement fell. Fix: rotate gold, and build new gold from adjudicated items.

Labelling as a last-minute task. A release decision waits for labels, the team rushes 500 items through untrained labellers in two days, and the decision rests on labels of unknown quality. Fix: a standing labelling capacity and a calendar that schedules labelling before the decisions that need it.

What to track

A labelling operation's dashboard shows agreement (raw, and kappa or alpha) per criterion and per week; per-labeller gold accuracy, peer agreement, throughput and label distribution; the adjudication rate and the most common adjudication reasons; the guideline version in use; and cost per final label. A sudden drop in agreement usually means the input distribution or the guideline changed. Find out which before blaming labellers.

Design and diagnose: labelling for a judge validation set

You need 300 human-labelled support replies to validate an LLM judge on two criteria: policy_correct (pass/fail) and tone (1-4 scale: rude, curt, neutral, warm). You have four experienced support agents for one week, 10 hours each, and a senior support lead available for 5 hours. After a calibration round on 40 items, agreement on policy_correct is 88% raw with kappa 0.62 (82% of items pass), and Krippendorff's alpha on tone, using ordinal distances, is 0.48. Design the labelling, and decide what to do about tone.

Answer

Capacity. 40 agent-hours. If a reply with both criteria takes about 2 minutes, that's 30 an hour, or about 1,200 labels. 300 items double-labelled is 600 labels, leaving room for a second calibration round and gold items. The lead's 5 hours at about 2 minutes per item can adjudicate around 150 items.

policy_correct. Kappa 0.62 with 82% prevalence is "substantial" but borderline for a validation set. 88% raw agreement means 12% disagreement, about 36 of 300 items, which the lead can adjudicate. Before production, read the calibration disagreements. If they cluster on, say, partial refunds, add a rule and example to the guideline. Then double-label all 300, auto-accept agreements and send disagreements to the lead. Also report positive specific agreement on fail, since failures are the rare class the judge must catch.

tone. Alpha 0.48 is well below Krippendorff's tentative threshold of 0.667. The scale is not yet a reliable measurement, and validating a judge against it would be meaningless. Diagnose: are disagreements mostly adjacent (neutral vs warm), or large (curt vs warm)? If adjacent, the boundaries are unclear. Rewrite the level definitions with two concrete examples each, or collapse to a binary "acceptable tone / not". If large, people mean different things by tone. Define what it covers (greeting, acknowledgement of the problem, no blame) and run a second calibration round on 40 fresh items. If alpha still falls short, treat tone as subjective: collect ratings from all four agents on a subset and evaluate the judge against the average rating, or drop tone from the judge's gating criteria and rely on online signals.

Quality. Include 15 gold items, agreed by the lead, spread through each agent's queue, and check each agent's accuracy on them midweek. Stamp every label with the guideline version.
