A company builds an assistant that answers employees' questions from the internal knowledge base: HR policies, engineering runbooks, product documentation. It uses retrieval-augmented generation (RAG): retrieve the most relevant passages, then ask an LLM to answer from them with citations. The team tracks one number, the share of answers that experts mark as correct on a 300-question set. It starts at 78%. After a quarter of changes (a new embedding model, a different chunk size, a rewritten prompt, a cheaper LLM) it is 70%, and nobody can say which change hurt.

When an engineer finally digs in, the story is tangled. The new chunk size split many policy sections in two, so the retriever often found half an answer. The new embedding model was better on engineering questions and worse on HR. The cheaper LLM ignored the retrieved context on about one question in ten and answered from memory, sometimes from an outdated version of a policy it had seen in training. And a quarter of the "wrong" answers were for questions whose documents had been updated after the reference answers were written. The single end-to-end number had moved for four reasons, two of them pulling in opposite directions.

A RAG system is a pipeline of stages that fail independently. Documents must be in the index and parsed correctly. The retriever must find the right chunks. Context assembly must keep them and put them where the model will use them. The model must read them and answer faithfully. lm9 built this pipeline and named its four failure points. This lesson is about measuring it. That means retrieval metrics computed from relevance labels: hit rate, recall, precision, mean reciprocal rank and nDCG, with nDCG@5 worked by hand for one query. It means generation metrics that don't need a reference answer, faithfulness, answer relevance and context relevance, as defined in the RAGAS paper, which is this lesson's case. It means citation checks, unanswerable questions, and the attribution table that tells you, for every failure, which stage to blame.

Think of a RAG system as a research assistant working in a library. You ask a question. The assistant goes to the catalogue, pulls some books off the shelves (retrieval), picks the pages that seem relevant and lays them on the desk (context assembly), then reads them and writes you an answer with page references (generation). If the answer is wrong, the fix depends on where it went wrong. The library may not have the book. The assistant may have pulled the wrong books, or the right book but left the key page on the trolley. Or the assistant had the right page open and misread it, or ignored it and answered from memory. You wouldn't fix all of these by asking the assistant to "read more carefully".

The analogy holds well, with one difference worth noticing. A human assistant who doesn't find the answer usually says so. An LLM tends to answer anyway, fluently, from whatever it has. That makes it especially important to measure whether answers are supported by what was retrieved, not just whether they sound right.

Where each metric looks

Retrieval metrics compare the retriever's ranked list with relevance labels, which say which chunks are relevant to each question. They answer: did we find the right material, and did we rank it near the top? Context-assembly checks ask whether the relevant chunks survived the token budget and where they ended up in the prompt. Generation metrics compare the answer with the context and the question. Is every claim supported by the context (faithfulness)? Does it address the question (answer relevance)? Do its citations hold up? And above all of it sits end-to-end correctness: is the answer right, as judged against a reference or by an expert, including the cases where the right answer is "the documents don't say"?

The metrics also differ in what they need you to prepare in advance, which shapes how teams actually adopt them. Generation metrics like faithfulness need nothing but the question, the retrieved context and the answer, so they can run on any trace, including live production traffic, from day one. Retrieval metrics need relevance labels, which take real labelling effort but then let you compare retrievers in minutes. End-to-end correctness needs reference answers or expert review, the most expensive of the three, and goes stale as documents change. A sensible order of investment is therefore: reference-free faithfulness and citation checks first, a few hundred questions with relevance labels next, and a curated set with reference answers for release decisions.

A useful way to hold these together: component metrics are for diagnosis, end-to-end metrics are for decisions. A change that improves recall@10 but doesn't move end-to-end correctness hasn't helped users. A drop in end-to-end correctness with no change in retrieval metrics points straight at generation.

1. Relevance labels: the ground truth for retrieval

Every retrieval metric needs to know, for each question, which chunks are relevant. Producing those labels is the expensive part of retrieval evaluation, and its design decides how trustworthy the metrics are.

Graded relevance is more useful than yes/no. A common four-level scale: 3 means the chunk fully answers the question; 2 means it contains part of the answer or key supporting information; 1 means it is on topic but doesn't help answer; 0 means irrelevant. Grades let metrics distinguish "found the chunk that answers it" from "found something about the same topic". That distinction is exactly what was hidden in the opening story, where the new chunking produced chunks that were on topic but contained only half the answer.

You can't label every chunk for every question in a large corpus. The standard technique, developed for the TREC (Text REtrieval Conference) evaluations run by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology, is pooling. Run several different retrievers on each question, for example keyword search (BM25), a dense embedding retriever, a hybrid, and each with a reranker. Take the union of each system's top 10-20 results, and label that pool. Chunks outside the pool are assumed irrelevant. This assumption biases metrics in favour of systems similar to those that built the pool, because a genuinely new retriever may find relevant chunks nobody labelled, and it will be scored as if they were irrelevant. So include diverse retrievers, and re-pool when you evaluate something new.

The questions matter as much as the labels. Real queries from logs are best, because users phrase things in ways documentation authors don't ("how do I get my laptop fixed" against a page titled "Hardware Incident Management Procedure"). Questions written by reading a passage and asking something it answers tend to reuse the passage's words, which makes keyword retrieval look unrealistically good. If you generate questions this way, by people or by LLM (ev10), paraphrase them away from the source wording, and keep the real-query set as the primary measure.

LLMs can pre-label pools for relevance, which cuts cost sharply. Validate them against human labels on a sample first, exactly as for any judge (ev4).

2. Hit rate, recall, precision and MRR

lm9 introduced recall@k and MRR. It defined recall@k as the fraction of questions with at least one relevant chunk in the top k. That quantity is more precisely called hit rate@k, and it is worth separating from recall@k proper, because they diverge exactly when it matters.

For one question with a set of relevant chunks R and the system's top-k list:

  • Hit rate@k = 1 if any chunk in R is in the top k, else 0. Suitable when one relevant chunk is enough to answer.
  • Recall@k = |R ∩ top-k| / |R|, the share of all relevant chunks found. Suitable when the answer needs several chunks, such as a policy with conditions spread across sections.
  • Precision@k = |R ∩ top-k| / k, the share of the top k that is relevant. Low precision means the prompt is full of noise.
  • Reciprocal rank = 1 / (rank of the first relevant chunk), or 0 if none in the list. Averaged over questions it is the MRR, which rewards putting a relevant chunk first.

Each is averaged over the question set to give the system's score. Which k? The k you actually put in the prompt. If the system sends the top 5 chunks to the model, recall@20 is irrelevant to answer quality, because chunks 6-20 never reach the model. Reporting recall at a larger k than you use is a common way to make a retriever look better than the system is.

3. nDCG: rewarding order and grades

Recall and precision ignore both order and grade within the top k. A list with the fully-answering chunk at rank 1 and one with it at rank 5 score the same recall@5. But order matters. Models use early context more reliably than material buried in the middle of a long prompt, and many systems take only the top few chunks after reranking. Discounted cumulative gain (DCG), defined by Järvelin and Kekäläinen in 2002, captures both. Each result contributes its relevance grade (its gain), divided by a discount that grows with rank:

DCG@k = Σi=1..k reli / log2(i + 1)

The discounts for ranks 1-5 are 1/log22 = 1, 1/log23 = 0.631, 1/log24 = 0.5, 1/log25 = 0.431 and 1/log26 = 0.387. DCG on its own isn't comparable across questions, because a question with many highly relevant chunks has a higher possible DCG. So divide by the ideal DCG (IDCG), the DCG of the best possible ordering of the labelled chunks, to get nDCG@k, between 0 and 1.

Worked example, one query. An employee asks: "How do I cancel my annual software subscription and get a prorated refund?" The labelled relevant chunks are:

  • D7, "Cancellation and refund policy for annual plans": grade 3, fully answers.
  • D2, "Steps to cancel a subscription in the admin console": grade 2.
  • D9, "How proration is calculated": grade 2.
  • D4, "Billing FAQ": grade 1, on topic but not helpful.

The retriever returns this top 5: D2, D5, D7, D1, D4, where D5 and D1 are irrelevant (grade 0).

Now compute every metric for this query.

  • Hit rate@5 = 1: there is a relevant chunk in the top 5.
  • Recall@5, counting grade ≥ 1 as relevant: 3 of 4 relevant chunks retrieved (D2, D7, D4; D9 missing) = 0.75. Counting only grade ≥ 2 ("actually helps"): 2 of 3 = 0.67.
  • Precision@5 = 3/5 = 0.60 at grade ≥ 1.
  • Reciprocal rank = 1/1 = 1.0, since D2 at rank 1 is relevant. At the stricter "fully answers" level (grade 3), the first such chunk is D7 at rank 3, so reciprocal rank is 0.33.
  • DCG@5 = 2/1 + 0 + 3/2 + 0 + 1/2.585 = 2 + 1.5 + 0.387 = 3.887.
  • IDCG@5: the ideal order is D7 (3), D2 (2), D9 (2), D4 (1), then nothing: 3/1 + 2/1.585 + 2/2 + 1/2.322 = 3 + 1.262 + 1 + 0.431 = 5.693.
  • nDCG@5 = 3.887/5.693 = 0.68.

Some systems use an exponential gain, 2rel − 1, which rewards highly relevant items even more. With it, this ranking's nDCG@5 is 6.887/10.823 = 0.64. Always say which variant you report. Now suppose a reranker moves D7 to rank 1 and D2 to rank 2, keeping D4 at rank 4. With linear gain, nDCG@5 rises to about 0.82 while recall@5 is unchanged. That is exactly the improvement a reranker exists to make, and recall can't see it.

The different views of the same ranking explain a lot of RAG debugging. MRR at "any relevance" says the system is perfect for this query. MRR at "fully answers" and nDCG say it's mediocre. Recall says a quarter of the needed material is missing. Which matters depends on how the generator uses the context, which is why retrieval metrics must eventually be tied to end-to-end results.

4. Generation metrics without a reference: faithfulness

Generation evaluation asks what the model did with the context. The most important property is faithfulness, also called groundedness: every claim the answer makes can be inferred from the retrieved context. lm15 distinguished faithfulness (consistent with the provided source) from factuality (true in the world). In RAG, faithfulness is the generator's responsibility, and factuality also depends on whether retrieval brought back the right, current documents.

Faithfulness is measured claim by claim, because answers mix supported and unsupported statements. The method used in the RAGAS paper (Es et al., 2023) has two steps, each an LLM prompt. First, an LLM decomposes the answer into short, self-contained statements. Second, for each statement, an LLM decides whether it can be inferred from the context, giving a brief explanation and a yes/no verdict. The faithfulness score is F = |V|/|S|, the number of supported statements over the total.

Worked: the answer to the subscription question is "You can cancel from the admin console under Billing. Annual plans cancelled within 30 days get a full refund. After that you get a prorated refund for the remaining full months. Refunds take 5-7 business days. You'll also get a $20 credit for the inconvenience." Decomposed into five statements, checked against the retrieved chunks D2, D7 and D4:

StatementSupported by context?
Cancel from the admin console under BillingYes (D2)
Full refund within 30 days for annual plansYes (D7)
Prorated refund for remaining full months after thatYes (D7)
Refunds take 5-7 business daysYes (D4)
A $20 credit for the inconvenienceNo: not in any retrieved chunk

Faithfulness = 4/5 = 0.80. The unsupported claim is the dangerous kind: a specific, plausible promise that the company may be held to. Notice that the proration statement is supported by D7 even though D9, the detailed proration chunk, was never retrieved. Had the answer said how proration is calculated, the claim would have had nothing to check against.

Faithfulness has a blind spot worth repeating: it is relative to the context. If retrieval returned last year's policy, an answer that faithfully repeats it scores 1.0 and is wrong. That is why faithfulness must sit alongside correctness against a reference, and why the eval set's references must be updated when documents change (ev2).

5. Answer relevance and context relevance

RAGAS defines two more reference-free metrics.

Answer relevance asks whether the answer addresses the question, ignoring factual accuracy, and penalising incomplete answers and redundant padding. The RAGAS method is indirect. An LLM generates n questions that the answer would be a good answer to. Each is embedded and compared with the original question by cosine similarity, and the score is the mean: AR = (1/n) Σ sim(q, qi). The idea is that an answer which addresses the question will suggest questions close to it. An answer that wanders, or covers only part, will suggest different ones. Worked: three generated questions with cosine similarities 0.92, 0.88 and 0.71 to the original give AR = 0.837. The third generated question was "What compensation do customers get for problems?", prompted by the unsupported $20 credit. Off-topic padding lowers answer relevance as well as faithfulness.

Context relevance asks how focused the retrieved context is. An LLM extracts the sentences from the context that are needed to answer the question, and CR = extracted sentences / total sentences in the context. If 6 of 40 retrieved sentences are needed, CR = 0.15. Low context relevance means wasted tokens, higher cost and more distraction. It is a precision-like signal for the retrieval and assembly stages that needs no relevance labels. The current RAGAS library has evolved beyond the paper's three metrics. Its documentation lists context precision and context recall, faithfulness, response relevancy, noise sensitivity and others, some of which use reference answers.

How well do these LLM-computed metrics track people? The RAGAS authors built WikiEval: 50 Wikipedia pages about events since the start of 2022, chosen to post-date the model's training. Questions were generated from each page and answered, and two annotators made pairwise judgements on each quality dimension. They agreed with each other about 95% of the time on faithfulness and context relevance and about 90% on answer relevance. Measured against those human preferences, the RAGAS metrics agreed 95% of the time on faithfulness, 78% on answer relevance and 70% on context relevance. That was well above two baselines that simply asked ChatGPT for a 0-10 score (72%, 52%, 63%) or a preference (54%, 40%, 52%). The pattern is instructive. Decomposing faithfulness into claim-level checks works very well. The subtler relevance judgements are harder, and the authors note the model struggled to pick crucial sentences from long contexts. Treat faithfulness as a candidate gate metric after validating it on your data, and the relevance metrics as diagnostics.

6. Citations

Many RAG products show citations, so users can check claims. Citations need their own evaluation, because a wrong citation is worse than none: it lends false authority. Three checks, from cheapest to most demanding:

  • Validity (an assertion): every cited ID exists and was in the retrieved context. A citation to a document that wasn't retrieved is fabricated by definition.
  • Citation recall: each statement that makes a claim is fully supported by the passages it cites. This is checked with an entailment model or a judge given the statement and only its cited passages.
  • Citation precision: each citation is actually needed. A citation that supports nothing in its sentence is noise that makes checking harder.

The ALCE benchmark (Gao et al., 2023) formalised citation recall and precision and found that even strong models of the time often failed to fully support their statements with the passages they cited. Track uncited claims separately. A factual sentence with no citation in a product that promises citations is a failure even if it happens to be true.

7. Unanswerable questions and abstention

A RAG system's most embarrassing failure is a confident answer to a question its documents don't answer. The model fills the gap from training memory, which may be outdated or about a different company, or it invents. The eval set must therefore include unanswerable questions: questions on topic for the product whose answers are absent from the corpus, labelled with the expected behaviour ("the documentation doesn't cover this", or an escalation).

Score two rates. The first is false answers on unanswerable questions, the share where the system answered anyway. The second is needless refusals on answerable questions, the share where it said it didn't know although the answer was retrieved. lm15 showed why binary grading that ignores abstention rewards guessing. Here the remedy is concrete: measure both, set a target for each, and watch the trade-off when the prompt changes. A prompt that says "only answer if you are certain" will cut false answers and raise refusals, and only measuring both tells you whether the trade is worth it.

8. Component versus end-to-end: attributing failures

Component metrics and end-to-end metrics answer different questions. The bridge between them is failure attribution: for each eval question, record whether retrieval succeeded (a chunk sufficient to answer was in the final context) and whether the answer was correct. That gives a 2×2 table. Worked on 200 questions:

Answer correctAnswer wrongTotal
Sufficient chunk in context12822 (generation failure)150
No sufficient chunk12 (right without evidence)38 (retrieval failure)50
Total14060200

Reading it: end-to-end accuracy is 140/200 = 70%. Retrieval succeeds on 150/200 = 75% of questions. Given a sufficient chunk, the generator is right 128/150 = 85.3% of the time. Of the 60 failures, 38 are retrieval failures and 22 are generation failures, so retrieval is the first investment. If retrieval were perfect and generation accuracy held at 85%, end-to-end accuracy would rise to about 85%, so the generator then becomes the ceiling. The 12 "correct without evidence" answers deserve a look too. They were answered from the model's training memory, which is fragile, may be outdated and can't be cited.

Two refinements make this sharper. An oracle-context evaluation gives the generator the labelled relevant chunks directly, bypassing retrieval, to measure generation accuracy cleanly. And the "sufficient chunk" label can be split further to separate retrieval misses (never in the top k) from assembly losses (retrieved but cut by the token budget). That distinction matters because the fixes differ.

The attribution must be redone after each change, because stages interact. A retriever that returns longer chunks can raise retrieval success and lower generation accuracy at the same time. A reranker that changes order can change how well the generator uses the same chunks.

Pooling bias and incomplete judgements

Relevance labels built by pooling are incomplete: relevant chunks that no pooled system retrieved are unlabelled and counted as irrelevant. Recall is then computed against the labelled set, which overstates it for every system, and most for systems similar to the pool's. When a new retriever finds genuinely relevant chunks outside the pool, it is penalised for them. Two remedies are used in practice. Re-pool, labelling the new system's unlabelled top results before comparing. Or report metrics that are robust to unjudged items, for example by computing them only over judged results, and state the number of unjudged chunks in each system's top k. A large unjudged share is a warning that the comparison is unreliable.

Chunk-level versus document-level relevance

A document can be relevant while most of its chunks are not. Labelling at document level is cheaper but hides chunking problems: the retriever finds the right document but the wrong chunk. Labelling at chunk level ties to chunking choices, so labels must be redone when chunking changes. A middle path is to label relevant spans (character ranges) in documents, then map them to whatever chunks the current chunking produces. A chunk is relevant if it contains enough of a relevant span. That makes labels survive re-chunking, which matters because chunk size is one of the most commonly tuned RAG parameters.

Faithfulness is not enough; neither is correctness

The two failure combinations are instructive. Faithful but incorrect: the context was wrong (outdated, about a different product, a misleading near-miss), and the model repeated it honestly. The fix lies in the corpus or retrieval. Unfaithful but correct: the model ignored the context and answered from memory, happening to be right. That is still a defect, because the answer can't be cited, will go stale and fails silently when memory is wrong. A complete generation eval reports both metrics and the cross-tabulation, and treats unfaithful-but-correct as a bug, not a success.

Noise sensitivity and position effects

Generators are affected by what else is in the context. Irrelevant chunks can distract them. Near-miss chunks, on the same topic but with a different answer such as a different product's policy, can mislead them. Relevant chunks in the middle of long contexts may be used less reliably than those at the start or end. Test this deliberately: hold the relevant chunk fixed and vary the number and kind of distractors and its position, and measure faithfulness and correctness. This is the generator-side counterpart to retrieval precision, and it tells you how much precision you need. A robust generator tolerates a noisy top 10; a fragile one needs a clean top 3.

Tuning k end to end

Recall@k rises with k and precision@k falls. End-to-end accuracy usually rises at first and then flattens or falls, as more relevant material is offset by noise, cost, latency and position effects. The right k is an empirical question, and it should be answered by end-to-end accuracy, faithfulness and cost per question at several k values on the same eval set, not by retrieval metrics alone. The answer changes when the generator changes. A model with better long-context use can profit from a larger k.

Evaluating freshness and conflicting sources

Real corpora contain several versions of the same policy, documents that contradict each other, and content that changes weekly. Evaluation sets need cases for these. Include questions whose answer changed recently (does the system prefer the current version?) and questions where two documents conflict (does it notice and say so, or pick one silently?). Track reference answers against document versions so that a document update triggers a review of the eval cases that depend on it.

When the retriever is an agent

In agentic RAG the model issues its own search queries in a loop, decides when it has enough, and may call several tools. Evaluation adds new questions. Are the queries well-formed and diverse, or does it repeat itself? Does it stop at the right time? How many steps and tokens does a correct answer cost? Did it actually use the evidence it gathered? End-to-end correctness remains the anchor, and trajectory metrics (steps, queries, redundant retrievals) become first-class. The next track, on agentic systems, develops this.

The public case: the RAGAS paper (Es et al., 2023)

RAGAS set out to answer a practical problem: teams building RAG systems usually have no human-annotated reference answers, yet need to evaluate retrieval and generation quickly and repeatedly. The authors proposed three reference-free metrics, each computed by prompting an LLM, gpt-3.5-turbo-16k in their experiments. Faithfulness extracts statements from the answer and verifies each against the context, F = supported/total. Answer relevance generates n questions from the answer and averages their embedding cosine similarity to the real question, using OpenAI's text-embedding-ada-002. Context relevance extracts the sentences needed to answer and divides by the context's sentence count.

To validate them, they built WikiEval from 50 Wikipedia pages on events from 2022 onward, after the model's training cutoff. Two annotators made pairwise judgements, with about 95% inter-annotator agreement on faithfulness and context relevance and about 90% on answer relevance. The RAGAS metrics agreed with the humans' preferences 95%, 78% and 70% of the time on the three dimensions. Direct "score 0-10" prompting reached 72%, 52% and 63%, and a direct "which is better" prompt 54%, 40% and 52%. The headline lesson is methodological. Decomposing a judgement into narrow, checkable steps (extract claims, verify each) produced a far more reliable judge than asking for an overall score, the same principle ev4 drew for rubrics. The library has since grown many more metrics and integrations. The paper's numbers apply to its specific prompts, model and dataset, and any team using such metrics should re-validate them on its own data.

Failure modes in production

Index drift. Documents are added, updated and deleted daily. Reference answers go stale and relevance labels point at chunks that no longer exist. Symptom: correctness falls with no system change. Fix: version the corpus snapshot used by each eval run, link eval cases to the documents they depend on, and re-label on change.

Silent re-chunking. A pipeline change alters chunk boundaries, and chunk-level relevance labels silently stop matching. Recall drops to near zero for some questions because the labelled chunk IDs no longer exist. Fix: span-level labels mapped to chunks at eval time, and an assertion that every labelled chunk exists.

Permission leaks. A metadata change breaks access filtering, and the assistant retrieves documents the user shouldn't see. No quality metric notices. Fix: an always-on assertion in CI and production that no retrieved chunk violates the asker's permissions, with test users at different access levels in the eval set.

The memory answer. After a model upgrade, faithfulness falls while correctness holds. The new model answers more from its training memory. It looks fine until a policy changes and the model keeps giving the old answer. Fix: gate on faithfulness as well as correctness, and include questions whose answers changed recently.

What to track

For each release and on a daily production sample, track: recall@k and nDCG@k at the k in use, per slice; the attribution 2×2 with generation accuracy given good context; faithfulness and the unsupported-claim rate; citation validity and recall; false-answer and needless-refusal rates on unanswerable questions; context tokens and cost per question; and the corpus snapshot and labels version used.

  2. Check on the body's query: ranked = ["D2","D5","D7","D1","D4"], rel = {"D7":3, "D2":2, "D9":2, "D4":1}. Expect recall@5 0.75, precision@5 0.6, nDCG@5 0.683 (linear) and 0.636 (exponential).
  3. Simulate a reranker by moving D7 to the front, and print every metric again. Which ones change?
  4. Build a toy corpus: 30 questions, each with 1-3 relevant chunks among 200 chunk IDs. Create a "retriever" that places each relevant chunk at a random rank drawn from a geometric distribution (with random.Random(1)), filling the rest with random irrelevant IDs. What to look for. The reranker changes MRR at the strict level and nDCG, but not recall or precision: order-aware metrics see what set metrics can't. In step 4, hit rate climbs faster than recall with k on questions with several relevant chunks. In step 5, the attribution should show most wrong answers among questions without a good chunk, and generation accuracy given good context near 0.85. Try raising the 0.2 "memory" rate. The end-to-end number improves while faithfulness, which you aren't measuring here, would get worse.

Diagnose: the RAG upgrade that didn't help

Your support-documentation assistant shows the top 4 retrieved chunks to the model. A new embedding model raised recall@20 on your labelled set from 0.71 to 0.83, and the team expected a big quality gain. End-to-end expert-graded accuracy went from 74% to 75%, and faithfulness from 0.91 to 0.86. Users report more answers that "mix up products". What do you investigate, and what do you expect to find?

Answer

Measure at the right k. The model sees 4 chunks, so recall@20 is the wrong number. Compute recall@4, hit rate@4 and nDCG@4 for both embedding models. It's common for a new model to improve deep recall while changing little, or even worsening, the top few positions.

Look at what's in the top 4. "Mixing up products" and falling faithfulness suggest near-miss chunks: passages about a sibling product with similar wording, which the new embeddings rank highly because they're semantically close. Measure precision@4 and the share of top-4 chunks from the wrong product, using product metadata. If that rose, the fix is a metadata filter on product, or a reranker that reads query and chunk together and distinguishes products, rather than reverting the embeddings.

Attribute failures. Build the 2×2 table for both versions: sufficient chunk in the top 4 against correct answer. If the share with a sufficient chunk barely moved, the recall@20 gain never reached the model. If generation accuracy given good context fell, the new distractors are confusing the generator. Check by holding the relevant chunk fixed and adding sibling-product distractors (a noise-sensitivity test).

Check faithfulness details. Read unsupported claims. Are they claims about the other product taken from a retrieved near-miss? Then "faithfulness" may be counting them as supported, because they are in the context, and the real problem is that the context contains the wrong product. That calls for a correctness check against the reference and a product-consistency criterion.

Decide. Keep the better retriever only with a product filter or reranker added. Re-evaluate at k = 4 with end-to-end accuracy, faithfulness, wrong-product rate and cost. From now on, gate retrieval changes on metrics at the k in use plus the attribution table, not on deep recall alone.
