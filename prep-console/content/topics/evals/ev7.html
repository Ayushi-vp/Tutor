A team is choosing between two prompts for their assistant. On a 500-item eval, prompt B scores 83.8% and prompt A scores 80.0%. B wins by 3.8 points, which feels decisive, and they ship B. A month later, a teammate re-runs the comparison with a fresh sample of 500 items from the same traffic and gets A ahead by 0.6 points. Another teammate re-runs the original items at a different random seed and gets B ahead by 1.5. Which result is right? All of them are. Each is one draw from a noisy measurement, and nobody wrote down how noisy.

This happens constantly in LLM teams. A prompt tweak "improves" a metric by 1.5 points on 300 items and is merged. A model upgrade "regresses" one slice by 4 points on 40 items and a release is blocked. A leaderboard ranks two models 0.8 points apart as if the order meant something. Each of these is a statistical claim made without statistics. Some of the claims are right by luck, and many are wrong. The cost is real: weeks of work chasing noise, good changes rejected, bad ones accepted.

The prerequisites gave the core tools. math6 built hypothesis tests and confidence intervals. math7 covered sample sizes and multiple comparisons for A/B tests. lm14 and rs8 introduced standard errors for eval scores and the idea of pairing. ev2 sized a golden set from a confidence interval. This lesson assembles them into the statistical practice an eval result needs.

It separates the two sources of noise, which questions were sampled and which answers the model sampled, and shows what reduces each. It covers clustered standard errors for questions that come in groups. It works the bootstrap by hand on a tiny example and then on the paired comparison above. It shows why pairing makes comparisons several times more efficient, and works the sample size needed to detect a 2-point difference, which surprises most people. It covers resampling at temperature and the floor that resampling can't get under, seeds and A/A runs for measuring an eval's own noise, and the multiple-comparisons trap in slice-level gates. The case is Anthropic's 2024 paper and post on adding error bars to evals.

Imagine judging two archers by having each shoot at a target. You could give each archer different arrows and different targets in different winds. Or you could have them shoot side by side, with the same arrows, at the same targets, in the same wind. In the first setup, a difference in scores mixes skill with luck of conditions. In the second, conditions are shared, so the difference reflects skill much more cleanly. That is pairing, and it is the single most valuable idea in eval statistics. Two systems evaluated on the same items face the same easy and hard questions, so per-item differences remove the shared difficulty.

The archery picture also shows the two sources of noise. One is which targets were set up: some are easier, and a different set of targets would give different scores. That is question variance. The other is the wobble in each shot: the same archer at the same target doesn't hit the same spot twice. That is sampling variance, the model's randomness. More shots at the same targets average out the wobble but can't change which targets you chose. Only more targets fix that.

The analogy breaks in one way that matters. Archers' shots are independent, but eval questions often aren't. Five questions about the same document, or ten variants of one template, behave like one big target split into five, and they rise and fall together. Treating them as independent overstates how much evidence you have. That is the problem clustered standard errors solve.

The three tools

Almost every eval statistics question is answered by one of three tools. The first is the standard error, √(variance/n), from the central limit theorem: the typical size of the noise in an average, which gives the familiar estimate ± 1.96 SE interval. The second is the bootstrap: when there's no convenient formula, resample your data many times, recompute the statistic each time, and read the noise off the spread. The third is the power calculation: before running an eval, work out how many items you need to detect the difference you care about. The figure shows why the third matters, using the paired comparison from the opening story.

There is also a habit of mind that matters more than any formula: decide what difference you care about before you look. If a cheaper model is acceptable as long as it is no more than 2 points worse, then the eval's job is to tell you whether the loss is under 2 points, and its size should be chosen so that it can. If you only look at results and then decide what counts as meaningful, every noisy wiggle invites a story. Writing the decision rule down first, the minimum effect, the comparison, the slices that gate, is what turns an eval run from an exploration into evidence.

Keep this picture in mind for the rest of the lesson: the estimate is a point, the evidence is an interval, and the design of the eval decides how wide that interval is.

1. Two sources of noise, made precise

Suppose an eval has n questions, and the system has a true probability pi of passing question i. Some questions are easy (pi near 1), some hard (near 0), some in between. A run of the eval draws one answer per question, and the score is the average pass. Two things vary from one hypothetical eval to another.

Question variance. The n questions are a sample from a larger population of possible questions: all the support tickets of this kind, all the maths problems at this level. A different sample would contain different pi values. The spread of the pi across questions, σ²between, drives this part.

Sampling variance. On a given question, the model passes with probability pi, so a single answer is a coin flip with that bias. Its variance is pi(1 − pi), and averaging over questions gives σ²within.

If each question gets K independent answers and the per-question score is their average, the variance of the overall score is approximately

Var(score) ≈ (σ²between + σ²within / K) / n

For a pass/fail eval with one answer per question, the two terms add up to p̄(1 − p̄), the familiar binomial variance, which is why the simple formula √(p(1 − p)/n) from lm14 and ev2 works for K = 1. The decomposition matters because the remedies differ: more answers per question shrink only the second term; more questions shrink both. We will come back to this with numbers in section 7.

A third source, grader noise, adds variance when a judge or human grades outputs inconsistently. If the judge is run at temperature above zero, or humans disagree, the same output can be graded differently, and that adds to the within-question term. It is reduced by the same means: grade several times and average, or use a more consistent grader (ev4, ev5).

2. Standard errors and the central limit theorem

The central limit theorem (CLT) says that the average of many independent observations is approximately normally distributed around the true mean, with standard deviation equal to the observations' standard deviation divided by √n. That standard deviation of the average is the standard error (SE). It justifies the 95% interval estimate ± 1.96 × SE. Anthropic's statistics guidance for evals starts exactly here: report the standard error of the mean with every eval score.

For a pass rate, SE = √(p(1 − p)/n). For a continuous score (a judge's probability, a per-question average over K samples, a BLEU score), SE = s/√n, where s is the sample standard deviation of the per-question scores. ev2 computed the 78%-on-200 case (SE = 2.93 points). Here are a few more, to build intuition for how precise typical evals are:

The CLT approximation needs a reasonable number of independent observations and works poorly for pass rates very near 0 or 1 on small sets. Use the Wilson interval there (ev2), or the bootstrap.

3. Clustered questions

The CLT needs independent observations, and eval questions frequently aren't independent. Reading-comprehension evals ask several questions about each passage. Synthetic evals generate many variants of each template. Agent evals run several tasks in each repository. Multi-turn evals score every turn of a conversation. Within such a cluster, results are correlated. If the system misreads a passage, it tends to get all of that passage's questions wrong.

Correlation within clusters means fewer independent pieces of evidence than items. The design effect quantifies it: for clusters of size m with within-cluster correlation ρ, the variance of the mean is inflated by about 1 + (m − 1)ρ. With 5 questions per passage and ρ = 0.3, the design effect is 1 + 4 × 0.3 = 2.2. Variance is 2.2 times what the naive formula says, and the standard error is √2.2 ≈ 1.48 times larger. An eval of 500 questions on 100 passages has the precision of about 500/2.2 ≈ 230 independent questions.

The fix is a clustered standard error: treat the cluster as the unit. One simple way is to compute each cluster's total score and use the variance of cluster totals. Another, often easier, is to bootstrap whole clusters (section 4). Anthropic reported that on popular evals, clustered standard errors could be more than three times as large as naive ones. Some published differences between models disappear when the clustering is accounted for. The practical rule: if your eval has any grouping structure, find it, record the cluster ID with each item, and use it in the analysis.

4. The bootstrap, by hand

Formulas exist for simple statistics. For anything more complex, such as a paired difference with clustering, a median, a judge-corrected pass rate or a ratio, the bootstrap (Efron, 1979) is the general tool. The idea is to treat your sample as a stand-in for the population, and simulate drawing new samples from it.

The algorithm: (1) from your n items, draw n items with replacement, so some are picked twice and some not at all; (2) compute the statistic on this resample; (3) repeat B times (typically 2,000-10,000); (4) the standard deviation of the B statistics estimates the standard error, and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles give a 95% percentile interval.

A tiny example makes it concrete. Eight items, each with a paired difference between system B and A: +1 if only B passed, −1 if only A passed, 0 if they agreed.

5. Worked: a paired comparison, three ways

Now the opening story with full numbers. Prompts A and B were run on the same 500 items. Cross-tabulating the results gives 50 items where only B passed, 31 where only A passed, and 419 where both passed or both failed. A scored 80.0% and B 83.8%.

Way 1: the paired standard error. Define the per-item difference d = (B passed) − (A passed): +1 on 50 items, −1 on 31, 0 on 419. The mean difference is d̄ = (50 − 31)/500 = 19/500 = 0.038. The mean of d² is (50 + 31)/500 = 0.162, so the variance of d is 0.162 − 0.038² = 0.1606. The standard error of the mean difference is √(0.1606/500) = 0.0179, or 1.79 points. The 95% interval is 3.8 ± 1.96 × 1.79 = 3.8 ± 3.5, from 0.3 to 7.3 points. It excludes zero.

Way 2: McNemar's test (math6), which looks only at the 81 discordant items. If the prompts were equally good, each discordant item would be equally likely to favour either, so B's share would be about 50%. B won 50 of 81. The test statistic is z = (50 − 31)/√(50 + 31) = 19/9 = 2.11, a two-sided p of about 0.035. This is the same conclusion as way 1, as it should be.

Way 3: the bootstrap. Resample the 500 item-pairs with replacement 10,000 times, keeping each item's A and B results together, and recompute the mean difference each time. In a run of this, the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles were +0.4 and +7.4 points, the bootstrap standard error was 1.78 points, and 1.7% of resamples were at or below zero. This is almost identical to way 1, because this statistic is simple. The bootstrap earns its keep when it isn't: with clustering (resample clusters), with several samples per item (resample items, carrying all their samples), or with a judge-corrected score (resample both the eval items and the judge's calibration set).

What if you ignored the pairing? Treat the scores as two independent samples of 500. The standard error of the difference is √(0.80 × 0.20/500 + 0.838 × 0.162/500) = √(0.000320 + 0.000272) = 0.0243, or 2.43 points. The interval is 3.8 ± 4.8, from −1.0 to 8.6, which includes zero. Same data, different conclusion. The unpaired analysis throws away the fact that 419 of 500 items behaved identically under both prompts.

The verdict, stated properly: B is probably better, by somewhere between a fraction of a point and about 7 points, with p ≈ 0.035. That is enough to prefer B if the switch is cheap. It is not enough to claim a "3.8-point improvement" in a slide without the interval. And the teammates' re-runs in the opening story, A ahead by 0.6 on fresh items and B ahead by 1.5 at a new seed, are both comfortably inside what this interval allows.

6. Why pairing works, and how many items you need

Pairing works because systems' results are correlated across items. Easy questions are easy for both, hard ones hard for both. The variance of a difference is Var(A) + Var(B) − 2 Cov(A, B). The covariance term, which the unpaired analysis assumes is zero, removes the shared difficulty. Anthropic reported that frontier models' per-question results had correlations between 0.3 and 0.7. In the worked example, the correlation between A's and B's pass indicators is about 0.46. That is why the paired SE (1.79) is so much smaller than the unpaired one (2.43). Variants of the same system, such as two prompts on one model, are often even more strongly correlated.

The payoff shows up most clearly in sample size. How many items do you need to detect a 2-point improvement around 80% accuracy, with 80% power at α = 0.05 (two-sided)?

Unpaired (math7's formula): n per system ≈ (zα/2 + zβ)² × [p1(1 − p1) + p2(1 − p2)] / δ² = (1.96 + 0.84)² × (0.80 × 0.20 + 0.82 × 0.18)/0.02² = 7.84 × 0.3076/0.0004 ≈ 6,000 items.

Paired: what matters is the share of items on which the systems disagree, pd. With δ the true difference, a standard approximation for McNemar's test is n ≈ (zα/2√pd + zβ√(pd − δ²))² / δ².

Worked for pd = 0.10: √0.10 = 0.316 and √(0.10 − 0.0004) = 0.316, so (1.96 × 0.316 + 0.84 × 0.316)² / 0.0004 = (0.620 + 0.265)²/0.0004 = 0.783/0.0004 ≈ 1,960.

Three lessons follow. First, a 2-point difference is expensive to detect even with pairing. Most product evals of a few hundred items cannot see it at all, and that is fine early on, when changes are large (ev1). Second, the smaller the change between systems, the lower the discordance, so pairing helps most exactly when you most need it: comparing two prompts on one model often gives pd around 5-10%. Third, compute the minimum detectable effect before running an eval. If your 400-item eval can only detect 5-point differences, don't present 2-point differences from it as findings.

7. Temperature, resampling and the variance floor

If the product samples at temperature above zero, a single answer per question adds the model's randomness to the score. Running K answers per question and averaging them shrinks that part. But only that part, as section 1's formula says. Worked, for 200 questions with an overall pass rate of 78%:

The total single-answer variance per question is p̄(1 − p̄) = 0.78 × 0.22 = 0.1716. Suppose, from a pilot with several samples per question, that the between-question variance of the true pass probabilities is σ²between = 0.12. Then the within-question part is 0.1716 − 0.12 = 0.0516. The standard error of the overall score is √((0.12 + 0.0516/K)/200):

K = 1 gives 2.93 points, K = 3 gives 2.62, K = 5 gives 2.55, K = 10 gives 2.50, and infinitely many samples give 2.45. Five samples per question cost five times as much and cut the standard error by 13%. Doubling the questions instead would cut it by 29%. Resampling is still worthwhile. It stabilises per-question verdicts, which matters for reading individual failures and for regression lists. It also makes paired comparisons cleaner, because random flips on individual items inflate the discordance count. But it is not a substitute for questions.

Two refinements reduce within-question noise more cheaply. For multiple-choice or short-answer evals without chain-of-thought, Miller's paper recommends using the model's probability of the correct answer, from next-token probabilities where the API exposes them, as a continuous per-question score. This removes the coin flip entirely. For chain-of-thought evals, where the answer comes after reasoning, resampling K answers and averaging per question is the practical route.

8. Seeds, non-determinism and A/A runs

A seed initialises a random-number generator so that random choices can be reproduced: which items were sampled, the shuffle order, the order of answers shown to a judge, and the sampling itself where the API supports a seed parameter. Record every seed with the eval run. It makes runs reproducible and lets you separate "the system changed" from "the dice changed".

Seeds don't make hosted LLMs perfectly deterministic. Many serving stacks batch requests together, and floating-point operations on GPUs can produce tiny differences depending on batch composition. Those differences can flip a near-tied token choice and change the rest of the output, even at temperature 0. Providers document seed parameters as giving best-effort, not guaranteed, reproducibility. So the noise of an eval has to be measured, not assumed.

The tool is the A/A run. Evaluate the same system configuration twice, or more, and compare the runs exactly as you would compare two different systems. Any difference is noise from sampling, serving non-determinism and grader variability. Repeating this, say 10 times, gives the distribution of differences under no change. If A/A differences on your release suite range up to ±1.5 points, a gate that blocks on a 1-point drop will block releases at random. Set thresholds above the A/A noise, or tighten the noise first (more items, more samples per item, a more consistent judge). ae7 covered flaky gates from the release side. The A/A run is how you measure the flakiness.

9. Many slices, many chances to be fooled

Eval reports break results down by slice (ev2), and release gates often check each one. The trap is multiple comparisons (math7). If you test 10 independent slices at α = 0.05, the chance that at least one shows a "significant" regression when nothing changed is 1 − 0.9510 = 40%. With 20 slices it is 64%.

The remedies, in order of usefulness:

  • Pre-declare which few slices are gates (the costly ones) and which are monitored. A monitored slice that looks worse triggers investigation, not a block.
  • Correct across the gate slices. Bonferroni divides α by the number of tests. The Holm step-down procedure controls the same family-wise error rate with more power: sort the p-values, compare the smallest with α/m, the next with α/(m − 1), and so on, stopping at the first that fails.
  • Require a minimum effect as well as significance. A slice must drop by more than its margin (set from product judgement and A/A noise) and do so significantly.
  • Size slices for their job. A 40-item slice can't detect anything smaller than a large regression. If the slice is critical, make it bigger. If it isn't, don't gate on it.

The same logic applies to trying many prompt variants and keeping the best. With 20 variants, the best one's advantage on the dev set is inflated by selection, the winner's curse. Confirm the winner on a held-out set (ev2) before believing its margin.

Which interval for which statistic

For a single pass rate, use Wilson (ev2). For a mean of continuous per-item scores, CLT intervals are fine at n in the hundreds. For a paired difference of pass rates, use the paired SE or McNemar, with the bootstrap as a check. For clustered data, use clustered SEs or a cluster bootstrap. For medians, percentiles, ratios and corrected scores, use the bootstrap. For very small samples or extreme rates, use exact methods (Clopper-Pearson, exact McNemar) or a Bayesian interval. The bootstrap's percentile interval can be biased when the statistic's distribution is skewed. The bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) variant fixes much of that, and most statistics libraries provide it.

Hierarchical models for many slices

When there are many small slices, each slice's raw pass rate is noisy, and the most extreme slices are mostly extreme by chance. A hierarchical (multilevel) model treats slice rates as drawn from a common distribution and shrinks each slice's estimate toward the overall mean in proportion to its noise. Small slices are shrunk a lot, large ones little. The result ranks slices more reliably and reduces false alarms, at the cost of a modelling assumption. It is the statistically principled version of "don't panic about the 12-item slice that dropped 20 points".

Bayesian reporting

Instead of a confidence interval and p-value, a Bayesian analysis reports a posterior distribution for the difference: "probability that B is better than A: 98%; probability the improvement exceeds 2 points: 70%". With a flat Beta prior on each item's pass probability, this is easy to compute. It answers the question decision-makers actually ask. It has the same dependence on correct units (items, clusters) and the same vulnerability to optional stopping if misused. For release decisions the two approaches usually agree. What matters more is pre-declaring the decision rule.

Non-inferiority, not "no significant difference"

"B is not significantly worse than A" is guaranteed by a small or noisy eval: with 50 items, almost nothing is significant. A non-inferiority test (math6, ae7) flips the burden. B passes only if the interval for B − A lies entirely above −margin, for example above −2 points. A noisy eval then fails the test instead of passing it, which is the right incentive. Cheaper-model swaps, refactors and infrastructure changes should be gated this way.

Variance reduction beyond pairing

Online experiments use CUPED (Controlled-experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data), adjusting each unit's outcome by a pre-experiment covariate (math7). The offline analogue is to adjust each item's difference by a covariate that predicts difficulty, such as the item's pass rate under previous versions or its length. Stratifying the comparison by slice and combining within-slice differences also removes between-slice variance. These can cut required sample sizes noticeably when difficulty varies a lot. The cleanest option, where available, remains continuous per-item scores such as probability of the correct answer instead of a single pass/fail draw.

Peeking and adaptive evaluation

Teams often watch an eval run's partial results and stop when they look good. That inflates false positives just as peeking at an A/B test does (math7): a fixed-sample interval read repeatedly is no longer a 95% interval. If you must decide early, use a sequential design with planned looks, or always-valid intervals. Similarly, iterating on a prompt until the dev-set number crosses a threshold makes that number optimistic. That is the eval-overfitting problem from ev1 in statistical form, and it is why a held-out set is consulted only at the end.

Reporting effect sizes, not just significance

A p-value says how surprising a difference would be if nothing had changed; it says nothing about whether the difference matters. With 50,000 items, a 0.3-point difference can be highly significant and practically irrelevant; with 200 items, a 6-point difference can fail to reach significance and still be the most important thing in the report. Always lead with the estimated difference and its interval, in the metric's own units, and let the decision rule, not the p-value, say whether that size matters.

When the standard formulas mislead

Agent evals with few, long tasks (say 40 tasks in 10 environments) have small, clustered samples and heavy per-task variance. Intervals from naive formulas will be far too narrow. Report per-task results, cluster by environment, use several trials per task, and be explicit that only large differences can be resolved. Similarly, evals graded by a judge carry the judge's error. If you report a judge-corrected rate (ev4), its interval must include the uncertainty in the judge's sensitivity and specificity, which the bootstrap handles by resampling the calibration set too.

The public case: "Adding Error Bars to Evals" (Miller, Anthropic, November 2024)

Evan Miller's paper, published with an accompanying Anthropic research post titled "A statistical approach to model evaluations", argued that LLM evaluation should adopt the statistical practice of experimental science. Treat questions as samples from a population of possible questions, and report results so as to minimise noise and maximise information. Its five recommendations map onto this lesson. First, report the standard error of the mean from the central limit theorem with every score, with 1.96 × SE intervals. Second, cluster standard errors on the unit of randomisation when questions come in groups; the post notes clustered SEs on popular evals "can be over three times as large as naive standard errors". Third, reduce within-question variance, by resampling answers and averaging per question for chain-of-thought evals and by using next-token probabilities for evals without chain-of-thought. Fourth, analyse paired differences when comparing models, because frontier models tend to get the same questions right and wrong, with per-question correlations "between 0.3 and 0.7". Fifth, use power analysis to decide how many questions are needed to detect a hypothesised difference, such as whether one model outperforms another by 3 percentage points.

The significance of the paper is less in any new technique, since all of these are standard statistics, than in a frontier lab publicly stating that model comparisons without error bars are not informative. It gave a concrete reporting standard for both benchmark authors and product teams. Its recommendations apply directly to product evals: the same clustering, pairing and power arguments decide whether a release gate is meaningful.

Failure modes in production

The flapping gate. A regression gate set at "any drop over 1 point" on a 300-item suite blocks every third release. Engineers learn to re-run until green. Symptom: re-runs flip the result. Fix: measure A/A noise, set thresholds above it, or enlarge the suite and use paired comparisons.

The 40-item slice panic. A slice of 40 items drops from 90% to 80%, four items, and a release is blocked for a day of investigation. The slice's interval was ±9 points. Fix: report slice intervals, gate only on pre-declared slices sized for their job, and shrink small slices' estimates or treat them as monitors.

Clustered data, naive errors. A synthetic eval of 2,000 items generated from 100 templates showed a "significant" 1.5-point gain. With template-clustered errors the interval was three times wider and included zero. Fix: record cluster IDs at generation time and use them.

The winner's curse in prompt search. Thirty prompt variants were tried on the dev set, and the best was 3 points above baseline. On the held-out set it was 0.5 points above. Fix: always confirm a selected winner on data not used for selection.

What to put in every eval report

The score with a 95% interval and n. The cluster structure and whether intervals account for it. The number of samples per item and the temperature. For comparisons, the paired difference with its interval, the discordant counts, and the test used. The A/A noise level for this suite. The minimum detectable effect at this n. Seeds, versions of the system, set, judge and harness, and which slices are gates and how they were corrected.

Estimate and decide: is the new prompt worth shipping?

Your team tested a new system prompt on the 600-item release suite, with 3 samples per item at temperature 0.7, averaging the samples per item. Old prompt: 76.4%. New prompt: 78.9%. The suite has 120 source documents with 5 questions each. Two A/A runs of the old prompt last month gave 76.4% and 77.5%. The product manager wants to ship and announce "a 2.5-point quality improvement". What analysis do you do, what would you expect, and what do you tell the PM?

Answer

Noise level. The A/A runs differ by 1.1 points, so differences of around 1 point arise from noise alone at this suite size and K = 3. Two A/A runs is a thin sample of the noise. Run 5-10 more if possible, to see its range.

Paired analysis. Compute per-item differences (new minus old, each an average of 3 samples, so values in steps of 1/3) and their mean, 2.5 points. With item-level averages the per-item differences are continuous, so use the paired SE, sd/√600.

Clustering. Questions come 5 per document. Compute the SE with documents as the unit: average the per-item differences within each document, then take the SE over 120 document means. Or bootstrap documents. If within-document correlation of differences is around 0.3, the design effect is 1 + 4 × 0.3 = 2.2, and the SE grows by about 1.5×.

Expected result. Suppose the naive paired SE comes out around 0.9 points. The clustered SE is then around 1.3, giving a 95% interval of roughly 2.5 ± 2.6, from about −0.1 to +5.1 points. That is borderline: probably an improvement, possibly zero, possibly as large as 5.

What to tell the PM. "The new prompt is likely somewhat better. Our best estimate is 2.5 points, but the evidence is consistent with anything from no change to about 5 points. It doesn't regress any gate slice, so shipping is reasonable. Announcing a specific '2.5-point improvement' isn't supported. If we want a firm number, we'll confirm with an A/B test on resolution rate, or extend the eval to about 1,500 items." Also check per-slice results with Holm correction across the gate slices before shipping.
