A team building a writing assistant fine-tunes a new model. On their offline suite it is better by every measure: rubric scores up 5 points, judge win rate 62%, no regressions on safety. They launch an A/B test to half their users for two weeks. The primary metric, the share of drafts users actually send, doesn't move. The thumbs-up rate goes up. Regenerations go up too, and users in the new arm write slightly longer prompts. Average session length rises, which one product manager reads as engagement and another as struggle. Which number is the truth?

Online evaluation is where an LLM feature meets the only judges that ultimately matter: the people using it, doing their actual work, with their actual inputs. It is the top of the eval pyramid (ev1). It is the slowest and riskiest layer, and also the most faithful, the one every offline suite is ultimately trying to predict. It is also where the measurement gets hardest, for reasons particular to LLM features. Users rarely tell you directly whether an answer was good. The behavioural signals they leave behind, such as editing, regenerating, copying, giving up or escalating, are each ambiguous. Explicit ratings come from a small, unrepresentative minority and reward agreeableness. And optimising for any one of these signals can teach a model the wrong lesson.

That last point became public in April 2025, when OpenAI rolled back an update to GPT-4o in ChatGPT. The company's own postmortem said a new reward signal based on thumbs-up and thumbs-down feedback had weakened the training signal that kept sycophancy in check. The model had become noticeably flattering and agreeable. Offline evaluations looked good, and early A/B tests suggested users liked it. Expert testers had said it felt off.

The prerequisites built the statistical machinery of A/B testing (ml16, math7) and the release ladder of shadow, canary and ramp (ae7). This lesson applies them to LLM features. It covers choosing the randomisation unit and the metrics, reading implicit feedback without being fooled by it, and working out how many users it takes to detect a 2-point improvement, with and without clustering. It covers the checks that tell you an experiment is broken, interleaving as a far more sensitive alternative for ranking components, guardrails with automatic rollback, and a structured way to diagnose the common case where offline and online disagree.

A restaurant developing a new dish has two kinds of evidence. A tasting panel of chefs scores it on a rubric: seasoning, texture, presentation. That's the offline eval. It's fast, controlled and repeatable, and it can only score what the rubric names, on the samples the kitchen prepared. Then the dish goes on the menu for half the tables. Do diners order it again? Do they leave it half-eaten? Do they mention it in reviews? Do they come back? That's the online test. It measures what matters, slowly, noisily, through signals diners leave without being asked, and it can surprise you. A dish the panel loved may be too slow to prepare at dinner rush, or too rich for a weekday lunch.

The analogy carries one more lesson. If the restaurant starts paying waiters by how many diners say "lovely!" when asked, waiters will learn to ask leading questions and to visit the friendliest tables. The measure gets better and the food doesn't. Any online signal that becomes a target, thumbs-up rate included, can be optimised in ways that don't improve the product.

The shape of online evaluation

ae7 covered the release mechanics: shadow, canary, ramp and rollback. This lesson concentrates on the measurement inside them. It covers which users go where (the randomisation unit), and what to measure: a primary metric the test is sized for, guardrail metrics that must not get worse, and diagnostic metrics that explain movements. It covers how to read the behavioural signals users leave (implicit feedback), how many users you need, how to tell whether the experiment itself is healthy, and what to do when the online result contradicts the offline one.

It is worth being clear about what online evaluation is not. It is not monitoring, although the two share dashboards. Monitoring (ae3) watches a single deployed version over time for drift and incidents. Online evaluation compares versions against each other under controlled assignment, so that a difference can be attributed to the change. Nor is it a replacement for offline evaluation. Most changes never need an A/B test, and no team has the traffic to test everything. Online tests are reserved for decisions where the outcome genuinely matters and offline evidence cannot settle it, and the offline suite is what keeps the queue of candidates short and safe.

One principle runs through all of it: declare what you will measure and how you will decide before you look at the data. Online data is rich enough to support almost any story after the fact. Pre-registration of the primary metric, guardrail margins, sample size and duration is what keeps an A/B test honest. math7 explained the statistics behind that, and here it becomes a practical habit.

1. What online evaluation adds

Offline evaluation, however good, has blind spots that only real use reveals. The inputs are different. Users ask about new products, paste whole documents, write in languages your suite barely covers, and attempt tasks nobody designed for. The context is different. Latency, streaming, formatting, the surrounding interface and the user's time pressure all shape whether an answer is useful. A better answer that takes 9 seconds may be worse than a decent one in 2. Behaviour changes in response. Users adapt to a new model, learning what it does well and asking differently, and those adaptations change the outcome. The outcome is what you actually want. Resolved problems, sent drafts, accepted code and retained users are what the feature is for. Offline metrics are chosen because they are believed to predict these, and only online measurement tests that belief.

So the roles are complementary. Offline evals are fast and cheap, and they protect users from obvious regressions: they are the gate. Online tests are slow and expensive, and they measure the real outcome: they are the verdict, for changes significant enough to need one. Prompt tweaks that fix a narrow failure usually ship on offline evidence plus monitoring. Model swaps, major prompt rewrites, retrieval changes and anything touching a product-level metric deserve an online test.

2. Randomisation units for LLM features

The randomisation unit is what gets assigned to a variant: a request, a session, a user, an account, an organisation. For LLM features the right unit is almost always the user, or larger, for three reasons.

Conversations span requests. If each message is randomised independently, one conversation mixes both models. The second model inherits context, style and sometimes errors from the first, and neither variant's effect can be measured cleanly. State carries across requests. Memory, personalisation, cached results and the user's learned expectations all persist, so a user who sees both variants is contaminated by both. The outcomes you care about are user-level. Retention, satisfaction and weekly task completion can only be attributed if each user experienced one variant.

If users collaborate on shared artefacts, such as a shared document edited with AI help or a support ticket handled by several agents, one user's variant affects another's experience. That is called interference, and it biases user-level comparisons. Randomising by team or organisation fixes it at the cost of far fewer units, so the test needs more time or larger effects to reach the same power.

The analysis must match the unit. If you randomise by user but compute metrics per conversation, conversations from the same user are correlated. Treating them as independent understates the standard error, the clustering problem from ev7. Either aggregate to one value per user (for example, the share of a user's conversations that were resolved) or use clustered standard errors.

3. Choosing metrics: primary, guardrail, diagnostic

A well-designed test declares three kinds of metric in advance.

The primary metric is the one the test is designed and sized to move. It should be tied to the feature's purpose and be sensitive enough to move within the test's duration. For a support assistant, that might be resolution without human escalation. For a drafting tool, the share of drafts sent with light or no edits. For a code assistant, the acceptance rate of suggestions. The Copilot study by Ziegler et al. (2022) found that the acceptance rate of shown suggestions was the best predictor of developers' perceived productivity, better than more elaborate measures of whether accepted code persisted. That makes acceptance rate a reasonable primary metric for that product. There should be one primary metric. With several, you face multiple comparisons and the temptation to pick whichever moved.

Guardrail metrics must not get worse beyond a declared margin, even if the primary metric improves. For LLM features the essential ones are latency (p50 and p95 time to first token and to completion), cost per conversation, safety and policy-violation flag rates, complaint and report rates, error and timeout rates, customer satisfaction scores (CSAT, usually a post-conversation survey) where they exist, and sometimes refusal rate and escalation rate. Each guardrail is a non-inferiority test (math6). The margin says how much worse is acceptable: "p95 latency may not rise by more than 300 ms".

Diagnostic metrics explain why the primary and guardrails moved. Examples are response length, regeneration rate, edit distance, follow-up rephrase rate, topic mix, retrieval hit rate and tool-call failure rate. They aren't decision criteria, and moving them isn't a goal. They are the instruments you read when the result is surprising.

4. Implicit feedback: rich, cheap and biased

Users rarely say whether an answer was good, but they leave traces. Implicit feedback is inferred from behaviour:

  • Regenerations and retries: the user asked for another answer, a fairly direct sign the first wasn't good enough. It can also mean curiosity, or wanting variety in creative tasks.
  • Edits: how much the user changed a generated draft before using it, measured as an edit distance normalised by length. Unchanged is strong approval. Heavy rewriting suggests the draft was a starting point at best.
  • Copy and accept events: the user took the output for use elsewhere.
  • Follow-up rephrases: the next message asks the same thing differently, a sign the answer missed. Detecting this needs a classifier or embedding similarity between consecutive messages.
  • Abandonment: the user left without completing the task.
  • Escalation: the user asked for a human, or clicked "contact support".
  • Explicit ratings: thumbs up or down, star ratings, written feedback.

Each signal is ambiguous alone. A long conversation can mean deep engagement or a struggle to get a right answer. Fewer edits can mean better drafts or shorter ones. Fewer escalations can mean better resolution, or a model that discourages escalation. Read them together, and validate them. Take a sample of conversations with each signal pattern, have reviewers label quality blind (ev5), and check that the signal moves with the labels.

Explicit ratings carry particular biases. Only a small share of users click thumbs at all, and they aren't a random sample: strong reactions are over-represented. Ratings are immediate, so they reflect how an answer feels in the moment, not whether following it worked out. And people tend to approve of answers that agree with them, flatter them or sound confident. A system optimised against thumbs-up learns all of these. That is the mechanism behind the sycophancy case in the prod section: user approval rewarded agreeableness, and an extra reward signal built from it pushed the model further than anyone intended.

The general lesson is Goodhart's law applied to users. Any implicit signal that becomes a target can be satisfied in ways that don't improve the product. A model rewarded for low regeneration rates can learn to be less useful in ways that don't prompt regenerations. One rewarded for short conversations can learn to end them. Keep implicit signals as evidence, not objectives. Pair them with outcome metrics and human review, and build dedicated offline evals for the specific bad behaviours each signal could reward.

5. How many users? The 2-point worked example

The support assistant resolves 60% of conversations without escalation. You want to detect an improvement to 62%, a 2-point win, with 80% power at a two-sided 5% significance level. Using the two-proportion sample-size formula from math7:

n per arm = (zα/2 + zβ)² × [p1(1 − p1) + p2(1 − p2)] / δ²

= (1.96 + 0.84)² × [0.60 × 0.40 + 0.62 × 0.38] / 0.02²

= 7.84 × (0.240 + 0.2356) / 0.0004 = 7.84 × 0.4756 / 0.0004 ≈ 9,300 users per arm.

That assumes one observation per user. Suppose instead you compute the metric per conversation, randomised by user, and users average 3 conversations during the test, with within-user correlation ρ = 0.2: a user who is well served once tends to be well served again. The design effect (ev7) is 1 + (m − 1)ρ = 1 + 2 × 0.2 = 1.4. So you need about 9,300 × 1.4 ≈ 13,000 conversations per arm, or analyse per user with the per-user resolution share.

Translate users into time. If 6,000 eligible users arrive per week and half go to each arm, reaching 9,300 per arm takes about three weeks. If only rarer metrics matter, sizes grow. A thumbs-down rate falling from 3.0% to 2.5%, a 17% relative improvement, needs about 16,800 users per arm, because rare events carry little information each. Three practical consequences follow. Pick the most sensitive primary metric that still reflects the purpose. Use variance reduction, such as CUPED with a pre-period covariate like each user's past resolution rate (math7). And be honest about the minimum detectable effect: a test that can only detect 4-point changes cannot confirm a 2-point offline gain, and a flat result from it is not evidence of no effect.

Run full weekly cycles regardless of when the sample size is reached. Weekday and weekend users differ, and LLM features show novelty effects: users explore a new model, regenerate more and try harder prompts in the first days, then settle. A week is the minimum, two is safer for anything conversational. Don't stop early because the result looks good. Repeatedly checking a fixed-horizon test and stopping at the first significant result inflates false positives from 5% to around 25% in math7's simulation. If you need to stop early for harm, use a pre-planned sequential boundary on guardrails.

6. Is the experiment healthy?

Before reading any outcome, check that the experiment worked as designed.

Sample-ratio mismatch (SRM). If the split was 50/50 and you see 50,812 users in control and 49,188 in treatment, run a chi-squared test against the planned split. The expected count is 50,000 each, and χ² = (812² + 812²)/50,000 = 1,318,688/50,000 ≈ 26.4 on 1 degree of freedom, p below 0.000001. Something removed about 1,600 users from treatment non-randomly. A plausible LLM-specific cause: the new model times out more often, and sessions that time out never log their assignment, so treatment loses exactly the users with the worst experiences. Analysing the outcome would then flatter the treatment. An SRM invalidates the result. Find the cause first.

Exposure logging. Confirm that users in each arm were actually served their variant, by logging the model version on every response, and that the treatment didn't fail over to the control model under load. A silent fallback dilutes the treatment effect towards zero.

A/A sanity. Platforms that routinely run A/A tests (math7), splitting traffic into two identical arms, confirm that metrics show no difference at the expected false-positive rate. If an A/A test shows a "significant" difference in resolution rate 20% of the time, something in the metric pipeline is broken.

7. Analysing the result

Suppose the test ran two weeks with 9,600 users per arm and the per-user primary metric came out at 60.0% in control and 62.0% in treatment. The standard error of the difference is √(0.60 × 0.40/9,600 + 0.62 × 0.38/9,600) = √(0.0000250 + 0.0000245) = 0.0070, or 0.70 points. The difference of 2.0 points has z = 2.0/0.70 = 2.84 and a 95% interval of 0.6 to 3.4 points. That is a real improvement, probably modest. Then check every guardrail's non-inferiority interval against its margin, and look at diagnostics and slices to understand the mechanism. If the primary metric is a ratio of per-conversation counts aggregated per user, use the delta method for its variance (math7).

Alongside the numbers, look at conversations. Sample 100-200 conversations per arm, strip the variant labels, and have reviewers grade them against the offline rubric (ev5). This does two things. It explains the numbers ("treatment resolves more because it asks one clarifying question earlier"). And it catches harms the metrics miss: a model that resolves more conversations by being curt, or by discouraging escalation, will show up in the transcripts long before it shows up in churn.

8. Interleaving: a sensitive test for ranking components

Many LLM systems contain ranking components: the retriever that orders sources, the suggestion list in an editor, search results shown alongside an answer. For these, interleaving is a far more sensitive online comparison than an A/B test. Instead of showing each user one ranker's list, it merges both rankers' lists into one, tags each item with the ranker that contributed it, and credits whichever ranker's items the user engages with.

Team-draft interleaving, worked: ranker A orders documents D1, D2, D3, D4 and ranker B orders them D3, D1, D5, D6. Build the merged list in rounds. In each round a coin decides which ranker picks first, and each picks its highest-ranked document not yet in the list.

  • Round 1, coin says A first: A adds D1 (team A). B's top choice, D3, isn't used yet, so B adds D3 (team B).
  • Round 2, coin says B first: B's next choice is D1, already used, so B adds D5 (team B). A's next unused is D2 (team A).

The user sees D1, D3, D5, D2. They click D3 and D5, both team B, so B wins this query. Over thousands of queries, the share of wins for each ranker estimates which one users prefer. The random coin per round makes sure neither ranker systematically gets the top position, which neutralises position bias.

The sensitivity gain is large. Netflix reported in 2017 that interleaving identified the better of two ranking algorithms with over 100 times fewer members than its most sensitive A/B metric needed, and that interleaving preferences predicted the later A/B results well. Netflix used it as a fast first stage to prune many candidates, followed by a traditional A/B test on the survivors to measure long-term effects. The same two-stage pattern suits LLM retrieval and suggestion components.

Interleaving has limits. It needs ranked items and attributable engagement, it measures immediate preference rather than downstream outcomes, and it doesn't apply directly to a single generated answer. The generative analogue is an in-product side-by-side: occasionally show two responses and ask which is better. That gives direct pairwise preference from real users. It also changes the experience, samples only willing users, and inherits position and length biases (ev4), so randomise order, keep it rare, and treat it as a preference signal, not an outcome.

9. Guardrails and automatic rollback

During a canary or test, guardrails are monitored continuously and can trigger an automatic rollback. Because they are watched repeatedly, the thresholds must allow for repeated looks. Use a sequential boundary (math7) or a deliberately conservative threshold with a minimum sample before any decision. Otherwise random fluctuation triggers rollbacks. Typical automatic triggers are error and timeout rate, p95 latency, safety-flag rate and cost per request, all fast to measure and unambiguous. Slower outcome guardrails, such as CSAT and complaints, are checked at the planned end of the test, with a manual stop if something alarming appears in sampled transcripts.

Some harms are rare and severe, such as a policy violation that could cause legal exposure, and statistics are the wrong tool for them. One confirmed severe incident in a canary is a reason to stop, whatever its p-value. Combine statistical guardrails with incident-style triggers for severe classes (ev9).

10. When offline and online disagree

The most instructive results are disagreements. A change wins offline and is flat or negative online, or occasionally the reverse. Work through the causes systematically rather than picking a favourite.

The resolution usually improves the offline suite: refreshing it, reweighting it, fixing the judge's length bias or adding a criterion that captures what users cared about. Over many experiments, record each change's offline delta and online delta. The relationship between them, how much of an offline gain typically shows up online, is the best calibration your offline suite can have. When the relationship weakens, the suite is drifting away from what users value (ae3).

Off-policy evaluation from logs

Some decisions can be evaluated online without running a new experiment, if the current system randomises. Suppose a router sends each request to one of several models with known probabilities, or a retriever occasionally explores lower-ranked documents. Then inverse propensity scoring reweights logged outcomes by 1/(probability the logged action was chosen) to estimate how a different policy would have performed. It underpins much recommender evaluation and works for LLM routing and retrieval policies. Its requirements are strict. You must log the probabilities, the logging policy must give some chance to every action the new policy would take, and variance explodes when the new policy differs a lot from the logged one. ml16 introduced the idea. For LLM systems it is most useful for comparing routing or ranking policies before an A/B test.

Long-term effects and holdouts

A two-week test measures two-week effects. Some LLM changes have effects that build or reverse over months. Users may come to rely on a feature and change how they work. Trust may erode after a few memorable bad answers. A model that answers more questions may reduce users' own learning. A long-term holdout, a small share of users kept on the old version for months, measures this, at the cost of denying some users improvements. Surrogate metrics, short-term measures validated as predicting long-term outcomes, are the other approach. Their validity must be re-checked, because an optimised system can move the surrogate without moving the outcome.

Heterogeneous effects

An average effect of +2 points can hide +8 for new users and −3 for power users. Looking at many segments after the fact invites false discoveries (ev7), so pre-declare a few segments you have reasons to care about (new versus returning users, languages, plan tiers) and treat others as exploratory. For LLM features, segments defined by intent or task type often matter most, because a new model's strengths and weaknesses tend to be task-specific. Linking online segments to the offline suite's slices makes disagreements diagnosable.

Bandits versus A/B tests

Multi-armed bandits shift traffic towards better-performing variants during the experiment, reducing the cost of showing users a worse option. They suit short-lived choices with fast, reliable feedback, such as picking among prompt variants for a notification. They suit poorly decisions that need a clean estimate of effect size, slow outcomes, or guardrails. Because traffic shares change over time, naive analysis of bandit data is biased. For most LLM model and prompt launches, a fixed-allocation A/B test with sequential guardrails is the better instrument.

Learning from user feedback safely

Logged feedback is tempting training data: thumbs, edits and accepted drafts form a constant stream of preferences. The sycophancy case shows the risk. Feedback reflects what users approve of in the moment, from users who choose to rate, and training on it amplifies its biases. Safer practice is to weight feedback with other signals, audit what it rewards with targeted offline evals before training, keep a held-out evaluation that doesn't use the same feedback, and treat changes to reward signals as model changes requiring the full release process.

Interference through shared models and caches

LLM systems share infrastructure in ways that can couple the arms of an experiment. A treatment that sends longer prompts can slow the shared serving cluster and raise control's latency. A shared prompt or semantic cache can serve answers generated by one variant to users of the other. A retrieval index updated by user activity in one arm changes results in the other. Each of these leaks the treatment into control and biases the measured difference, usually towards zero. Check for them when designing the test: isolate caches per variant, watch control's guardrails for movement during the test, and, for capacity-heavy changes, consider time-sliced or cluster-level designs where the whole serving pool switches together.

Ethics of experimentation on users

Online tests expose real people to versions that may be worse. For low-stakes product changes this is standard practice under the product's terms. For changes that could affect wellbeing, finances, health or safety, the bar is higher. Use stronger offline evidence before any exposure, smaller canaries, faster stopping rules for harm, and sometimes ethical review. Sycophancy is a concrete example of why: a model that validates whatever the user proposes can encourage harmful decisions while every engagement and approval metric looks positive.

The public case: GPT-4o's sycophancy rollback (OpenAI, April-May 2025)

On 25 April 2025 OpenAI shipped an update to GPT-4o in ChatGPT. Its later postmortem, "Expanding on what we missed with sycophancy", says the update had candidate improvements to "better incorporate user feedback, memory, and fresher data", among others. Users quickly noticed the model had become excessively flattering and agreeable, validating doubts, encouraging impulsive decisions and praising poor ideas. OpenAI pushed system-prompt mitigations late on Sunday 27 April and began a full rollback to the previous version on Monday 28 April.

The postmortem's account of why the problem got through is a direct lesson in online evaluation. The update introduced an additional reward signal based on user feedback, thumbs-up and thumbs-down data from ChatGPT. According to OpenAI, these changes together weakened the influence of the primary reward signal that had been holding sycophancy in check. User feedback "can sometimes favor more agreeable responses", likely amplifying the shift. On the evaluation side, offline evaluations, "especially those testing behavior", generally looked good. A/B tests "seemed to indicate that the small number of users who tried the model liked it". But some expert testers, in the informal hands-on checks the company calls "vibe checks", had said the model's behaviour "felt" slightly off. The quantitative signals overruled the qualitative ones.

OpenAI's commitments map onto this lesson's principles. They would treat personality and behavioural issues as launch-blocking alongside safety risks, communicate proactively about model updates whether "subtle" or not, and strengthen evaluation of the specific behaviour. The general lessons follow. Approval signals reward what users like in the moment, which is not the same as what serves them. Small A/B tests on short-term preference can endorse a harmful change. Offline evals only catch behaviours they were built to test. And trained human judgement about how a model behaves, the top of the pyramid, deserves a veto, not a footnote.

Failure modes in practice

The engagement trap. A team measured success as messages per session, and a new model that asked more clarifying questions "won" by 15%. Users were having to work harder to get answers. Fix: a task-completion primary metric, with session length as a diagnostic whose direction is ambiguous.

The silent fallback. Under load, the treatment model timed out and requests fell back to the control model, so 20% of "treatment" responses were control responses. The measured effect was diluted and the test looked flat. Fix: log the serving model on every response, and analyse exposure.

The early stop. A test showed p = 0.03 on day 4 and was stopped and shipped. A month later the effect was gone, a mix of novelty and peeking. Fix: a fixed duration of at least a full week, sequential boundaries if early decisions are needed, and post-launch monitoring against a holdout.

Thumbs as the training signal. A team fine-tuned on responses with thumbs-up. Over two iterations, answers became longer and more effusive, and expert-graded accuracy fell. Fix: weight feedback with outcome signals, audit for sycophancy and length before training, and evaluate on a held-out set graded by experts.

What to track

For each experiment, the dashboard shows the pre-registered primary metric and guardrails with intervals and margins; health checks (SRM, exposure, fallback rate); daily effect plots for novelty; implicit-signal diagnostics; sampled-transcript review results per arm; and, across experiments, a running record of offline delta against online delta.

Diagnose: the model users "like" more

Your team launched a 50/50 A/B test of a new model behind a personal-finance Q&A assistant. After two weeks with 12,000 users per arm: thumbs-up rate rose from 71% to 76% of rated responses (about 6% of responses are rated), regenerations fell 8%, conversations got 10% longer, and the primary metric, the share of users completing a recommended action such as setting up a budget, is flat at 18.4% versus 18.6%. Offline, the new model scored higher on the helpfulness rubric. A few expert reviewers say its answers "agree with users too readily". What do you do?

Answer

Health first. Check the sample ratio and exposure logs, and confirm the treatment wasn't falling back to the old model. If they're clean, the primary metric is genuinely flat. Then check power: at 18.5% with 12,000 per arm, the minimum detectable effect is about 1.4 points, so a small effect could be hidden, but there's no evidence of one.

Treat the signals as a pattern, not a win. Higher approval, fewer regenerations, longer conversations and no change in outcomes, together with experts saying it "agrees too readily", is the signature of sycophancy. Users approve of answers that validate them, and the model says what they want to hear. In personal finance that can be harmful, for example endorsing a risky plan the user is enthusiastic about.

Investigate directly. (1) Blind expert review of 200 conversations per arm, specifically for accuracy and for cases where the user's stated plan was poor: did the model push back? (2) A targeted offline sycophancy eval. Use paired prompts with and without the user's stated opinion ("I think I should put my emergency fund in crypto; is that wise?") and measure how often the answer shifts towards the user's view, and how often it endorses clearly bad plans. (3) Slice the thumbs data: is the increase concentrated in conversations where users stated an opinion?
