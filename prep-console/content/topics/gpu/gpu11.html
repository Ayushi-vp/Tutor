A startup training a foundation model had a dashboard it trusted: every GPU in the cluster showed 100% utilisation, all day. The natural conclusion was that the hardware was saturated and more GPUs were the only way to go faster. When Trainy, an infrastructure company, looked at the job, the model was achieving about 20% model FLOPs utilisation. The GPUs were always running something — which is all "100% utilisation" means — but much of that time went to memory-bound kernels such as a naive softmax that used a small part of each SM, while the tensor cores sat idle. After fusing kernels and fixing the hot spots, MFU rose to 38%: close to double the training throughput from the same hardware, found in a profile rather than a purchase order.

Stories like this are common because GPU performance problems are invisible without the right tools and the right numbers. A GPU that waits for the CPU looks the same on a dashboard as one doing useful work. A kernel running at 5% of its potential produces correct results. An out-of-memory error says how much memory was requested, not what filled it. And because GPU work is asynchronous, the obvious ways of timing code — a stopwatch around a Python line — measure the wrong thing entirely.

This lesson is the practical counterpart to the rest of the track: a method for finding out why GPU code is slow or runs out of memory. It starts with the step most people skip — computing what the performance should be — then covers timing correctly, reading a PyTorch profiler or Nsight Systems timeline, recognising the half-dozen signatures that account for most slowness, drilling into a single kernel with Nsight Compute, choosing fleet metrics that measure efficiency rather than activity, and debugging memory with snapshots. It ends with the production side: stragglers, hardware errors and catching regressions before they cost a training run.

Vital signs, scans and biopsies

A good doctor doesn't start with surgery. First the vital signs — temperature, pulse, blood pressure — which are cheap and continuous and say whether something is wrong. Then a scan, which shows where. Only then a biopsy, which explains exactly what is wrong in one small piece of tissue. And before any of it, the doctor knows what normal looks like; a pulse of 70 means nothing without knowing that 60-100 is healthy.

GPU performance work has the same levels. The vital signs are per-step metrics — step time, tokens per second, MFU or MBU, memory in use — and fleet metrics from DCGM, collected continuously. The scan is a timeline profile — PyTorch profiler or Nsight Systems — showing every CPU operation and GPU kernel over a few steps, so you can see where time goes and where the GPU sits idle. The biopsy is Nsight Compute on one kernel, explaining why that kernel runs at the speed it does. And "normal" is the roofline estimate from gpu2 and gpu3: what the step should take given its FLOPs and bytes.

The first trap: GPU utilisation

The number most people look at first, nvidia-smi's "GPU-Util", is the fraction of the sampling period during which at least one kernel was executing. A tiny kernel occupying 2 of 132 SMs counts exactly as much as a matmul using every tensor core. It is useful for one thing — detecting a GPU that is completely idle — and misleading for everything else. Efficiency is measured by MFU (the model's required FLOPs per second divided by peak, gpu3) for compute-bound work, MBU (bytes that must be read per second divided by peak bandwidth, gpu2) for memory-bound decoding, and DCGM's SM activity and tensor-pipe activity for a hardware-level view.

What a profile can and cannot tell you

A profile records what happened; it doesn't say what should have happened. It will show that a softmax kernel took 2 ms, but not whether 2 ms is good — that needs the kernel's bytes and the bandwidth (about 0.1 ms would be the roofline for a typical case, so 2 ms is terrible). It will show a 30 ms gap, but not whether the gap came from the data loader, a lock, or the operating system — that needs the CPU side of the timeline. And it covers only the window you recorded. So profiles are always read against an expectation and alongside continuous metrics, never on their own.

The method

Every investigation follows the same loop: compute the expectation; measure correctly; look at the timeline for gaps and at the breakdown of kernel time for the largest gaps between measured and achievable time; form one hypothesis; change one thing; measure again. Performance work goes wrong mainly by skipping the first step (so there's no way to tell whether a number is good) or by changing several things at once (so there's no way to tell which one mattered).

1. Start with the expected number

Before opening a profiler, compute what the step should take. For training: FLOPs per step ≈ 6 × parameters × tokens (plus attention at long context); at a realistic 40-50% of dense peak, that gives a target step time. For decoding: bytes per step (weights plus KV cache) at 70-80% of bandwidth. Then compute MFU or MBU from the measured step time.

Worked example: a 1.3B-parameter model trains on 8 H100s, global batch 64 sequences × 2,048 tokens = 131,072 tokens, at 1.2 s per step. FLOPs ≈ 6 × 1.3 × 109 × 131,072 ≈ 1.0 × 1015 per step; achieved ≈ 8.5 × 1014 FLOP/s over 8 GPUs, 1.06 × 1014 per GPU — MFU ≈ 11%. A reasonable target for a model this size is perhaps 35-45% (small models have smaller matmuls and proportionally more element-wise work), so the step "should" take about 0.3-0.4 s. There is roughly 0.8 s per step to find, and the profile's job is to show where it goes.

2. Timing correctly

CUDA launches are asynchronous: Python returns as soon as the kernel is queued. A timer around y = model(x) measures queuing, not computing — unless something in the timed region happens to synchronise, in which case it measures that, plus whatever earlier work was still queued. The correct pattern:

for _ in range(5):                       # warm-up: compile, autotune, allocate
    step()
torch.cuda.synchronize()
start = torch.cuda.Event(enable_timing=True); end = torch.cuda.Event(enable_timing=True)
start.record()
for _ in range(20):
    step()
end.record(); torch.cuda.synchronize()
print(f"{start.elapsed_time(end) / 20:.2f} ms per step")

Warm-up excludes one-time costs — torch.compile, autotuning, cuDNN algorithm selection, the caching allocator requesting memory from the driver. Averaging over many iterations smooths noise. For published comparisons, record the GPU model, driver and library versions, and clocks, since a GPU limited by power or temperature can run 10-20% slower than an identical one next to it.

3. The PyTorch profiler

The PyTorch profiler records CPU-side operators and GPU kernels together, linking each kernel to the operator that launched it:

from torch.profiler import profile, ProfilerActivity, schedule, tensorboard_trace_handler
with profile(activities=[ProfilerActivity.CPU, ProfilerActivity.CUDA],
             schedule=schedule(wait=2, warmup=2, active=3),   # skip start-up steps
             on_trace_ready=tensorboard_trace_handler("./traces"),
             record_shapes=True, profile_memory=True, with_stack=True) as prof:
    for step_idx in range(7):
        train_step()
        prof.step()
print(prof.key_averages().table(sort_by="cuda_time_total", row_limit=15))

Two views matter. The table aggregates time by operator (or by kernel), sorted by GPU time — the quickest way to see which operation types dominate, with input shapes when record_shapes is on. The trace (open the exported JSON in Perfetto or TensorBoard) shows the timeline: CPU threads on top with Python and operator ranges, GPU streams below with kernels, and arrows linking each launch to its kernel. Profiling adds overhead — especially with_stack and memory profiling — so use a short, scheduled window after warm-up, and measure the untraced step time separately.

For multi-GPU jobs, Meta's Holistic Trace Analysis (HTA) library reads traces from all ranks and computes breakdowns such as idle time per rank (split into host wait, kernel wait and other), computation versus communication and their overlap, and the most expensive kernels — the same analysis you would do by eye, automated across hundreds of files.

5. Hidden synchronisations

A host-device synchronisation makes the CPU wait until the GPU has finished everything queued so far. The GPU then finishes and sits idle while the CPU resumes, runs Python and launches the next kernels. Syncs hide in innocuous code: loss.item() for logging, print(tensor), .cpu() or .tolist(), if (x > 0).any():, torch.nonzero and boolean-mask indexing (whose output size depends on the data, so the CPU must learn it), and copying from pageable host memory. One sync per step can cost a few percent; one per layer can halve throughput. torch.cuda.set_sync_debug_mode("warn") prints a warning at every synchronising call, which is the quickest way to find them. A related trap is logging: total_loss += loss without .detach() keeps every step's computation graph alive and leaks memory, while total_loss += loss.item() syncs every step — accumulate loss.detach() on the GPU and read it every N steps.

6. Nsight Systems: the whole system

Nsight Systems records a system-wide timeline with low overhead: all CPU threads (including data-loader processes), CUDA API calls, kernels on every stream, memory copies, NCCL operations and, optionally, operating-system events. It is the tool for questions the PyTorch profiler answers less well: whether copies overlap with compute, which CPU thread is blocking, how several processes on one node interleave, or why a gap exists when no PyTorch operator is running.

nsys profile -t cuda,nvtx,osrt --capture-range=cudaProfilerApi -o step_trace python train.py

Adding NVTX ranges — torch.cuda.nvtx.range_push("forward") / range_pop(), or an emit_nvtx context — labels regions of the timeline, which makes long traces readable. Capture ranges (torch.cuda.profiler.start() and stop()) limit recording to a few steady-state steps.

7. Nsight Compute: one kernel in depth

When a specific kernel is far from its expected performance, Nsight Compute explains why. It replays the kernel many times to collect hardware counters, so it is slow; filter to the kernels you care about:

ncu --kernel-name regex:softmax --launch-skip 10 --launch-count 1 --set full -o softmax_report python train.py

Read the report top-down. Speed of light gives achieved compute and memory throughput as percentages of peak — the kernel's position on the roofline (gpu2). If memory is near 80-90% and compute low, it is memory-bound: only moving fewer bytes helps. If compute is high, it is compute-bound. If both are low, it is latency- or overhead-bound, and the next sections explain why: launch statistics and occupancy (grid size relative to SM count, registers and shared memory per thread limiting resident warps), memory workload analysis (bytes per request — a coalescing check, as in gpu5 — cache hit rates, bank conflicts), and warp state statistics, which show what warps were waiting for — "long scoreboard" stalls mean waiting on global memory without enough parallelism to hide it, "barrier" stalls mean waiting at __syncthreads(), "math pipe throttle" means the arithmetic units are saturated (good).

8. Fleet metrics that measure efficiency

For continuous monitoring, NVIDIA's DCGM exports per-GPU metrics that most clusters feed into Prometheus and Grafana. Beyond GPU-Util, the profiling metrics are the useful ones:

  • SM activity (DCGM_FI_PROF_SM_ACTIVE): fraction of time SMs had at least one warp resident, averaged over all SMs — how much of the chip had work.
  • SM occupancy (DCGM_FI_PROF_SM_OCCUPANCY): resident warps relative to the maximum — how much parallelism each SM had.
  • Tensor-pipe activity (DCGM_FI_PROF_PIPE_TENSOR_ACTIVE): fraction of cycles the tensor cores were busy — the closest hardware proxy for MFU.
  • DRAM activity (DCGM_FI_PROF_DRAM_ACTIVE): fraction of cycles the memory interface was busy — how bandwidth-bound the work is.
  • NVLink and PCIe throughput, power, temperature, clocks, ECC error counts and Xid errors for health.

In the Trainy case, GPU-Util was 100% while the softmax kernel that dominated time had low SM efficiency; SM activity and tensor activity would have flagged it immediately. A useful dashboard shows GPU-Util and tensor activity side by side: a large gap between them is the signature of memory-bound or poorly parallelised work.

9. Debugging memory

An out-of-memory error reports the failed request, the total, and two numbers: memory allocated by live tensors and memory reserved by PyTorch's caching allocator. Reserved far above allocated means fragmentation: enough memory is free in total, but not in one piece. PYTORCH_CUDA_ALLOC_CONF=expandable_segments:True usually fixes that. Otherwise, find what is allocated with a memory snapshot:

torch.cuda.memory._record_memory_history(max_entries=100_000)
try:
    for _ in range(3):
        train_step()
finally:
    torch.cuda.memory._dump_snapshot("mem.pickle")   # open at pytorch.org/memory_viz
    torch.cuda.memory._record_memory_history(enabled=None)

The visualiser shows every allocation as a band over time, stacked to the total, with the Python stack that created it. A training step has a characteristic shape: memory rises through the forward pass as activations are saved, peaks at the start of backward, and falls as activations are freed. Anything that doesn't follow the shape stands out: a band that keeps growing across steps (a leak, such as the retained loss graph), a huge single allocation (the logits), or activations much larger than the 34·s·b·h estimate from gpu3 (often a fallback to non-flash attention). The fixes follow from gpu9: checkpointing, smaller micro-batches with gradient accumulation, chunked loss, sharding or offload.

10. Profiling inference

Inference servers need the same tools with different expectations. A decode step is expected to be memory-bound, so the check is MBU: bytes of weights and KV cache per step divided by step time, against peak bandwidth — 60-80% is healthy. Timelines of servers show a CPU-side scheduler loop between steps; if it takes a noticeable fraction of the step (tokenisation, sampling, request bookkeeping in Python), the GPU idles between steps, which is why engines overlap scheduling for step n+1 with GPU work for step n and capture decode steps in CUDA graphs. Prefill steps should look compute-bound, with long dense matmul kernels; long attention kernels in decode at large contexts are expected (gpu7), while long element-wise kernels are a fusion opportunity.

11. A worked triage

The lab analyses a synthetic trace of one step of a small model on one GPU: 14.5 ms, with the GPU busy 82% of the time — which nvidia-smi would report as roughly 82% utilisation — and an MFU of about 28%. The breakdown shows matmuls at 64.5% of the step, communication 6.2%, attention 4.8%, element-wise 4.0%, optimiser 2.8%, and two idle gaps: 1.8 ms at the start (waiting for data) and 0.5 ms after a loss.item() synchronisation. The order of fixes follows from the sizes: the data-loader gap (12% of the step, fixed by prefetching and more workers) and the sync (4%, fixed by logging every 50 steps) are cheap and large; the exposed all-reduce (6%) needs better bucketing or overlap; the element-wise kernels (4%) are a fusion candidate but small. Only after those would it be worth asking whether the matmuls themselves reach their expected throughput. This is exactly how real investigations go: idle time and overhead first, then the biggest kernel groups, with the roofline telling you when to stop.

Debugging correctness on GPUs

GPU errors surface asynchronously: an out-of-bounds access in one kernel is reported at a later synchronising call, often in unrelated code. CUDA_LAUNCH_BLOCKING=1 makes every launch synchronous so the Python traceback points at the right operation (far slower; debugging only). NVIDIA's compute-sanitizer finds out-of-bounds and misaligned accesses (memcheck), shared-memory races (racecheck), invalid barrier use (synccheck) and reads of uninitialised memory (initcheck) in custom kernels. For device-side assertion failures such as an index out of range in an embedding lookup, the message only appears after synchronisation, and TORCH_USE_CUDA_DSA builds give more detail.

Profiling overhead and observer effects

Every profiler changes what it measures. The PyTorch profiler with stacks and memory tracking slows Python-heavy code noticeably; Nsight Compute serialises and replays kernels, destroying any overlap; even NVTX ranges add small costs. Worse, profilers can hide problems: synchronisations inserted by tracing can mask races or change scheduling, and replays run with warm caches. Always compare traced step time with untraced step time, and treat timings inside heavyweight profilers as relative, not absolute. Performance bugs that disappear under the profiler are usually timing-dependent — contention, scheduling, or thermal behaviour.

Clocks, power and thermal throttling

GPUs lower their clocks when they hit power or temperature limits, and the effect is large enough to masquerade as a software problem: one GPU in a server consistently 10% slower, or a job that slows in the afternoon. nvidia-smi -q -d PERFORMANCE lists the active throttle reasons (power cap, thermal slowdown, hardware slowdown), and DCGM exports clocks, power and temperature per GPU. Meta observed a 1-2% daily variation in Llama 3 training throughput tied to temperature. When benchmarking, record clocks, and when comparing GPUs, compare at equal power limits.

Reading memory-system counters

Nsight Compute's memory charts show traffic at every level — global loads and stores, L1 and L2 hit rates, HBM reads and writes. Two comparisons are particularly revealing. Comparing HBM bytes with the kernel's minimum bytes (inputs once, outputs once) shows whether it re-reads data it should have kept on chip, which is a tiling problem. Comparing sectors per request with the ideal (4 for a warp reading consecutive 4-byte values) shows uncoalesced access. Both connect directly to the fixes in gpu5.

Aligning traces across ranks

In distributed jobs, the rank that looks slow in a collective is usually the one that arrived first — it waits longest for the others — while the true culprit arrived last. Reading per-rank traces requires aligning them in time and asking, for each collective, which rank entered last. Tools such as HTA and PyTorch's flight recorder (which keeps a ring buffer of recent collectives per rank and dumps it on a hang) do this; ByteDance's MegaScale describes the same idea at 10,000+ GPUs, recording collective timings per rank to find stragglers.

Profiling compiled code

After torch.compile, the timeline shows generated Triton kernels with names such as triton_poi_fused_add_mul_0 (pointwise) or triton_red_fused_… (reduction) instead of familiar operators. The name lists the fused operations, and TORCH_LOGS="output_code" prints the generated source for each. Graph breaks show up as stretches of eager kernels between compiled regions, and recompilations as long CPU gaps in the middle of otherwise steady steps. The profiler's view of a compiled model is therefore also a check that compilation did what you expected.

Python and CPU-side overhead

When the timeline shows the GPU waiting on the CPU and no obvious sync, the question moves to the CPU: what is Python doing? A sampling profiler such as py-spy (py11) attached to the training process shows which Python functions consume the time — often data transforms in the main process, logging, or framework overhead from many small operations. The fixes are the ones from gpu6 (fewer, larger operations; compilation; CUDA graphs) or moving work into DataLoader workers.

Performance regressions as a discipline

Many performance problems are regressions: a library upgrade changes which attention kernel runs, a model change adds a synchronisation, a data change lengthens sequences. Teams that catch them early keep benchmarks in CI — kernel microbenchmarks, a short training run measuring tokens per second and peak memory, inference latency at fixed batch sizes — run on the target GPU type with pinned software, compared against a stored baseline with a threshold above measured noise. The benchmark records the full environment, because a performance number without its driver, CUDA, PyTorch and library versions can't be compared with anything.

Monitoring a training fleet

Large training jobs are monitored at two levels. Per job: step time per rank (to spot stragglers), tokens per second, MFU, loss and gradient norm, data-loader wait time, checkpoint duration and memory high-water mark. Per GPU, from DCGM: SM and tensor activity, memory bandwidth, NVLink traffic, clocks, power, temperature, ECC error counts and Xid errors. Alerts fire on deviations from each job's own recent baseline rather than fixed thresholds, because "normal" MFU differs from model to model.

Hardware errors and health checks

At scale, hardware faults are routine (gpu1 cites Meta's 419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days on 16K H100s, most of them hardware). The driver reports faults as Xid codes in the system log — for example uncorrectable ECC memory errors, or a GPU that has "fallen off the bus" and disappeared from the PCIe topology. HBM uses row remapping to retire failing memory rows, and a growing count predicts failure. Operators run DCGM diagnostics at several depths — quick checks between jobs, longer stress tests before a node joins a large job — and automatically drain nodes that fail them. Silent data corruption, where a GPU produces wrong results without any error, is the hardest case; large operators detect it by periodically running known computations and comparing results across GPUs.

Inference monitoring

For serving, the vital signs are different (gpu10): TTFT and inter-token latency percentiles, queue length, running and waiting sequences, KV-cache utilisation, preemptions, prefix-cache hit rate and tokens per second per GPU. DRAM activity is expected to be high and tensor activity low for decode-heavy services; a sudden change in either, without a traffic change, points to a configuration or software regression — for example quantisation or a kernel back end silently disabled after an upgrade.

Making profiling routine

The teams that avoid situations like the 100%-utilisation, 20%-MFU cluster don't profile only when something breaks. They profile every new model or configuration for a few steps before long runs, keep a dashboard of MFU and tensor activity per job, and treat a drop in either as an incident. Some training frameworks capture a short profiler trace automatically at a fixed step and store it with the job's logs, so a trace from a healthy run is always available to compare with a slow one. The cost is minutes; the alternative is weeks of a large cluster running at half its potential.

An investigation checklist

  1. Compute the expected step time or MFU/MBU and confirm the accounting.
  2. Time the step correctly, after warm-up.
  3. Profile a few steady-state steps; measure idle time and its causes first.
  4. Break GPU time down by kernel type; compare each with its roofline bound.
  5. Drill into the kernels with the largest gaps with Nsight Compute.
  6. Change one thing, re-measure, and keep the benchmark so the gain is protected.

The same checklist, applied to the gpt-fast case from gpu2, explains each of its steps: overhead first (CUDA graphs, 25 → 107 tokens/s), then MBU showed the bandwidth ceiling (72%), then the bytes were reduced (quantisation) — the profile and the arithmetic, together, chose each move.

Goal: practise the triage from section 10 on a trace: total busy and idle time, time by kernel category, the largest gaps, and MFU versus the "utilisation" nvidia-smi would report. This machine has no NVIDIA GPU, so the trace is synthetic; the same analysis applies to a real exported trace. Pure Python 3.12.

TRACE = [  # (name, category, start_us, duration_us) for one training step
    ("dataloader wait", "idle", 0, 0), ("gemm_qkv", "matmul", 1800, 900),
    ("rmsnorm", "elementwise", 2700, 160), ("rope", "elementwise", 2860, 140),
    ("flash_attn_fwd", "attention", 3050, 700), ("gemm_o", "matmul", 3800, 300),
    ("add", "elementwise", 4100, 60), ("gemm_mlp_up", "matmul", 4200, 1300),
    ("silu_mul", "elementwise", 5500, 220), ("gemm_mlp_down", "matmul", 5720, 650),
    ("loss.item() sync", "idle", 6370, 0), ("backward (all kernels)", "matmul", 6900, 6200),
    ("nccl_allreduce (exposed)", "comm", 13100, 900), ("adamw_foreach", "optimizer", 14000, 400),
]
STEP_US = 14500
busy = {}
for name, cat, start, dur in TRACE:
    if dur:
        busy[cat] = busy.get(cat, 0) + dur
total = sum(busy.values())
print(f"step {STEP_US / 1e3:.1f} ms | GPU busy {total / STEP_US:.0%}")
for cat, us in sorted(busy.items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1]):
    print(f"  {cat:12s} {us / 1e3:5.2f} ms  {us / STEP_US:5.1%}")
prev_end = 0
for name, cat, start, dur in sorted(TRACE, key=lambda r: r[2]):
    if start - prev_end > 100:
        print(f"  gap of {(start - prev_end) / 1e3:.2f} ms before '{name}'")
    prev_end = max(prev_end, start + dur)
flops = 6 * 1.3e9 * 512                        # 1.3B params, 512 tokens per GPU per step
print(f"MFU {flops / (STEP_US / 1e6) / 989e12:.0%} vs 'utilisation' {total / STEP_US:.0%}")

What to look for. The GPU is busy 82% of the step, yet MFU is 28%. Matmuls take 64.5%; the two gaps — 1.8 ms waiting for data and 0.53 ms after the loss.item() sync — are 16% of the step together and are the cheapest wins; exposed communication (6.2%) is next. Try deleting the data-loader gap (shift every start time down by 1,800 µs and shorten STEP_US) and see how MFU changes.

Work it out

A 1.3B-parameter model trains on 8 H100s at 1.2 s per step with a global batch of 64 sequences × 2,048 tokens (BF16). nvidia-smi shows 98% utilisation. What is the MFU, and what would you check, in order?

Answer

Tokens per step = 64 × 2,048 = 131,072. FLOPs per step ≈ 6 × 1.3 × 109 × 131,072 ≈ 1.0 × 1015. Achieved ≈ 1.0 × 1015 / 1.2 s ≈ 8.5 × 1014 FLOP/s across 8 GPUs → 1.06 × 1014 per GPU → MFU ≈ 11%. The GPUs are "98% utilised" while the tensor cores sit idle most of the time.

  1. The accounting: real tokens after padding (padding can waste 30%+), global versus per-GPU batch, the parameter count, and the dense BF16 peak (not the sparse datasheet figure). A wrong MFU sends you chasing the wrong problem.
  2. A profiler trace of steady-state steps: gaps between steps (data loading) and within steps (host syncs such as logging loss.item() every step).
  3. Many tiny kernels: a 1.3B model with 8 sequences per GPU has modest matmuls and many element-wise operations, so it is partly overhead- and memory-bound. torch.compile (fusion), CUDA graphs and a larger micro-batch are the likely fixes; confirm FlashAttention is in use.
  4. DCGM tensor-pipe activity to confirm the diagnosis, and exposed all-reduce time — 2.6 GB of BF16 gradients per step is small over NVLink (a few milliseconds), so communication is unlikely to be the main cause.

The lesson: GPU-Util is not an efficiency metric. Compute MFU from first principles and let the profiler explain the gap.
