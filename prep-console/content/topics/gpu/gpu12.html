A team is about to commit a seven-figure budget to serving a 70-billion-parameter model. The options on the table: rent H100s, which are easy to get; rent H200s, which cost more per hour; wait for B200 capacity; try AMD MI300X instances, which have 192 GB per GPU; use TPUs in Google Cloud; or skip hardware entirely and pay per token through an API. A spreadsheet ranked by "price per GPU-hour" picks H100s. A spreadsheet ranked by "TFLOPS per dollar" picks something else. Both are the wrong spreadsheet, because neither asks what this workload is actually limited by, or what the output — tokens served within a latency target — costs.

Hardware decisions are where everything in this track turns into money. The roofline (gpu2) says whether a workload is limited by FLOPs or bytes; the memory arithmetic (gpu3) says how many chips a model needs just to fit; the serving model (gpu10) says how many tokens per second a replica delivers at a latency target; the communication model (gpu8) says whether tensor or expert parallelism will run fast enough on a given interconnect. Put those together with prices and utilisation, and "which accelerator?" becomes a calculation with a defensible answer. Get it wrong and the cost is not a slower benchmark but a bill two or three times larger than necessary for the life of the product — or a training run that takes months longer.

This lesson turns the track into a decision method. It covers the four questions that select hardware (fit, bottleneck, communication, cost per useful unit), a verified comparison of current accelerators from NVIDIA, AMD and Google, the formulas for cost per million tokens and cost per training run, the effect of utilisation, total cost of ownership, and the build, rent or API decision — with real public numbers from Meta's Llama 3.1 training, DeepSeek's production service and Epoch AI's price-trend data, and a calculator that ranks hardware for a concrete serving scenario.

Choosing a delivery van

Imagine choosing vans for a delivery business. Four things matter. Capacity: can the van carry the load at all, or do you need two vans per delivery? Speed: how fast does it move once loaded? Coordination: if a delivery is split across several vans, how well can the drivers keep in step? And cost per package delivered, not cost per van per hour: a van that costs more to rent but carries three times as much is cheaper per package, and any van that sits in the depot half the day doubles its cost per package. The right van depends on the deliveries — many small parcels, or a few pianos.

Accelerators are the same. Capacity is HBM: does the model plus its KV cache or training state fit, and on how many chips? Speed comes in two kinds — memory bandwidth, which sets decode speed, and FLOPs at your precision, which set prefill and training speed — and which one matters depends on the workload. Coordination is interconnect: NVLink domains, network bandwidth. And the only cost that matters is cost per useful unit: per million tokens served at your latency targets, or per training run at your achieved MFU.

Why the obvious spreadsheets mislead

Two spreadsheets appear in almost every hardware discussion, and both mislead in predictable ways. Price per GPU-hour favours older, cheaper chips and ignores that a newer chip may serve two or three times the tokens in that hour. Peak TFLOPS per dollar favours whichever chip has the biggest headline number — often a sparse, lowest-precision figure — and ignores that most serving time is decode, which is limited by memory bandwidth, and that the headline format may not be usable for your model. Both also ignore memory capacity, which decides how many chips a replica needs and how many users fit in its KV cache, and both ignore utilisation, which in practice changes cost per token by a factor of two or three. The only comparison that survives is cost per useful unit of output, measured on your workload: tokens per second at the latency target per dollar-hour, divided by the fraction of time you actually use it.

A quick illustration of why the last step matters. An 8B model serving 2,500 output tokens per second on one GPU rented at $2.50 per hour produces 9 million tokens per hour: $0.28 per million tokens if the GPU is always busy. If traffic keeps it busy only 30% of the time on average, every token served carries the cost of the idle hours too: $0.93 per million. Utilisation often moves cost per token more than the choice between two GPUs does. Keep that in mind throughout: the best chip for a workload that runs at 30% utilisation may lose to a merely good chip that can be kept 70% busy.

2. Does it fit?

Memory is the first filter. For inference, weights plus KV cache plus overheads (gpu3, gpu10):

  • 8B model: 16 GB in BF16 — fits on almost anything, including a 24 GB L4 with a small KV cache.
  • 70B model: 140 GB in BF16, 70 GB in FP8, about 35 GB in 4-bit. BF16 needs two 80 GB GPUs just for weights (four or eight in practice for KV cache), or one 141-192 GB GPU with little room; FP8 fits on one H100 with a small cache, comfortably on one H200 or MI300X.
  • 405B model: 810 GB in BF16, 405 GB in FP8 — one 8-GPU H100 server in FP8 (640 GB), one 8-GPU H200 or MI300X server in BF16-to-FP8 territory.
  • 671B MoE (DeepSeek-V3): about 671 GB in FP8, plus a large KV budget — a multi-GPU deployment, often multi-node for decode efficiency.

For training, about 16 bytes per parameter of state plus activations (gpu9): a 70B model's 1.1 TB of state must be sharded across dozens of 80 GB GPUs. Fewer, larger-memory chips reduce the number of GPUs per replica and the tensor-parallel communication between them — one of the main reasons high-memory accelerators are attractive for serving large models.

3. What is the bottleneck?

Different workloads stress different specs, so the ranking of chips changes with the workload.

  • Decode-heavy chat and agents: memory-bound (gpu2, gpu10). Rank by bandwidth per dollar and by capacity per dollar (capacity sets the KV-limited batch). An H200 has the same FLOPs as an H100 but 43% more bandwidth.
  • Prefill-heavy work — long documents, retrieval-augmented generation with large contexts, classification over long inputs: compute-bound. Rank by FLOPs per dollar at the precision you can use (FP8 doubles effective FLOPs on Hopper and later).
  • Training: compute-bound per GPU, but at scale interconnect and reliability decide MFU (gpu8, gpu1); a cheaper chip on a weaker network can cost more per training run.
  • Mixture-of-experts serving: capacity and interconnect, because experts must be spread across many chips with all-to-all traffic every layer; large NVLink domains (NVL72) help most here.
  • Small models at high volume: often limited by overheads and batch size rather than by the chip; cheap, smaller GPUs with many replicas can win.

4. From throughput to cost per million tokens

cost per 1M tokens = (price per accelerator-hour × accelerators per replica)
                     / (tokens per hour per replica / 10^6)
                     / average utilisation

Throughput must be measured (or modelled, gpu10) at your latency targets — the batch size that meets p95 inter-token latency, not the maximum possible. Utilisation is the fraction of paid time spent serving: provisioning for daily peaks plus headroom usually gives 30-50% for interactive services unless troughs are filled with batch work.

Worked comparison. The lab models a 70B model in FP8 (weights and KV cache), 3,000 tokens of context per sequence and a 40 ms inter-token latency target, on H100, H200 and B200 at illustrative prices of $2.50, $3.50 and $5.00 per GPU-hour. For each, it tries tensor-parallel degrees 1-8 and finds the largest batch that fits in memory and meets the latency target. With these assumptions all three do best at eight GPUs per replica with very large batches, giving roughly $0.16 (H100), $0.15 (H200) and $0.13 (B200) per million output tokens at full utilisation, and about 2.5× that at 40% utilisation. The absolute numbers rest on optimistic efficiency factors and on prices you must replace with real quotes; the method — fit, model the step, apply the latency target, divide by price and utilisation — is the point. Note also how close the options are: a 20% price difference or a 20% gap between modelled and measured throughput can reverse the ranking, which is why a proof of concept is the final step.

Where the model is optimistic. It assumes 80% of peak bandwidth and 50% of peak FLOPs, perfectly steady decode with no prefill interleaved, every sequence at the same context length, and a scheduler that keeps batches full. Real engines lose some of each: prefill steps interrupt decode, contexts vary, and batches drain and refill. Measured throughput at a latency target is commonly well below such a model — the worked plan in section 6 assumes about half — which is exactly why cost estimates must come from measurements before money is committed.

A real anchor. DeepSeek's published production data (gpu10) gives a real point on this curve: about 14,800 output tokens per second per 8-GPU H800 node in decode, at an assumed $2 per GPU-hour — roughly $0.30 per million output tokens for decode nodes, for a 671B mixture-of-experts model with average contexts near 5,000 tokens. Over a day, their 226.75 nodes cost $87,072.

5. The cost of a training run

GPU-hours = 6 · N · D / (peak FLOP/s × MFU × 3,600)       (gpu3)
cost      = GPU-hours × price per GPU-hour × (1 + overheads)

Meta's Llama 3.1 model card reports 1.46 million H100-hours for the 8B model, 7.0 million for 70B and 30.84 million for 405B — 39.3 million in total. At an illustrative $2 per GPU-hour that is about $2.9 million, $14 million and $62 million of compute. For comparison, the bare 6ND estimate at 40% MFU for the 8B model on 15 trillion tokens is about 0.5 million GPU-hours: real programmes spend two to three times the idealised compute once lower MFU, failures and restarts, long-context phases, post-training and experiments are included. DeepSeek-V3's report of 2.788 million H800-hours (about $5.6 million at $2) shows the same arithmetic for a mixture-of-experts model — and, as its authors note, excludes prior research, ablations and data costs. A sensible training budget therefore starts from 6ND, applies a realistic MFU measured on the target cluster, and adds explicit multipliers for overheads and experimentation.

6. A worked plan, end to end

Put the pieces together for a concrete, if simplified, scenario. A product expects 20 million requests a day with an average of 500 output tokens each — 10 billion output tokens a day — served by a 70B model under a 40 ms inter-token latency target. Traffic peaks at about twice the daily average.

  • Demand: 10 × 109 tokens / 86,400 s ≈ 116,000 output tokens per second on average, about 230,000 at peak.
  • Per-replica supply: from a proof of concept (not the optimistic model), suppose an 8-GPU H200 replica sustains 25,000 output tokens per second at the latency target once prefill, real context lengths and scheduling overheads are included.
  • Replicas: 230,000 / 25,000 ≈ 9.2 at peak; with 20% headroom for failures and bursts, 11 replicas — 88 GPUs.
  • Cost: 88 GPUs × 24 h × $3.50 (illustrative) ≈ $7,400 per day, about $0.74 per million output tokens delivered. Average utilisation is 116,000 / (11 × 25,000) ≈ 42%, so idle capacity more than doubles the cost compared with a perfectly busy fleet (about $0.31 per million).
  • Levers: filling night-time troughs with batch jobs (raising utilisation towards 70% cuts cost per token by about 40%), prefix caching (cutting prefill work), FP4 on Blackwell, or a smaller distilled model for easy requests.
  • Comparison: an API at, say, $1 per million output tokens costs $10,000 per day for the same traffic, with no engineering or on-call. Self-hosting saves about $2,600 a day, roughly $950,000 a year — enough to fund a small serving team, not much more, and the margin shrinks with every API price cut.

Every number above is an assumption to be replaced — traffic, measured throughput, prices, headroom — but the structure is what a real plan contains, and each line maps to a lesson in this track.

7. Build, rent or call an API

  • API: no fixed cost, pay per token, instantly elastic, and the provider absorbs utilisation risk. The right default until volume is high, or data can't leave your boundary, or you need a model the API doesn't offer.
  • Rented accelerators (on-demand, reserved, spot): self-hosted open models become cheaper than APIs at sustained high utilisation; reserved capacity lowers price but locks in commitment; spot capacity suits interruptible batch work and checkpointed training. You now own the serving stack, upgrades and on-call.
  • Owned hardware: lowest unit cost at very high, steady utilisation over years; the highest commitment and operational load, and exposure to obsolescence in a fast-moving market.

The break-even is a utilisation: with the drill's numbers ($0.28 per million at full utilisation versus an API at $0.40), self-hosting wins only above about 70% average utilisation — before counting engineering time. And the comparison moves: Epoch AI measured the price of reaching fixed capability levels falling between 9× and 900× per year depending on the task (about 40× per year for GPT-4-level performance on PhD-level science questions). Decisions should be revisited on a schedule.

The API side has its own hidden costs and benefits: rate limits and quotas at high volume, data-processing terms, model deprecations on the provider's timetable, and latency you can't tune — but also access to frontier models you couldn't host at all, and no capacity planning.

Total cost of ownership

Owning accelerators means paying for much more than chips: servers, NICs, switches and optics; storage; data-centre space; power and cooling; spares; software support; and the staff who run it all. Power alone is significant: an H100 draws up to 700 W and a B200 around 1,000 W, before CPUs, networking and the data centre's overhead (its PUE, often 1.1-1.5). At, say, $0.10 per kWh, a 1 kW accelerator slot running all year with a PUE of 1.3 costs about $1,100 in electricity — small next to the hardware, but large across tens of thousands of GPUs and a binding constraint where power is scarce. Depreciation is the subtle item: in a market where each generation offers several times the tokens per dollar, hardware can become uneconomic before it wears out, so owners assume short useful lives, which raises the effective hourly cost.

Performance per watt and power-limited growth

Where data-centre power is the binding constraint, the figure of merit becomes useful work per joule rather than per dollar. Newer generations and lower-precision formats deliver more tokens per joule, which is why operators replace working hardware and why FP8 and FP4 matter beyond speed. Rack-scale systems concentrate power — NVL72-class racks draw on the order of 100 kW or more and need liquid cooling — so facilities, not just budgets, decide what can be deployed where.

Software ecosystem and portability

Specifications are only reachable through software. NVIDIA's CUDA ecosystem has the broadest kernel coverage (FlashAttention versions, FP8/FP4 paths, NCCL); AMD's ROCm now runs PyTorch, vLLM and SGLang, and AMD highlights large-memory advantages such as fitting bigger models per GPU, but new kernels and model architectures sometimes arrive later; TPUs are programmed mainly through JAX and XLA (PyTorch/XLA exists), which is excellent for teams already there and a porting cost for others. Portable layers — PyTorch, Triton (which targets NVIDIA and AMD), vLLM and SGLang — reduce switching costs, and a multi-vendor strategy improves negotiating position and supply resilience, at the cost of testing and tuning each stack.

Heterogeneous fleets

Large operators rarely run one accelerator type. Prefill and decode can run on different hardware (compute-rich chips for prefill, bandwidth- and memory-rich chips for decode, gpu10); older generations serve small models and batch jobs; and training and serving compete for the newest parts. The scheduling and routing layer that places each workload on the cheapest hardware that meets its targets becomes part of the cost model.

Reading benchmarks critically

Vendor performance claims typically use sparse FLOPs, the most favourable precision, ideal batch sizes and bespoke tuning. MLPerf Training and Inference results from MLCommons are more comparable — fixed models, scenarios (including latency-constrained server scenarios) and rules, with full system disclosures — but the models and sequence lengths may not match yours, and submitters tune heavily. Use benchmarks to build a shortlist, then decide with your own model, precision, sequence-length distribution and latency targets.

Depreciation and the pace of improvement

Suppose a GPU costs $30,000 and is expected to be useful for four years: straight-line depreciation is about $0.86 per hour, before power, networking, space and staff. If a new generation arrives after two years serving three times the tokens per dollar, the old hardware's economic value collapses even though it still works — its tokens now cost three times the market rate. Owners therefore plan for shorter useful lives or for cascading hardware to less demanding work (smaller models, batch jobs, experiments) as it ages. Renters avoid this risk and pay for it in the rental premium.

Spot capacity and training

Interruptible "spot" or pre-emptible capacity is often much cheaper than on-demand. Training can use it if checkpoints are frequent and restarts fast — the same machinery needed anyway for hardware failures at scale (gpu1, gpu9) — but large synchronous jobs need many GPUs at once in one place, and losing a few nodes stalls everyone, so spot capacity fits best for smaller runs, experiments and batch inference.

Availability, supply and constraints

The cheapest accelerator you can't obtain in the quantity, region and timeframe you need is not an option. GPU supply has repeatedly been the constraint on AI projects, and export controls shape what is available where: DeepSeek trained on H800s, a variant with reduced NVLink bandwidth, and designed its parallelism (node-limited expert routing, heavily overlapped communication) around that constraint (gpu8). Contracts, reservations and multi-cloud or multi-vendor plans are part of hardware strategy, not an afterthought.

How teams actually choose

A disciplined hardware decision follows the funnel in the primer and ends with a proof of concept: deploy the real model, at the intended precision, in the intended serving engine, on each shortlisted option; replay production-like traffic (prompt and output length distributions, prefix sharing, burstiness); measure goodput at the latency targets; and compute cost per million tokens at the expected utilisation. The POC regularly overturns datasheet rankings — a kernel missing on one platform, an engine that doesn't yet support FP8 KV on another, or a network that underperforms.

Watching cost in production

Once running, cost per million tokens should be a monitored metric alongside latency: accelerator-hours per day (including idle), tokens served per day per model, and the ratio, broken down by product and model. Changes in traffic shape (longer contexts, more agents) raise cost silently by shrinking batches (gpu10). Levers that often reduce cost without new hardware: quantisation (FP8 weights and KV cache), prefix caching, routing easy requests to smaller models, filling troughs with batch jobs, and right-sizing tensor parallelism.

Common mistakes

Buying for peak FLOPs when the workload is decode. The bottleneck is bandwidth; FLOP-rich chips sit idle. Ignoring utilisation. A self-hosting business case built at 100% utilisation that runs at 35%. Comparing sparse and dense figures across vendors. Forgetting the people: on-call, upgrades and security for a serving stack cost real engineering time. Locking in too long: multi-year commitments in a market where tokens per dollar improve several-fold per generation and API prices fall by an order of magnitude or more per year.

Planning horizons and contracts

Capacity decisions live on different horizons. Serving capacity scales day to day, but only within reserved or available capacity, and new GPU capacity can take weeks or months to obtain. Training clusters are planned months ahead and usually reserved for the length of the run. Contracts trade flexibility for price: one- to three-year reservations are much cheaper per hour than on-demand, but commit you to a generation that may be superseded. A common pattern is to reserve a base load at a discount, cover peaks on demand or with an API, and keep a written plan for migrating to the next generation when its cost per token justifies it.

Making the decision reviewable

Write the decision down in the shape an interviewer — or a finance partner — expects: "Our workload is decode-heavy chat at N requests per second with p95 targets; its bottleneck is bandwidth and KV capacity; the candidates were A and B; a POC measured X and Y tokens per second per replica at target; at our expected utilisation that is $P and $Q per million tokens, against an API at $R; we chose A, with a review date in six months." Every number in it comes from this track's arithmetic or a measurement, which is what makes it defensible and revisable.

Revisit it when any input moves materially: a new hardware generation or price, a model change, a shift in traffic shape, or an API price cut.

Goal: rank three accelerators for serving a 70B model under a latency target by cost per million output tokens, using the serving model from gpu10. Pure Python 3.12, no GPU needed. Prices are illustrative placeholders — replace them with real quotes.

GPUS = {  # name: ($/GPU-hour, HBM GB, TB/s, dense FP8 TFLOP/s)
    "H100": (2.50, 80, 3.35, 1979),
    "H200": (3.50, 141, 4.8, 1979),
    "B200": (5.00, 180, 8.0, 4500),
}
N, KV_TOK, CTX, ITL = 70e9, 164e3, 3000, 0.040   # FP8 weights + KV, 40 ms target
W = N * 1                                          # bytes of FP8 weights

def best(price, hbm, bw, tf):
    out = None
    for tp in (1, 2, 4, 8):
        free = hbm * 1e9 * 0.9 * tp - W
        if free <= 0:
            continue
        for b in range(int(free / (KV_TOK * CTX)), 0, -1):
            mem = (W + b * CTX * KV_TOK) / tp / (bw * 1e12 * 0.8)
            comp = 2 * N * b / (tp * tf * 1e12 * 0.5)
            step = max(mem, comp) + (0.003 if tp > 1 else 0.0005)
            if step <= ITL:
                usd = price * tp / (b / step * 3600 / 1e6)
                if out is None or usd < out[0]:
                    out = (usd, tp, b, b / step)
                break
    return out

for name, spec in GPUS.items():
    usd, tp, b, tps = best(*spec)
    print(f"{name}: TP={tp} batch={b} {tps:,.0f} tok/s  ${usd:.3f}/M busy, ${usd / 0.4:.3f}/M at 40%")

What to look for. All three choose eight GPUs per replica and very large batches, landing near $0.13-0.16 per million output tokens at full utilisation and 2.5× that at 40%. The spread between chips is smaller than the effect of utilisation. Try: change CTX to 30,000 (long contexts — memory capacity becomes decisive), tighten ITL to 0.020, or change prices by 20% and see whether the ranking flips.

Work it out

An 8B model serves 2,500 output tokens/s per H100 at your latency targets. H100s cost $2.50 per GPU-hour. (a) Cost per million output tokens at 100% utilisation? (b) At 30% average utilisation? (c) An API offers a comparable model at $0.40 per million output tokens — when does self-hosting win, and what could change the answer?

Answer

(a) Tokens per hour = 2,500 × 3,600 = 9 million → $2.50 / 9 ≈ $0.28 per million tokens.

(b) At 30% utilisation you pay for idle time as well: $0.28 / 0.3 ≈ $0.93 per million — more than twice the API price.

(c) Self-hosting wins on compute cost only with sustained utilisation above roughly 0.28 / 0.40 ≈ 70%, before counting engineering and on-call time. Things that move the break-even towards self-hosting: filling troughs with batch workloads, spot capacity for interruptible work, FP8 or 4-bit quantisation and prefix caching (more tokens per GPU-hour), a cheaper GPU for a small model (an L4 or L40S may serve an 8B model at lower cost per token), and non-cost needs such as data residency or a fine-tuned model. Things that move it against: API price cuts (historically fast), low or spiky traffic, and the team time a serving stack needs.
