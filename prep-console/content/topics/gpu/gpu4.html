In 2021 and early 2022, the BigScience collaboration trained prototype 104-billion-parameter language models on NVIDIA V100 GPUs, using 16-bit floating-point numbers (FP16) to make training fast enough to be affordable. Several runs diverged: the loss shot up, and it never came back down. Weeks of compute on hundreds of GPUs were lost, and not because the model design or the data was wrong — the numbers themselves had run out of room. FP16 cannot represent anything larger than 65,504, and somewhere in a large network, activations or gradients crossed that line. When the team trained the final 176-billion-parameter BLOOM model on A100s, they switched to a different 16-bit format, bfloat16, which spends its bits differently, and the problem went away.

A second failure is quieter. A team decides to save memory by storing everything — weights included — in bfloat16. Training runs, no errors, no NaNs, but the loss plateaus early and never improves. Each optimiser update is a tiny adjustment, often a thousandth of the weight's value, and in bfloat16 an update that small is simply rounded away. The model is not learning, and nothing tells you why.

Both stories come from the same trade-off. Every number on a GPU is stored in a fixed number of bits, and each format splits them between range (how large and small numbers can get) and precision (how finely they are spaced). Fewer bits are enormously valuable — each halving roughly doubles tensor-core throughput and halves memory traffic, which is where most of the hardware progress from A100 to Blackwell has come from — but each halving also makes overflow, underflow and rounding loss more likely.

This lesson explains how floating-point numbers are built, then walks through every format an ML engineer meets: FP32, TF32, FP16, BF16, FP8 (E4M3 and E5M2) and FP4 (MXFP4 and NVFP4). We see exactly how mixed-precision training works — which operations run in which format, why master weights and accumulators stay in FP32, and how loss scaling rescues FP16 — and then how FP8 and FP4 are made usable with scale factors, using DeepSeek-V3's FP8 training and NVIDIA's 4-bit pretraining as real examples. Finally we cover the production side: debugging NaNs and loss spikes, and why the same prompt can give different answers at temperature 0.

Scientific notation with a fixed budget

You already know how floating point works from scientific notation. Avogadro's number is written 6.022 × 1023: some significant digits (6.022) and a power (23). The power lets one notation cover everything from atoms to galaxies; the significant digits say how precisely you know the value. Now imagine you may only write six characters in total. You could spend them as 6.022 × 1023 — four digits of precision, powers up to 99 — or as 6.0 × 10123 with only two digits but a three-digit power. That is exactly the choice every floating-point format makes, in binary.

A float has three fields: a sign bit; exponent bits, the power of two, which set the range; and mantissa bits (also called the significand), the significant binary digits, which set the precision. More exponent bits mean a wider range; more mantissa bits mean finer spacing between neighbouring values.

How much precision does ML need?

Less than you might expect, and in a specific way. Training uses stochastic gradient descent: each update is computed from a random mini-batch, so gradients are already noisy estimates — typically their batch-to-batch noise is far larger than a 0.4% rounding error. Rounding errors that are random and unbiased mostly average out over millions of updates. What a network does not tolerate is systematic loss: values that vanish to zero, explode to infinity, or updates that are always rounded away in the same direction. So ML formats are designed to keep range generous and accept coarse precision, and the engineering around them — FP32 accumulators, master weights, scale factors — exists to prevent the systematic failures, not to make individual numbers exact. That single idea explains almost every choice in this lesson, from BF16's generous exponent to the per-block scales of FP4.

The two things that go wrong

With too few exponent bits, numbers fall off the ends of the range: a value too large becomes infinity (overflow), and one too small becomes zero (underflow). That was BLOOM's prototype problem in FP16. With too few mantissa bits, numbers survive but lose detail: adding a small number to a large one can leave the large one unchanged (swamping). That is the "stalled training in pure BF16" problem.

Mixed precision in one sentence

Do the heavy matrix multiplications with low-precision inputs, accumulate their sums in FP32, keep a few numerically sensitive operations in FP32, and keep the master copy of the weights and the optimiser state in FP32. That recipe gets nearly all the speed of the low-precision format and nearly all the accuracy of FP32, and it is how essentially every large model is trained. The rest of the lesson explains why each part of the recipe is there, and how it stretches to 8 and 4 bits.

1. How a float is stored

A normal floating-point value is (−1)sign × 1.mantissa × 2exponent − bias. Three details make it work. The leading "1." is implicit — every normal binary number starts with a 1, so it isn't stored, which gains a free bit. The exponent is stored with a bias (127 for FP32 and BF16, 15 for FP16, 7 for FP8 E4M3) so that both negative and positive powers can be written as unsigned bit patterns. And the all-ones exponent is reserved for infinity and NaN ("not a number") in the formats defined by the IEEE 754 standard (FP32 and FP16).

Decode a BF16 example: the bits 0 10000000 1001001. Sign 0: positive. Exponent 10000000 = 128, minus the bias 127 = 1, so ×21. Mantissa 1001001: 1.1001001 in binary = 1 + 1/2 + 1/16 + 1/128 = 1.5703125. Value = 1.5703125 × 2 = 3.140625. That is BF16's best approximation of π — correct to three significant figures. FP32 would give 3.1415927.

Two consequences follow from this layout. First, relative precision is constant: with m mantissa bits, neighbouring values are about 2−m of the number apart. For BF16, 2−7 ≈ 0.78%; for FP16, 2−10 ≈ 0.1%; for FP32, 2−23 ≈ 0.0000119%. Second, absolute spacing grows with magnitude: the gap between neighbours — called the ULP, unit in the last place — doubles every time the value crosses a power of two. In BF16, the gap between 1 and the next number is 0.0078; between 256 and the next it is 2.

Below the smallest normal number, formats use subnormal numbers: the implicit leading 1 becomes 0, filling the gap down to zero with evenly spaced values of reduced precision. FP16's smallest normal is 2−14 ≈ 6.1 × 10−5, and its subnormals reach down to 2−24 ≈ 6 × 10−8. Anything smaller becomes zero.

Two things jump out. BF16, TF32 and FP32 share one range; they differ only in precision. And the 8- and 4-bit formats have ranges so narrow that real tensors cannot be stored in them directly: a typical activation might range from 10−4 to 102, and FP4 has exactly eight magnitudes. TF32 deserves a note because it is on by default in some settings and not others. It is not a storage format: tensors stay in FP32 in memory, and the tensor cores internally round the inputs to 10 mantissa bits for the multiply, accumulating in FP32. That gives FP32 code about 494 dense TFLOP/s on an H100 instead of 67. PyTorch turned TF32 off by default for matmuls in version 1.12 after users were surprised by the precision loss; torch.set_float32_matmul_precision("high") turns it back on, and for training it is almost always safe.

3. Rounding, and the three ways low precision fails

Every arithmetic result is rounded to the nearest representable value (ties to the value with an even last bit). That makes three failure modes, and each one has a signature.

Overflow. In FP16, 300 × 300 = 90,000 exceeds 65,504 and becomes infinity; anything touched by an infinity becomes infinity or NaN, and a NaN loss poisons every weight within one optimiser step. Exponentials are the usual culprit: e12 ≈ 163,000 already overflows FP16, which is why softmax is computed after subtracting the maximum, and in FP32.

Underflow. A gradient of 2 × 10−8 becomes exactly 0 in FP16, so that parameter stops learning. Gradients of deep networks are often this small, especially early in training or in layers far from the loss.

Swamping. In BF16, 1.0 + 0.003 = 1.0, because 0.003 is less than half the ULP at 1.0 (0.0039). The small number disappears without any error or warning. Long sums are vulnerable: add 4,096 products of size 0.001 to a BF16 accumulator and once the running total passes about 0.5, every further term is lost or badly rounded.

4. Why fewer bits are worth the trouble

Two separate gains come from narrower formats, and it helps to keep them apart (gpu2). Bytes: a BF16 tensor is half the size of FP32, FP8 a quarter, FP4 an eighth. Memory-bound operations — element-wise work, decoding, communication between GPUs — speed up in proportion, and more fits in memory. FLOPs: tensor cores process narrower inputs faster. On an H100 the dense figures are about 67 TFLOP/s in FP32 on CUDA cores, 494 in TF32, 989 in BF16 and 1,979 in FP8; a B200 adds FP4 at about 9 PFLOP/s, four times its BF16 rate. The datasheet figures are double these ("with sparsity"); plan with the dense ones. From V100 (FP16) to A100 (BF16, TF32) to H100 (FP8) to Blackwell (FP4), a large share of each generation's headline speed-up is a new, narrower format.

5. Accumulation: why tensor cores sum in FP32

A matmul output element is a dot product: in a layer with d = 8,192, it sums 8,192 products. If the running sum were BF16, the swamping problem from section 3 would appear: by the time the sum reaches, say, 40, its ULP is 0.25, and products smaller than 0.125 would be rounded away one after another. The error grows with the length of the sum.

So tensor cores multiply BF16 (or FP16, FP8, FP4) inputs but keep the running sum — the accumulator — in FP32, with its 23 mantissa bits. Only the final result is rounded to BF16 when written out. This is why "BF16 matmul" is nearly as accurate as FP32 for training purposes: each input carries 0.4% rounding error, but those errors are random and largely cancel in the sum, while the sum itself is exact to about seven digits. Hopper's FP8 tensor cores are an exception worth knowing: DeepSeek found their accumulation keeps only about 14 bits of precision, so their FP8 kernels copy partial sums into FP32 registers on the CUDA cores every 128 elements of the inner dimension.

6. Mixed-precision training in practice

In PyTorch, mixed precision is mostly two lines. torch.autocast decides per operation which precision to use:

model = model.cuda()                      # parameters stay FP32: the master weights
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4)   # FP32 moments
for x, y in loader:
    with torch.autocast(device_type="cuda", dtype=torch.bfloat16):
        loss = loss_fn(model(x), y)       # matmuls run in BF16, softmax/norm/loss in FP32
    loss.backward()                       # gradients land in FP32 .grad
    opt.step(); opt.zero_grad()

Inside the autocast region, operations on PyTorch's lower-precision list — matmuls, linear layers, convolutions, attention via scaled_dot_product_attention — cast their inputs to BF16 and run on tensor cores. Operations on the FP32 list — softmax, log-softmax, cross-entropy, layer and RMS normalisation, exponentials, large reductions — run in FP32. The parameters themselves stay FP32; autocast makes BF16 copies on the fly. That gives the full recipe: BF16 compute, FP32 accumulation (inside the tensor cores), FP32 for sensitive operations, and FP32 master weights and optimiser state.

Large-scale frameworks (Megatron-LM, FSDP with a mixed-precision policy) organise the same idea more explicitly for memory: they store BF16 weights for compute and keep FP32 master weights and Adam moments in the optimiser, which is where gpu3's 16 bytes per parameter come from. Why the master copy matters: a typical update is learning rate × a normalised gradient, often 10−4-10−3 of the weight. In pure BF16, a weight of 1.0 receiving 1,000 updates of 10−4 stays exactly 1.0; in FP32 it reaches 1.1. The lab shows this.

7. FP16 and loss scaling

On GPUs before the A100 (the V100 generation), tensor cores supported FP16 but not BF16, so mixed precision meant FP16 — and FP16's narrow range needed a trick. The 2017 "Mixed Precision Training" paper (Micikevicius et al., NVIDIA and Baidu) introduced loss scaling. Many gradient values in a network are below FP16's smallest subnormal (about 6 × 10−8) and underflow to zero. If you multiply the loss by S before backpropagation, then by the chain rule every gradient is multiplied by S too. With S = 65,536 (216), a gradient of 2 × 10−8 becomes 1.3 × 10−3, comfortably representable. Before the optimiser step, the gradients are converted to FP32 and divided by S.

A fixed S risks overflow instead, so dynamic loss scaling adjusts it: start large; if any gradient is infinite or NaN, skip that step and halve S; after N clean steps (PyTorch's default is 2,000), double S. PyTorch's torch.amp.GradScaler implements this with scaler.scale(loss).backward(), scaler.step(opt) and scaler.update(). It works, but it adds skipped steps and fragility, and it does nothing about activations overflowing in the forward pass — the BLOOM prototypes' problem.

BF16 removed the need: its smallest normal value is about 10−38, so gradients don't underflow, and activations don't overflow. BigScience's BLOOM paper describes exactly this: divergences in 104B-parameter FP16 experiments on V100s, which they attributed to FP16's limited range, and a switch to BF16 for the final 176B model on A100s. Since the A100, BF16 has been the default and FP16 has mostly been a legacy and inference format.

8. FP8: scaling tensors into a narrow range

Hopper introduced two 8-bit float formats, standardised by NVIDIA, Arm and Intel in 2022. E4M3 (4 exponent, 3 mantissa bits) has more precision and a maximum of 448; it drops infinities to gain range. E5M2 (5 exponent, 2 mantissa bits) has more range, up to 57,344. The original recipe uses E4M3 for weights and activations and E5M2 for gradients, whose dynamic range is wider.

Even E5M2 can't hold arbitrary tensors, so each tensor gets a scale factor: before casting, multiply by s so the largest absolute value lands near the format's maximum; after the matmul, divide the result by the product of the input scales. For a tensor with maximum absolute value 10 cast to E4M3, s = 448/10 = 44.8. NVIDIA's Transformer Engine library manages this automatically. Its classic "delayed scaling" mode records each tensor's maximum absolute value ("amax") over a window of recent steps and uses that to set the next step's scale, so it never needs an extra pass over the data; newer modes compute the scale from the current tensor.

The weakness is outliers. One scale per tensor is set by its single largest value. LLM activations are known to contain a few channels with values tens or hundreds of times larger than the rest. If a tensor's typical value is 0.01 and one value is 2,000, the scale maps 2,000 to 448, and 0.01 becomes 0.0022 — in E4M3's subnormal range, where values are spaced 0.002 apart. Most typical values keep one significant bit or become zero. The lab measures this: with one outlier, per-tensor scaling gives about 28% relative error on typical values, and per-block scaling about 3%.

A real case: DeepSeek-V3. DeepSeek trained its 671B-parameter mixture-of-experts model with FP8 matmuls on H800s (the export version of the H100) — the first public demonstration of FP8 pre-training at that scale. Its recipe, from section 3.3 of the report: all three matmuls of each linear layer (forward, input gradient, weight gradient) run in FP8, using E4M3 for every tensor; activations are scaled per 1 × 128 tile (per token, per 128 channels) and weights per 128 × 128 block, with scales computed online from the current values; partial sums are promoted to FP32 every 128 elements to work around Hopper's limited FP8 accumulation precision; and embeddings, the output head, normalisation, attention and the optimiser states stay in BF16 or FP32. The result: training loss within 0.25% of a BF16 baseline, with the matmuls running at FP8 speed.

9. FP4 and micro-scaling

FP4 E2M1 has one sign bit, two exponent bits and one mantissa bit: its magnitudes are 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4 and 6. Per-tensor scaling is hopeless at this width, so FP4 is always used with micro-scaling: small blocks of values share a scale stored alongside them.

  • MXFP4 (Open Compute Project's microscaling specification, backed by AMD, Arm, Intel, Meta, Microsoft, NVIDIA and Qualcomm): blocks of 32 values share one 8-bit power-of-two scale (E8M0). Effective storage 4 + 8/32 = 4.25 bits per value. OpenAI's gpt-oss-120b was released with its mixture-of-experts weights in MXFP4, which is how a 117B-parameter model fits on a single 80 GB GPU.
  • NVFP4 (NVIDIA): blocks of 16 values share an FP8 E4M3 scale — finer blocks and a scale that isn't limited to powers of two — plus one FP32 scale per tensor. About 4.5 bits per value, with smaller error than MXFP4.

Blackwell's tensor cores read the block scales and apply them inside the matrix instruction, so there is no separate dequantisation pass; that is what makes about 9 dense PFLOP/s of FP4 on a B200 usable rather than theoretical. FP4 is well established for inference weights and increasingly for activations. For training, NVIDIA's 2025 paper "Pretraining Large Language Models with NVFP4" trained a 12B hybrid Mamba-Transformer on 10 trillion tokens with NVFP4 matmuls and matched an FP8 baseline — using random Hadamard transforms (a fast rotation that spreads outliers across a block), two-dimensional block scaling so weights quantise consistently in forward and backward passes, stochastic rounding for gradients, and a few sensitive layers kept in higher precision. Each ingredient attacks a specific failure: outliers, inconsistency, and biased rounding.

Stochastic rounding

Round-to-nearest is biased for small updates: 1.0 + 0.003 always rounds to 1.0 in BF16. Stochastic rounding rounds up with probability equal to how far the value is toward the next representable number — 0.003 / 0.0078 ≈ 38% of the time to 1.0078, otherwise to 1.0. Each individual result is noisier, but the expected value is exact, so a thousand small updates move the weight by the right amount on average. This is why low-precision training recipes (NVFP4's gradients, some BF16-only optimisers, Graphcore and Cerebras hardware) use it: it recovers much of what the FP32 master copy provides without the 4 extra bytes. It needs a source of random bits in the kernel, which newer hardware supports in its conversion instructions.

Why outliers exist, and rotations

Large transformers develop a few hidden-state channels with very large magnitudes; the LLM.int8() paper (Dettmers et al., 2022) found such outlier features emerging systematically around the 6.7B-parameter scale. They appear to be used by the model (for example to implement "no-op" attention), so they can't be clipped away. Two families of fixes exist. Rescaling methods such as SmoothQuant move the difficulty from activations into weights by dividing each channel by a factor and multiplying the corresponding weight row. Rotation methods (QuaRot, SpinQuant, the Hadamard transforms in NVFP4 training) multiply activations and weights by an orthogonal matrix that leaves the matmul result unchanged — (xR)(RTW) = xW — but spreads each outlier's energy across all channels of a block, so no single value dominates the scale. Hadamard matrices are used because they can be applied in O(n log n) time, like a fast Fourier transform.

Accumulation order and non-determinism

Floating-point addition is commutative but not associative: in BF16, (1.0 + 0.003) + 0.003 = 1.0, but 1.0 + (0.003 + 0.003) = 1.0078. GPU kernels sum in whatever order their tiling dictates, and libraries choose different tilings for different shapes; atomic additions from many threads finish in unpredictable order. So the same model can produce slightly different logits when the batch size changes. Thinking Machines Lab (2025) showed this is the main reason LLM servers give different completions for the same prompt at temperature 0: your request is batched with a varying amount of other traffic, the batch size changes the reduction order in normalisation, matmul and attention kernels, and near-tied logits flip. Their batch-invariant kernels fix the order regardless of batch size and make outputs reproducible, at a throughput cost. The same issue affects reinforcement-learning (RL) fine-tuning, where the policy's probabilities from the inference engine should match those computed by the trainer; a 2025 paper even argued for FP16 over BF16 in RL for its extra precision, reducing that mismatch.

INT8 and INT4 versus floating point

An integer format with a scale places its values on an evenly spaced grid; a float format places them logarithmically. Weight distributions are roughly bell-shaped, with most values near zero, which suits floats — but a well-chosen integer grid with per-group scales (one scale per 32-128 weights) does almost as well and has long had fast kernels on every GPU. That is why inference quantisation (GPTQ, AWQ, int4 weight-only) developed on integer formats, while training moved to floating point FP8 and FP4, which handle the wider dynamic range of activations and gradients. On Blackwell, FP4 with micro-scaling has tensor-core support that INT4 lacks, and it is displacing INT4 for new deployments (lm12 covers the inference side).

Choosing scale granularity

Scales can be attached per tensor, per row or column (per channel, per token), per block of 16-128 values, or per two-dimensional tile. Finer scales track local magnitudes better but cost storage (an 8-bit scale per 32 values adds a quarter-bit per value), cost arithmetic to apply, and must line up with how the matmul consumes data: a scale per row of A and per column of B can be applied once to each output element, while arbitrary blocks along the inner dimension need hardware support — which is exactly what Blackwell added. A subtle problem is transposition: the backward pass multiplies by WT, so a weight quantised in 1 × 16 blocks along its rows must be re-quantised along its columns, giving slightly different values forward and backward. NVIDIA's NVFP4 training recipe uses 16 × 16 two-dimensional blocks for weights so the same quantised values serve both directions.

What stays in high precision, and why

Across every recipe the same operations stay in BF16 or FP32: the embedding and output layers (a vocabulary-wide softmax is sensitive to small logit errors), normalisation (it divides by a computed standard deviation), attention softmax (exponentials), the loss, optimiser states and master weights, and all reductions across GPUs where precision can accumulate error (gradient all-reduce is usually BF16 or FP32). These cost few FLOPs, so keeping them precise costs little speed, which is why "FP8 training" in practice means FP8 matmuls inside an otherwise mixed-precision model.

Debugging NaNs and loss spikes

The most common precision incident is a loss that turns into NaN or spikes sharply. A practical order of investigation:

  1. Find the first non-finite value. Log the loss, the global gradient norm and per-layer activation maxima every step. A gradient norm that climbs for tens of steps before the NaN points to optimisation dynamics; a sudden jump points to a specific batch or operation. On a reproduction, torch.autograd.set_detect_anomaly(True) reports the operation that produced the first NaN in the backward pass (slowly).
  2. Check precision placement. Is softmax or the loss running in BF16 because someone called .half() or .bfloat16() on the whole model instead of using autocast? Is a custom kernel accumulating in BF16?
  3. Check FP16-specific causes. If on FP16, watch the loss scale: a scale repeatedly collapsing to 1 means real overflow, not underflow.
  4. Replay the batch. Save the data indices for each step so the offending batch can be re-run; corrupt or extreme data (a very long repeated token sequence) is a frequent root cause.

Standard mitigations are gradient clipping (a maximum global norm, typically 1.0), a lower peak learning rate or longer warm-up, QK normalisation (normalising queries and keys before the attention dot product so attention logits can't grow without bound), and a small z-loss penalty on the log of the softmax normaliser to keep output logits from drifting large. Large training runs routinely roll back to a checkpoint a few hundred steps before a spike and skip the offending data — the published OPT-175B logbook from Meta describes many such restarts, including learning-rate reductions after repeated divergences.

Adopting FP8 without surprises

Teams moving from BF16 to FP8 typically: use Transformer Engine layers or a framework integration (Megatron-LM, NeMo, torchao's float8 training) rather than hand-written scaling; keep the first and last layers in BF16; run the FP8 and BF16 configurations side by side for a few billion tokens and compare loss curves; and watch for slowly growing divergence rather than immediate failure. The speed gain depends on how much of the step is matmul: typical end-to-end improvements are 1.3-1.5× for large dense models, less for small ones where the non-matmul work and communication are a bigger share. On A100s there is no FP8 tensor-core support, so FP8 there saves memory at best.

Inference: formats as a serving knob

For serving, formats are chosen per tensor type: FP8 weights and activations on Hopper and Blackwell (vLLM and TensorRT-LLM support "FP8 dynamic" quantisation with little accuracy loss for most models), an FP8 KV cache to double concurrent sequences, and FP4 or INT4 weights where memory or bandwidth is the constraint. Meta released an official FP8-quantised Llama 3.1 405B so it could be served on a single 8×H100 node; gpt-oss-120b's MXFP4 experts let it fit on one 80 GB GPU. Each change should be validated on the product's own evaluation set, because average benchmark scores can hide regressions on long-context or maths tasks, which are more sensitive to precision.

Reproducibility in production

If exact reproducibility matters — regression tests, audits, RL training — pin library versions and kernel choices (torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True), fixed seeds, CUBLAS_WORKSPACE_CONFIG), and for serving use batch-invariant kernels or fixed batch sizes. Otherwise, write evaluation and test tolerances that expect small numerical differences between runs, GPUs and library versions, rather than bit-exact matches.

Goal: see swamping, underflow, the value of FP32 master weights and the value of per-block scaling, by simulating the formats with the Python 3.12 standard library (no GPU or NumPy needed).

import math, random, struct

def bf16(x):                       # round FP32 to BF16, nearest-even
    b = struct.unpack("<I", struct.pack("<f", x))[0]
    b = (b + 0x7FFF + ((b >> 16) & 1)) & 0xFFFF0000
    return struct.unpack("<f", struct.pack("<I", b))[0]

def fp16(x):
    try: return struct.unpack("<e", struct.pack("<e", x))[0]
    except OverflowError: return math.inf

def fp8_e4m3(x):                   # 3 mantissa bits, max 448, subnormals below 2**-6
    if x == 0: return 0.0
    s, a = math.copysign(1, x), min(abs(x), 448.0)
    step = 2.0 ** (max(math.floor(math.log2(a)), -6) - 3)
    return s * round(a / step) * step

print("1.0 + 0.003 in BF16 ->", bf16(1.0 + 0.003))
print("FP16: 70000 ->", fp16(70000.0), "| 2e-8 ->", fp16(2e-8),
      "| 2e-8 * 65536 ->", fp16(2e-8 * 65536))

w_bf16, w_master = 1.0, 1.0        # 1,000 updates of 1e-4
for _ in range(1000):
    w_bf16 = bf16(w_bf16 + 1e-4)
    w_master += 1e-4
print(f"BF16-only weight {w_bf16}, FP32 master {w_master:.4f}")

random.seed(0)
x = [random.gauss(0, 0.01) for _ in range(1024)]
x[7] = 2000.0                      # one outlier
def rel_err(vals, block):          # error on the typical values only
    e = 0.0
    for i in range(0, len(vals), block):
        blk = vals[i:i + block]
        s = max(abs(v) for v in blk) / 448.0
        e += sum(abs(fp8_e4m3(v / s) * s - v) for v in blk if abs(v) < 1)
    return e / sum(abs(v) for v in vals if abs(v) < 1)
print(f"per-tensor scale: {rel_err(x, 1024):.1%}   per-32 block: {rel_err(x, 32):.1%}")

What to look for. 1.0 + 0.003 stays 1.0 in BF16 (swamping). FP16 turns 70,000 into infinity and 2 × 10−8 into 0, but the loss-scaled value survives. The BF16-only weight is still exactly 1.0 after 1,000 updates while the FP32 master reaches 1.1 — the reason for master weights. One outlier makes per-tensor FP8 scaling lose about 28% of the typical values' magnitude; per-block scaling cuts that to about 3%.

Work it out

(a) A weight equals 1.0 in BF16. Which of the updates +0.001, +0.003 and +0.005 change it? (b) An FP16 gradient is 2 × 10−8. What happens to it, and what does a loss scale of 65,536 do? (c) A tensor holds values up to 1,000 with most around 0.01; what goes wrong with per-tensor FP8 E4M3 scaling, and what fixes it?

Answer

(a) BF16 spacing just above 1.0 is 2−7 = 0.0078; values round to the nearest representable number, so any update below half the spacing (0.0039) is lost. +0.001 and +0.003 vanish; +0.005 rounds up to 1.0078 — which overshoots it by 56%. Pure BF16 either drops updates or distorts them; hence FP32 master weights (or stochastic rounding).

(b) FP16's smallest subnormal is 2−24 ≈ 6 × 10−8, so 2 × 10−8 rounds to 0 and that parameter gets no gradient. Scaling the loss by 65,536 scales every gradient by the same factor (chain rule), making it about 1.3 × 10−3 — comfortably representable with full FP16 precision; the gradients are divided by 65,536 in FP32 before the update. If some gradient then overflows, dynamic loss scaling skips the step and halves the scale.

(c) The scale must map 1,000 to at most 448, i.e. multiply by about 0.45. Then 0.01 becomes about 0.0045 — below E4M3's smallest normal value (2−6 ≈ 0.0156) and in its subnormal range, where values are spaced 2−9 ≈ 0.002 apart — so typical values keep one or two significant bits or round to zero. One outlier destroyed the precision of everything else. Fixes: finer-grained scales (per 1 × 128 tile or per 32-value block, as in DeepSeek-V3 and the MX formats), per-channel scales if the outlier lives in a fixed channel, or a rotation (Hadamard transform) that spreads the outlier across the block before quantising.
