A team's model trains well on one 8-GPU server. They move to eight servers — 64 GPUs — expecting eight times the throughput and get 5.5 times. At 512 GPUs they get barely more than at 256. Nothing is broken: the GPUs are just as fast as before. What changed is that they now spend a growing share of each step talking to each other — averaging gradients, gathering sharded parameters, passing activations between pipeline stages — and the links between servers are about nine times slower than the links inside one. On some steps, one slow GPU makes every other GPU wait. On a bad day, a single faulty cable makes the whole job hang.

Every large model is trained this way. Meta trained Llama 3 405B on up to 16,384 H100s; ByteDance reported 55.2% MFU training a 175B model on 12,288 GPUs, and identified communication overlap, network tuning and straggler detection as the difference between that and far lower numbers. Inference needs communication too: a 70B model served across eight GPUs with tensor parallelism exchanges activations twice per layer for every generated token, and mixture-of-experts models shuffle tokens between GPUs at every expert layer. For large systems, the network is as much a part of the computer as the GPUs.

This lesson covers the communication layer from the ground up. First the collective operations that distributed training and inference are built from — all-reduce, reduce-scatter, all-gather, all-to-all — and why an all-reduce is a reduce-scatter plus an all-gather. Then the physical links, from NVLink and NVSwitch inside a server or rack to InfiniBand and Ethernet between servers, with verified bandwidths for each generation. Then the cost model and the ring algorithm that makes all-reduce nearly independent of GPU count, plus the tree and in-network alternatives. Finally, each parallelism strategy's communication pattern with worked numbers — which explains the rule "tensor parallelism inside the node, data parallelism across nodes" — and what goes wrong in production at thousands of GPUs.

Summing receipts around a table

Four friends each have a shopping bag's worth of receipts, sorted into four categories — food, travel, books, other — and all four need to know the group's total per category. One way: everyone sends their whole bag to one person, who adds everything and sends the totals back. That person becomes a bottleneck, handling four bags in and four sets of totals out while everyone else waits. The better way: sit in a circle. Each friend is responsible for one category. In each round, everyone passes one category's running total to their left neighbour, who adds their own receipts for that category. After three rounds, each friend holds the complete total for one category. Three more rounds of passing the finished totals around, and everyone has all four. Everyone was busy every round, and no one handled more than their share. That is the ring all-reduce.

The GPU version has the same parts: each GPU holds a tensor (say, its gradients), and they must all end up with the sum. The analogy's limit: GPUs pass data over physical links with fixed bandwidths, some links are far faster than others, and every round waits for the slowest friend.

The four collectives

All-reduce is the workhorse of data parallelism: every GPU computed gradients on its own slice of the batch, and all need the sum. Reduce-scatter and all-gather are its two halves, and they matter separately for sharded training (gpu9), where each GPU only needs its own slice of the summed gradients and gathers full parameters just before using them. All-to-all is the irregular one, used by mixture-of-experts models to send each token to the GPU holding its chosen expert.

The links, in one sentence each

Inside a server, GPUs talk over NVLink through NVSwitch chips at up to 450 GB/s in each direction per H100 (900 GB/s per B200). Between servers, each GPU typically has its own network card on an InfiniBand or Ethernet fabric at 400 Gb/s — 50 GB/s, note the bits — or 800 Gb/s on newer systems. The gap between the two, roughly nine to one on H100 systems, shapes almost every decision about how to split a model across GPUs.

A first estimate

How long does it take eight H100s in one server to average the gradients of a 7B-parameter model? The gradients are 14 GB in BF16. The ring algorithm below makes each GPU send about 1.75 × 14 ≈ 24.5 GB, and at 450 GB/s that is about 55 ms — small next to a training step of a few seconds. Put the same eight GPUs in eight different servers, so every byte crosses a 50 GB/s network link, and it becomes about half a second. Same data, same algorithm, nine times the cost, purely because of which link it travels over. Most of this lesson is a more careful version of that calculation.

1. Why GPUs have to talk: the parallelisms and what they exchange

dl9 introduced the ways of splitting training across GPUs; here is what each one sends.

  • Data parallelism (DP): every GPU holds the whole model and processes a different slice of the batch. After the backward pass, gradients must be summed across GPUs so every copy applies the same update: an all-reduce of the full gradient, once per step.
  • Sharded data parallelism (ZeRO-3 / FSDP, gpu9): each GPU holds only a slice of the parameters, gradients and optimiser state. Before each layer it all-gathers that layer's parameters; after computing gradients it reduce-scatters them so each GPU gets the summed slice it owns.
  • Tensor parallelism (TP): each weight matrix is split across GPUs, so each GPU computes part of every layer's output. Partial results are combined with an all-reduce (or reduce-scatter plus all-gather) of activations — twice per transformer layer in the forward pass and twice in the backward pass.
  • Pipeline parallelism (PP): layers are split into stages on different GPUs; activations are sent point to point from one stage to the next, and gradients flow back.
  • Expert parallelism (EP): in mixture-of-experts models, experts live on different GPUs; tokens are sent to their experts and back with all-to-all.
  • Context parallelism (CP): a long sequence is split across GPUs, which exchange keys and values for attention (gpu7).

Each strategy moves a different amount of data at a different frequency, with more or less computation available to hide it behind. That is what decides which links it can tolerate.

Three details trip people up. NVIDIA quotes NVLink as bidirectional totals: "900 GB/s" means 450 GB/s out and 450 GB/s in simultaneously. Network speeds are in bits: 400 Gb/s is 50 GB/s. And NVSwitch is what makes NVLink all-to-all: in an 8-GPU H100 server, each GPU's 18 NVLinks connect to four NVSwitch chips, so any GPU can talk to any other at full speed, rather than only to neighbours. In a GB200 NVL72 rack, NVSwitch trays connect all 72 GPUs into one NVLink domain.

Between servers, the network cards use RDMA (remote direct memory access): the card reads and writes memory directly without involving either CPU, and with GPUDirect RDMA it reads and writes GPU memory directly, so data goes HBM → PCIe → NIC → network → NIC → PCIe → HBM without staging in host memory. Two families of network carry it: InfiniBand, designed for high-performance computing (HPC) with credit-based lossless flow control and in-switch reduction, and RoCE (RDMA over Converged Ethernet), which runs RDMA on Ethernet switches with priority flow control and congestion notification. Both run the largest clusters in the world.

3. The alpha-beta cost model

Sending n bytes as one message costs roughly α + n/β: a fixed latency α (software launch, NIC processing, switch hops — a few microseconds) plus the bytes divided by the bandwidth β. A 1 KB message over a 50 GB/s link costs about 5 µs of latency and 0.02 µs of transfer: latency-bound. A 1 GB message costs 5 µs of latency and 20 ms of transfer: bandwidth-bound. Collectives are sequences of such messages, so their cost has the same two parts, and the right algorithm depends on which dominates.

4. Ring all-reduce, step by step

Arrange p GPUs in a ring; GPU r sends only to GPU r+1 and receives only from r−1. Split each GPU's n-byte tensor into p chunks of n/p bytes.

Reduce-scatter phase (p − 1 steps): in step 1, GPU r sends its chunk r to GPU r+1, which adds it to its own chunk r. In step 2, each GPU sends the partial sum it just received and updated to the next GPU, which adds its own contribution again. After p − 1 steps, each chunk has visited every GPU and been summed, and each GPU holds one fully reduced chunk. All-gather phase (p − 1 more steps): the finished chunks travel around the ring, being copied rather than added, until every GPU has every reduced chunk. The lab simulates this on four GPUs and checks the result.

Count the bytes: 2(p − 1) steps, each sending n/p bytes, so each GPU sends 2(p − 1)/p × n bytes and receives the same. For p = 8 that is 1.75n; for p = 1,024, 1.998n. The time, in the alpha-beta model:

T_ring ≈ 2(p − 1)·α  +  2(p − 1)/p · n / β

The bandwidth term is nearly independent of p — the reason ring all-reduce "scales" — and it is essentially optimal: without help from the network, every GPU must send out about (p − 1)/p of its data to be reduced and receive about the same back. The latency term grows linearly with p, so for small messages on many GPUs the ring is slow.

Worked example. All-reducing the BF16 gradients of a 7B model (14 GB) across 8 GPUs over NVLink at 450 GB/s each way: 2 × 7/8 × 14 GB = 24.5 GB per GPU, about 54 ms — nearer 65 ms at a realistic 80-85% of link speed. The same all-reduce across 8 GPUs in 8 different servers at 50 GB/s each: about 490 ms.

5. Beyond the ring: trees, hierarchy and in-network reduction

Trees. A tree all-reduce sums data up a tree and broadcasts it back down, needing about 2 log₂ p steps instead of 2(p − 1); NCCL uses a "double binary tree" (two trees, each carrying half the data, so all links are used) and picks tree or ring per message size and topology. For small messages at large p, trees win on latency.

Hierarchy. A multi-node all-reduce is built in levels that match the links: reduce-scatter among the 8 GPUs of each node over NVLink, so each GPU holds 1/8 of its node's sum; all-reduce those slices across nodes over the network, each GPU talking to its counterpart ("same rail") in other nodes; then all-gather within each node over NVLink. The slow network carries only 1/8 of the data per GPU, and each GPU's own NIC is used in parallel.

In-network reduction. InfiniBand switches with SHARP, and NVSwitch with NVLink SHARP (NVLS), can add data as it passes through. Each GPU sends its tensor into the switch once and receives the summed result once: about n bytes each way per GPU instead of the ring's 2(p − 1)/p × n, in a number of steps that doesn't grow with p. That roughly halves all-reduce time for large messages and removes the reduction arithmetic from the GPUs' SMs.

6. Measuring what you actually get

Before planning around datasheets, teams measure with nccl-tests, NVIDIA's benchmark suite, which reports two numbers per message size. Algorithm bandwidth is size divided by time. Bus bandwidth multiplies it by the fraction of data each GPU must move — 2(p − 1)/p for all-reduce, (p − 1)/p for all-gather, reduce-scatter and all-to-all — so it can be compared directly with link speed regardless of p. On healthy hardware, large-message bus bandwidth reaches roughly 70-90% of the link's per-direction speed; small messages are latency-bound and far lower. A node whose numbers fall well short has a problem — a degraded NVLink, a mis-seated NIC, a bad cable, a misconfigured network — and is exactly the kind of node that later becomes a straggler.

7. Each parallelism's bill, with numbers

Data parallelism. One all-reduce of all gradients per step: 2N bytes in BF16. For a 7B model on 64 GPUs in 8 nodes, hierarchically: 24.5 GB per GPU over NVLink (about 55 ms) plus 2 × 7/8 × 1.75 GB ≈ 3.1 GB per GPU over the network (about 60 ms). Against a step of a few seconds of compute, and overlapped with the backward pass, that is small. Data parallelism tolerates slow links because it communicates once per step and can overlap.

Tensor parallelism. Take a 70B model (d = 8,192, 80 layers) with TP = 8 and a micro-batch of 8,192 tokens. Each all-reduce carries the full activation, 8,192 × 8,192 × 2 bytes = 134 MB; there are two per layer forward and two backward, so 320 per step. Over NVLink each costs about 2 × 7/8 × 134 MB / 450 GB/s ≈ 0.52 ms: about 170 ms per step. The step's compute per GPU is 6 × 70 × 109 × 8,192 / 8 ≈ 4.3 × 1014 FLOPs — about 0.87 s at 50% of peak. Communication is roughly 20% of compute and sits on the critical path (each layer waits for its all-reduce), so it can only partly be hidden. Over a 50 GB/s network each all-reduce would take nine times longer, about 1.5 s per step — more than the compute. That arithmetic is the rule: tensor parallelism stays inside an NVLink domain.

Pipeline parallelism. Only the activations at stage boundaries move, point to point: one 134 MB tensor per micro-batch per boundary in the example above, about 2.7 ms at 50 GB/s. Cheap enough for the network, which is why PP spans nodes; its cost is idle "bubbles" in the schedule rather than bandwidth (dl9).

Expert parallelism. Each MoE layer needs two all-to-alls: dispatch tokens to the GPUs holding their experts and combine the results. The volume is tokens × hidden size × top-k experts per token, and it is irregular, depending on routing. DeepSeek-V3's report shows the engineering this requires across nodes: each token may only be routed to experts on at most four nodes, limiting InfiniBand traffic; tokens cross the network once to the target node and are then forwarded to the right GPU over NVLink; and custom all-to-all kernels using about 20 of the H800's 132 SMs run concurrently with computation so the exchange is hidden.

8. Overlap: paying only for exposed communication

Communication can run while the GPU computes, because it uses the NICs, NVLink and a few SMs while the tensor cores work on something else. PyTorch's DistributedDataParallel groups gradients into buckets (25 MB by default) and launches each bucket's all-reduce as soon as the backward pass has produced all its gradients. The backward pass runs from the last layer to the first, so the last layers' gradients are being communicated while earlier layers are still computing, and only the final bucket's communication is exposed. FSDP prefetches the next layer's parameters (all-gather) during the current layer's computation and reduce-scatters gradients as they appear. Tensor parallelism is hardest to overlap because each layer needs its result immediately; techniques such as splitting a matmul into chunks and communicating one chunk while computing the next recover part of it. When profiling, the number to watch is exposed communication: time when the GPU is idle waiting for a collective.

Communication competes with computation for SMs

NCCL's collectives are themselves GPU kernels: they use SMs to move data between buffers, run the reduction arithmetic and drive the NVLink and network transfers. While they overlap with computation, they take some SMs and memory bandwidth away from it, so "overlapped" communication still slows the concurrent matmuls somewhat. Large systems budget this explicitly — DeepSeek-V3 restricted its communication kernels to about 20 SMs per GPU — and newer hardware offloads more of the work: NVLink SHARP performs reductions in the switch, and copy engines and TMA-based transfers can move data without occupying many SMs. When a profile shows matmuls running slower during communication phases, SM and bandwidth contention is the likely reason.

Topology, rails and oversubscription

Large clusters use multi-layer Clos (fat-tree) networks: leaf switches connect to servers, spine switches connect leaves, sometimes with a third layer. In a rail-optimised design, NIC i of every server connects to the same set of leaf switches (rail i), because hierarchical collectives mostly exchange data between same-index GPUs in different servers; that traffic then crosses only one switch. A network is oversubscribed when upper layers have less total bandwidth than the lower layers feeding them — cheaper, but collectives that must cross the spine slow down. Job placement matters for the same reason: a job whose ranks are scattered across distant parts of the fabric sees lower bandwidth than one packed under the same leaves, so schedulers try to allocate topologically close nodes.

Why AI traffic is hard on networks

Ordinary data-centre traffic is many small, independent flows, and hashing each flow onto one of several equal-cost paths (ECMP) spreads the load well. Training traffic is the opposite: a small number of huge, long-lived flows between fixed pairs, all starting at the same moment when a collective begins. Hashing can put two large flows on the same link while a parallel link sits idle, halving their speed and stalling the collective for every GPU. Meta's account of the RoCE networks behind Llama 3 describes working through several routing schemes — including splitting traffic across more connections and routing that is aware of the collective's flows — plus congestion-control tuning, to get near-optimal throughput. InfiniBand fabrics address the same problem with adaptive routing in the switches.

Stragglers: the maximum of many

A synchronous collective finishes when its slowest participant does. If each GPU's step time varies randomly by a few percent, the expected maximum over p GPUs grows with p, so large jobs are systematically slower than their average GPU. Persistent stragglers are worse: a GPU throttling for heat, a node with a failing NIC, a host process stealing CPU. ByteDance's MegaScale report describes tooling that records per-rank timing of every collective to find the rank everyone is waiting for, and it treats straggler mitigation as a core part of reaching 55% MFU at 12,288 GPUs. The operational rule: monitor per-rank step and collective times, not just the job average, and evict slow nodes quickly.

GPU-initiated communication

Classic NCCL collectives are launched by the host as separate kernels. For irregular, fine-grained patterns — MoE dispatch where each token goes to a different place — it is faster for GPU threads to initiate transfers themselves, writing directly into remote GPUs' memory over NVLink or issuing RDMA operations from inside a kernel. NVIDIA's NVSHMEM library provides this, and DeepSeek's open-source DeepEP all-to-all library builds MoE dispatch and combine on it, including low-latency modes for inference decoding. The trend is towards communication fused into compute kernels, the same way fusion (gpu6) removed boundaries between computations.

Scale-up versus scale-out

NVIDIA's rack-scale systems blur the line between the two networks. In a GB200 NVL72, 72 GPUs share one NVLink domain at 1.8 TB/s each, so the "fast network" is a rack, not a server. That lets tensor and expert parallelism span 72 GPUs, which matters most for serving large MoE models, where all-to-all traffic over InfiniBand would otherwise dominate latency. Rubin's NVLink 6 doubles per-GPU NVLink bandwidth again to 3.6 TB/s. Across racks, the scale-out network (800 Gb/s per GPU on the newest systems) remains an order of magnitude slower, so the layout rule persists at a larger granularity: the chattiest parallelism goes inside the NVLink domain.

Burn-in and acceptance testing

Before a cluster (or a newly repaired node) runs production jobs, teams run nccl-tests all-reduce, all-gather and all-to-all across it and compare bus bandwidth with expected values, plus GPU stress tests. A single node with a degraded NVLink or a NIC negotiating a lower speed will pass basic health checks and then quietly slow every job it joins. nvidia-smi topo -m shows how GPUs, NICs and CPUs are connected in a server — useful to confirm that each GPU has a NIC on its own PCIe switch rather than sharing one.

Hangs, time-outs and failures

When one rank in a collective dies or stalls, the others don't get an error; they wait. A training job at 10,000 GPUs "hangs" with every GPU at 100% utilisation (spinning in a NCCL kernel) and no progress. PyTorch's NCCL watchdog aborts collectives that exceed a time-out (timeout in init_process_group, typically minutes) so the job fails and can restart from a checkpoint instead of hanging for hours; flight-recorder dumps of in-flight collectives on each rank show which rank never arrived. NCCL_DEBUG=INFO logs the topology NCCL detected and the algorithms it chose, the first thing to check when bandwidth is unexpectedly low. At scale, failure is routine: in the Llama 3 405B run, network switches and cables caused 35 of the 419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days (8.4%), on top of GPU and memory failures. Jobs are designed for it — frequent asynchronous checkpoints, automatic detection and exclusion of bad nodes, and restart times of minutes.

Common performance problems and their signatures

Scaling efficiency collapses when a job crosses from one node to two. Something that should be on NVLink is on the network — usually tensor parallelism laid out across nodes, or process groups created in an order that doesn't match the physical topology.

FSDP is fast on 8 GPUs and slow on 512. Sharding across all 512 means every layer's parameters are all-gathered across the network. Hybrid sharding (shard within a node, replicate across nodes) keeps the per-layer traffic on NVLink.

Periodic slow steps across the whole job. A straggler — check per-rank step times; or synchronised background work such as checkpoint writes saturating the network.

MoE throughput much lower than a dense model of equal active size. All-to-all load imbalance or network-bound dispatch; look at expert load statistics and all-to-all time, and consider capacity limits, node-limited routing or larger NVLink domains.

Planning with the numbers

Keep the unit conversions in the plan explicit, because they cause more planning errors than anything else: gigabits versus gigabytes, and bidirectional versus per-direction NVLink figures.

A back-of-envelope communication budget belongs in every parallelism plan: bytes per collective, collectives per step, link each one uses, and how much can overlap. Then check it against nccl-tests numbers for the actual cluster, and against a profile of the first real steps. The published large-scale runs — Meta's Llama 3 on RoCE, ByteDance's MegaScale, DeepSeek-V3 on H800s with reduced NVLink bandwidth — all read as the same story: model the communication, lay parallelism out along the link hierarchy, overlap aggressively, and build tooling to find the slowest component.

Goal: simulate a ring all-reduce to see that it is correct and that each GPU sends 2(p−1)/p × n values, then use the alpha-beta model to compare NVLink with the network for three message sizes. Pure Python 3.12; no GPUs needed.

import random
random.seed(1)
p, n = 4, 8                                   # GPUs, values per GPU
data = [[random.randint(0, 9) for _ in range(n)] for _ in range(p)]
expected = [sum(col) for col in zip(*data)]
buf, c, sent = [row[:] for row in data], n // p, 0
for step in range(p - 1):                     # reduce-scatter
    msgs = [(r, (r - step) % p) for r in range(p)]
    payload = [buf[r][k * c:(k + 1) * c] for r, k in msgs]
    for (r, k), vals in zip(msgs, payload):
        for i, v in enumerate(vals):
            buf[(r + 1) % p][k * c + i] += v
        sent += c
for step in range(p - 1):                     # all-gather
    msgs = [(r, (r + 1 - step) % p) for r in range(p)]
    payload = [buf[r][k * c:(k + 1) * c] for r, k in msgs]
    for (r, k), vals in zip(msgs, payload):
        buf[(r + 1) % p][k * c:(k + 1) * c] = vals
        sent += c
print("correct:", all(b == expected for b in buf), "| sent per GPU:", sent // p)

def ring(nbytes, p, bw, alpha=5e-6):          # bw in bytes/s per direction
    return 2 * (p - 1) * alpha + 2 * (p - 1) / p * nbytes / bw
for label, nb in [("7B grads", 14e9), ("TP activation", 134e6), ("1 KB", 1e3)]:
    print(f"{label:14s} NVLink {ring(nb, 8, 450e9) * 1e3:8.3f} ms   "
          f"400G network {ring(nb, 8, 50e9) * 1e3:8.3f} ms")

What to look for. The simulation prints correct: True and 12 values sent per GPU — exactly 2(p−1)/p × n for p = 4, n = 8. Change p to 8 and n to 16: the per-GPU count becomes 28 = 1.75n, approaching 2n. In the cost table, the 7B gradients take about 55 ms on NVLink and 490 ms across the network; one tensor-parallel activation takes about 0.6 ms versus 4.7 ms (and there are hundreds per step); the 1 KB message costs about 70 µs either way, because it is pure latency.

Work it out

Data-parallel training of a 7B model with BF16 gradients (14 GB). (a) How long does a ring all-reduce take across 8 GPUs in one node over NVLink at 450 GB/s per direction? (b) Across 16 nodes (128 GPUs) with 50 GB/s per GPU between nodes, using a hierarchical scheme (reduce-scatter inside nodes, all-reduce of each 1/8 shard across nodes, all-gather inside nodes)? (c) Is that a problem if a step's compute takes 2 s, and what would change your answer?

Answer

(a) Data sent per GPU = 2 × 7/8 × 14 GB = 24.5 GB → 24.5 / 450 ≈ 54 ms, about 65 ms at a realistic 80-85% of link speed.

(b) Intra-node reduce-scatter plus all-gather together send the same 24.5 GB per GPU over NVLink ≈ 54 ms. Each GPU then all-reduces its 14/8 = 1.75 GB shard with its counterparts in the other 15 nodes: 2 × 15/16 × 1.75 ≈ 3.3 GB at 50 GB/s ≈ 66 ms, with all eight NICs per node working in parallel. Total ≈ 120 ms, more in practice. Note the network part barely grows with the number of nodes: at 128 nodes it would be 2 × 127/128 × 1.75 ≈ 3.5 GB, still about 70 ms plus a larger latency term.

(c) Against 2 s of compute it is about 6% if fully exposed, and bucketed all-reduce overlapped with the backward pass hides most of it — data parallelism scales well here. It becomes a problem with a much larger model (bytes grow with N), a smaller per-GPU batch (less compute to hide behind, the usual consequence of adding GPUs at a fixed global batch), a slower or oversubscribed network, or stragglers. Then the remedies are sharding within nodes (gpu9), larger per-GPU batches or gradient accumulation (communicate once every several micro-batches), in-network reduction, or lower-precision gradient communication.
