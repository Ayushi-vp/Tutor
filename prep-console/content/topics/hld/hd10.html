Every time someone opens a large social or content app, a few slots in their feed are reserved for ads, and within a tenth of a second a system must decide which of millions of active ads fills them. Advertisers have said what they will pay: two dollars per click for a running-shoe brand, eighty cents per click for a meal-kit company, fifty dollars per purchase for a furniture store. The platform's revenue, the advertisers' return and the user's tolerance for ads all depend on one number computed for each candidate ad: the probability that this person, in this moment, will click it.

Get that number wrong and every party loses at once. If the model over-predicts clicks for the shoe brand, the shoe ad wins slots it should not and pays too little for them; the meal-kit company loses impressions it deserved; the user sees an ad they ignore; the platform earns less. If the model is right on average but drifts by 10% on mobile in one country, nobody notices for a week, and that week costs real money. Ads click-through-rate (CTR) prediction is one of the oldest large-scale machine-learning problems in industry precisely because small errors are multiplied by billions of impressions and by bids.

That makes it a classic senior design interview with a twist: unlike most ranking problems, getting the order right is not enough. The probability itself enters an auction, so the interview probes calibration, the metrics that measure it (log loss and normalised entropy), and every mechanism that silently distorts it: sampling, delayed labels, stale models, feedback from the auction itself. It also probes scale: the feature spaces are enormous and sparse, the traffic is a hundred thousand requests a second, and the model must learn from yesterday, or ideally from the last hour.

This lesson follows the interview: requirements including revenue, calibration, latency, cost and safety; estimates by hand for request rate, candidates, features, fleet, logs and training data; the API and data model; the architecture from ad request to auction; deep dives on why pCTR × bid ranks ads, log loss, NE and calibration on real numbers, sampling and its correction, feature hashing and crosses, the joiner and delayed conversions, and online learning; then evaluation, failure modes, the Facebook and Google papers that defined the field, and a lab that computes log loss, NE and calibration on simulated CTRs. The models themselves (logistic regression, boosted trees, deep networks with embeddings and crosses) are taught in rec6; here the subject is the system around them.

The problem in one sentence

For each ad request (a user, a placement, a moment), estimate for every eligible ad the probability of a click (and, for advertisers who pay per conversion, of a conversion after the click), accurately enough that multiplying it by bids picks the right winners and sets fair prices, within tens of milliseconds, for a hundred thousand requests a second, with a model that keeps up with a market that changes hourly.

The mental model: a bookmaker, not a judge

A judge at a talent show only has to order the contestants; nobody cares whether the winner scored 9.1 or 8.7. A bookmaker sets odds, and the odds are money: if the bookmaker thinks a horse has a 30% chance when the truth is 15%, every bet on that horse is mispriced, regardless of whether the horses were ranked correctly. A CTR model in an ads system is a bookmaker. Its outputs are multiplied by bids and turned into prices, so they must be right as probabilities, which is the property called calibration: among all impressions where the model said 2%, about 2% should be clicked.

The analogy also shows the feedback problem. A bookmaker's odds change how people bet, which changes the data the bookmaker learns from. An ads model decides which ads are shown, and it only ever learns about ads it showed. Where the analogy breaks: a bookmaker sees each race's result within minutes, while an ads model waits seconds for a click and sometimes a week for a purchase, and must decide what to do with impressions whose outcome is not yet known.

A little vocabulary before the numbers. An impression is one showing of an ad to one user. A click is the user tapping it; a conversion is the action the advertiser ultimately wants (a purchase, an app install, a sign-up), reported later. Advertisers choose how they pay: per click (CPC, cost per click), per thousand impressions (CPM, cost per mille), or per conversion. The platform converts every kind of bid into one common currency, expected revenue per thousand impressions (eCPM), so that a CPC bid, a CPM bid and a conversion bid can compete in the same auction. Turning a CPC bid into eCPM needs pCTR; turning a conversion bid into eCPM needs pCTR and pCVR. That conversion of currencies is where the model's probabilities enter the money.

Two layers of prediction usually exist. pCTR is the probability of a click given an impression. pCVR is the probability of a conversion (a purchase, an install) given a click. An advertiser who pays per click needs pCTR; one who pays per conversion needs pCTR × pCVR, because the platform shows an impression and the conversion has to happen through a click. A reader who stops here knows the shape: a funnel that ends in an auction, a model whose probabilities must be calibrated, and a learning loop that must be fresh but careful.

1 · Requirements

Functional. For each ad request from the feed, return up to three ads chosen by an auction among eligible ads, with each winner's price. Eligibility comes from targeting (audience, location, schedule), budget and policy. Predict pCTR for every ad that reaches the final stage and pCVR for conversion-optimised campaigns. Log every impression with the features used, join clicks and conversions, and train models continuously. Out of scope, said aloud: the advertiser interface, creative review and billing, though billing depends on our logs being exact (in9).

Quality targets. Offline normalised entropy improves or holds for every launch; calibration ratio (predicted clicks over observed clicks) within 0.95-1.05 overall and within 0.9-1.1 for every slice with more than 100,000 daily impressions (placement, country, device, advertiser vertical, ad age). Online, revenue per thousand requests up without hurting advertiser value or user metrics.

Latency targets. The ads path runs in parallel with organic feed ranking and has a p99 budget of 100 ms server-side, including the auction. Model freshness: sparse weights updated within 15 minutes of events; full retraining daily.

Cost targets. Serving cost per thousand requests well below the revenue per thousand; training compute bounded by down-sampling.

Safety and integrity targets. Policy-violating ads never served (enforced outside the model); sensitive targeting categories excluded by policy; frequency caps so one user does not see the same ad endlessly; click spam filtered before labels and billing; user privacy respected in features and logs (ae5).

Clarifying questions. "Is it pay-per-click, pay-per-conversion, or both?" (decides whether pCVR and delayed labels are in scope). "Feed ads or search ads?" (search ads have a query and a stronger relevance signal; feed ads rely on user interest). "Second-price or first-price auction?" (changes how prices depend on our predictions). "Do advertisers have daily budgets?" (they almost always do, which complicates experiments).

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Traffic. 200 million daily active users each generating about 20 ad requests a day as they scroll: 4 billion requests a day, 4 × 109 / 86,400 ≈ 46,000 per second on average, about 100,000 per second at peak. With an average of 1.5 ads shown per request: 6 billion impressions a day. At a 1% CTR, 60 million clicks a day; if 3% of clicks convert, 1.8 million conversions a day, arriving over days.

Candidates per stage. 10 million active ads. Targeting retrieval (an index from audience attributes to ads, plus embedding retrieval for interest-based campaigns) returns about 2,000 eligible ads after budget and frequency filters. A light model (a small network or boosted trees on a few dozen features) keeps 200. The heavy model predicts pCTR and pCVR for those 200. The auction picks one to three.

Features per request. The heavy model reads about 100 sparse features (IDs and crosses, each looked up in an embedding table) and 200 dense features (historical CTRs of the ad, the advertiser and the user, time since last ad, device, placement). User features are fetched once per request; ad features are cached in memory on serving hosts because the active ad set is small (10 million ads × 2 KB = 20 GB, replicated per host or sharded across a few); cross features are computed per (user, ad) pair. Per request: 200 × 300 = 60,000 values.

Serving compute. 100,000 requests × 200 candidates = 20 million predictions per second. If the heavy model costs about 10 million floating-point operations per prediction (the dense layers), that is 200 TFLOP/s, a handful of modern GPUs at realistic utilisation. But each prediction also reads about 100 embedding rows from tables that total hundreds of gigabytes, which is memory-bound work: 20 million × 100 lookups × 64 dimensions × 2 bytes ≈ 256 GB/s of random reads. The fleet is therefore sized by memory capacity and bandwidth, typically a few dozen large-memory hosts per region plus redundancy, with the user-side part of the network computed once per request rather than 200 times.

Embedding tables. Suppose user IDs, ad IDs and the main crosses hash into 1 billion rows in total, at 64 dimensions × 2 bytes: 128 GB for serving. Training keeps 32-bit weights and optimiser state, about four times more, sharded across the training cluster. Google reported that encoding weights in 16-bit fixed point instead of 64-bit floats saved 75% of coefficient memory with no measurable loss, which shows how much of this problem is memory engineering.

Logs. 6 billion impressions × about 2 KB of logged features = 12 TB a day before compression. Clicks and conversions are tiny by comparison.

Training data. Training on every impression is wasteful because 99% are non-clicks. Keep all 60 million clicks and a fraction w = 0.025 of the 5.94 billion non-clicks, about 149 million: roughly 210 million examples a day, a 28x reduction. Facebook found 0.025 gave the best NE among the rates it tried; Google weighted the kept non-click examples by 1/r instead. Either way, the correction must be applied (section 8).

The joiner's buffer. Clicks mostly arrive within minutes. Holding impressions for a 15-minute click window at the average 70,000 impressions per second × 900 seconds × 2 KB ≈ 126 GB of state, sharded across joiner instances. Conversions cannot be buffered this way; they are joined later in batch against stored impressions (section 10).

Evaluation cost per release. Offline evaluation is cheap: NE and calibration on a day of unsampled impressions (6 billion rows, or a uniform 1% sample of 60 million) take minutes on a cluster. The expensive part is the online test. Ads revenue is noisy, so detecting a 0.5% revenue change needs a large share of traffic for one to two weeks, and budget interference forces budget-split designs (section 11). That traffic is the real budget for releases.

The insight to say out loud. The model is not the hard part of the arithmetic. The hard parts are memory (embedding tables), data volume (sampling), latency of label arrival (joiners and delays), and the money attached to every percentage point of calibration.

The response carries the prediction and model version so that billing, debugging and calibration monitoring can compare predictions with outcomes per model. Conversion events usually come from the advertiser (a pixel on their site or a server-to-server call) hours or days later, keyed by a click ID the platform issued, which is how a purchase is attributed to an impression.

5 · Data model

Ads: ad ID, campaign, advertiser, creative, targeting spec, bid type (CPC, CPM, cost per conversion) and amount, budget and pacing state, status. Impression log: request ID, timestamp, user, placement, position, ad ID, price, pCTR, pCVR, model version, sampled feature vector. Click log and conversion log: request ID or click ID, ad ID, timestamp, source, spam score. Training examples: impression features, label, label time, sampling rate (so the correction is exact), and for conversions the elapsed time since the click when the example was created. Model registry: versions with training data window, offline NE and calibration by slice, calibration layer parameters.

Two fields are easy to forget and painful to miss: the sampling rate attached to each training example, since rates change over time and per slice, and the model version attached to each impression, without which a calibration regression cannot be traced to a release.

6 · High-level design

The ad server receives the request, reads user features once, and calls targeting, which returns eligible ads from an inverted index keyed by audience attributes plus an embedding retriever for interest-based campaigns, filtered by budget, frequency caps and policy. The light ranker scores about 2,000 candidates and keeps 200. The heavy ranker computes pCTR and pCVR with a deep model (embeddings for sparse IDs and crosses, interaction layers, multi-task heads, rec6). The calibration layer maps raw outputs to calibrated probabilities per slice. The auction computes each ad's expected value, applies reserve prices and pacing multipliers, picks winners and computes prices. The logger writes impressions with features.

Behind it, a click joiner (a stream job) attaches clicks to impressions within a 15-minute window and emits labelled examples; a conversion joiner runs in batch hourly, attaching conversions to stored clicks within the attribution window. An online trainer consumes the labelled stream and updates sparse weights and the last layers every few minutes; a batch trainer retrains the full model daily on down-sampled data from the last several weeks; both publish to the registry after automated checks. A monitoring service computes calibration by slice hourly and can freeze the online trainer or roll back a model.

7 · Deep dive: why pCTR × bid ranks ads, and what the auction does with it

Three ads compete for one slot. Ad A bids $2.00 per click with pCTR 1.0%; ad B bids $0.80 with pCTR 3.0%; ad C bids $5.00 with pCTR 0.3%. The platform is paid only on a click, so the expected revenue of showing each ad once is bid × pCTR, conventionally quoted per thousand impressions as eCPM = 1,000 × pCTR × bid: A earns $20, B $24, C $15. Ranking by bid would show C, the ad users are least likely to want, and earn the least. Ranking by eCPM shows B. That is the whole argument for the formula, and it is also why the probability must be right: it is multiplied by money.

Pricing. In a generalised second-price (GSP) auction the winner pays the minimum per click that would still keep its position: the runner-up's eCPM divided by the winner's pCTR. B pays $20 / (1,000 × 0.03) ≈ $0.67 per click, less than its $0.80 bid. Expected revenue per thousand impressions: 1,000 × 0.03 × $0.67 = $20, the runner-up's value. Many platforms now run first-price auctions or add a user-value term to the ranking score (a penalty for ads predicted to annoy users), but in every variant pCTR scales both who wins and what they pay.

What miscalibration costs. Suppose the model over-predicts B by 2x, saying 6% when the truth is 3%. B still wins (its predicted eCPM is $48), but its price becomes $20 / (1,000 × 0.06) ≈ $0.33 per click. True clicks stay at 3%, so revenue per thousand impressions falls to 1,000 × 0.03 × $0.33 = $10, half of what a calibrated model would earn from the same auction. Under-prediction is just as bad in the other direction: an advertiser whose pCTR is halved loses auctions it should win. A global over-prediction can be fixed with one multiplier; uneven miscalibration across advertisers or placements is what really damages a marketplace, which is why calibration is monitored per slice.

Conversion bids. An advertiser who bids $50 per purchase is valued at $50 × pCTR × pCVR per impression. With pCTR 1% and pCVR 3%, that is $0.015 per impression, $15 eCPM. Now two models multiply, and their errors compound: a 20% over-prediction in each gives a 44% over-valuation.

8 · Deep dive: log loss, normalised entropy and calibration on real numbers

Log loss charges each impression the negative log of the probability the model gave to what actually happened. Predict 2% and the user clicks: the cost is −ln 0.02 = 3.91. Predict 2% and the user does not click: the cost is −ln 0.98 = 0.020. Averaged over billions of impressions, the rare clicks contribute most of the loss, and a model that confidently predicts near zero for an ad that does get clicked pays heavily. Log loss is minimised, in expectation, by predicting the true probability, which is why it rewards calibration as well as ordering.

Normalised entropy fixes a nuisance of log loss: it depends on the base rate. A placement with a 0.1% CTR has a much lower log loss than one with a 5% CTR even for equally good models. NE divides the model's average log loss by the log loss of the trivial model that always predicts the background CTR p, which is the entropy H(p) = −[p ln p + (1 − p) ln(1 − p)]. For p = 1%: H = −(0.01 × ln 0.01 + 0.99 × ln 0.99) = 0.0560 nats. NE below 1 means better than the average; Facebook's paper reports results as NE relative to a baseline, with improvements of fractions of a percent counting as significant.

A worked world. Imagine traffic with two equal-sized segments: half the impressions have a true CTR of 0.4%, half 1.6%, so the background CTR is 1%. A perfect model predicts 0.4% and 1.6% for the right segments. Its average log loss is 0.5 × H(0.004) + 0.5 × H(0.016) = 0.0541, so its NE is 0.0541 / 0.0560 = 0.965. Even perfect knowledge only beats the average by 3.5%, because clicks are rare and mostly unpredictable at the level of a single impression. That is why NE numbers live close to 1 and why a 0.5% relative improvement is a large win.

Now take the same perfect ordering but double every prediction: 0.8% and 3.2%. AUC is unchanged, since the order is identical. Log loss rises to 0.0572 and NE to 1.021, worse than predicting the average for everyone. Halving every prediction gives NE 1.000. NE punishes miscalibration that AUC cannot see, and a single global multiplier would restore the perfect model, exactly the example Facebook's paper gives.

Calibration, measured. The calibration ratio is total predicted clicks divided by observed clicks: on 1 million impressions where the predictions sum to 10,400 and 10,000 clicks happen, the ratio is 1.04. Overall ratio is necessary but not sufficient: a model can be 1.0 overall while over-predicting by 30% on new ads and under-predicting on old ones. A reliability diagram bins impressions by predicted CTR and plots the observed CTR in each bin; a calibrated model lies on the diagonal.

The calibration layer. Raw model outputs are corrected by a monotone function per slice, fitted on recent data the model did not train on: Platt scaling (a logistic fit on the raw logit) for a simple shift and scale, or isotonic regression (a piecewise-constant non-decreasing map) for bent curves, as Google described (ml15 covers both). Because the map is monotone, the order within a slice is unchanged, so ranking quality is preserved while the probabilities become usable.

Down-sampling and its correction. Training on 5.9 billion non-clicks a day is wasteful, so keep a fraction w of them. The model then learns the sampled data's CTR: with true CTR 1% and w = 0.025, the training CTR is 0.01 / (0.01 + 0.99 × 0.025) ≈ 28.8%. To get back to true probabilities, apply q = p / (p + (1 − p) / w): a sampled-space prediction of 0.29 becomes 0.29 / (0.29 + 0.71 / 0.025) ≈ 1.01%. The formula follows from Bayes' rule: sampling multiplies the odds of a click by 1/w, so dividing the odds by 1/w undoes it. Facebook's paper gives this formula and found w = 0.025 best for NE in its experiment; Google instead kept a fraction r of queries with no clicks and gave them importance weight 1/r in the loss, so no correction is needed afterwards. Both work; both require the rate to be recorded on every example.

9 · Deep dive: features, crosses and hashing

Facebook's paper found that historical features (an ad's past CTR, a user's past CTR) dominated: the top 10 features by importance were all historical, and a model with only contextual features (time, device, page) was 4.5% worse in NE than the full model, while removing contextual features cost less than 1%. Contextual features still matter for cold start: a new ad or user has no history. These counters are produced by real-time counting pipelines (in9) and must be computed as of the impression time to avoid leakage (hd8).

Crosses carry interactions. A logistic model with features "advertiser = running brand" and "country = BR" can learn that each matters, but not that this brand does unusually well in Brazil. The cross "advertiser × country" can. Deep models learn crosses from embeddings (DCN, DLRM in rec6), but explicit crosses of IDs remain common because they memorise specific combinations cheaply. Facebook's trick was to let boosted trees discover useful conjunctions and feed each tree's leaf index to a linear model as a categorical feature; the combination reached 96.58% of the trees-only model's NE, a 3.4% relative improvement.

Feature hashing. Cross vocabularies are enormous (millions of advertisers × hundreds of countries × several placements) and change every day as new campaigns start. Storing a vocabulary that maps each value to an embedding row means maintaining that vocabulary in training and serving. The hashing trick skips it: row = hash(feature name, value) mod m. New values get a row immediately, and memory is fixed at m rows. The price is collisions: two unrelated values share a row and their signals blur. If n distinct values are hashed into m buckets, the chance that a given value shares its bucket is about 1 − e−n/m. With 50 million distinct cross values into 224 ≈ 16.8 million buckets, n/m ≈ 3 and about 95% of values collide; with 228 ≈ 268 million buckets, n/m ≈ 0.19 and about 17% collide. Collisions mostly hurt rare values (whose rows are dominated by a frequent neighbour), and Google reported it could not hash its feature space below several billion without measurable loss. In practice: hash generously, give the most frequent values their own rows, and measure NE as a function of table size.

Keeping the table small. Most sparse values are rare (Google noted that in some models half the unique features occur only once in billions of examples), so teams avoid allocating rows for values seen once: Google admitted a feature into the model only after it had been seen several times, using a counting Bloom filter (which saved about 66% of memory at n = 2 in its table) or a random inclusion probability. Rows unused for weeks are evicted. Lower-precision weights (16-bit) halve or quarter memory again.

10 · Deep dive: the joiner, delayed conversions and freshness

Clicks. There is no "did not click" event; an impression becomes a negative only when enough time has passed. The click joiner keeps impressions in a keyed buffer (a hash map from request ID plus a time-ordered queue for expiry, which Facebook called a HashQueue) and emits each impression when its window ends, labelled positive if a click joined. The window is a trade-off. Shorter windows give fresher data and a smaller buffer; longer windows catch more late clicks. Missed clicks bias the empirical CTR down; Facebook reported that this bias can be reduced to fractions of a percent with manageable windows, and that the residual can be measured and corrected. Click spam is removed before joining, so neither billing nor training counts it.

Conversions are slower by orders of magnitude: a user clicks a furniture ad tonight and buys next week. Suppose, from historical data, 60% of eventual conversions arrive within a day of the click, 85% within three days and essentially all within the 7-day attribution window. A pCVR model trained on clicks from yesterday sees only 60% of their eventual conversions; the other 40% look like negatives. The model's pCVR is then biased low by about 40% for recent data, and advertisers who pay per conversion are under-served.

There are three families of fixes. Wait: train only on clicks older than the attribution window. Labels are clean, but the model is a week stale and new campaigns are invisible to it. Correct: train on fresh data but use the measured delay distribution to adjust; for example, weight or relabel by the probability a conversion is still to come given the time elapsed, or calibrate aggregate pCVR by dividing by the completion fraction. Model the delay: Chapelle (2014) trained two models jointly, one for whether a click converts and one for the delay given that it converts, so an unconverted click observed for only a day contributes "either won't convert, or will convert later than a day" rather than a hard negative. A widely used streaming variant emits each click immediately as a provisional negative and emits it again as a positive if a conversion arrives, then corrects the resulting bias with importance weights in the loss; Ktena et al. (2019) compared such losses and report gains over naive log loss in an online test. The choice depends on how fast campaigns change compared with how slowly conversions arrive.

Freshness. Facebook trained on one day and tested on each of the following six days: NE degraded as the gap grew, and training daily instead of weekly improved NE by about 1%. That is why the architecture has two trainers. The batch trainer rebuilds the full model daily from weeks of data. The online trainer updates from the joiner's stream every few minutes; Facebook trained the logistic-regression layer online over tree features that were retrained less often, and found per-coordinate learning rates best; Google's FTRL-Proximal used per-coordinate rates too, reporting an 11.2% AucLoss reduction from them versus a global rate. Per-coordinate rates matter because sparse features appear at wildly different frequencies; a single global rate decays too fast for rare features.

Guarding the loop. Online learning is a tight feedback loop: if the click stream stalls, the joiner emits only negatives, the trainer learns that nobody clicks, pCTRs collapse, and ads stop winning auctions. Facebook's paper describes exactly this risk and suggests disconnecting the trainer automatically when the training data's distribution changes abruptly. Production systems check each mini-batch's empirical CTR, label delay and feature null rates against recent history before applying updates, and keep the last good model ready to restore.

11 · Evaluation plan

Offline. NE and calibration ratio on the day after the training window, on unsampled (or correctly reweighted) data, overall and per slice; AUC as a sanity check on ordering; reliability diagrams for the largest slices; for online-trained models, progressive validation, scoring each example before training on it, which Google used instead of a held-out set because it reflects exactly what serving sees. pCVR is evaluated only on clicks old enough to have complete labels, or with the delay correction applied.

Online. A/B tests measure platform revenue per thousand requests, advertiser value (conversions per dollar spent, cost per acquisition against advertisers' targets), and user experience (ad CTR, ad hides and reports, session length). Advertiser budgets create interference: if the treatment model spends a campaign's daily budget faster, control sees less of that campaign, and both arms are distorted. Budget-split experiments give each arm its own share of every budget. Long-term holdouts (rec13) catch slow effects such as users learning to ignore ads.

Guardrails. Calibration ratio per large slice within bounds; p99 latency; ad load (ads per session) unchanged unless intended; policy-violation rate; advertiser cost per click not jumping for any large advertiser (a sign of mispricing).

The trade-offs to name: freshness against label completeness (the conversion-window dilemma); model size against memory and latency (hashing, embedding dimensions, number of trees: Facebook found almost all NE gains came from the first 500 trees); exploration against short-term revenue (new ads need impressions to get history, which costs auctions); platform revenue against user experience (ad load and quality terms); and simple, well-calibrated models against complex ones that need heavier calibration layers.

Calibration is a property of a slice, not of a model

A model can be perfectly calibrated overall and badly miscalibrated on new ads, on a new placement, or on one country after a holiday. What matters for the auction is calibration conditional on everything the auction compares across: advertisers, placements, bid types. That is why calibration layers are fitted per slice and why the slices chosen for monitoring are those where advertisers compete. A useful senior habit is to ask, for any proposed feature or model change, "which slice could this miscalibrate?", because a change that helps NE overall can still shift a slice by 10%.

Selection bias from the auction itself

The model learns only from impressions that won auctions, and ads win when their pCTR is high. For an ad whose pCTR is over-estimated, the system collects many impressions and corrects itself; for one whose pCTR is under-estimated, it rarely wins, collects few impressions and stays under-estimated. Over time this biases the model against ads it misjudged early, especially new ones. The remedies are explicit exploration (a small share of auctions with boosted or randomised scores for new ads, logged with their probabilities so the data can be reweighted), uncertainty-aware bidding (Facebook noted that a Bayesian model's predictive distribution can drive explore/exploit, and Google described cheap per-prediction confidence estimates), and contextual features that let new ads borrow strength from similar ones.

Position and context in the label

An ad shown in the first feed slot is seen more than one in the fifth (rec10). If position is ignored, the model attributes position's effect to the ad. The usual approach trains with position as a feature and predicts at a fixed reference position, then applies a separate position factor in the auction for the slot being filled. Other context matters similarly: the organic content around the ad, the number of ads already seen in the session (ad fatigue) and the time since the last ad all change click propensity and should be features, not noise.

Why two-speed models keep appearing

Facebook's hybrid used boosted trees, retrained daily, as a learned feature transform and a logistic regression on top, trained online. The pattern separates what changes slowly (which feature conjunctions matter) from what changes fast (how much each matters today). Modern systems keep the structure with different parts: a large deep model retrained daily or continuously, with the sparse embeddings and final layers updated online, and a small calibration layer refit hourly. The slow part carries capacity; the fast part carries freshness and calibration.

Multi-task heads and conversion bias

pCVR is learned only on clicked impressions, but it is applied to all impressions in the auction. That mismatch is a selection bias: users who click are not a random sample. A common answer is to model the full chain on all impressions: predict pCTR and pCTCVR (click and convert) on every impression, and derive pCVR as their ratio, sharing embeddings between tasks so the sparse conversion signal benefits from the plentiful click signal. It also makes the conversion head see the same input distribution in training and serving.

Cold start for new ads and advertisers

A new ad has no historical CTR, which is the strongest feature family; its ID embedding is untrained (or shares a hashed row with unrelated ads). The model falls back on what it can see: the advertiser's history, the creative's content (text and image embeddings), the targeting, and contextual features, which is why contextual features matter even though they rank low on importance for established ads. Two system mechanisms help. Historical features are smoothed toward a prior, so an ad with 3 clicks in 100 impressions is not credited with a 3% CTR but shrunk toward its advertiser's or category's rate, with the shrinkage fading as impressions accumulate. And a small, logged exploration budget guarantees new ads a minimum number of impressions, so the system learns about them before its own early mistakes become self-fulfilling.

Experiments in a market

Ads experiments violate the independence an A/B test assumes, because arms share advertisers' budgets and compete in the same auctions. A model that raises one advertiser's predictions in treatment changes the prices that control advertisers pay. Budget splitting, cluster randomisation by advertiser, and simulation of auctions on logged data (counterfactual replay with the new model's predictions) all help; no single method removes interference, which is why ads teams combine them and look for consistent direction rather than precise numbers from any one.

How it fails in practice

The click stream that stopped. A logging change drops click events for one app version. The joiner emits those impressions as negatives; the online trainer, doing exactly what it was built to do, lowers pCTR for ads popular on that app version. Revenue on that version falls within an hour while the overall dashboard shows a small dip. Facebook's paper describes this class of failure and the fix: detect abrupt changes in the training stream (empirical CTR, click-join rate) and disconnect the trainer automatically.

The sampling change nobody told the calibrator about. A team doubles the negative sampling rate to speed training but the serving correction still uses the old rate. Every prediction is off by a near-constant factor; AUC is perfect, NE is worse, and prices shift. Recording the rate on every example and checking calibration ratio before every model publish prevents it.

The new-campaign trap. pCVR is trained only on clicks older than seven days. During a holiday sale, many new campaigns launch, and the model knows nothing about them for a week. Conversion advertisers under-deliver exactly when they spend most. A delay-aware loss on fresh data, plus contextual features for new campaigns, fixes it.

The hash table that filled. A new cross feature with a huge vocabulary is added to a fixed-size hash table already near capacity; collision rates for existing rare features jump, and NE for small advertisers degrades while large ones are unaffected. Monitoring NE by advertiser size and sizing tables from distinct-value counts catches it.

Metrics and dashboards

Per model version and slice, hourly: calibration ratio, NE, prediction distribution, revenue per thousand requests, cost per click, ad CTR. Pipeline health: join rate (share of clicks matched to impressions), join latency, training-stream CTR, conversion arrival curves, trainer lag, model publish times. Serving: latency per stage, fallback rate, embedding cache hit rate.

Knobs people tune

Negative sampling rate, click-join window, conversion handling (wait versus correct), online learning rate and update frequency, hash table sizes and feature admission thresholds, calibration slices and refit frequency, exploration share for new ads, reserve prices and the weight of user-experience terms in the ranking score.

The real cases: Facebook 2014 and Google 2013

Facebook's "Practical Lessons from Predicting Clicks on Ads at Facebook" (He et al., ADKDD 2014), written when Facebook had over 750 million daily active users and over 1 million active advertisers, is the clearest public description of a production CTR system. Its model fed the leaf indices of boosted decision trees into a logistic regression; that hybrid's NE was 96.58% of the trees-only model's (LR alone was 99.43%), a gain the authors note dwarfs typical feature-engineering experiments. It used NE and calibration as its main metrics and explained why AUC is not enough: a model that over-predicts by 2x can be fixed by a global multiplier, which improves NE without changing AUC. It measured freshness (about 1% NE from daily versus weekly retraining), compared online learning-rate schemes (per-coordinate best, close to a Bayesian probit model), described the online joiner and its waiting window, showed that almost all NE gains came from the first 500 trees, that the top 10 features carried about half the total importance and were all historical, that training on 10% of data cost only about 1% in NE, and that negative down-sampling at 0.025 worked best, with the recalibration formula q = p / (p + (1 − p) / w).

Google's "Ad Click Prediction: a View from the Trenches" (McMahan et al., KDD 2013) describes search-ads CTR prediction at billions of predictions a day with billions of coefficients. Its learner was FTRL-Proximal, an online algorithm with per-coordinate learning rates (11.2% AucLoss reduction over a global rate, where 1% counts as large) and L1 regularisation for sparse models. Much of the paper is memory engineering: admitting features only after they appear several times (Bloom-filter inclusion saved 66% of RAM at n = 2), 16-bit q2.13 fixed-point weights (75% RAM saved, no measurable loss), training many similar models over one shared data structure, and subsampling unclicked queries with importance weights. It also reports that aggressive feature hashing could not go below several billion features without loss, uses progressive validation instead of held-out sets, and calibrates predictions with isotonic regression per slice, noting that the system's feedback loop makes theoretical guarantees for calibration impossible.

Goal: see log loss, NE and calibration move on simulated ad traffic, including a 2x over-prediction that leaves AUC untouched, and undo negative down-sampling with the correction formula. Python 3.12, standard library only.

  1. Save as ctr_calibration.py and run python ctr_calibration.py.
  2. Compare the three models. The "perfect" one predicts each impression's true CTR; the "2x" one doubles it; the "flat" one predicts the average. Check that 2x has the same AUC as perfect but a worse NE, and that its calibration ratio is about 2.
  3. Read the reliability table for the 2x model: each bin's observed CTR is about half its mean prediction.
  4. Look at the down-sampling block: training on negatives kept at w = 0.025 shifts the average prediction to about 29%, and the correction brings it back near 1%.
  5. Change SEGMENTS to make CTRs more spread out (for example 0.1% and 5%) and watch the perfect model's NE fall further below 1: more predictable traffic, more room for a model to help.
"""Log loss, NE, calibration and the down-sampling correction on simulated CTRs.
Python 3.12, stdlib only."""
import math, random

rng = random.Random(3)
SEGMENTS = [0.004, 0.016]          # true CTRs, equal traffic -> background CTR about 1%
N = 400_000
data = []                          # (true_p, clicked)
for _ in range(N):
    p = rng.choice(SEGMENTS) * math.exp(rng.gauss(0, 0.3))   # spread within segment
    data.append((p, rng.random() < p))

def logloss(preds, labels):
    return -sum(math.log(q) if y else math.log(1 - q) for q, y in zip(preds, labels)) / len(labels)

def auc(preds, labels):                      # rank-based, ties ignored (continuous preds)
    order = sorted(range(len(preds)), key=preds.__getitem__)
    rank_sum = sum(r + 1 for r, i in enumerate(order) if labels[i])
    P = sum(labels); Nn = len(labels) - P
    return (rank_sum - P * (P + 1) / 2) / (P * Nn)

labels = [y for _, y in data]
ctr = sum(labels) / N
H = -(ctr * math.log(ctr) + (1 - ctr) * math.log(1 - ctr))
models = {"perfect": [p for p, _ in data],
          "2x":      [min(2 * p, 0.999) for p, _ in data],
          "flat":    [ctr] * N}
print(f"background CTR {ctr:.4f}, entropy {H:.4f} nats")
for name, preds in models.items():
    ll = logloss(preds, labels)
    a = auc(preds, labels) if name != "flat" else 0.5
    print(f"{name:8s} logloss {ll:.4f}  NE {ll / H:.3f}  AUC {a:.3f}  "
          f"calibration {sum(preds) / sum(labels):.2f}")

print("\nreliability, 2x model: bin -> mean prediction vs observed CTR")
bins = {}
for q, y in zip(models["2x"], labels):
    b = min(int(q / 0.005), 7)
    s = bins.setdefault(b, [0, 0.0, 0])
    s[0] += 1; s[1] += q; s[2] += y
for b in sorted(bins):
    n, sq, sy = bins[b]
    print(f"  [{b * 0.5:.1f}%, {b * 0.5 + 0.5:.1f}%)  n={n:6d}  pred {sq / n:.4f}  obs {sy / n:.4f}")

w = 0.025                                    # keep 2.5% of negatives
sample = [(p, y) for p, y in data if y or rng.random() < w]
sampled_ctr = sum(y for _, y in sample) / len(sample)
print(f"\ndown-sampled: {len(sample):,} rows, training CTR {sampled_ctr:.3f}")
# a model trained on the sample learns sampled-space probabilities:
p_sampled = [p / (p + (1 - p) * w) for p, _ in data]          # what it would predict
q = [ps / (ps + (1 - ps) / w) for ps in p_sampled]             # the correction
print(f"mean prediction in sampled space {sum(p_sampled) / N:.3f}, "
      f"after correction {sum(q) / N:.4f} (true {ctr:.4f})")

What to look for: NE of the perfect model a few percent below 1, the 2x model above 1 with an identical AUC, calibration ratios near 1.0, 2.0 and 1.0, and the corrected mean prediction back near the background CTR. The sampled-space line uses the exact odds transformation that training on down-sampled data induces, so the correction recovers the true probabilities exactly.

Work it out

A placement shows ads with a background CTR of 0.5%. Your new model's average log loss on tomorrow's data is 0.0300 nats; the current model's is 0.0304. (a) What are the two models' NE values? (b) The new model's predictions sum to 11,500 on a sample with 10,000 clicks. What does that tell you, and what happens to auctions if it ships as is? (c) You trained with negatives sampled at w = 0.05. A raw prediction is 0.09. What is the corrected probability?

Answer

(a) H(0.005) = −(0.005 × ln 0.005 + 0.995 × ln 0.995) = 0.005 × 5.298 + 0.995 × 0.00501 ≈ 0.0265 + 0.0050 = 0.0315 nats. Current NE = 0.0304 / 0.0315 ≈ 0.966; new NE = 0.0300 / 0.0315 ≈ 0.953. A relative improvement of about 1.3%, which is large by ads standards.

(b) Calibration ratio 1.15: the model predicts 15% more clicks than happen. If the over-prediction were perfectly uniform, click-priced ads would keep the same order and even the same second-price prices, because price = runner-up eCPM / winner pCTR and both are inflated by the same 1.15. The damage is elsewhere: inflated eCPMs clear reserve prices they should not, beat ads bid per impression (whose eCPM is their bid and is not inflated), and mislead budget pacing. And over-prediction is rarely uniform; where it is uneven, wrong ads win and prices fall, as in section 7. Fit the calibration layer on recent holdout data and check the ratio per slice before launch; the NE gain may partly survive calibration and partly vanish, so re-measure NE after calibrating.

(c) q = 0.09 / (0.09 + 0.91 / 0.05) = 0.09 / (0.09 + 18.2) = 0.09 / 18.29 ≈ 0.49%.

Diagnose it

On Monday morning, revenue from conversion-optimised campaigns is down 12% against last Monday, click-priced campaigns are flat, and pCVR predictions for campaigns launched in the last three days are about 35% below their eventual observed conversion rates. Nothing was deployed over the weekend except a change that switched pCVR training from "clicks older than 7 days" to "clicks from the last 24 hours" for freshness. What happened and what do you do?

Answer

Delayed feedback. Training on clicks from the last 24 hours treats every click whose conversion has not arrived yet as a negative. If roughly 60-65% of conversions arrive within a day, the fresh data shows only that share of eventual positives, and pCVR is biased low by about 35-40%, matching the observation. New campaigns are hit hardest because all their data is fresh. Lower pCVR lowers their expected value, so they lose auctions and spend less, and conversion revenue falls; click-priced campaigns don't use pCVR and are unaffected.

Fix now: roll back to the previous training window. Fix properly: keep fresh data but make labels delay-aware: weight unconverted recent clicks by the probability they will still convert given elapsed time (from the measured delay distribution), or use a delayed-feedback loss (provisional negatives with later positives and importance weights, or a joint conversion-and-delay model). Evaluate pCVR only on matured clicks, and add a guardrail comparing pCVR calibration on matured data per campaign age, so a freshness change cannot ship with this bias again.
