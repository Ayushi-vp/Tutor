Open a photo and short-video app and the first screen of the main feed has already been chosen for you: a post from a friend, a clip from a creator you follow, a recipe video from someone you have never heard of, a sports highlight. Behind those ten items, a system considered hundreds of millions of candidates, predicted for each of the few hundred finalists how likely you are to watch it to the end, like it, comment, share it, follow its creator or hide it, combined those predictions into one score, and then rearranged the result so the page does not show four cooking videos in a row. All in a quarter of a second, for 80,000 people a second at the evening peak.

Now imagine it going wrong in the ways real feeds do. The ranker learns that outrage gets comments and the feed gets angrier. A retrieval model trained with a shortcut quietly stops recommending anything popular, or anything new. Creators who got lucky early get all the exposure, and new creators give up. Users see the same video three times because the "already seen" filter lags. Engagement looks great for two weeks and retention drops for six months. Every one of these is a system design failure, not a modelling failure, and every one is something a senior interviewer will probe.

hd3 designed a real-time recommendation system and taught the funnel, two-tower retrieval, multi-task ranking and feedback loops at a high level. This case goes one level deeper on the parts that make a personalised feed hard at scale: training a two-tower retriever with in-batch negatives and the sampling-bias correction, worked by hand; a multi-stage ranker in which the cheap stage is trained to agree with the expensive one; a multi-objective value model whose weights decide what the product is; diversity and freshness on the page; and feedback loops that the system creates for itself. The theory has a home in the Search & recsys track (rec4 for two towers, rec6 for rankers, rec12 for re-ranking, rec10 for biases); the subject here is the system.

The lesson follows the interview: requirements including engagement, latency, cost and integrity targets; estimates by hand for request rate, candidates per stage, features, compute, embeddings, logs and evaluation cost; the API and data model; the architecture; four deep dives; the evaluation plan; failure modes; and two real cases, YouTube's sampling-bias-corrected retrieval and Instagram Explore's multi-stage ranking. The lab trains a tiny two-tower model with and without the correction.

The problem in one sentence

For each feed request, choose and order about ten items, out of hundreds of millions, that maximise the user's long-term satisfaction with the app, as estimated through many short-term signals, within about 250 ms, while keeping the page varied, fresh and safe and giving new items and creators a fair chance.

The mental model: a newspaper with one edition per reader

Think of a newspaper that prints a separate front page for every reader, every time they open it. Scouts (retrieval sources) bring candidate stories from different beats: the reader's favourite topics, the people they follow, what is trending, what just happened. A sub-editor (the first-stage ranker) quickly discards most of them, trying to guess what the editor-in-chief would want. The editor-in-chief (the second-stage ranker) estimates, for each remaining story, how this reader will react: read it fully, share it, write a letter, cancel the subscription. A policy decides how much each reaction is worth (the value model). The layout desk (re-ranking) makes sure the front page is not five stories about the same match and that at least one new columnist gets a slot.

The analogy shows the two layers of the problem: predicting reactions, and deciding what reactions are worth. Where it breaks: a newspaper editor learns from sales figures that are not caused by the layout, while a feed learns from reactions to what it chose to show, so its mistakes feed back into its training data.

What the system learns from

A feed has no query, so everything it knows about intent comes from behaviour. Explicit signals are actions the user chooses to take: like, comment, share, follow, save, and the negative ones, hide, "see less", report, unfollow. They are strong but rare; most users like a few percent of what they see. Implicit signals are by-products of viewing: whether a video was watched past the first seconds, how much of it, whether the user paused on a photo or scrolled past in under a second, whether they opened the comments. They are plentiful but noisy: a long watch might mean fascination or a phone left on a table. A good design uses both, gives each head its own label, and lets the value model decide what each is worth. The same signals also feed three different consumers at three speeds: the user's session state within seconds (what to show on the next scroll), streaming counters within minutes (how this item is doing right now), and training data within hours (what the models learn).

A reader who stops here has the right picture: diverse retrieval, two ranking stages that must agree, a weighted combination of predicted reactions, and a page builder that cares about the set. Everything else is numbers, training details and the ways the loop goes wrong.

1 · Requirements

Functional. Serve the main feed: on open and on scroll, return the next page of about 10 items (photos, short videos, text posts) mixing followed accounts and recommended content, personalised per user and context. Never show an item twice within a configurable window; respect blocks, mutes and "see less" feedback immediately; respect integrity decisions (removed or demoted content). Log every impression and interaction. Out of scope, said aloud: ads (hd10 covers their auction; they are blended into the page at reserved slots), notifications, and the content moderation pipeline itself (hd6).

Quality targets. The north-star is long-term satisfaction, measured by retention (daily return rate) and by surveys, because it cannot be optimised directly; the online proxy is a value metric combining time spent and meaningful interactions. Guardrails: hides, "see less" and reports per thousand impressions not up; share of impressions going to the top 1% of creators not up; fresh content (under 24 hours old) at least a set share of impressions.

Latency targets. Server-side p99 under 250 ms for the first page; subsequent pages can be served from a buffer of ranked candidates computed with the first, refreshed when stale.

Freshness targets. A new post eligible for recommendation within 5 minutes; the user's own actions reflected in the next page (if they hide a creator, the next request knows); engagement counters within a minute.

Cost targets. A serving budget per thousand requests, which mostly constrains the width of the second stage and the size of the sequence model over user history.

Safety targets. Removed content never served; borderline content demoted by policy; minors' feeds follow stricter rules; recommendations from unconnected accounts filtered more strictly than followed content.

Clarifying questions. "What is the ratio of followed to recommended content today, and is that a product decision?" "Which signal does the company treat as 'meaningful' (comments, shares, follows)?" "Video or images, and how long are videos?" (watch time is only a good signal when lengths are comparable). "Do we have a survey or other direct satisfaction signal to calibrate against?"

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Traffic. 150 million daily active users, each opening the feed several times and scrolling through about 20 pages a day: 3 billion requests a day, 3 × 109 / 86,400 ≈ 35,000 per second on average, and about 80,000 per second at the evening peak. With 10 items a page, 30 billion impressions a day.

Corpus. Users post about 100 million items a day; the recommendable corpus (items that passed integrity checks, from the last few months, with some quality signal) is about 200 million. Followed content is a separate, much smaller pool per user: someone following 300 accounts sees perhaps a few hundred new posts a day from them.

Candidates per stage. Two-tower ANN 1,000; followed accounts 500; co-engagement 300; trending in the user's region and interests 150; fresh items 150. After merging, de-duplicating and removing seen and blocked items, about 2,000. The first stage keeps 300; the second stage scores 300; re-ranking returns 10, and keeps the next 40 ranked as a buffer for the following pages.

Two-tower serving. The user tower runs once per request (a few milliseconds of CPU, or batched on GPU). Item vectors: 200 million × 128 dimensions × 2 bytes = 51 GB, plus an HNSW graph of similar size; sharded into 4 shards of about 25 GB, each searched in a few milliseconds. At 80,000 searches per second and roughly 2,000 searches per second per replica, about 40 replicas per shard, 160 nodes, or fewer with quantised vectors.

Ranking compute. The first stage is a small two-tower-style model or MLP: about 10 µs per item × 2,000 = 20 ms of CPU per request, 1,600 cores busy at peak. The second stage predicts six heads with a large network over user history: at about 20 MFLOP per item, 80,000 × 300 × 20 × 106 = 480 TFLOP/s. If an inference GPU sustains around 150 TFLOP/s on this workload, that is 3-4 GPUs of arithmetic; a sequence model over the user's last 500 actions, embedding lookups and batching inefficiency multiply it several times, so plan a few dozen GPUs per region plus redundancy. Going from 300 to 500 candidates in the second stage costs 67% more of this fleet, which is why the first stage's quality matters so much.

Features per request. User: profile, counters and the last 500 action IDs (fetched once). Items: 2,000 records from a host-local cache of hot items (most impressions concentrate on recent popular items) with misses in one multi-get. User-item crosses (affinity with the creator, past interactions with the topic): about 50 per item from the feature store (hd8). The second stage reads about 500 features per item: 150,000 values per request.

Seen filter. A Bloom filter of the last 10,000 impressions per user at about 10 bits per item (around 1% false positives) is 12.5 KB per user; for 150 million users, about 1.9 TB in a key-value store, one read per request and updated from the impression stream.

Logs and training data. 30 billion impressions a day; logging 1.5 KB of features each is 45 TB a day, so log IDs, positions, sources, stage scores and head outputs for all (about 200 bytes, 6 TB a day) and full feature vectors for a sample plus all impressions with positive engagement. The two-tower model trains on positive engagements: perhaps 15 billion a day (150 million users × 100 meaningful views or interactions). At a batch size of 8,192 that is about 1.8 million steps a day, so the retriever trains continuously and is never caught up for long without significant hardware.

Evaluation cost per release. Offline: recall@K of the retriever on the next day's engagements, per-head log loss and calibration of the ranker, value-weighted ranking metrics, for one day of logs, a few hundred GPU-hours. Online: a two-week A/B test at a few percent of users; plus a long-term holdout of 1% of users kept on last quarter's system. The holdout is the most expensive item, because those users deliberately get an older, worse feed for months.

5 · Data model

Items: ID, creator, media type and length, content embeddings (image, text, audio), topic labels, integrity status, creation time. Item embeddings for retrieval: ID, vector, model version. User state: profile, follow graph (from the social graph service), recent action sequence, suppression lists (hidden creators and topics), seen filter. Counters: item engagement by window (last hour, day), creator-level rates, user-topic affinities, all streaming. Impression log as estimated above. Training sets: retrieval positives (user context, item, reward weight, timestamp), ranking examples (features, labels for each head, position, exploration probability).

Each training example for the retriever carries a reward: Yi et al. used per-example weights such as 0 for a clicked video with little watch time and 1 for a fully watched one, so the retriever learns engagement, not just clicks. And the item's estimated sampling probability is computed during training, not stored, because it changes as popularity changes.

6 · High-level design

The feed service receives the request, loads user state and the seen filter, and calls the user tower service for the user embedding. The retrieval layer queries the sources in parallel: ANN shards for two-tower candidates, the follow graph plus a per-creator recent-posts index for followed content, a co-engagement table (items engaged by users who engaged with the user's recent items), and precomputed trending and fresh lists per region and topic. The first-stage ranker scores the merged candidates; the second-stage ranker, on GPUs, predicts the heads; the value model combines them; the re-ranker builds the page with diversity, freshness, exploration and integrity rules and blends ads at reserved slots; the hydration step fetches media metadata. The response and buffer are cached per user for a few minutes.

Offline and nearline: an item embedding pipeline computes vectors for new items from content features within minutes and re-embeds the whole corpus when the item tower changes; an index builder updates ANN shards incrementally and rebuilds them daily. The joiner attaches events to impressions with windows per event type (seconds for watch, hours for follows). Trainers: the retriever trains continuously on positives with in-batch negatives; the second stage retrains daily with incremental updates in between; the first stage is distilled from the second stage's logged scores. A registry keeps the user tower, item tower and index on the same version, because a user vector from version 12 searched against item vectors from version 11 returns nonsense.

7 · Deep dive: training the two-tower retriever

The objective. The retriever is trained as classification over the whole corpus: given a user context x, the probability of item y is a softmax over all items of s(x, y) = u(x) · v(y) / τ, where u and v are the two towers, and τ is a temperature (Yi et al. normalised both vectors and used τ around 0.05). Computing that softmax over 200 million items for every example is impossible, so it is approximated with a sample of negatives (rec4).

In-batch negatives. Take a batch of B (user, engaged item) pairs. Compute B user vectors and B item vectors, then the B × B matrix of scores. Row i's positive is item i; the other B − 1 items in the batch are its negatives. The cost is one matrix multiply, and no extra items need to be fetched or encoded. With B = 8,192, as in Yi et al.'s YouTube experiments, the score matrix has 67 million entries, trivial for a GPU.

The bias. An item enters a batch only as someone's positive, so its chance of appearing is proportional to its popularity. A video watched by 1% of all engagement events appears in almost every batch of 8,192 and is used as a negative for nearly every user; a niche video appears rarely. Compared with the full softmax, the model receives far too much "push down" on popular items and learns to under-score them.

The correction, worked. The logQ correction subtracts log pj, the log of item j's sampling probability, from every logit in which j appears: sc(x, yj) = s(x, yj) − log pj. Consider one row of a batch: user u2 engaged with niche item B (sampling probability 0.01); the other items in the batch are A, a popular item (0.30), and C, another niche item (0.01). The model currently scores s(u2, B) = 1.5, s(u2, A) = 1.5, s(u2, C) = 0.0.

B (positive)A (popular negative)C (niche negative)
Raw logit1.51.50.0
Plain softmax0.4500.4500.100
Corrected logit (− ln p)1.5 + 4.61 = 6.111.5 + 1.20 = 2.700.0 + 4.61 = 4.61
Corrected softmax0.7960.0270.178

For softmax cross-entropy, the gradient pushing a negative's score down equals its softmax probability. Without correction, A receives a push of 0.45, as large as anything in the row, simply because it is in the batch; with correction its push is 0.027, while niche C's rises from 0.10 to 0.18. The intuition: A will appear as a negative in about 30 times as many rows as C, so each appearance should count for correspondingly less. Mathematically, the corrected sampled softmax is an unbiased estimate of the full softmax gradient, which is what makes the trained scores mean what the full model would have meant.

Estimating pj from a stream. Item popularity changes daily and new items appear constantly, so there is no fixed table of frequencies. Yi et al. estimate them from the training stream with two hashed arrays: A[h(y)] stores the last step at which item y appeared, B[h(y)] an estimate of the average gap between its appearances. Each time y appears at step t, B[h(y)] ← (1 − α) · B[h(y)] + α · (t − A[h(y)]) and A[h(y)] ← t; the estimated per-batch probability is 1 / B[h(y)]. If B is 20 and the item reappears 10 steps later with α = 0.1, B becomes 0.9 × 20 + 0.1 × 10 = 19, so p̂ ≈ 0.053 per batch. Hash collisions inflate estimates for colliding items, which multiple hash functions (taking the best estimate, like a count-min sketch) reduce. In their YouTube setup the arrays had 50 million entries and α = 0.01.

What else matters. Uniformly sampled extra negatives ("mixed negative sampling") fix the opposite blind spot: items that never appear as positives, fresh and long-tail ones, are never pushed at all by in-batch negatives alone. Hard negatives (items the user saw and skipped) sharpen the retriever but must be few, or it learns to reject plausible items. Rewards as example weights align the retriever with the value the ranker will compute. And the item tower should take content features, not only IDs, so a brand-new item gets a sensible vector before anyone has engaged with it.

8 · Deep dive: two ranking stages that agree

The first stage exists to spend the second stage's budget well. Its quality is not its own accuracy against user engagement but its stage recall: of the items the second stage would place in its top 50 if it scored all 2,000 candidates, what share does the first stage keep in its 300? Measure it on a sample by running the second stage over all candidates offline. If stage recall is 85%, then 15% of the expensive model's ideal picks never reach it.

The cleanest way to raise stage recall is to train the first stage on the second stage's decisions. Instagram's Explore does this: the first stage is a two-tower-style model trained by distillation with the label "the item landed in the top K of the second stage's ranking", using the second stage's logged scores. Because the second stage logs its scores for the candidates it saw, the distillation data is free. The first stage can be a two-tower model (so the item side is precomputed and only a dot product is needed per candidate) or a small MLP with a few dozen features.

Two practical details. Distillation needs examples beyond the first stage's own top 300, or it never learns about items it currently discards; log second-stage scores for a random sample of first-stage rejects too. And both stages must be retrained together when the value model changes, because the second stage's top K is defined by the value model.

9 · Deep dive: the multi-objective value model

The second stage is a multi-task network: shared lower layers over user, item, context and cross features (often with a mixture-of-experts layer so tasks with conflicting gradients do not hurt each other, rec6), and one head per outcome. Typical heads: P(watch past 3 seconds), expected watch time, P(like), P(comment), P(share), P(follow the creator), P(hide or "see less"), P(report). Each head is trained with its own loss on its own labels, and each must be calibrated, because the value model adds them.

The value model is a weighted sum, as Instagram's post writes it: Expected Value = Wclick × P(click) + Wlike × P(like) − Wsee less × P(see less) + and so on. Work an example with weights like = 1, comment = 4, share = 6, watch time = 0.05 per second, hide = −20, and three candidates.

X's value is 0.08 + 4 × 0.005 + 6 × 0.02 + 0.05 × 12 − 20 × 0.004 = 0.08 + 0.02 + 0.12 + 0.60 − 0.08 = 0.74. Y gets 0.15 + 0.16 + 0.012 + 0.30 − 0.02 = 0.60; Z gets 0.05 + 0.04 + 0.06 + 1.00 − 0.60 = 0.55. Now change the weights. Raise the comment weight to 8 (the product wants more conversation): Y becomes 0.762 and edges past X at 0.760. Lower the hide penalty to −5 (someone thought hides were noisy): Z jumps to 1.00 and ranks first, because its long watch time is no longer offset by the 3% of users who hide it. The weights are not tuning details; they are the product's definition of a good feed, and small changes reorder pages for hundreds of millions of people.

Choosing the weights. Start from principles (which interactions represent value, what a hide costs), put the heads on comparable scales, and then tune empirically. Each candidate weight vector is an online experiment, so teams run a few per round, or use Bayesian optimisation over weights with online metrics as the objective (Instagram's post names both Bayesian optimisation and offline tuning), always with guardrails. Weights must be revisited when a head is retrained: if the new comment head predicts 20% higher on average, the comment weight has effectively risen 20%.

Watch time and its traps. Expected watch time favours long videos (a 60-second video can earn more watch seconds from a casual viewer than a 10-second one from an enthusiast), so it is usually normalised by length or replaced by completion probability plus a length-aware term. YouTube's 2016 paper trained watch-time-weighted logistic regression for exactly this reason: clicks reward clickbait, watch time rewards content people actually watch.

10 · Deep dive: diversity, freshness and feedback loops

Diversity on the page. The ten highest-value items are often similar: same creator, same topic, same format. Re-ranking builds the page greedily, slot by slot, scoring each candidate by its value minus a penalty for similarity to items already placed (MMR, rec12), and enforcing hard rules such as Instagram's "do not show items from the same authors in a sequence", at most two items of one topic in any five, and a mix of followed and recommended content. The similarity uses item embeddings or topic labels. Diversity typically lowers the sum of predicted values on the page and raises session length and satisfaction, because predicted values are individually estimated and ignore that the second cooking video is worth less after the first.

Freshness. A new item has no engagement history and a weak position against items with thousands of interactions. Freshness comes from several places at once: the fresh source in retrieval; content-based item embeddings so new items are in the ANN index within minutes; item age and early-engagement-rate features in the ranker (an item with 40 likes in its first 10 minutes is a strong signal); and a re-ranking boost that decays with age, for example multiplying value by 1 + 0.3 × 2−age/6h, so the boost halves every six hours. The boost and its half-life are tuned by experiment, with "share of impressions from items under 24 hours old" as a guardrail.

Feedback loops. The system trains on engagement with what it showed. Items and creators shown early collect engagement, which raises their features and retrieval scores, which gets them shown more. Items never shown never collect labels. Users shown a narrow set of topics engage with that set, which narrows their history and their future feed. The defences combine: exploration slots (say one in ten positions) filled by a policy that gives uncertain items a chance, with the probability of each choice logged so training can reweight by inverse propensity (rec10, rec11); the logQ correction on the retriever side; caps on creator and topic repetition; a guardrail on creator exposure concentration (for example the share of impressions to the top 1% of creators); and long-term holdouts that reveal whether a loop is changing user behaviour over months.

Negative feedback is a first-class signal. A hide or "see less" is rare (well under 1% of impressions) but highly informative, and it must act at two speeds: immediately, as a per-user suppression of that creator or topic in the next request, and in training, as a head whose weight in the value model makes items that annoy users lose even when they hold attention.

11 · Evaluation plan

Offline. Retriever: recall@K (K = 100, 500, 1,000) of the next day's engaged items for held-out users, by item popularity band and age, because the logQ correction and mixed negatives should improve the tail without hurting the head. First stage: stage recall against the second stage. Second stage: log loss and calibration per head and per slice, AUC per head, and a value-weighted ranking metric (NDCG with the value as gain) on logged sessions. Every offline result on logged data inherits the old system's exposure, so it filters ideas rather than deciding them.

Online. A/B tests of one to two weeks at a few percent of users. Primary: a value metric aligned with the north-star (for example time spent weighted by meaningful interactions, or sessions per user). Secondaries: each engagement type, so a change in the primary can be explained. Guardrails: hides, see-less and reports per thousand impressions; creator exposure concentration; share of fresh content; integrity incidents; p99 latency. Social products have network effects (a user in treatment shares more, which changes control users' feeds), so major changes may need cluster-randomised tests (rec13).

Long-term. A holdout of about 1% of users stays on the previous quarter's system; comparing retention and session frequency between holdout and everyone else shows whether launches that each looked positive over two weeks add up to long-term value.

The trade-offs to name: wider stages (better recall) against GPU cost and latency; immediate engagement against long-term satisfaction; personalisation against diversity and serendipity; freshness against quality signal (new items have none); creators' fairness of exposure against users' predicted value; and the speed of a continuously trained retriever against the stability of a daily-retrained ranker.

Why the towers must stay apart, and what it costs

The two-tower model scores a pair by a dot product of independently computed vectors, which is what lets item vectors be precomputed and searched with an ANN index. The price is that no feature can describe the user and the item together inside the model: "this user has watched this creator's last five videos" cannot be expressed. That is why the ranker, which sees cross features, is still needed, and why retrieval quality is judged by recall of what the ranker will want, not by its own precision. A senior answer also notes that the user tower can take rich context (time of day, the item just watched) because it runs per request, while the item tower must use only item features.

Temperature and normalisation change what is learned

With normalised vectors, the dot product is a cosine in [−1, 1], and dividing by τ = 0.05 stretches it to [−20, 20], which lets the softmax separate positives from many negatives sharply. Too high a temperature and all items look alike; too low and training focuses on a few hard negatives and becomes unstable. Yi et al. found the best temperature differed between plain and corrected softmax and stressed tuning it; on their YouTube data, recall@50 rose from 0.4586 (plain, τ = 0.05) to 0.5322 (corrected, τ = 0.05).

Sequence models over user history

The strongest user features are the user's own recent actions: the last few hundred items watched, liked or skipped, with their timestamps. Transformer-style encoders over this sequence (attending to the history with the candidate item as the query, in the ranker) capture intent shifts within a session that averaged features miss. They are the main reason second-stage cost grows, which is why the user-history part is computed once per request and only the candidate attention is repeated per item.

The value model is a policy, so treat changes like policy changes

Because the weights decide what the feed rewards, changes to them deserve the process a policy change gets: a stated reason, expected effects per content category and per creator group, guardrails chosen in advance and a long-term readout. A weight tweak that raises comments by rewarding argumentative content is the classic route by which feeds drift toward outcomes nobody chose. Some teams add survey-based heads ("was this worth your time?") precisely to give the value model a signal closer to satisfaction than raw engagement.

Buffers, prefetching and the cost per page

Running the full funnel for every scroll is wasteful: ranking 50 items on the first request and serving pages 2-5 from the buffer cuts second-stage work several-fold. The buffer goes stale as the user acts (they hide a creator, or watch three cooking videos in a row and the right next page changes), so production systems re-rank the buffer with fresh session features on each scroll, which is cheap, and rerun the whole funnel only after a few pages or a strong signal. This is one of the largest real cost levers and rarely mentioned by candidates.

Position bias in a feed

A feed is consumed sequentially, so position effects are strong and depend on device and layout (rec10). A common technique is to train the ranker with position as an input to a separate shallow tower whose output is added to the logit during training and dropped at serving, so the main network learns position-free relevance; YouTube's multitask ranking paper used such a shallow tower. Without it, items that historically appeared at the top keep their advantage through the labels.

Offline metrics that lie for retrievers

Recall@K on logged engagements only counts items that some earlier system chose to show, so a new retriever that finds genuinely good items the old system never surfaced gets no credit, and one that imitates the old system gets full credit. This is why retriever improvements are judged by their incremental contribution online (share of final impressions and engagements that only this source supplied) and why exploration traffic, which shows items regardless of the current retriever, is valuable as an evaluation set as well as for training.

Creator-side health

A feed is a two-sided market: creators decide whether to keep posting based on the exposure they get. If new creators' first posts reach almost nobody, the supply of new content falls, and the long-term feed gets worse even if every short-term metric was optimised. That is why exploration budgets are often framed per creator (each new creator's first posts get a minimum number of impressions to a well-matched audience) and why creator-side metrics sit beside user metrics in launch reviews.

How it fails in practice

The index from yesterday's model. A new two-tower model ships; the user tower updates immediately but the index rebuild with the new item tower fails silently. User vectors from the new model are searched against item vectors from the old one; the two-tower source returns near-random items, the ranker scores them low, and the feed quietly falls back on other sources. Engagement dips 1-2% and nobody connects it to retrieval. Version tags on vectors, refusing to serve mismatched pairs, and monitoring each source's share of final impressions catch it.

The disappearing hits. A team reimplements the retriever's training pipeline and drops the frequency estimation step. Offline recall on niche items rises; online, globally popular items stop appearing from the two-tower source, and users notice they are missing what everyone is talking about. Recall by popularity band, computed for every model, makes this visible before launch.

The weight change that wasn't. The share head is retrained with a new label definition and its predictions rise 30% on average. Nobody changes the value weights, but shares now count 30% more, and the page tilts toward highly shareable content, much of it low quality. Calibration checks per head at every retrain, and a rule that weight tuning is re-run when any head's mean shifts, prevent it.

The engagement-bait spiral. A content format that provokes comments ("comment your answer!") spreads; the comment head learns it, the value model rewards it, creators copy it. Comments per session rise, satisfaction surveys fall. Integrity classifiers that detect bait, demotion rules, and survey heads in the value model are the defences.

Metrics and dashboards

Per source: candidates returned, share of final impressions, latency. Per stage: stage recall (sampled), latency, fallback rate. Per head: mean prediction, calibration ratio by slice, log loss on recent data. Page level: diversity (distinct creators and topics per page), fresh-content share, exploration-slot share. Outcomes: engagement by type, hides, reports, session length, return rate. Creator side: exposure concentration, share of new creators receiving impressions.

Knobs people tune

Candidates per source and per stage; ANN search parameters (recall versus latency); the temperature and negative mix of the retriever; value-model weights; diversity penalty strength and repetition rules; freshness boost size and half-life; exploration share; buffer size and refresh policy.

The real cases: YouTube's corrected retriever and Instagram Explore

Yi et al.'s "Sampling-Bias-Corrected Neural Modeling for Large Corpus Item Recommendations" (RecSys 2019) describes the retrieval model YouTube added to its nomination stage. Both towers were three-layer networks ([1024, 512, 128]) sharing video-ID embeddings, trained with Adagrad (learning rate 0.2) and a batch size of 8,192 on streaming daily data, with a 15-day catch-up phase before the trainer starts waiting for new data, and serving from an index of about 10 million videos built periodically. Each clicked video's example weight was a reward reflecting engagement (0 for clicks with little watch time, 1 for a full watch). The key idea was the logQ-corrected in-batch softmax with item frequencies estimated from the stream by the two-array algorithm above (H = 50 million, α = 0.01). Offline, on a 10-million-video corpus, recall@50 was 0.4586 for plain softmax and 0.5322 for corrected softmax at τ = 0.05. In live experiments, adding the retriever to production raised the engagement metric by 0.20% without correction and 0.37% with it.

Meta's 2023 post "Scaling the Instagram Explore recommendations system" (Explore is visited by hundreds of millions of people daily) describes a four-stage funnel: retrieval, first-stage ranking, second-stage ranking and final re-ranking. Retrieval includes a two-tower model whose "user and item embeddings can be cached, making inference … extremely efficient", with item embeddings generated offline daily and served from ANN services such as FAISS or HNSW, alongside other sources including some precomputed during off-peak hours. The first stage is distilled from the second with the label "media in top K results ranked by the second stage". The second stage is a multi-task multi-label network predicting engagement events, combined by "Expected Value = W_click × P(click) + W_like × P(like) − W_see_less × P(see less) + etc.", with weights tuned to balance online engagement trade-offs. The final stage applies integrity filtering and diversity rules such as not showing items from the same author in sequence. Every design choice in this lesson's architecture has a counterpart there.

Goal: train a tiny two-tower model with in-batch negatives, with and without the logQ correction, on synthetic data with a heavy popularity skew, and see what the correction does to recall for popular and long-tail items. Uses NumPy from the existing C:\ra_projects\how-to-train-your-gpt\gpt_env virtual environment (no installs); runs in under a minute.

  1. Save as two_tower_logq.py and run it with that environment's Python: C:\ra_projects\how-to-train-your-gpt\gpt_env\Scripts\python.exe two_tower_logq.py.
  2. Read the two result blocks. Each prints recall@20 on held-out engagements split by item popularity (head = top 10% of items, tail = the rest).
  3. Expect the plain model to do worse on head items than the corrected one, because in-batch sampling made popular items over-represented negatives.
  4. Change TAU from 0.1 to 0.05 and 0.3 and watch both models move: temperature interacts with the correction, as Yi et al. found.
  5. Replace the true sampling probabilities with uniform ones (set USE_TRUE_P = False): the "corrected" model becomes the plain one, which shows the correction is only as good as the frequency estimate.
"""Two-tower retrieval with in-batch softmax, with and without the logQ correction.
NumPy (gpt_env). Synthetic users/items with Zipf popularity."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
U, I, D, TRUE_D = 2_000, 1_000, 16, 8
pop = 1 / np.arange(1, I + 1) ** 1.1; pop /= pop.sum()          # Zipf item popularity
tu = rng.normal(size=(U, TRUE_D)); ti = rng.normal(size=(I, TRUE_D))
def sample_pairs(n):                                              # engagement ~ popularity x affinity
    users = rng.integers(0, U, n)
    out = []
    for u in users:
        logits = ti @ tu[u] + np.log(pop)
        p = np.exp(logits - logits.max()); p /= p.sum()
        out.append(rng.choice(I, p=p))
    return users, np.array(out)
train_u, train_i = sample_pairs(60_000)
test_u, test_i = sample_pairs(5_000)
item_freq = np.bincount(train_i, minlength=I) / len(train_i)      # stands in for streaming estimate
head = np.argsort(-item_freq)[: I // 10]

def train(correct, TAU=0.1, B=256, steps=3_000, lr=0.05, USE_TRUE_P=True):
    Eu = rng.normal(scale=0.1, size=(U, D)); Ei = rng.normal(scale=0.1, size=(I, D))
    q = item_freq if USE_TRUE_P else np.full(I, 1 / I)
    for _ in range(steps):
        idx = rng.integers(0, len(train_u), B)
        u, i = train_u[idx], train_i[idx]
        uv, iv = Eu[u], Ei[i]
        logits = uv @ iv.T / TAU                                  # B x B, diagonal = positives
        if correct:
            logits -= np.log(q[i] * B + 1e-12)[None, :]           # subtract log sampling prob
        logits -= logits.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
        P = np.exp(logits); P /= P.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
        G = P.copy(); G[np.arange(B), np.arange(B)] -= 1          # dLoss/dlogits
        G /= (B * TAU)
        gu, gi = G @ iv, G.T @ uv
        np.add.at(Eu, u, -lr * gu); np.add.at(Ei, i, -lr * gi)
    return Eu, Ei

def recall_at(Eu, Ei, k=20):
    scores = Eu[test_u] @ Ei.T
    top = np.argpartition(-scores, k, axis=1)[:, :k]
    hit = (top == test_i[:, None]).any(axis=1)
    is_head = np.isin(test_i, head)
    return hit.mean(), hit[is_head].mean(), hit[~is_head].mean()

for name, corr in [("plain in-batch", False), ("logQ-corrected", True)]:
    r, rh, rt = recall_at(*train(corr))
    print(f"{name:15s} recall@20 all {r:.3f}  head {rh:.3f}  tail {rt:.3f}")

What to look for: the corrected model should recover more of the head items that dominate real engagement, and its overall recall should be at least as good; the plain model's systematic under-scoring of popular items is the bias the lesson describes. The numbers are small because the model is tiny; the direction is the point.

Work it out

Your feed serves 50,000 requests per second at peak. The second stage costs 2 µs of GPU time per item scored when items are batched (including the share of the per-request user-history encoding), and you run GPUs at 60% target utilisation. (a) How many GPUs does scoring 300 items per request need? (b) The first stage's stage recall is 82% at 300 items and 91% at 600. What does moving to 600 cost, and how would you decide? (c) Pages 2-5 are served from a 50-item buffer re-ranked with fresh session features. If 60% of requests are pages 2-5, how much does the buffer save?

Answer(a) 50,000 × 300 = 15 million items per second × 2 µs = 30 GPU-seconds of work per second. At 60% utilisation each GPU supplies 0.6 GPU-seconds per second, so 30 / 0.6 = 50 GPUs per serving region at peak, before redundancy (add N+1 per zone and a spare region's worth if regions fail over to each other). If per-item cost were 20 µs because the user-history encoder ran per item instead of per request, the answer would be 500 GPUs, which is why sharing user-side computation across the 300 items is the first optimisation to mention.

00.

(b) Doubling to 600 doubles second-stage cost, from 50 to 100 GPUs per region. It raises stage recall from 82% to 91%, so 9% more of the second stage's ideal top picks reach it. Decide with an online test of 300 versus 600 on a few percent of traffic: if the value metric rises meaningfully, compare the gain with the doubled fleet; often a better-distilled first stage gets most of the 9 points for far less, so try that first.

(c) If 60% of requests are buffer re-ranks that cost, say, 5% of a full funnel run, the average cost per request is 0.4 × 1 + 0.6 × 0.05 = 0.43 of the no-buffer cost: the buffer saves about 57% of ranking compute. The price is staleness, which the re-rank with fresh session features limits.

Diagnose it

A week after launching a retrained second-stage model, overall time spent is up 0.8% but "see less" taps are up 9%, the share of impressions going to the top 1% of creators rose from 18% to 23%, and the share of fresh content fell. The value-model weights did not change. What do you investigate?

Answer

Calibration shifts in the heads. With unchanged weights, a change in a head's average prediction changes its effective weight. Compare each head's mean prediction and calibration ratio before and after: if the watch-time head now over-predicts for established creators, their items gain value; if the see-less head under-predicts, its penalty shrank. Either produces exactly this pattern.

Feature changes favouring incumbents. If the retrain added or strengthened creator-level engagement features, established creators gain and new ones lose, pushing concentration up and freshness down. Check feature importance shifts and stage-by-stage source shares (did the fresh source's items stop surviving the first stage, which was distilled from the old second stage?).

Action. Roll back or hold the launch on guardrail breaches (see-less and concentration), recalibrate heads, re-distil the first stage from the new second stage, and re-tune weights. Add per-head calibration checks and creator-concentration and fresh-share guardrails to the automated launch gate so a retrain cannot ship these shifts silently.
