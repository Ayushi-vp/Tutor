A customer writes to an online retailer at 11 pm: "my parcel says delivered but it's not here, and I need it for Saturday". Today that message waits in a queue until a human agent starts their shift; the agent asks for the order number, looks up the courier's tracking, checks the policy for missing parcels, offers a replacement or a refund, and logs the outcome. It takes eleven minutes of the agent's time and arrives nine hours late. Multiply by 100,000 contacts a day and the support centre is a large, expensive organisation whose customers mostly wait.

An AI agent could answer at 11 pm in under a minute: look up the order, see that the courier's GPS put the delivery at the neighbour's address, tell the customer, and if needed start a replacement. That is the promise companies have announced: Klarna said in February 2024 that its assistant was handling two-thirds of its customer service chats in its first month, doing the work it equated to 700 full-time agents.

It is also where things go wrong in public. A chatbot that confidently states a refund policy that does not exist creates a liability: in Moffatt v. Air Canada, a Canadian tribunal held the airline responsible for what its chatbot told a grieving customer about bereavement fares. A bot that refunds whatever a persuasive customer asks for leaks money. A bot that never hands off traps frustrated customers in loops, and a bot that hands off everything saves nothing. And in 2025 Klarna's chief executive was reported to have said that the company's emphasis on cost had lowered quality, as it began hiring human agents again.

The senior design question is to build the agent so that it resolves the contacts it should, hands off the rest at the right moment with the right context, never takes an action the customer is not entitled to, says only what the company's policies say, and works on the phone as well as in chat. The lesson follows the interview: requirements and targets; estimates for traffic, tokens, voice minutes and the human costs that dominate the economics; the API and the architecture; deep dives on tools and permissions, the handoff threshold worked as a cost and CSAT curve, and the voice latency budget; the evaluation plan built on true resolution rather than deflection; failure modes and liability; and what changes at ten times the scale.

What the agent does

A customer-support agent is a language model with three abilities wrapped around it. It can look things up: the help centre's articles and the company's policies, found by retrieval (hd2). It can act: call tools that read and change the customer's orders, returns, subscriptions and payments (ag3). And it can stop: transfer the conversation to a human agent, with a summary, when it should not continue. Most of the design is about the last two: what it is allowed to do, and when it must stop.

Each conversation is about an intent, what the customer wants: track an order, report a missing parcel, return an item, change an address, dispute a charge, close an account. Intents are the unit of everything in this design: routing (which flow, which tools), policy (what the agent may do automatically), reporting (where the AI is good and where it is not) and handoff rules.

The mental model: a new support hire on probation

Think of a capable new employee in their first month. They have read the help centre and can look things up quickly. They can use the order system, but their login is limited: they can issue refunds up to $100 without approval, and the system itself blocks anything larger. Their manager has told them which topics always go to a senior colleague (bereavement, fraud, legal threats, anyone who sounds vulnerable), and that when they are not sure, it is better to pass a customer on with a good note than to guess. Their work is reviewed by sampling, by intent, before they are trusted with more.

The AI agent is that hire, with two differences that the design must handle. It does not get tired or slower at 3 am, and it handles thousands of conversations at once, so a mistake in its instructions repeats thousands of times before anyone notices. And it does not reliably know when it is guessing: a language model states a non-existent policy as fluently as a real one. So the "manager" (confidence scoring, policy code, handoff rules, sampling and evaluation) has to be built as a system, not assumed.

The two numbers that decide the economics

Human support is expensive: a fully loaded agent costs tens of dollars an hour and handles a handful of chats an hour, so each human contact costs several dollars. A model-handled conversation costs cents. The bill is therefore set almost entirely by how many contacts the AI resolves and how many it attempts and fails (a failed attempt costs the AI's cents, then the human's dollars, plus an annoyed customer). Both are set by the handoff policy. The model choice matters for quality and latency; for cost, the handoff threshold is the lever.

A reader who stops here has the shape: intents route the conversation; retrieval answers questions from current policy; tools act for the authenticated customer within limits enforced by code; a calibrated confidence and a set of rules decide when to hand off; and success is measured by real resolution and satisfaction per intent.

1 · Requirements

Functional. Handle support contacts in chat (web and app) and by phone, in the company's markets and languages. Authenticate the customer (logged-in session for chat; one-time code or account verification for voice). Classify intent; answer questions from the help centre and policies with links; perform actions through tools: order status and tracking, missing-parcel claims, returns and refunds, address changes before dispatch, subscription changes. Hand off to human agents with a summary, verified identity and actions taken; accept handoffs back for routine follow-up steps. Out of scope, said aloud: the human agents' own desktop tooling, email support (similar but asynchronous), and outbound marketing.

Quality targets. True resolution (no handoff and no repeat contact on the same issue within 7 days) of at least 60% of AI-attempted chats; CSAT on AI-resolved contacts at least equal to human-handled contacts for the same intents; policy-statement accuracy of 99%+ on the policy evaluation set; zero refunds outside policy limits; human requested by the customer honoured on the next turn, always.

Latency targets. Chat: first response token within 1.5 s at p95 for answers without tools, 4 s with tools (showing a "checking your order" indicator). Voice: about 800 ms from the caller finishing to the first audio of the reply at p50 and 1.2 s at p95; tool calls covered by spoken filler.

Cost targets. AI cost per conversation in cents; total support cost per contact down by at least 40% against all-human, with CSAT not more than 2 points below it.

Safety and compliance targets. Actions only on the authenticated customer's own accounts; personal data redacted from logs per retention policy (ae5); recordings and transcripts kept per consent rules; vulnerable-customer and legal intents always routed to humans; an audit trail for every action proposed and executed.

Clarifying questions. "What share of contacts are which intents, and which are transactional versus informational?" "What may an agent do today without a supervisor?" "How is a human contact costed, and is there a backlog?" "Which markets and languages, and is voice in scope at launch?" "What are the regulatory constraints, for example on credit products or call recording?"

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Traffic. 100,000 contacts a day: 75,000 chats and 25,000 calls. Contacts concentrate in about 12 busy hours, so chats peak at about 75,000 / 12 × 1.5 ≈ 9,400 an hour. With an average chat lasting about 8 minutes of wall-clock time, about 1,250 chats are open at once at peak.

Human cost baseline. Assume a fully loaded agent cost of $30 an hour. A chat agent handles about 6 chats an hour (some concurrently): $5 a chat. A phone agent handles about 4 calls an hour with wrap-up: about $8 a call (these are assumptions for the exercise, not industry figures). All-human: 75,000 × $5 + 25,000 × $8 = $375,000 + $200,000 = $575,000 a day, about 2,000 agent-shifts of eight hours.

AI chat cost. A chat averages 6 customer turns. Each turn's model call carries the system prompt and tool definitions (about 1,500 tokens), retrieved policy passages (about 1,000), the conversation so far and tool results (about 1,000): about 3,500 input tokens, and produces about 150 output tokens. At assumed prices of $3 and $15 per million: 3,500 × $3/10⁶ + 150 × $15/10⁶ = $0.0105 + $0.00225 = $0.0128 per turn, about $0.08 per conversation with tool and retrieval calls. Prompt caching of the stable 1,500-token prefix cuts the input side by roughly a third. That is about 1.5% of a human chat.

AI voice cost. An average call lasts 5 minutes with about 15 turns. Voice uses a smaller, faster model for latency: at an assumed $0.30 and $1.50 per million tokens, with 2,500 input and 60 output tokens per turn, about $0.0008 a turn, $0.013 a call. Speech recognition at an assumed $0.01 per minute ($0.05), text-to-speech for about 2.5 minutes of agent speech at an assumed $0.015 per minute ($0.04), and telephony at an assumed $0.01 per minute ($0.05): about $0.15 a call, against $8 for a human.

Voice concurrency. 25,000 calls in 12 hours with a peak of 1.5 × average is about 3,100 calls an hour; at 5 minutes each, about 260 concurrent calls at peak (Little's law: arrival rate × duration). Each needs a streaming ASR session, a TTS session and a model call every 20 seconds or so: modest for the model, but ASR and TTS capacity and the telephony connection must be sized for 260 concurrent streams with headroom.

Model traffic. Chat: 75,000 × 6 = 450,000 turns a day; voice: 25,000 × 15 = 375,000 turns. About 825,000 model calls a day, about 30 a second at peak. Small for an LLM gateway (hd13); the latency, not the throughput, is the engineering problem.

The insight to say out loud. The model bill is about $6,000 a day for chat and $4,000 for voice; the human bill it can save is hundreds of thousands. Every design choice that raises true resolution by a point is worth more than any model-cost optimisation, and every failed attempt that annoys a customer before reaching a human costs more than the model ever will.

4 · High-level design

A message arrives at the conversation manager, which holds the conversation state (intent, identity level, pending actions) durably so a crash or a handoff never loses it (hd7's durable execution). The intent router, a fast classifier over the latest message and context, picks the flow and the tools available; for a missing parcel that is order lookup, courier tracking, and the claim tool. Some intents are human-only by policy and are handed off at once. For the rest, the manager assembles the prompt: system instructions, the flow's instructions, tool definitions, retrieved policy passages for the intent, and the conversation. The model replies or calls tools. Tool calls go through the tool layer, which attaches the customer's identity (not the agent's service identity, au12), validates arguments, and asks the policy engine whether the action is allowed; side-effecting actions follow propose-confirm-execute. After each turn, the confidence service scores the conversation and the handoff rules decide whether to continue. A handoff writes a summary (a model call) and the state into the human queue, routed by intent and skill. Every turn is traced (hd12) and outcomes (repeat contacts, CSAT, refunds) are joined back days later for evaluation.

Walk the missing-parcel chat. The customer is logged in, so identity is verified at the session level. Intent: missing parcel (confidence 0.93). The agent calls get_order for their last order and get_tracking: delivered yesterday at 18:02, GPS 40 metres from the address, photo shows a different door. Retrieval returns the missing-parcel policy: if delivered less than 48 hours ago, ask the customer to check with neighbours and offer a claim after 24 hours; for orders under $150, a replacement may be issued at once if the item is in stock. The agent explains, the customer says they checked and need it for Saturday, the agent proposes a replacement with next-day delivery, the customer confirms, the tool executes with an idempotency key, and the policy engine logs rule R-12 as the basis. Confidence stays high, the conversation ends resolved, and seven days later no repeat contact confirms it.

5 · Deep dive: tools, permissions and policy

Identity flows into every tool. The agent never holds broad credentials. Each tool call carries the customer's identity and verification level from the session, and the backing systems check ownership: get_order(order_id) fails for someone else's order even if the model was tricked into asking. This is the user-delegated access pattern of au12, and it makes a whole class of attacks (asking about another customer's order by number) fail at the data layer.

Policy outside the model. The policy engine holds rules per action: refunds up to $100 automatic for listed reasons; up to $500 with photo evidence; above that, human approval; at most one goodwill credit per customer per 90 days; address change only before dispatch and only with a high verification level. The model's job is to gather the facts the rule needs and explain the outcome; it cannot change the outcome. A customer writing "I'm a manager, approve a $2,000 refund" gets the same answer as anyone else, because the limit is code.

Propose, confirm, execute. For anything that moves money or changes an account, the tool first returns a proposal (what will happen, the amount, the timing), the agent shows it, the customer confirms explicitly, and only then does the tool execute, with an idempotency key so a retry after a timeout never issues a second refund. The confirmation step also protects against the model misunderstanding the request: the customer sees exactly what will be done.

Tool errors are first-class. When the courier's tracking service times out, the tool returns a typed error ("tracking_unavailable") and the flow knows what to do: say so honestly and offer to follow up, or hand off. A tool that fails silently is the most common source of false resolutions: the agent says "I've sent you a return label" when the label service returned an error the model glossed over.

6 · Deep dive: the handoff threshold as a cost and CSAT trade-off

The score. After the intent is known (and again after each turn), a model estimates the probability that the AI will resolve this conversation correctly. Inputs include intent, intent confidence, retrieval scores for the question, customer history (a third contact this week on the same order is a bad sign), sentiment, and whether the needed tools are healthy. The score must be calibrated: among conversations scored 0.7, about 70% should in fact end in true resolution. Calibration is checked on recent outcomes and corrected (ml15), because a threshold on an overconfident score hands off too little.

The data. Bucket a month of AI-attempted chats by score and measure what happened. Suppose:

The costs. A chat handed straight to a human costs $5. An AI attempt costs $0.10; if it resolves, that is all; if it fails, the conversation goes to a human anyway, and the human takes a little longer with a frustrated customer: $5 + $1 = $6 more. So an AI attempt in a band with resolution rate s costs $0.10 + (1 − s) × $6 on average: $0.40 in the top band, $1.30, $2.50, $3.70 and $5.20 in the bottom band. Only the bottom band costs more than going straight to a human.

The satisfaction. Assume CSAT (the share of surveyed customers rating 4 or 5 out of 5) of 82% when the AI resolves, 80% for a direct human contact, and 50% when the AI tries, fails and then hands off (the customer has wasted time and often repeats themselves).

Check one row by hand. At 0.5, the AI attempts the top three bands: costs 40 × $0.40 + 25 × $1.30 + 15 × $2.50 = $16 + $32.50 + $37.50 = $86, and the remaining 20 chats cost 20 × $5 = $100: $186. It resolves 38 + 20 + 9 = 67 chats (CSAT 0.82 each), fails 13 (0.50 each), and 20 go to humans (0.80 each): (67 × 0.82 + 13 × 0.50 + 20 × 0.80) / 100 = (54.9 + 6.5 + 16.0) / 100 = 77.4%.

Choosing. Cost alone picks 0.3 ($170). But going from 0.5 to 0.3 saves $16 per 100 chats while losing 2 CSAT points. The business must say what a point is worth; suppose leadership values one CSAT point at $0.10 per conversation (from churn analysis: unhappy customers buy less). Score each threshold as −cost + 100 × $0.10 × CSAT%: 0.7 gives −224 + 791 = 567; 0.5 gives −186 + 774 = 588; 0.3 gives −170 + 754 = 584. 0.5 wins, narrowly. With a CSAT point worth $0.25, 0.7 wins. The point for an interview is not the number but the method: calibrated scores, measured outcomes per band, explicit costs, and an explicit exchange rate, with the decision revisited per intent (a billing dispute has a very different failure cost than order tracking).

Mid-conversation handoff. The threshold applies at intake and is re-checked after each turn, and hard rules override it: the customer asks for a person (always honoured on the next turn), the intent turns out to be policy-sensitive, the same step fails twice, sentiment drops sharply, or a tool returns needs_human_approval. Every handoff carries a summary (issue, identity verified, facts found, actions taken or proposed), so the human starts where the AI stopped; a handoff that makes the customer repeat themselves is the main reason failed attempts cost CSAT.

7 · Deep dive: the voice channel and its latency budget

A phone conversation feels natural when the reply starts within about a second of the caller stopping; longer silences make callers repeat themselves or talk over the agent. mm7 teaches the components; here is the budget for one turn.

What changes for voice. The model must be fast, so voice uses a smaller model or a fast tier with the system prompt cached, and answers are short (one or two sentences per turn; no lists, no links). Numbers, names and codes are confirmed back ("that's order 8-8-1-2-3, is that right?") because recognition errors on them are common and a wrong order number sends the conversation off course. Barge-in (the caller talking over the agent) must stop the agent's audio within a couple of hundred milliseconds and discard the rest of its planned reply. Identity on the phone is weaker than a logged-in session, so voice requires a verification step (a one-time code sent to the registered phone) before account actions, and the policy engine allows fewer automatic actions at that verification level. Handoff on voice is a warm transfer with the summary on the human agent's screen, and its threshold is usually higher than chat's: a failed voice attempt costs more patience than a failed chat.

8 · Evaluation plan

Offline. Per intent, an evaluation set built from real transcripts (redacted) with the expected outcome, tool calls and handoff decision; a policy set of questions about every policy, with answers checked against the current policy text; and a simulated-customer harness, in which a model plays a scripted customer persona through a multi-turn conversation against a sandbox with fake order and refund systems, judged by the final state of those systems. τ-bench (Yao et al., 2024) built exactly this kind of environment for retail and airline tasks with policy documents, and found that agents' success fell sharply when the same task had to be passed several times in a row, which is the reliability property support needs. Metrics: task success by final state, policy violations (target zero), unnecessary handoffs, missed handoffs, and calibration of the confidence score.

Online. A/B test by customer, so one person does not see two policies. Primary metric: true resolution rate. Guardrails: CSAT per intent (with the survey sent to abandoned and handed-off contacts too), complaint rate, refund rate and average refund per intent against the human baseline (to catch leakage), repeat-contact rate, handle time of handed-off contacts (a bad summary makes it rise), and policy-error rate from quality-assurance (QA) sampling. Report per intent and per channel; aggregate wins can hide an intent that got much worse.

9 · Keeping knowledge and policy current

Most invented-policy failures are really stale-knowledge failures: the help centre changed and the agent's index did not, or two articles disagree and retrieval found the older one. The knowledge pipeline therefore treats policies like code. Each article and policy has an owner, a version and an effective date; publishing a change re-indexes it within minutes, retires the old version from retrieval (keeping it for audit), and runs the policy evaluation set for the affected intents before the change goes live, so a reworded refund rule that the agent now misreads is caught before customers see it. Duplicate and conflicting articles are detected by embedding similarity at publish time and sent to their owners. Policy rules in the policy engine and policy text in the knowledge base must change together, which is why both carry the same policy ID and the agent cites that ID in its logs. Seasonal and temporary rules (extended holiday returns, a recall) have explicit end dates so they expire on their own rather than lingering in the index.

11 · What changes at 10× scale

At a million contacts a day across many markets, per-market policy and language become the main complexity: policy engines and knowledge bases per jurisdiction, evaluation sets per language, and voice models with local accents. The human queue shrinks in volume but not in difficulty, so routing by skill and the quality of summaries matter more. Model spend reaches several hundred thousand dollars a month, making a fine-tuned smaller model for the high-volume intents and aggressive prompt caching worthwhile. And the volume of conversations makes continuous QA possible: automatic review of every conversation by a validated judge (hd12) for policy errors and missed handoffs, with humans auditing a sample of the judge.

Calibration drifts with every change

The handoff threshold is set on calibrated scores, but calibration is a property of a specific model, prompt, policy set and customer mix. A new model that resolves more contacts makes the old score too pessimistic; a policy change that makes refunds harder makes it too optimistic for refund intents. Recalibrate on a rolling window of recent outcomes per intent, monitor the observed success rate in each score band daily, and treat a gap of more than a few points as an alert. Without this, a threshold chosen carefully in March is silently wrong by May.

Outcome labels arrive late and biased

True resolution needs a seven-day window, CSAT surveys are answered by a minority with a skew toward very happy and very unhappy customers, and refunds may be disputed weeks later. So the labels the evaluation depends on are delayed and selective. Use early proxies for fast feedback (did the customer confirm the solution, did the conversation end with an action executed) validated against the late labels, weight survey responses to correct for who answers, and keep a small random sample of conversations reviewed by trained QA staff as the unbiased reference.

Summaries are a product for the human agent

The handoff summary decides whether the human starts in ten seconds or two minutes. It should state the issue in one line, what the customer wants, what was verified, what was found (tracking says delivered, photo shows a different door), what was proposed or done, and the customer's mood. Evaluate it like any generated text: human agents rate summaries, and handle time after handoff is compared with handle time for direct contacts of the same intent. A summary that invents a fact is worse than none, so summaries are grounded in the logged tool results, not in the conversation alone.

Per-intent flows versus one general agent

A single agent with every tool is flexible and handles combinations ("change my address and also where's my other order"), but its behaviour is harder to test and its tool list is long, which degrades tool selection. Per-intent flows (a state machine with a model inside each step, ag7) are predictable and testable but brittle at the edges. Most production systems combine them: an intent router hands the conversation to a flow with a small tool set, and a general fallback agent with read-only tools handles the rest or hands off. The router's own errors then become a measured failure mode: conversations bounced between flows.

Vulnerable customers and duty of care

Some contacts involve bereavement, financial hardship, illness or distress, and many regulators expect companies to recognise and treat such customers appropriately. A classifier for vulnerability signals routes them to trained humans at once, with no attempt at automated resolution, and the threshold for that classifier is set for high recall: a false positive costs a few dollars of human time; a false negative can cause real harm and regulatory action. This is one place where the cost-CSAT curve does not apply, because the cost of an error is not commensurable with a few dollars.

Cost per resolution, not per conversation

Comparing $0.08 per AI conversation with $5 per human chat flatters the AI. The honest unit is cost per true resolution: total AI cost plus the human cost of failed attempts and their extra handle time, divided by contacts truly resolved. In the worked example at threshold 0.5, the 80 attempted chats cost $86 and resolve 67, so the AI's cost per true resolution, charging it for its failures, is about $1.28 against $5 for a human; still a large saving, but sixteen times the naive per-conversation figure.

Workforce planning changes shape, not just size

When the AI resolves the easy two-thirds, the human queue keeps the hard third: disputes, multi-issue contacts, upset customers, and the AI's failures. Average handle time for the remaining contacts rises (say from 10 to 14 minutes), so headcount falls less than the contact count. Staffing models that size queues for a target wait time (the Erlang C formula used in contact centres) must be rerun with the new arrival pattern and handle time, and the AI's own outages become a staffing risk: if the agent goes down at peak, the human queue receives three times its planned volume within minutes. Plans therefore keep a surge buffer, a degraded mode (the AI answers informational intents only), and a callback queue instead of unbounded waits.

The best contact is the one that never happens

Conversation logs, classified by intent and joined to outcomes, are the best map a company has of where its product confuses customers. If 12% of contacts ask where an order is because the tracking page shows the wrong carrier, fixing the page removes more cost than any threshold. A mature support-AI programme reports the top contact drivers each week to product teams, with volumes and example transcripts, and treats a falling contact rate per order, not a rising automation rate, as the long-run goal.

How it fails in practice

The policy the bot made up. The pattern of Moffatt v. Air Canada: a customer asks about a special fare or refund, the bot gives a confident, plausible and wrong answer, the customer relies on it, and the company is held to it. In that case the tribunal found Air Canada liable for negligent misrepresentation, rejected the argument that the chatbot was a separate legal entity responsible for its own actions, and was unconvinced that the customer should have checked the bot's answer against another page of the same website. The engineering response is grounding policy statements in current documents, approved templates for consequential intents, and logging each answer with the sources it used.

The return label that never existed. A label-generation service started returning errors for one carrier; the agent's tool wrapper returned the error text in a field the model read as success, and the agent told thousands of customers that their label was in their email. Repeat contacts for that intent doubled within two days, which is how it was found. Tools now return typed status codes, the flow checks status before telling the customer anything, and an alert fires when the repeat-contact rate of any intent moves.

The cost-first rollout. Klarna's own account is the cleanest public example of both sides. Its February 2024 release claimed that in the first month its assistant had 2.3 million conversations, two-thirds of its customer service chats; did work it equated to 700 full-time agents; had customer satisfaction on par with human agents; reduced repeat inquiries by 25%; cut resolution time from 11 minutes to under 2; ran in 23 markets in more than 35 languages; and was estimated to improve profit by $40 million in 2024. These are company-reported figures from one month after launch. In May 2025, reporting of a Bloomberg interview quoted the chief executive saying that cost had been too predominant an evaluation factor and that the result was lower quality, and the company was hiring human agents again to work alongside the AI. Read together, they argue for exactly the method in this lesson: measure quality per intent, value it explicitly against cost, and keep humans for what the AI does badly.

What to monitor

Per intent and channel, daily: AI attempt share, true resolution, handoff rate and reasons, CSAT by outcome, repeat-contact rate, refund rate and amounts, policy-error rate from QA, observed success per score band (calibration), average turns and duration, tool error rates, voice latency percentiles and barge-in counts, and the human queue's wait time and handle time after handoff.

Knobs people tune

Handoff threshold per intent and channel; hard-rule triggers (failed-attempt count, sentiment drop); automatic action limits in the policy engine; human-only intent list; voice endpointing silence; model tier per channel; retrieval depth for policy questions; summary format.

"""Handoff threshold: cost and CSAT per threshold from score bands. Python 3.12, stdlib only."""
BANDS = [  # (lower bound, share of chats, true resolution rate)
    (0.9, 0.40, 0.95), (0.7, 0.25, 0.80), (0.5, 0.15, 0.60), (0.3, 0.12, 0.40), (0.0, 0.08, 0.15)]
AI_COST, HUMAN, FAIL_EXTRA = 0.10, 5.00, 1.00
CSAT_AI_OK, CSAT_HUMAN, CSAT_AI_FAIL = 0.82, 0.80, 0.50

def evaluate(threshold, bands, n=100):
    cost = csat = 0.0
    for lo, share, s in bands:
        k = share * n
        if lo >= threshold:                                  # AI attempts this band
            cost += k * (AI_COST + (1 - s) * (HUMAN + FAIL_EXTRA))
            csat += k * (s * CSAT_AI_OK + (1 - s) * CSAT_AI_FAIL)
        else:
            cost += k * HUMAN; csat += k * CSAT_HUMAN
    return cost, csat / n

THRESHOLDS = [1.01, 0.9, 0.7, 0.5, 0.3, 0.0]                  # 1.01 = all human
print(f"{'threshold':>9} {'cost/100':>9} {'CSAT':>7}")
for t in THRESHOLDS:
    c, q = evaluate(t, BANDS)
    print(f"{'human' if t > 1 else t:>9} {c:9.1f} {q:7.1%}")

for value in (0.0, 0.10, 0.25):                               # $ per CSAT point per chat
    best = max(THRESHOLDS, key=lambda t: -evaluate(t, BANDS)[0] + 100 * value * 100 * evaluate(t, BANDS)[1])
    print(f"CSAT point worth ${value:.2f}: best threshold {best}")

real = [(lo, sh, max(0.0, s - 0.10)) for lo, sh, s in BANDS]  # model is overconfident by 10 pts
chosen = max(THRESHOLDS, key=lambda t: -evaluate(t, BANDS)[0] + 1000 * evaluate(t, BANDS)[1])
ideal = max(THRESHOLDS, key=lambda t: -evaluate(t, real)[0] + 1000 * evaluate(t, real)[1])
pc, pq = evaluate(chosen, BANDS); rc, rq = evaluate(chosen, real)
print(f"threshold {chosen}: planned ${pc:.0f} and {pq:.1%}, actually ${rc:.0f} and {rq:.1%}")
print(f"best threshold on the real rates: {ideal}")

What to look for: the cost curve flattening below 0.3 while CSAT keeps falling; the best threshold rising as a CSAT point becomes more valuable; and, with the overconfident score, a plan that promised $186 and 77.4% per 100 chats delivering roughly $234 and 74.9%. Here the best threshold happens to stay at 0.5, but the business case, the staffing plan and the CSAT forecast were all wrong; with a larger miscalibration the threshold itself moves. That gap is the price of skipping calibration.

Work it out

Using the lesson's bands and costs, the company handles 75,000 chats a day. (a) What is the daily cost at threshold 0.7 and at 0.5? (b) How many more chats a day go to humans at 0.7, and how many agents is that (6 chats per agent-hour, 8-hour shifts)? (c) CSAT differs by 1.7 points. What value per CSAT point per chat makes the two thresholds equally good?

Answer

(a) Per 100 chats: $224 at 0.7 (more exactly $223.50) and $186 at 0.5. Daily: 750 × $223.50 = $167,600 and 750 × $186 = $139,500. The difference is about $28,000 a day, about $10 million a year.

(b) At 0.7, 35 of every 100 chats go straight to humans; at 0.5, 20; and failed attempts add 7 and 13 respectively. Total human chats: 42 versus 33 per 100, so 9 more per 100, 6,750 more a day. At 48 chats per agent-shift, about 140 more agent-shifts a day.

(c) The cost difference per chat is ($223.50 − $186) / 100 = $0.375; the CSAT difference is 79.06% − 77.44% = 1.62 points. Equal when value × 1.62 = $0.375, so about $0.23 per point per chat. Below that, 0.5 is better; above it, 0.7. The decision rests on how well that value is estimated, which is why it should come from churn and lifetime-value analysis, not opinion.

Diagnose it

Two weeks after launching voice, the AI's resolution rate on calls is 20 points below chat for the same intents, and the most common handoff reason is "customer requested agent", usually within the first three turns. Latency dashboards show p50 turn latency of 1.4 s. What do you investigate and change?

Answer

Latency first. 1.4 s at p50 is well above the 800 ms budget; long silences make callers think the line is dead or the system is not listening, and early requests for an agent are the classic symptom. Break latency down by stage: endpointing silence too long, a large model on the voice path, an uncached prompt, TTS waiting for full sentences, or tool calls without filler. Fix the biggest item first, usually the model tier and prompt caching, then streaming TTS on the first clause.

Then the conversation design. Listen to calls: answers written for chat (long, with lists) read aloud are tiring; recognition errors on order numbers send flows off course; barge-in may not stop the agent's speech. Shorten answers, confirm numbers by read-back, make barge-in cut audio quickly, and greet with a short statement of what the agent can do and that a person is available, which reduces reflexive requests. And the threshold. A failed voice attempt costs more patience than chat, so set voice thresholds per intent from voice outcomes, not chat's.
