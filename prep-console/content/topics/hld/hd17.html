A company's accounts-payable team receives invoices: by email as PDFs, as scans from the post room, as photos from field staff, in forty layouts from four thousand suppliers. Today a clerk opens each one, types the supplier, invoice number, dates, line items, tax and total into the finance system, checks it against the purchase order and approves payment. A clerk handles a few dozen an hour. For a large company, or a business-process outsourcing firm that does this for hundreds of clients, the volume is millions of pages a day: invoices, insurance claim forms, onboarding forms, contracts.

Automating it looks easy in a demo: give a modern vision-language model an invoice image and ask for JSON. It works on the demo invoice. At five million pages a day the failure modes arrive. A model reads a total of 1,286.40 as 1,268.40 and the company overpays. A supplier changes its invoice design and accuracy for that supplier quietly collapses, hidden in the averages. A model asked for an invoice number on a page that has none writes a plausible one. Running the largest model on every page costs tens of thousands of dollars a day. And the documents the system is unsure about pile up in a review queue that nobody sized, until invoices are paid late and suppliers call.

The senior design question is to build the pipeline that turns documents into validated structured data at scale: choosing between OCR and vision-language models by cost and accuracy, understanding layout and tables, extracting into a schema with evidence for every value, deciding with calibrated confidence and validation rules which documents a person must check, sizing that review operation, meeting throughput and cost per page targets, and staying accurate as new templates appear. Document AI methods are taught in mm9 and vision-language models in mm3; this case is the production system.

The lesson follows the interview: requirements and targets; estimates for pages, compute, storage, model cost per page and the human review cost that dominates; the API and data; the architecture as a cascade; deep dives on OCR versus VLM cost and accuracy, extraction with evidence and validation, confidence and review-queue sizing, and drift on new templates; the evaluation plan; failure modes; and ten times the scale. The real cases are LayoutLM, which showed the value of layout-aware models, and Donut, which argued for skipping OCR, read for what they mean for a cost-tiered pipeline.

What the pipeline does

A document intelligence pipeline takes documents in and sends structured records out: for an invoice, the supplier, invoice number, dates, currency, line items, net amount, tax and total, each a typed field in a schema, each with a pointer to where on the page it was found. Between input and output sit a few kinds of work. Getting the text: either reading it from a PDF's embedded text layer, or OCR (optical character recognition: turning an image of text into characters with their positions on the page), or letting a vision-language model (VLM) read the image directly. Understanding layout: which text is a heading, a table cell or a label, and which value belongs to which label. Extraction: filling the schema. Validation: checking the result against arithmetic and reference data. And review: a person checks what the system is unsure of.

The mental model: a mail room with clerks of different seniority

Picture a large mail room. Envelopes are opened and sorted by type (invoices, claims, contracts). A junior clerk handles invoices from the 200 big suppliers whose forms they know by heart, filling in the ledger quickly. Unfamiliar forms go to a senior clerk, who is slower and more expensive but can read anything. Every entry passes a checker who adds up the columns and compares the bank details with the supplier file, whatever the clerk's confidence. Anything the clerks flag, or the checker rejects, goes to a supervisor's tray. The supervisor's tray must not overflow, because payments have deadlines.

The pipeline maps directly: the junior clerk is OCR plus a small layout-aware extractor trained on known templates; the senior clerk is a large VLM or LLM; the checker is the validation rules; the supervisor's tray is the human review queue. The analogy breaks in one important place. A human clerk who cannot read a number says so; a generative model may write a plausible number instead. That is why every value must carry evidence from the page, and why the checker runs on everything.

The two quantities that run the business

Straight-through processing (STP) is the share of documents that go from input to export with no human touch; accuracy of accepted documents is how often those untouched documents are right. They pull against each other through the review threshold: review more and accuracy rises while STP falls. Because a human check costs far more than any model call, the design's central question is how to raise STP while holding accepted-document accuracy at the required level.

A reader who stops here has the shape: cheapest text source first, a cascade of extractors by difficulty, evidence for every value, rules on everything, a calibrated decision about what a person must check, and a feedback loop from review to models.

1 · Requirements

Functional. Ingest documents by email, upload, scanner and API; store originals immutably; split multi-document files; classify document type (invoice, credit note, claim form, onboarding form, contract); extract fields defined per type in a versioned schema, including tables (line items); attach evidence (page, bounding box, source text) to every value; validate against rules and reference data (supplier master, purchase orders); route uncertain documents to a review interface; export accepted records to the finance or claims system; record corrections. Out of scope, said aloud: payment execution, contract negotiation analysis (clause risk review is a different product), and e-invoicing formats that arrive already structured (they bypass the pipeline).

Quality targets. Field-level accuracy of auto-accepted documents at least 99.5% on critical fields (supplier, invoice number, total, currency, bank details, due date) measured by a weekly audit sample; zero payments to a bank account not registered for the supplier without human approval; STP rate at least 90% for invoices from known suppliers.

Latency and throughput. Invoices: 95% exported or in review within 15 minutes of arrival; reviewed within 4 hours in business hours. Contracts: within 24 hours. Sustained 5 million pages a day with bursts of 3× the average.

Cost targets. Model and compute cost under one cent per page on average; total cost per document, including review, well below the manual cost it replaces.

Security and compliance. Documents contain personal and financial data: encryption, access by client and role, audit of who viewed what, retention per document type and jurisdiction (tax records are often kept for many years), and data residency per client.

Clarifying questions. "What share are born-digital PDFs versus scans?" "How many suppliers and templates, and how fast do new ones appear?" "What does an undetected error cost: a wrong payment, a compliance breach?" "What do reviewers cost and when do they work?" "Are there handwritten documents?"

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Volume. 5 million pages a day, averaging 2.5 pages per document: 2 million documents a day. 5 million / 86,400 ≈ 58 pages a second on average; documents arrive mostly in business hours, so plan for about 175 pages a second at peak. Mix: 60% invoices, 25% forms, 15% contracts by page count.

Text source. Suppose 40% of pages are born-digital PDFs with usable text layers: reading them costs microseconds of CPU. The other 60%, 3 million pages a day, need OCR.

Human review cost, the big number. Suppose 8% of documents go to review: 160,000 a day. A reviewer checks a highlighted document in about 60 seconds on average (most reviews confirm or fix one or two fields). That is about 2,700 reviewer-hours a day; at an assumed loaded cost of $25 an hour, about $67,000 a day, $0.42 per review, nearly five times the model cost of the whole cascade. Every percentage point of review rate is 20,000 documents and about $8,300 a day. This is the number the design must move.

Compute. If OCR is self-hosted instead of bought, a modern OCR model on one inference GPU handles on the order of 20 pages a second: about 9 GPUs at the 175-page peak. The layout extractor (a few hundred million parameters) handles more pages per second per GPU. A self-hosted VLM for the 10% hard path, at about 5 pages a second per GPU with batching, needs about 4 GPUs at peak. Compute is not the constraint; it is the cost line and the operations work.

Storage. Originals at about 150 KB per page are 750 GB a day, about 270 TB a year; with multi-year retention for tax records, petabytes in cold object storage over time. Extracted data and evidence are small (a few KB per document). Retention and deletion by client and jurisdiction is an engineering requirement, not an afterthought.

The insight to say out loud. The model choice moves cost by cents per page; the review rate moves it by dimes per document. The architecture is a cascade for model cost, but most design effort goes into calibrated confidence, validation and the review operation.

4 · High-level design

The pipeline is a set of stages connected by durable queues (in1), so each stage scales independently and a slow stage builds a backlog rather than failing. Ingest stores the original, computes its hash, and splits files that contain several documents (an emailed PDF with an invoice and a delivery note) with a page classifier that detects document boundaries. Text source checks each page for a usable text layer (characters present, plausible words, coverage of the page area) and sends the rest to OCR. Layout groups words into lines, blocks, tables and key-value pairs, and computes a layout embedding used to assign the document to a template cluster (in practice, mostly one supplier's design). The extraction cascade picks a path by cluster and difficulty. Validation runs rules and reference-data checks. Decision computes calibrated error probabilities per field and either exports or enqueues for review. Review presents the page with each uncertain field highlighted at its evidence location. Feedback stores corrections for calibration, monitoring and training.

Walk one invoice. A scanned two-page invoice arrives by email from a supplier the system has seen 4,000 times. No text layer, so OCR runs (about 300 ms per page). Layout assigns it to cluster c_1182 with high similarity. Path A, a layout-aware token classifier fine-tuned on that supplier family, labels the tokens for supplier, invoice number, dates, line items and totals in 40 ms. Normalisation turns "1.286,40 €" into 1286.40 EUR. Validation: line items sum to the net amount, net plus 21% tax equals the total, the invoice number is new for this supplier, purchase order PO-77120 is open and the goods receipt matches. But the IBAN on the invoice differs from the supplier's registered account: rule IBAN_MATCH fails. Bank-detail changes are a classic invoice-fraud pattern, so the document goes to review regardless of confidence, with the reason shown. A reviewer confirms with the supplier through a known channel; the exported record carries the decision.

5 · Deep dive: OCR versus VLM, and the cascade

OCR-based extraction. OCR produces words with boxes and confidences. A layout-aware model then labels them. LayoutLM (Xu et al., KDD 2020) showed the value of feeding a transformer not just the words but each word's 2D position on the page, pre-trained on millions of scanned documents: on form understanding it raised the benchmark score from 70.72 to 79.27, and it improved receipt understanding and document classification too. The strengths for production: every extracted value is a span of recognised text, so it exists on the page; per-token confidences are available; and a model of a few hundred million parameters is cheap to run and fine-tune per client. The weakness: OCR errors propagate (a 6 read as 8 is extracted faithfully), and the approach needs labelled examples for each new layout or field.

OCR-free and VLM-based extraction. Donut (Kim et al., ECCV 2022) read document images end to end without OCR, motivated by three problems the authors named in OCR pipelines: the computational cost of OCR, its inflexibility across languages and document types, and OCR errors propagating to later stages. Today's general vision-language models extend the idea: show the page image, give the schema, get JSON. They handle unseen layouts, handwriting, stamps, checkboxes and messy tables far better with no per-template training. Their weaknesses for production are cost per page (about seven times path A in the table), latency, and that they generate values: a VLM can produce a plausible invoice number that is not on the page or reorder digits without any OCR confidence to warn you.

The cascade. Use each where it is strongest. Known templates (most volume, since a few hundred large suppliers send most invoices) go to path A. Documents in unknown clusters, or where path A's calibrated confidence on a critical field is low, escalate to path B (an LLM over the OCR text and layout, prompted with the schema and a few examples from similar clusters). Hard cases (handwriting, low-quality scans, complex tables, B still unsure) escalate to path C, a VLM on the image. In the estimate, 70/20/10 routing gives about $0.0028 a page, a quarter of VLM-for-everything. The escalation rule is the same expected-cost logic as review: escalate when the cheaper path's probability of error times the cost of a review exceeds the extra cost of the stronger path.

Grounding the VLM. When path C is used, the prompt asks for each value's text exactly as printed and its approximate location; the pipeline crops that region, runs OCR on it, and checks the VLM's value against the OCR text (fuzzy for formatting). A value with no supporting text is marked unconfirmed and sent to review. This keeps the VLM's flexibility while restoring the property that values come from the page.

6 · Deep dive: schema-constrained extraction, evidence and validation

Schemas. Each document type has a versioned JSON Schema (ae4): field names, types (string, decimal, date, enum), required fields, patterns (an invoice-number pattern per client if known), and nested arrays for line items. LLM and VLM paths use constrained decoding or validate-and-retry so output always parses; the layout path fills the same schema from labelled spans. Normalisation is separate and deterministic: dates to ISO 8601 with the locale resolved (is 03/04 March or April? the supplier's country decides, and ambiguous cases are flagged), amounts to decimals with currency, using the locale's decimal separator ("1.286,40" is 1286.40 in German formatting).

Evidence. Every value carries the page, bounding box and source text. Reviewers see it highlighted; the system checks that normalised value and source text agree; and audits can show where a payment amount came from. Missing evidence is itself a review reason.

Validation rules are the cheapest, most reliable second opinion, because they check facts the model cannot know it got wrong:

  • Arithmetic: Σ(quantity × unit price) = net; net + tax = total; tax rate valid for the country and goods.
  • Check digits: an IBAN has two check digits validated by a mod-97 calculation, which catches nearly all single-character typos and transpositions; tax IDs often have their own checksums.
  • Reference data: supplier exists and is active; the bank account matches the supplier's registered account; the purchase order exists, is open and its amounts match; the goods receipt confirms delivery (a three-way match).
  • Duplicates: same supplier, invoice number and amount already processed, or the same file hash.
  • Plausibility: due date after invoice date; amount within the supplier's usual range.

A model that is 99% confident the total is 1,268.40 when line items sum to 1,286.40 fails the arithmetic rule and goes to review; a reviewer fixes a transposition the model could not see. Rules also make the system explainable: "held for review because the bank account changed" is a sentence an accounts-payable manager understands.

7 · Deep dive: confidence, the review threshold and queue sizing

From confidence to expected cost. Raw model confidences are usually overconfident (Guo et al.), so each extractor's confidence is calibrated per field and template cluster on reviewer outcomes: among totals scored 0.98, the observed error rate might be 4% rather than the 2% the score implies. The calibrated error probability p per critical field, and the cost of an undetected error on that field, give an expected error cost. A document is reviewed when, for any field, p × (cost of an undetected error) exceeds the cost of a review, or when any rule fails or evidence is missing.

Work the threshold. A review costs $0.42. An undetected error in the total or bank details costs, say, $50 on average (finding and correcting a wrong payment, supplier calls, occasional loss). Break-even p = 0.42 / 50 = 0.84%: review the document if the calibrated probability that the total is wrong exceeds 0.84%. For the invoice date, where an error costs perhaps $2 (a late-payment fee is rare), break-even p = 21%: almost never worth reviewing for the date alone. One global "95% confidence" threshold would review far too many documents for minor fields and might let through risky payment fields. Per-field thresholds from costs are both cheaper and safer.

Sizing the queue. Suppose 8% of 2 million documents, 160,000 reviews a day, arrive mostly in business hours: a morning peak of 12,000 an hour, 8,000 an hour in the afternoon, 6,000 in the evening. Each takes 60 seconds, so one reviewer completes 60 an hour. Keeping pace at peak needs 12,000 / 60 = 200 reviewers fully busy. Queues behave badly near 100% utilisation (small fluctuations become long waits), so planners staff to about 85%: about 235 reviewers. Now suppose only 180 are on shift. Capacity is 10,800 an hour, so during a four-hour peak the backlog grows by 1,200 an hour, reaching 4,800. In the afternoon, at 8,000 arrivals and 10,800 capacity, it drains at 2,800 an hour and is gone in under two hours. The worst wait is roughly the backlog divided by capacity, about 27 minutes on top of normal processing: within a 4-hour SLA. The same arithmetic shows when the SLA breaks: a Monday-morning peak of 20,000 an hour with 180 reviewers grows the backlog by 9,200 an hour.

Making reviews fast. Handle time is as important as review rate. The interface opens the page at the uncertain field, highlights the evidence, shows the rule that failed and the model's alternatives, and accepts a keystroke to confirm. Reviews where nothing is changed are tracked: a high "confirmed unchanged" share means thresholds are too cautious and calibration should be revisited. Hard cases (fraud suspicions, contracts) route to specialist queues.

8 · Deep dive: drift on new templates

Accuracy is not one number; it is one number per template cluster, and clusters change. New suppliers arrive weekly; existing ones redesign invoices; a client starts scanning on a new device at lower resolution. The pipeline watches per cluster: share of documents in no known cluster (a new layout), calibrated confidence distributions, rule-failure rates, review rate, and the reviewers' correction rate per field. A redesign shows up as a spike in rule failures and corrections for one supplier on the first batch, long before it moves the global averages. The response is automatic routing first (documents in a new or degraded cluster go to path B or C and to review at a lower threshold), then learning: once a few dozen corrected documents exist for the cluster, they become fine-tuning data for path A or few-shot examples for path B, and the cluster returns to the cheap path when its correction rate recovers. Retraining runs weekly with a frozen gold set per cluster, compared pairwise with the current model so a fix for one supplier does not quietly break another.

9 · Evaluation plan

Offline. A gold set per document type, stratified by template cluster, scan quality and language, with field-level labels and evidence locations, double-labelled on a subset to measure label agreement (ev5). Metrics: field-level exact match after normalisation, per field and cluster; table extraction accuracy (row and cell level); calibration error per field; rule coverage (how many real errors each rule catches); and, for the full pipeline, simulated STP and accepted-document accuracy at the proposed thresholds. Every model change is compared with the current one on the same documents.

Online. STP rate per type and client; accepted-document accuracy from a weekly random audit of auto-accepted documents (the only unbiased measure, since reviewed documents are by construction the hard ones); downstream correction rate (errors found later by finance staff); review rate and reasons; handle time; queue wait against SLA; cost per document including review. A/B testing a new extractor is done by routing a share of each cluster's documents to it, with accepted-document accuracy and review rate as the decision metrics.

11 · What changes at 10× scale

At 50 million pages a day across many clients, three things change. Self-hosting OCR and the VLM becomes clearly cheaper than buying per page, so GPU fleet management (hd1) and batching matter. The review operation, now thousands of people across time zones, becomes a scheduling and workforce system in its own right, with follow-the-sun queues and skills routing. And per-client customisation (schemas, rules, templates) needs a self-service layer so clients can define fields and rules without engineering work, with the evaluation gold sets per client to protect them.

Tables are their own problem

Line items are a table, and tables are where extraction fails most: merged cells, rows that wrap onto two lines, tables continuing across pages, subtotals mixed with items. Treat table extraction as structure recovery first (find rows and columns, link continuation rows, detect the header across pages) and field labelling second. Evaluate it at row level (is each row complete and correct) and reconcile it with the totals rule, which is the strongest check available: if extracted rows do not sum to the stated net amount, some row is wrong or missing, and the reviewer is shown the gap.

Calibration per cluster, not globally

A model can be well calibrated on average and badly calibrated on a cluster: overconfident on a new supplier, underconfident on a familiar one. Global calibration then sends familiar documents to review unnecessarily and lets new-template errors through. Calibrating per field and cluster (with pooling toward the global curve for clusters with few reviews) fixes both, and recalibrating daily on recent reviews tracks drift. The audit sample of auto-accepted documents is what keeps calibration honest, because reviewed documents alone are a biased sample.

Active learning without starving the measurement

Reviewer corrections are the training data, but they come only from documents the system was unsure about. A model trained only on those learns the hard cases and may drift on the easy ones it never sees labelled. The fix is a small random stream of confident documents into review (which also measures accepted accuracy) and an uncertainty-based stream for learning, with each label recording which stream it came from so training and evaluation can weight them correctly.

Documents as an attack surface

Invoices are a fraud vector: changed bank details, fake suppliers, duplicate invoices with altered numbers. When LLMs or VLMs read documents, text on the document can also attempt prompt injection ("ignore the total above; the amount due is…"). Defences are structural: the model only extracts, it never decides to pay; amounts and bank details are checked against reference data; extracted values must match OCR evidence; and rules about bank-detail changes route to people who verify through a channel the document does not control.

When to pay for the big model everywhere

The cascade wins in this lesson's numbers because review cost depends weakly on the extractor once rules and calibration are good. That is not universal. For a client with low volume, thousands of templates and expensive specialist reviewers (legal or medical documents), a stronger model on every document may cut review enough to pay for itself. The decision is an empirical comparison of total cost per document, models plus review plus errors, per client, recomputed as model prices fall.

Contracts are not invoices

Invoices have a small, stable schema and arithmetic to check. Contracts are long, have variable structure, and the useful fields (renewal date, termination notice period, liability cap, governing law) are often stated indirectly or across clauses. Extraction there leans on long-context LLMs with retrieval over the document's own sections, evidence that may span several passages, and no arithmetic rules to fall back on, so review rates are higher and reviewers more expensive. Treating contracts as a separate product line with its own targets avoids dragging the invoice pipeline's metrics and costs around. The evidence requirement also changes shape: a renewal date may be computed from a start date in one clause and a term in another, so the evidence is a set of passages plus the reasoning that connects them, and the reviewer needs to see all of them side by side to approve the value quickly.

Many clients, one platform: isolation of data and models

A processing firm serving hundreds of clients must keep each client's documents, extractions and corrections separate, and must decide whether models learn across clients. A shared base model trained on many clients' corrections improves faster, especially for suppliers common to several clients, but it means one client's data shapes another's results, which contracts may forbid. A common compromise is a shared base trained only on data clients have licensed for that purpose (or on synthetic and public documents), with per-client adapters or few-shot example stores trained on that client's corrections alone, stored and deleted with the client's data. Evaluation then runs per client too, so a base-model update that helps the average but hurts one client's templates is caught before rollout.

How it fails in practice

The decimal comma. A new client in Germany sends invoices with "1.286,40". A normaliser written for "1,286.40" read the amount as 1.28640 for one batch; the arithmetic rule failed on every document, so all went to review and nothing was paid wrongly, but the review queue tripled for a day. The fix took the locale from the supplier's country and the client's settings, and added a plausibility rule on amount magnitude per supplier. The lesson: rules turned a potential payment disaster into a queue spike.

The redesign hidden in the average. A large logistics supplier moved its total to the bottom left and added a "total excluding fuel surcharge" line. The layout model began extracting the wrong total for that supplier. The global accuracy dashboard moved by 0.1%; the per-supplier correction rate went from 1% to 40% on the first day. Per-cluster alerting caught it, routing switched that cluster to path B, and 50 corrected documents later path A was retrained.

The invoice number that was never there. Testing a VLM on credit notes, which often have no separate invoice number, the model produced plausible "CN-" numbers for 6% of them. Evidence checks against OCR flagged every one as unsupported. Without grounding, they would have been exported as references that did not exist.

The Monday backlog. Mondays carried the weekend's email backlog, a peak of 20,000 reviews an hour against a 180-reviewer roster built from the weekly average. The SLA broke every Monday until staffing followed the arrival curve and documents were prioritised by payment due date rather than arrival time.

What to monitor

Per client, type and cluster: pages and documents per hour, text-source mix, cascade path mix, confidence distributions, rule failure rates by rule, review rate and reasons, correction rate per field, "confirmed unchanged" share, handle time, queue backlog and oldest item age, STP, audit accuracy of accepted documents, downstream corrections, cost per document by component, new-cluster rate.

Knobs people tune

Per-field review thresholds (from error costs); escalation thresholds between paths; new-cluster similarity threshold; audit sample rate; reviewer staffing by hour; queue priority rules; retraining cadence; image-quality thresholds for re-scan requests.

The real case: LayoutLM and Donut

LayoutLM (Xu, Li, Cui, Huang, Wei and Zhou; KDD 2020) pre-trained a transformer jointly on text and its 2D layout in scanned documents, and reported new results on three benchmarks: form understanding (from 70.72 to 79.27), receipt understanding (from 94.02 to 95.24) and document image classification (from 93.07 to 94.42). It established that where a word sits on the page is as informative as the word itself, the foundation of the cheap, OCR-grounded path in this design. Donut (Kim et al., ECCV 2022) went the other way, reading the document image directly with a transformer and no OCR step, motivated by OCR's computational cost, its inflexibility across languages and document types, and the propagation of OCR errors into later stages; the authors reported state-of-the-art speed and accuracy on several visual document understanding tasks. A production pipeline does not have to choose: OCR plus layout models for the high-volume known templates, OCR-free or VLM reading for the long tail, grounded back to the page in both cases.

Work it out

Your pipeline handles 2 million documents a day with a review rate of 8% at $0.42 per review and a model cost of $0.007 per document. A VLM upgrade for all documents would raise model cost to $0.026 per document. (a) What review rate must the upgrade reach to break even? (b) If it reaches 5%, what is the daily saving? (c) What would you measure before believing the 5%?

Answer

(a) Current cost per document: $0.007 + 0.08 × $0.42 = $0.0406. Upgrade: $0.026 + r × $0.42. Break-even when 0.026 + 0.42r = 0.0406, so r = 0.0146 / 0.42 ≈ 3.5%.

(b) At 5%: $0.026 + $0.021 = $0.047 per document, which is more than $0.0406: a loss of about $0.0064 per document, about $12,800 a day. The upgrade does not pay unless review falls below 3.5%, or errors in accepted documents fall enough to be worth the difference.

(c) Run both on a stratified gold set and on a shadow share of production per cluster; measure calibrated review rate at the same accepted-accuracy target (not at the same raw threshold), the accuracy of accepted documents from the audit sample, hallucinated-value rate from evidence checks, latency, and per-cluster results, since gains often concentrate in hard clusters. Then consider routing only those clusters to the VLM, which may capture most of the review reduction at a fraction of the cost.

Diagnose it

On Tuesday the review rate jumps from 8% to 19%. The share of reviews where the reviewer changes nothing rises from 30% to 70%. No model was deployed. What happened, and what do you check?

Answer

Most extra reviews are unnecessary (nothing changed), so the extractions are mostly right but something is sending them to review. Candidates: a rule failing spuriously because reference data changed (the supplier master or purchase-order feed from the client's system broke or went stale, so account-match or PO rules fail); a calibration update that made confidences look worse (a bad batch of recent reviews skewing the calibration curve); an upstream change such as scan resolution dropping for one client, lowering OCR confidence without hurting values much. Check review reasons by rule and field, the freshness of each reference-data feed, the calibration job's last run and inputs, and the increase by client and cluster. Fix the cause, then make reference-data staleness its own alert and require calibration updates to pass a check against the audit sample before they take effect.
