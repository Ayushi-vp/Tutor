Someone joins a social network. They add three friends, look around, see a feed with almost nothing in it, and leave. Whether they come back depends largely on one thing: whether the network helps them find the people they already know. A new user with ten connections sees a living feed; a user with two sees an empty room. That is why every social and professional network has a "People You May Know" panel, and why it is among the most consequential recommenders a social product runs: it shapes the graph itself, and the graph drives everything else.

The obvious approach is friends of friends: if you and Priya share twelve friends, you probably know Priya. It works, and at scale it explodes. A user with 200 friends whose friends each have hundreds more has a hundred thousand friends-of-friends. A celebrity account with a million connections makes everyone who follows it a "friend of a friend" of a million people. Doing this for hundreds of millions of users every day is an engineering problem before it is a modelling one.

And the problems that matter most are not computational. A suggestion can reveal something: that a stranger uploaded your phone number, that you and your therapist share a mutual connection, that a person you blocked is still around. Suggestions can help spammers, who send thousands of requests hoping some are accepted. They can annoy receivers with requests from people they do not know. They can entrench popularity, suggesting the already-popular to everyone while new users get nothing. And evaluating improvements is unusually hard, because the edges a new model should predict were partly created by the old model, and because users in an experiment send requests to users outside it.

The senior design question is to build the system: candidate generation from the graph and other signals, with the fan-out and triangle-counting maths under control; features and a two-sided ranking model; privacy, safety, spam and fairness rules; graph storage at hundreds of billions of adjacency entries; and evaluation offline and online that is not fooled by feedback loops and network effects. The real case is Twitter's "Who to Follow", one of the few such systems described in detail by the people who built it, including the surprising decision to hold the entire graph on one machine.

What the system does

People You May Know (PYMK) shows each user a short list of people they might want to connect with. Behind the list is the social graph: users are nodes and connections are edges. In a friendship network (Facebook, LinkedIn) an edge is mutual and the graph is undirected; in a follow network (Twitter, Instagram) edges have a direction. This lesson designs for a mutual-connection network and notes where follow graphs differ. The system works like other recommenders (hd3): several candidate generators propose a few thousand people, a ranking model scores them, re-ranking and rules produce the final list. What makes it different is that the "items" are people, who have their own interests, privacy and right not to be bothered.

The mental model: a good host at a party

A thoughtful host introduces guests. They introduce you to people several of your friends know well ("you both know Sam and Ana from the climbing club"), not to people who merely share a famous acquaintance. They do not introduce you to someone who has asked not to be introduced, or to someone you have fallen out with. They do not say "she's here because she found your number in her phone". They pay attention to whether introductions go well, and they make sure the shy newcomer meets a few people rather than everyone crowding around the most popular guest.

That is PYMK's job description: mutual connections weighted by how meaningful they are (Adamic-Adar), filters for blocks and opt-outs, reasons that do not reveal private information, a ranking that cares about the introduction being welcome on both sides (two-sided), and attention to exposure for newcomers (fairness). The analogy breaks on scale. A host knows a few dozen guests; the system must consider hundreds of thousands of friends-of-friends for each of hundreds of millions of users, so the maths of fan-out decides what is possible.

Why this recommender is unusual

Three properties set PYMK apart from recommending videos or products, and each shows up later in the design. First, both sides have a say: a video cannot refuse to be watched, but a person can ignore a request, so the system must predict whether the suggested person will welcome the connection, not just whether the viewer will click. Second, the recommendations change the data: every accepted suggestion adds an edge, which changes everyone's friends-of-friends, so the system is partly building the graph it learns from, and its biases compound. Third, the items carry privacy: the reason someone is suggested can reveal facts about them or about a third person, so what the system may use and say is constrained by rules, not just by accuracy. A candidate who keeps these three in mind will reach for a two-sided objective, careful evaluation and privacy rules without being prompted.

A reader who stops here has the shape: offline candidate generation from the graph (capped friends-of-friends), affiliations, contacts and embeddings; an online path that filters, ranks on both sides' probabilities and re-ranks; and privacy, spam and fairness rules wrapped around it.

1 · Requirements

Functional. Show each user a ranked list of suggested people on several surfaces (a panel in the feed, a full page, onboarding, after accepting a request), each with a short reason where safe ("12 mutual friends", "also studied at X"); let users send a request, dismiss a suggestion, or opt out of being suggested; react within minutes to new connections (after you connect with someone, their friends appear). Out of scope, said aloud: the feed itself (hd11), messaging, and follow suggestions for public figures, which behave like content recommendation.

Quality targets. Increase accepted connections that lead to interaction within 30 days; keep the ignore-and-report rate per request received below a set ceiling; every new user with at least one connection sees at least 10 credible suggestions within a minute of joining.

Latency targets. p99 of 100 ms for the online ranking request; new-connection-triggered candidates available within 5 minutes; precomputed candidates refreshed at least daily for daily active users.

Privacy and safety targets. Never suggest across a block in either direction; honour opt-outs within minutes; no adult-stranger suggestions to minors; no reasons that disclose another user's private data; no suggestions based on sensitive signals such as precise location co-presence or health-related affiliations.

Clarifying questions. "Mutual connections or follows?" "How many users and what is the degree distribution, including the largest accounts?" "Do we have contact uploads, and under what consent?" "Are there minors on the platform?" "What does success mean for the business: retention of new users, network density, engagement?"

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

The graph. 500 million monthly users, 200 million daily. Average degree 200, heavy-tailed: most users have tens of connections, some thousands, and a few organisational or celebrity accounts hundreds of thousands or more. Undirected edges: 500 million × 200 / 2 = 50 billion. Stored as adjacency lists, each edge appears twice: 100 billion entries. With 64-bit user IDs, 8 bytes each: 800 GB raw, before offsets, edge metadata (creation time, a tie-strength weight) and replication. Twitter's WTF paper (2012 numbers) is a useful contrast: over 20 billion edges among active users, held in memory on single servers with 144 GB of RAM at about five bytes per edge. Our graph is several times larger and carries metadata, so it is sharded by user ID across in-memory graph servers, a few dozen machines with three replicas.

Friends-of-friends fan-out for one user. The number of two-hop paths from user u is the sum of their friends' degrees. You might guess 200 × 200 = 40,000, but friends are not average users: well-connected people appear in many friend lists, so the average degree of a friend is higher than the average degree of a user. This is the friendship paradox, and its size is E[d²] / E[d]. With a heavy-tailed distribution, suppose friends average 600. Then a typical user has about 200 × 600 = 120,000 two-hop paths; with overlaps (many friends share friends), perhaps 50,000 to 60,000 distinct friends-of-friends. A user with 5,000 connections has millions.

Fan-out for the whole graph. Counting mutual friends for every pair is computing A² for the adjacency matrix A, and the cost is the number of two-paths: each node w contributes deg(w)² ordered pairs (every pair of its neighbours). The total is N × E[d²]. Since E[d²] = 600 × 200 = 120,000, the total is 500 million × 120,000 = 6 × 10¹³. A single hub with a million connections contributes 10¹² by itself. Even at a billion pair-updates a second, a full daily recomputation would take 17 hours of a large cluster just for counting. This is the number that forces the design: caps, sampling, hub handling and incremental computation (section 5).

Serving traffic. 200 million daily users see PYMK on about three surfaces a day: 600 million requests, about 7,000 a second on average and 20,000 at peak. Each request ranks about 500 candidates: 10 million model scorings a second at peak. A gradient-boosted tree model at about 5 microseconds per candidate per core is 50 core-seconds per second, plus feature fetching, which dominates. A modest CPU fleet, sized by feature-store reads more than by the model.

Precomputed candidates. For 200 million active users, 1,000 candidates each with an ID and a few precomputed scores (about 16 bytes): 3.2 TB in a key-value store, refreshed daily and patched incrementally.

The insight to say out loud. Storage fits in a few dozen machines; serving is ordinary ranking at 20,000 requests a second. The hard number is the two-hop fan-out, the sum of squared degrees, which the heavy tail of the degree distribution makes enormous.

4 · High-level design

Offline. A daily graph snapshot feeds the friends-of-friends job, which for each active user enumerates two-hop candidates under fan-out caps and computes mutual-friend count, Adamic-Adar and Jaccard. An affiliation join produces candidates sharing a school, employer or group (bounded per affiliation: a 100,000-person employer is not 100,000 candidates). Contact matching produces candidates from consented uploads. The embedding model's nearest-neighbour index produces candidates beyond two hops. The union, de-duplicated and pre-scored by a cheap model, is truncated to the top 1,000 per user and written to the candidate store.

Streaming. New edges, blocks, dismissals and opt-outs arrive as events. A new connection u–v triggers incremental candidate generation: v's friends become u's candidates (and u's friends become v's), scored and merged into the candidate lists within minutes. Blocks and opt-outs update filter sets immediately.

Online. A request loads the user's candidate list and any real-time additions, applies hard filters against fresh state (connections, pending requests, blocks, opt-outs, safety rules), fetches features for the top 500 by offline score, ranks them, re-ranks the top for diversity and exposure caps, attaches privacy-safe reasons, logs the impression, and returns 20.

5 · Deep dive: candidate generation and the fan-out maths

Mutual friends are triangles. If u and v share a friend w, the path u–w–v exists; suggesting u–v would close the triangle u, v, w. Graphs of human relationships are full of triangles (your friends tend to know each other), which is why mutual friends predict future edges so well. The common-neighbours score is the number of such w. Two refinements matter. Jaccard divides by the size of the union of both friend lists, so two users with 10 mutual friends out of 30 each score higher than two with 10 out of 3,000. Adamic-Adar sums 1 / log(deg(w)) over mutual friends w, so a mutual friend with 40 connections (1 / log 40 ≈ 0.27) counts about twice as much as one with 2,000 (1 / log 2,000 ≈ 0.13), and a celebrity hub with a million counts 0.07. In Liben-Nowell and Kleinberg's comparison of link-prediction scores on co-authorship networks, Adamic-Adar was among the strongest simple predictors.

Controlling fan-out. Four techniques, used together. Cap per friend: from each friend, take at most 100 neighbours, preferring strong ties (recent interaction) or sampling; a user's work is then at most 200 × 100 = 20,000 paths. Skip or down-weight hubs: friends with degree above a threshold (say 5,000) contribute no friends-of-friends; their Adamic-Adar weight is tiny anyway. Compute only for active users, daily for daily users and weekly for others. Incremental updates: when edge u–v is added, only u's and v's candidate lists change materially (each gains the other's friends), which costs reading two adjacency lists instead of recomputing anything. With caps, the daily job for 200 million active users is at most 200 million × 20,000 = 4 × 10¹² path visits in the worst case and far fewer in practice (most users have fewer than 200 friends), a large but ordinary batch job.

Random walks as an alternative. Instead of enumerating two hops, run short random walks from u that restart at u with some probability (personalised PageRank) and count visits; nodes visited often are "close" to u even beyond two hops. Twitter's WTF computed each user's circle of trust this way, using Monte Carlo walks whose memory cost is only the visit counters, then ran SALSA (a random walk alternating between a user's circle and the accounts they follow) to rank whom to follow. Walks bound the work per user by the number of steps, regardless of hubs, at the cost of sampling noise.

Beyond the graph. New users have almost no graph; and many real-world acquaintances share no mutual connection on the platform. Affiliations (same school and year, same employer and team, same group) catch them, bounded by taking members with the strongest overlap. Contact uploads, only with consent, match phone numbers and emails the user chose to share. Embeddings from a two-tower model (rec4) trained on accepted connections, with user features and graph features as inputs, find similar-network people via nearest-neighbour search. The sources' candidates are merged; their source becomes a feature for the ranker.

6 · Deep dive: features and two-sided ranking

Features. Graph: mutual friends, Adamic-Adar, Jaccard, number of mutual friends who connected recently, whether the candidate is a friend of a strong tie, random-walk visit counts. Affiliation: shared school, employer, group, overlap in time. Behaviour: profile views either way, prior impressions of this candidate and the user's responses (fatigue), how the candidate responds to requests generally (accept rate), how active each user is. Context: surface, time since joining. Trust: account age, verification, spam score. Degrees of both. What is deliberately absent: sensitive attributes (ethnicity, religion, health), precise location, and contact-upload details beyond a match flag.

The objective. The value of a suggestion is an accepted connection that leads to real interaction, and its cost includes bothering the receiver. A multi-task model (hd11) predicts p(send | shown), p(accept | sent), p(ignore or report | sent), and optionally the expected interaction after connecting. The ranking score is p(send) × p(accept) × value − λ × p(send) × p(negative). Work an example: candidate A has p(send) 0.10 and p(accept) 0.80, so 0.08 expected accepted connections; candidate B, a stranger with a large network and many mutual friends from a hub, has p(send) 0.20 but p(accept) 0.15, so 0.03, and p(negative) 0.5. A send-only ranker puts B first; the two-sided ranker puts A first by a wide margin. This is also the main defence against spam: accounts whose requests are ignored get low p(accept) and fall out of rankings.

Re-ranking. Diversity (not ten suggestions from the same company; rec12), exposure caps (no person suggested to more than a set number of users a day, so popular people are not flooded and requests stay manageable), reserved slots for newer users with small networks, and freshness (a suggestion shown and ignored five times is retired).

7 · Deep dive: privacy, safety, spam and fairness

What a suggestion reveals. Every suggestion is an implicit statement: "the system thinks you know this person". The reason makes it explicit. Rules: reasons show only signals visible to the viewer (mutual friends whose friendships the viewer can see; affiliations the candidate made visible); suggestions derived from someone else's contact upload carry no reason and require a second signal before being shown, so a stranger who has your number does not get you suggested on that basis alone; precise location co-presence is never a signal, because it can reveal visits (clinics, shelters, places of worship); and opt-outs from being suggested are honoured globally.

Safety. Blocks are symmetric hard filters, checked at serving time against live state. Minors are not shown adult strangers, and adults are not shown minors outside existing real-world ties the platform can verify (shared school with age-appropriate rules, mutual family connections). Accounts under investigation are excluded.

Spam and fake accounts. Spammers want to be suggested. They create clusters of fake accounts that connect to each other and to real users, manufacturing mutual friends. Defences: account-trust features and filters; the two-sided objective; per-account limits on how often they are suggested; detection of anomalous clusters (dense groups of new accounts with many outgoing requests and low acceptance); and rate limits on requests from new or low-acceptance accounts.

Fairness and feedback loops. PYMK changes the graph it learns from. Popular users are suggested more, gain more connections and become more popular. New users with few connections have few friends-of-friends and get few suggestions: a cold-start trap at exactly the moment retention is decided. Homophily (people connect with similar people) can make suggestions reinforce existing divisions. Measures: the concentration of exposure (share of all suggestion impressions going to the top 1% of users), accept rates and suggestion quality by network size and by region, and the time a new user takes to reach ten connections. Interventions: exposure caps, reserved exposure for newcomers, and diversity of affiliation in re-ranking.

8 · Deep dive: graph storage and the two-hop query

The graph lives in two forms. The system of record is a sharded, replicated store of edges (in2), optimised for writes (new connections) and per-user reads. The graph serving tier holds adjacency lists in memory for fast traversal: sharded by user ID, each adjacency list stored as a sorted array of IDs (so intersecting two lists to count mutual friends is a merge), with a compact encoding (delta-encoded sorted IDs compress well, but cost decode time). A two-hop query for u reads u's list from its shard, groups the friends by shard, sends one batched request per shard returning each friend's capped neighbour list, and merges counts in the caller. With 200 friends spread over 40 shards, that is one round trip of 40 parallel requests, perhaps 10 to 20 ms. That is fine for incremental updates and for real-time candidates after a new connection, but not something to run on every page view, which is why candidates are precomputed.

The WTF decision is worth discussing in an interview. In 2010 Twitter's team calculated that, at about eight bytes per edge naively and about five in practice, a server with 64 GB for data could hold roughly eight billion edges, and that memory growth would keep the graph fitting for 24 to 36 months. Holding the whole graph on each server made replication the scaling and fault-tolerance strategy, avoided graph partitioning entirely, and let them launch in a few months. The costs, which they described, were the scaling ceiling and little room for edge and vertex metadata. The lesson is not "use one machine" but "do the arithmetic before choosing the architecture".

9 · Evaluation plan

Offline. Link prediction on snapshots: take the graph at time T, generate and rank candidates, and check recall and precision at k against edges formed in (T, T + 30 days]. Report per user segment (new, small network, large network) and per candidate source. Correct for the feedback loop: edges formed because the old system suggested them favour models that imitate it, so weight positives by the inverse probability of having been shown (rec10), report edges formed outside PYMK as a separate slice, and use a small exploration share in production to collect less biased labels. Ranking models are also evaluated on logged impressions (send and accept prediction quality, calibration).

Online. The primary metric is accepted connections per user that lead to interaction within 30 days (WTF's equivalent concern led them beyond follow-through rate to an "engagement per impression" metric measured after acceptance). Guardrails: ignore-and-report rate per request received, requests received per user, opt-out rate, privacy complaints, and new-user time to ten connections. Network interference is the trap: a treated user sends more requests, many to control users, whose metrics then change. Cluster-randomised experiments (Ugander et al.) assign whole graph communities to arms so most edges stay within an arm; two-sided metrics attribute each request's outcome to the arm of the sender. Without this, user-level tests overstate or understate the effect.

11 · What changes at 10× scale

At 5 billion users and 500 billion edges the graph serving tier becomes thousands of machines, and cross-shard two-hop queries become expensive enough that graph-aware partitioning (placing densely connected communities on the same shard) pays for its complexity. Candidate generation shifts further toward embeddings and random walks with bounded work, with friends-of-friends computed incrementally only. Regional and legal differences multiply: data residency for the graph, different rules on contact uploads and minors. And experiments need cluster randomisation by default, because at that density almost every request crosses arms.

Directed graphs change the question

In a follow network, edges are one-way and the asymmetry matters: following a celebrity says you are interested, not that you know them. WTF's authors noted that Twitter's graph is closer to an interest graph than a social graph, and separated two problems: "interested in" (accounts you would like to read) and "similar to" (users with similar follows). SALSA fits this well: its walk alternates between a user's circle of trust (hubs) and the accounts they follow (authorities), ranking authorities by how much trusted hubs follow them. Mutual-friend measures still work for the social part, but interest suggestions behave more like content recommendation, and the two-sided accept term disappears because following needs no acceptance.

Ensembles of candidate generators

WTF reported that the production algorithm blended results from about 20 different algorithms. That is typical: no single generator covers everyone (new users need contacts and affiliations; heavy users need deep graph walks; dormant users returning need recency). The ranking model sees each candidate's sources as features and learns how much to trust each per segment, and the candidate quotas per source are tuned so cheap generators do not crowd out rare but valuable ones.

Incremental computation and the "just connected" moment

The most valuable time for suggestions is right after a new connection: the user is engaged and the new friend's network is fresh territory. Precomputed lists are a day old at that moment. A streaming job reading new-edge events computes the new friend's capped neighbourhood, scores it with a light model, and pushes it into the user's candidate list within seconds, while the batch job remains the source of truth. This is also where WTF's design had to compensate: its in-memory graphs were refreshed from daily snapshots, so it built a separate real-time path for new users.

Graph-aware sharding

Hashing users to shards spreads load evenly but puts most friends of a user on other shards, so every two-hop query fans out widely. Partitioning by communities (people in the same city, school or company placed together) keeps more edges local and cuts fan-out, at the cost of hot shards for large communities and rebalancing as the graph changes. WTF's authors called partitioning large, changing, power-law graphs a very difficult problem, which is one reason they avoided it. Most systems start with hashing plus replication of hub adjacency lists and adopt community-aware placement only when cross-shard traffic dominates.

Explainability is a product feature and a risk

Reasons ("12 mutual friends") raise acceptance, because they make the suggestion credible. But each reason type must be checked for what it discloses, to whom, and in combination: "you both went to X" plus "3 mutual friends" plus the suggestion's position may reveal more than any one signal. A review process for every new reason type, and a rule that reasons are computed only from data visible to the viewer, keeps explainability from becoming a leak.

What the graph does to the metrics

Because PYMK grows the graph, its effects compound: more connections mean more friends-of-friends, more candidates, more connections. Short experiments understate long-term effects, and long-term holdouts (a small group with a frozen older algorithm for months) are the only way to measure them. The same compounding makes harms grow too: a ranker that slightly over-suggests popular users concentrates the graph over months, which a two-week test will not show.

Tie strength makes the graph weighted

Not all friendships are equal: a colleague you message daily and a classmate you have not spoken to in ten years are both one edge. Weighting edges by tie strength (recent interactions, messages, profile views, shared photos) improves almost every part of the system: per-friend fan-out caps sample strong ties first, mutual friends who are strong ties of both users count more, and random walks follow strong ties more often. The cost is that the graph is no longer just IDs: each adjacency entry carries a weight that must be updated as interactions happen, which is the edge-metadata problem WTF's authors identified as a limit of their compact in-memory design (an 8-bit weight added 12.5% to their memory per edge). A practical compromise keeps weights quantised to a few bits and refreshes them daily.

How it fails in practice

The celebrity that broke the batch job. A public figure's account, configured as a mutual-connection profile, reached two million connections. The friends-of-friends job's shard holding that adjacency list ran for hours, and every connection of that account became a "friend of a friend" of two million people with one mutual friend each. Hubs above a degree threshold are now excluded from two-hop enumeration and handled as an interest signal instead.

The suggestion that revealed a contact upload. A user was suggested to a stranger with the reason "you may know each other", because the stranger had uploaded the user's phone number. The user inferred that the stranger had their number. The rule since: candidates whose only signal is someone else's contact upload are shown without a reason and only if a second independent signal exists.

The spam ring. A cluster of a few thousand new accounts connected to each other and then sent requests to real users at a steady rate, each request showing dozens of mutual friends (all fake). Acceptance from real users was low, which the two-sided model eventually picked up, but only after a week. Cluster detection on dense new-account subgraphs with low external acceptance now removes such candidates within hours.

The experiment that lied. A new ranker showed +6% accepted connections in a user-level A/B test, mostly because treated users sent many more requests to control users, who accepted them; control's own suggestions were crowded out. A cluster-randomised rerun measured +2%. The team now runs graph-cluster randomisation for every PYMK experiment.

What to monitor

Per surface and segment: impressions, send rate, accept rate, ignore and report rate, requests received per user, new users' time to ten connections, candidate-source mix, exposure concentration, opt-out rate, privacy complaints, offline job duration and the largest shard's time, candidate staleness, serving latency.

Knobs people tune

Per-friend fan-out cap; hub threshold; candidate quotas per source; λ for negative outcomes; exposure caps per person; reserved slots for new users; diversity strength; impression fatigue rules; refresh cadence per activity tier.

The real case: Twitter's WTF

"WTF: The Who to Follow Service at Twitter" (Gupta, Goel, Lin, Sharma, Wang and Zadeh, WWW 2013) describes a service that launched in summer 2010 after a few months of work by a small team. The design decision its authors highlight is holding the entire Twitter graph in memory on a single server: in August 2012 the graph had over 20 billion edges among active users, with power-law degrees (over 1,000 users with more than a million followers), and the team calculated about five bytes per edge in practice, using servers that grew from 72 GB to 144 GB of RAM. Their in-memory engine, Cassovary (open-sourced in 2012), stored adjacency lists as shared arrays of 32-bit integers; fault tolerance and scale came from replicas each holding the full graph, loaded from daily snapshots. Each server generated about 100 recommendations for a user in under 500 ms per thread. The algorithms started from a user's "circle of trust", computed with Monte Carlo random walks similar to personalised PageRank, then applied SALSA with about 500 top circle-of-trust accounts as hubs; in their reported experiment, SALSA had the highest follow-through rate, well above personalised PageRank and closure-based suggestions, while the production system blended about 20 algorithms. They also described the design's limits (memory ceiling, little room for features) and a move toward Hadoop and machine-learned models. It is a rare first-party account of the trade-offs this lesson is about.

Goal: build a heavy-tailed random graph, measure friends-of-friends fan-out and the friendship paradox, see what a hub does to the sum of squared degrees, and compare common neighbours, Jaccard and Adamic-Adar for predicting held-out edges. Python 3.12, standard library only.

  4. Read recall at 10 for the three scores on held-out edges. Adamic-Adar usually edges out common neighbours; Jaccard often does worse on graphs like this.

"""Friends-of-friends fan-out and link prediction on a preferential-attachment graph. Python 3.12."""
import math, random
from collections import Counter, defaultdict

rng = random.Random(5)
N, M = 20_000, 5
adj = defaultdict(set); targets = []                 # preferential attachment (Barabasi-Albert style)
for v in range(N):
    picks = set()
    while len(picks) < min(M, v):
        picks.add(rng.choice(targets) if targets else rng.randrange(v))
    for u in picks:
        adj[v].add(u); adj[u].add(v); targets += [u, v]
deg = {v: len(adj[v]) for v in range(N)}
d = list(deg.values()); mean = sum(d) / N; mean_sq = sum(x * x for x in d) / N
friend_mean = sum(deg[u] for v in range(N) for u in adj[v]) / sum(d)
print(f"mean degree {mean:.1f}  mean friend degree {friend_mean:.1f}  E[d^2]/E[d] {mean_sq / mean:.1f}")

sq = sorted((x * x for x in d), reverse=True)
print(f"sum of deg^2 = {sum(sq):,}  top 1% of nodes contribute {sum(sq[:N // 100]) / sum(sq):.0%}")
sample = rng.sample(range(N), 1_000)
paths = sum(sum(deg[w] for w in adj[u]) for u in sample) / len(sample)
capped = sum(sum(min(deg[w], 20) for w in adj[u]) for u in sample) / len(sample)
print(f"avg two-hop paths per user {paths:,.0f}; with cap 20 per friend {capped:,.0f}")

# hold out 2,000 edges, score candidates for their endpoints
edges = [(u, v) for u in range(N) for v in adj[u] if u < v]
held = rng.sample(edges, 2_000)
for u, v in held: adj[u].discard(v); adj[v].discard(u)
deg = {v: len(adj[v]) for v in range(N)}
def scores(u):
    cn, aa = Counter(), Counter()
    for w in adj[u]:
        for c in adj[w]:
            if c != u and c not in adj[u]:
                cn[c] += 1; aa[c] += 1 / math.log(max(deg[w], 2))
    jac = {c: cn[c] / len(adj[u] | adj[c]) for c in cn}
    return cn, aa, jac
hits = {"common": 0, "adamic-adar": 0, "jaccard": 0}
for u, v in held[:500]:
    cn, aa, jac = scores(u)
    for name, s in (("common", cn), ("adamic-adar", aa), ("jaccard", jac)):
        top = sorted(s, key=s.get, reverse=True)[:10]
        hits[name] += v in top
for name, h in hits.items(): print(f"recall@10 {name:12} {h / 500:.3f}")

What to look for: a mean friend degree several times the mean degree; the top 1% of nodes contributing a large share of the sum of squared degrees, which is the hub problem; a cap of 20 per friend cutting two-hop paths sharply; and recall at 10 low in absolute terms (a random graph has little real structure, unlike human networks full of triangles) but with Adamic-Adar at or above common neighbours. The absolute numbers are not meaningful; the comparisons are.

Work it out

Your network has 300 million users with an average of 150 connections; the average degree of a friend is 450. (a) How many adjacency entries and how much memory at 8 bytes each? (b) What is the total two-hop work, Σ deg²? (c) With a per-friend cap of 50 and hubs over 5,000 skipped, what is the worst-case daily work for 120 million active users? (d) How long would that take at 5 × 10⁸ path visits a second per 100-machine cluster?

Answer

(a) Edges: 300 million × 150 / 2 = 22.5 billion; adjacency entries 45 billion; × 8 bytes = 360 GB raw, before metadata and replication.

(b) E[d²] = mean friend degree × mean degree = 450 × 150 = 67,500. Σ deg² = 300 million × 67,500 ≈ 2 × 10¹³.

(c) Each active user's work is at most deg(u) × 50; on average 150 × 50 = 7,500 (hubs skipped only reduce it). 120 million × 7,500 = 9 × 10¹¹ path visits.

(d) 9 × 10¹¹ / 5 × 10⁸ = 1,800 seconds, about 30 minutes, against about 11 hours for the uncapped total at the same rate. Comfortable daily; incremental updates handle the rest of the day.

Diagnose it

After a ranking change, accepted connections per user rose 4% in the A/B test, but two weeks after launch, complaints about "requests from strangers" doubled and the accept rate per request received fell from 38% to 31%. What happened, and how should it have been caught?

Answer

The change probably increased sends more than accepts: more suggestions of weakly connected people, each with some chance of acceptance, so accepted connections per sender rose while receivers got many more unwanted requests. A user-level test credits the sender's arm with the accepts and does not charge it for the burden on receivers, many of whom were in control; network interference made the gain look larger. Check requests received per user, ignore and report rates per request received, and accept rate by mutual-friend bucket, before and after. It should have been caught by two-sided guardrails (receiver burden and negative outcomes per request received) and by cluster-randomised testing. Fix: raise λ on negative outcomes, require a minimum p(accept), add exposure caps per receiver, and re-test with cluster randomisation.
