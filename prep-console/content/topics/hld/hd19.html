A social app sends notifications. At first there are three kinds: someone messaged you, someone commented on your post, someone you know joined. They work: users come back. So every team adds their own. The video team sends "videos you might like". The events team sends "an event near you". Growth sends "you have 3 new friend suggestions". Marketing sends "you haven't posted in a while". Each team runs an A/B test, each test shows more visits, and each notification ships.

A year later the average user receives fourteen notifications a day. Push opt-in among new users has fallen. The share of users who have disabled notifications entirely has doubled. Notification click-through rate is down by half, and each team's response is to send more, because their own A/B tests still show gains. A senior engineer looks at the numbers and asks the questions nobody asked: how many of those visits would have happened anyway? What is a user who disabled push worth compared with one who did not? And who decides, for one user on one evening, whether the eighth notification of the day should be sent at all?

The senior design question is to build that decision system: what to send, to whom, when and on which channel. It must rank candidate notifications across all teams by their incremental effect rather than their click probability, account for the long-term cost of fatigue and lost channels, enforce volume limits in a principled way, choose send times and aggregate into digests, keep urgent messages instant, and be evaluated on site-wide, long-term outcomes with holdouts. Delivery itself (provider APIs, retries, idempotency) is the notification service of in4; this case is the brain in front of it.

The lesson follows the interview: requirements and targets; estimates for candidates, scoring, delivery and the value of a channel; the API and data; the architecture; deep dives on uplift versus response models, fatigue and volume with a worked value curve and a shadow price, and send-time optimisation with aggregation; the evaluation plan with holdouts; failure modes; and ten times the scale. The real case is LinkedIn's published work on email volume, which measured exactly the trade-off between volume, engagement and complaints and changed its objective as a result.

What the system decides

Events happen all day: a friend posts, someone replies to you, a price drops, a live stream starts. Each event can become a candidate notification for some users. The ranking system decides, per user and candidate: send or not; on which channel (push to the phone, email, SMS, or only a badge and an entry in the in-app inbox); when (now, later today at a better time, or folded into tomorrow's digest); and in what form (alone, or aggregated with similar ones). It decides under limits: each user's tolerance for interruptions, quiet hours at night, legal rules for marketing messages, and the costs of channels such as SMS.

The mental model: a personal assistant who guards your attention

Imagine a good executive assistant. Calls from your family and your boss go straight through. Everything else waits: they tell you about the five minor things in one summary after lunch, not in five interruptions. They do not bother you with news you would see anyway when you check your inbox. They know when you like to be reached and when you do not. They notice when you start ignoring their messages and send fewer. And they understand that if they annoy you enough, you will tell them to stop calling altogether, and then even the important calls will not get through.

That assistant is the design. Urgent and personal messages bypass ranking. Everything else is ranked by whether telling you now makes a difference (uplift), not by whether you would react (response). Minor items are batched (aggregation) and timed (send-time optimisation). Volume adapts to your responses (fatigue). And the cost of annoying you includes losing the channel for good (channel value). The analogy breaks in one place: an assistant serves one person, while this system arbitrates between many teams who each want your attention, so it needs a common currency for deciding whose notification goes out.

The currency: incremental value per send

To let notifications from different teams compete fairly, everything is converted into one number: the net incremental value of sending this notification, to this user, on this channel, now. It has two parts. The gain is the extra probability of a visit (or another valued action) that the notification causes, times what such a visit is worth to the business. The cost is the probability the notification makes the user disable notifications, unsubscribe or uninstall, times what that channel is worth over the future, plus any money the channel costs (an SMS). A comment notification and a video recommendation are then comparable, and the user's daily limit is spent on the candidates with the highest net value. Every deep dive in this lesson is about estimating one of these quantities honestly.

A reader who stops here has the shape: candidates from every team, filtered by eligibility, scored by incremental value minus the risk of losing the channel, admitted under a per-user threshold, timed and aggregated, delivered, and measured on site-wide engagement with holdouts.

1 · Requirements

Functional. Accept candidate notifications from any team through one API with a type, recipients, urgency class and payload; filter by user preferences, quiet hours, opt-outs and legal rules (marketing consent); de-duplicate and aggregate; decide send or not, channel and time; hand accepted notifications to the delivery service (in4); track delivery, opens, visits, dismissals, disables and unsubscribes; and expose per-type metrics to owning teams. Out of scope, said aloud: delivery mechanics, the content of each notification, and in-app feed ranking.

Quality targets. Increase site-wide weekly active users and sessions from all sources, not notification clicks; keep push-disable, unsubscribe and spam-report rates below set ceilings; never delay or drop transactional and security notifications.

Latency targets. Urgent notifications delivered within 2 seconds at p99 from the event; ranked notifications decided within a minute of the event; scheduled sends within 5 minutes of their chosen time.

Volume and cost targets. Per-user limits by channel (for example at most a set number of ranked pushes a day and emails a week), quiet hours by local time, SMS only for critical types because each costs money.

Clarifying questions. "Which notification types exist, and which are transactional?" "What is the business objective: daily actives, sessions, revenue?" "Do we have any randomised data today?" "How valuable is a user with push enabled versus disabled?" "Which regions and marketing laws apply?"

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Candidates. 300 million monthly users, 100 million daily. Each user is a candidate recipient for about 20 notifications a day (friends' posts, comments, recommendations, reminders): 6 billion candidates a day, about 70,000 a second on average and 200,000 at the evening peak. Scoring each with a gradient-boosted model or small neural network at tens of microseconds, plus feature lookups, is a few hundred CPU cores: substantial but ordinary. Feature fetches (user's recent activity, recent notifications, per-type history) dominate cost, so features are batched per user: score all of a user's pending candidates together.

Sends. If the system sends about 10% of candidates: 600 million a day, mostly push and in-app, some email. Evening peaks reach about 50,000 pushes a second to the platform push services (Apple's and Google's). Email at an assumed $0.0001 per message is $10,000 a day at 100 million emails; SMS at an assumed $0.0075 is reserved for critical messages.

Scheduler. If half of sends are delayed for timing or aggregation by an average of 4 hours, about 300 million a day pass through the delay queue, and at any moment about 50 million are waiting (Little's law: 300 million / 24 hours × 4 hours). The scheduler (in5) holds them in time-bucketed partitions and re-checks each before sending.

Logs. 6 billion candidate decisions a day at about 200 bytes each (features hashed, scores, decision, policy version) is 1.2 TB a day, kept long enough to train and audit; outcomes join them over the following days.

The value of a channel. Suppose users with push enabled generate, on average, 2 more active days a month than comparable users with push disabled, and an active day is worth $0.20 to the business (from advertising or subscriptions). Push is then worth about $0.40 a month per user; over the expected remaining lifetime of the channel, discounted, call it $4. If each push carries a 0.05% chance that the user disables push, each push costs 0.0005 × $4 = $0.002 in expected channel loss. That number turns "annoyance" into something the ranker can subtract.

The insight to say out loud. The computation is cheap; the decisions are expensive, because a wrong send costs a sliver of a channel worth dollars and a right send earns a sliver of a visit worth cents. The system lives in the difference between small probabilities, which is why calibration, randomised data and long-term measurement dominate the design.

4 · High-level design

Teams publish candidates to a Kafka topic. Candidate expansion resolves recipients (followers of a poster, members of a group) with fan-out limits. Transactional and urgent types skip ranking and go straight to delivery, after preference and channel checks. Everything else passes eligibility (preferences, quiet hours, opt-outs, marketing consent, de-duplication against what the user has already seen in the app), then aggregation (attach to a pending digest with the same key if one exists), then scoring: per candidate and channel, predicted uplift on visits, p(disable or unsubscribe), and the resulting net value. The volume optimiser compares net value with the user's threshold λ, adjusted for what they have already received today. Accepted candidates go to the send-time scheduler, which places them in the delay queue at their chosen time; at that time they are re-checked (still relevant? user already visited? digest grown?) and sent to delivery. A small random share of eligible candidates (say 3%) is held back regardless of score and logged as such: the control data for uplift.

Walk one candidate. At 19:12 Ana comments on Ben's post. Candidate: notify Ben, type comment_on_post, ranked, aggregation key "post/p_55:comments". Ben has push on, it is 19:12 in his time zone (no quiet hours), he has had two pushes today. No pending digest for that post. Scores: uplift on a visit in the next 24 hours 0.06 (Ben is a weekly user who opens about every third day), p(disable) 0.0004, value per incremental visit $0.20, channel value $4: net value = 0.06 × 0.20 − 0.0004 × 4 = 0.012 − 0.0016 = $0.0104. Ben's λ after two pushes today is $0.006, so the push is accepted. Send-time model: Ben usually opens the app around 21:00, and the uplift at 21:00 is predicted slightly higher; urgency allows delay, so it is scheduled for 20:55 with the aggregation window open. At 20:30 two more comments arrive and join the digest. At 20:55 the system re-checks (Ben has not opened the app since) and sends one push: "Ana and 2 others commented on your post".

5 · Deep dive: uplift, not response

Four kinds of user. For any notification, users fall into four groups: persuadables visit only if notified; sure things visit anyway; lost causes do not visit either way; and sleeping dogs are made worse by the notification (they disable push or churn). Only persuadables justify a send. A response model, p(visit | sent), cannot tell persuadables from sure things: both have high response. An uplift model estimates p(visit | sent) − p(visit | not sent) for this user and this notification.

A worked comparison. Two users, one "new posts from people you follow" notification each, budget for one send:

Userp(visit | sent)p(visit | not sent)UpliftResponse model picksUplift model picks
Daily user0.920.900.02✓
Lapsing user0.150.050.10✓

The response model sends to the daily user and gains 0.02 expected visits; the uplift model sends to the lapsing user and gains 0.10, five times more. LinkedIn's published segment numbers show this effect in a real system: when they changed their email objective from email-attributed sessions to site-wide weekly active users, the share of generated emails actually sent to daily-active members fell from 0.76 to 0.22, while for dormant members it rose from 0.32 to 0.40. Daily-active members were active anyway; the emails had been taking credit.

Getting the data. You never observe the same user both notified and not notified for the same event, so uplift cannot be learned from ordinary logs, where sends were chosen by the previous policy. The system therefore withholds a small random share of eligible candidates (3% here) and logs them as controls. Models are trained on this randomised data: the two-model approach fits p(visit | sent) and p(visit | not sent) separately and subtracts; the single-model approach includes "was sent" as a feature and evaluates both settings; other methods transform the outcome so one model learns the difference directly (Gutierrez and Gérardy review them). With 6 billion candidates a day, 3% is 180 million randomised controls a day, plenty for training per notification type and segment.

Evaluating uplift models. Accuracy on observed outcomes is meaningless because the quantity of interest is never observed for one person. Instead, on held-out randomised data, sort users by predicted uplift and plot the Qini curve: as you target the top 10%, 20%, … the cumulative difference between treated and control outcomes, compared with the straight line of random targeting. A good model's curve rises steeply at first (it finds the persuadables) and may bend down at the end (the sleeping dogs).

6 · Deep dive: fatigue, channel value and volume

Diminishing returns. The first push of the day to a user is the most valuable; each additional one adds less (the user is already engaged, or starts to tune notifications out) and costs more (the chance they disable notifications rises with daily volume). Model this with features for notifications received in the last 24 hours and 7 days, and opens among them, in both the uplift and the disable models.

A worked value curve. For a typical weekly user, suppose the k-th ranked push of the day has these predicted effects, with $0.20 per incremental visit and $4 of push channel value:

The optimum is three pushes a day for this user: the fourth earns less in visits than it costs in expected channel loss. Notice the last column: clicks keep coming at 7 to 12% per push, so a team measuring notification clicks sees every additional push as a win. This is the core argument for the site-wide objective and the channel-value term.

Volume as a constraint with a shadow price. Across all users and candidates, the optimisation is: maximise total net value subject to each user's expected volume not exceeding a limit (and global constraints such as complaint rate). Turning each constraint into a price, the Lagrangian, gives a simple per-candidate rule: send if net value exceeds the user's threshold λ. λ is found so that expected sends meet the constraint, and it is personalised: high for a user who already receives many valuable candidates, low for a user with few. LinkedIn's email volume optimisation used this structure, with dual multipliers personalised per member so each member's "diet" contained the emails they valued most. A hard cap per user and channel stays as a backstop against model errors, and a minimum spacing between pushes (for example 30 minutes) prevents bursts.

Across teams. All notification types compete in one ranking under one λ. A team cannot buy volume by sending more candidates; it gets volume by producing candidates with higher incremental value. Transactional types are outside the budget. This is the organisational purpose of the system as much as the technical one.

7 · Deep dive: send time, channels and aggregation

Urgency classes. Transactional (password reset, payment, security alert) and urgent (a direct message from a close contact) are sent immediately. Ranked notifications may be delayed within their expiry. Digest-only items (weekly summaries, low-value recommendations) are never sent alone.

Timing. For a delayable candidate, the system predicts the uplift of sending at each hour in the next 24, using the user's activity pattern by hour and day, past responses by hour, and time since their last visit, then picks the best hour that is outside quiet hours and before expiry. The trap is the naive version, sending at the user's most active hour: a notification at 21:00 to someone who opens the app at 21:00 every night has little uplift, because they would have seen it anyway. Better hours are often just before the user's usual visit (to pull it forward) or at times when they are reachable but not habitually present. Time zones and travel matter: quiet hours use the device's current time zone.

Aggregation. Delaying creates the opportunity to combine. Candidates with the same aggregation key (comments on one post, likes on one photo) wait in a pending digest; when the scheduled time arrives, one notification summarises them. A busy creator who would get forty like-notifications an evening gets two. Aggregation also lowers volume against λ, letting more distinct valuable notifications through.

Channel choice. The same candidate has different value per channel: push interrupts and has high uplift and high disable risk; email has lower uplift, lower interruption, and unsubscribe risk per email type; in-app only (a badge and an inbox entry) costs nothing in channel risk but has uplift only when the user opens the app. The system scores each candidate per channel and picks the best net value, so some notifications become in-app only for users near their push limit.

8 · Evaluation plan

Offline. On the randomised holdback data: Qini curves and their area for uplift models per type and segment, calibration of uplift in bins, calibration of p(disable), and a policy simulation estimating net value and volume under a proposed λ (with inverse propensity weighting where the logging policy was not random).

Online. A/B tests with the user as the unit and site-wide primary metrics: weekly active users and sessions from all sources. Guardrails: push-disable rate, email unsubscribes and spam reports, app uninstalls, notification volume per user, and latency of urgent notifications. Results are reported by user segment (new, daily, weekly, dormant), because policies that help dormant users can annoy daily ones.

Holdouts. A long-term holdout (about 1% of users kept on the previous policy for six months) measures slow effects: channel attrition, habituation, retention. Per-type holdouts (a small share of users who never receive a given type) measure each type's total incremental value, which is how a team learns whether its notification is worth its share of attention. LinkedIn's random-drop bucket, which received about half of generated emails, is an example: it measured the true cost of volume reduction (2.6% fewer page views) against the benefit (send-all had 45% more negative responses).

9 · Policy, consent and the legal layer

Marketing messages are regulated in many jurisdictions: email marketing usually requires consent or an existing relationship plus a working unsubscribe link, and SMS marketing typically requires explicit opt-in. The system therefore tags every notification type with a legal category (transactional, service, marketing) and checks per-user consent records for the category and channel before ranking, as a hard filter rather than a model input. Unsubscribe links are per type, and an unsubscribe propagates within minutes, including to items already waiting in the delay queue, which is why the scheduler re-checks eligibility at send time. Quiet hours also have legal force in some places for marketing calls and messages. Treating these as configuration owned by a policy team, versioned and audited, keeps them out of model experiments where a ranking change could accidentally relax them.

11 · What changes at 10× scale

At 3 billion users and 60 billion candidates a day, scoring becomes a large streaming system partitioned by user, and feature fetches dominate cost, so per-user batching and caching of user state matter. The delay queue holds hundreds of millions of items, so time-bucketed storage with efficient re-check is critical. Push providers' rate limits and regional delivery capacity become planning inputs. And the optimisation moves toward sequential decision-making: treating the user's notification history as a state and each decision as an action with long-term reward, as in LinkedIn's work framing notifications as a Markov decision process trained with offline reinforcement learning.

Why sequential decisions beat one-shot scoring

The one-shot model scores each notification as if it were independent, but today's send changes tomorrow's response: a user who was notified three times today is less responsive tomorrow, and a user re-engaged today generates organic visits next week. Framing notifications as a Markov decision process (state: the user's recent notifications and activity; action: send or not; reward: visits minus penalties, over time) lets a policy optimise long-run value. Learning such a policy from logs is offline reinforcement learning, which must guard against overestimating actions the logging policy rarely took; Prabhakar et al. used conservative Q-learning for this in a notification system. In an interview, the point is the reason: notifications have delayed effects one-shot models cannot see.

Estimating channel value honestly

The $4 channel value in this lesson is the most important and least certain number in the system. Comparing users with push enabled and disabled is biased (people who disable are different), so better estimates come from natural experiments, such as users whose operating system changed default permissions, or from the long-term holdout's effect of volume on later activity. Because the value drives how aggressive the system is, teams often use a conservative estimate and revisit it yearly, and they segment it: a channel matters more for an occasional user than for one who opens the app daily anyway.

Permission is the first and largest decision

On iOS an app must ask permission before sending push notifications, and a user who declines is hard to win back, since the setting then lives in system settings. The system therefore treats the permission prompt as a decision too: ask after the user has seen a reason (a message they want to know about), not at first launch, and measure opt-in rate as a top-level metric. Every policy that burns trust shows up eventually in new users' opt-in rates, which is a leading indicator for channel value.

Negative-uplift detection

Some notifications hurt: a "you haven't posted in a while" reminder can make a lapsing user feel nagged and uninstall. Uplift models that include negative outcomes (disables, uninstalls, long-term activity) can predict negative uplift for some segments, and the policy must be able to withhold a notification type from them entirely. These users are where the response model fails worst, since they still click sometimes.

Interaction with in-app surfaces

A notification about a new comment is less valuable if the user will see it in the app's activity tab in an hour, and the in-app badge count itself is a notification. The ranking system should know what the user will see in the app (pending activity items, feed content) so it does not send what the app would show anyway, and badge counts should reflect ranked, de-duplicated items rather than every event, or the badge becomes noise users learn to ignore.

Fairness between notification types and creators

Central ranking can starve some types (an events team whose notifications have modest uplift for most users) and some creators (small accounts whose followers get their posts' notifications less often than large accounts'). Both may matter for the product's long-term health: creators who get no engagement leave. Minimum exposure rules for new creators, per-type exploration, and measuring the creator-side effect of notifications in holdouts keep the optimiser from narrowing the product to what is easiest to measure.

Exploration costs real value, so spend it carefully

The random holdback that makes uplift estimation possible withholds notifications the system believes are valuable. At 3% of 6 billion candidates, that is 180 million withheld a day; if the average withheld candidate had a net value of $0.005, the holdback costs about $900 a day, around $330,000 a year. That is a reasonable price for unbiased training data, but it argues for targeting exploration where it is most informative: per type and segment, hold back more where the uplift estimate is uncertain or the policy is about to change, and less where estimates are stable. The holdback must still be random within each stratum, and every withheld candidate must be logged with its stratum's holdback probability, so estimates can be reweighted correctly.

How it fails in practice

The quarter of rising CTR and falling users. A team switched to a response model and sent to users with the highest predicted click probability. Notification CTR rose 30%; weekly active users did not move; push disables among daily users rose. The model had moved notifications from lapsing users (low response, high uplift) to daily users (high response, no uplift). The fix was the random holdback and an uplift model, with site-wide actives as the decision metric.

The digest that never closed. An aggregation bug kept extending a digest's send time whenever a new like arrived, so popular creators received their "likes" notification only after activity died down, hours late. Digests now have a maximum delay from the first item, after which they send regardless.

The time-zone storm. Quiet hours were computed from the account's registered time zone. A user travelling across eight time zones received pushes at 3 a.m.; at scale, travel season produced a wave of complaints. Quiet hours now use the device's current time zone, reported with each session.

LinkedIn's email volume, measured. LinkedIn's engineering team published how it approached email volume in 2016. Their random-drop experiment sent about half of generated emails to one bucket and all of them to another: compared with sending everything, the half-volume bucket had 2.6% fewer total page views (with drops of 1.4% on the homepage to 4.5% on profiles and People You May Know), while the send-all bucket produced 45% more negative responses, meaning unsubscribes and spam reports, the latter capable of getting all of a sender's mail filtered by email providers. They first formulated volume optimisation as minimising emails subject to keeping email-driven sessions above a target and complaints below a tolerance, with a send rule comparing expected positive and weighted negative outcomes. Then they changed the objective to site-wide weekly active users, because their data showed many daily-active members were active regardless of email, and solved it with per-member dual multipliers. The send ratio for daily-active members fell from 0.76 to 0.22, weekly-active from 0.75 to 0.61, monthly-active stayed about the same (0.67 to 0.66), and dormant members rose from 0.32 to 0.40: the uplift logic of this lesson, visible in a production system. Pinterest published a similar budgeting approach for notifications (Zhao et al., KDD 2018), predicting a user's utility and allocating a weekly volume budget per user.

What to monitor

Monitoring must show both sides of every decision, the visits a notification earned and the channels it cost, per type and segment, so no team reads only its own clicks. Per type and segment: candidates, sends, send ratio, opens, incremental visits from holdback comparisons, disables, unsubscribes, spam reports, uninstalls; per user distribution of daily volume; λ distribution; urgent-notification latency; delay-queue depth and lateness; quiet-hour violations (should be zero); opt-in rate for new users; site-wide actives in the long-term holdout versus the rest.

Knobs people tune

Holdback share; channel value per segment; volume limits and caps per channel; minimum spacing; λ targets; aggregation windows and maximum delays; quiet hours defaults; urgency classification of types; exploration per type.

"""Uplift vs response targeting, the value curve, and a random holdback. Python 3.12, stdlib only."""
import random

rng = random.Random(11)
SEGMENTS = {  # share, p(visit | not sent), p(visit | sent)
    "daily":   (0.30, 0.90, 0.92),
    "weekly":  (0.45, 0.30, 0.38),
    "lapsing": (0.25, 0.05, 0.15)}
users = []
for _ in range(100_000):
    r, acc = rng.random(), 0.0
    for seg, (share, p0, p1) in SEGMENTS.items():
        acc += share
        if r < acc:
            jitter = rng.uniform(0.8, 1.2)
            users.append((seg, min(1, p0 * jitter), min(1, p1 * jitter))); break

BUDGET = 20_000
def evaluate(order):
    chosen = set(order[:BUDGET])
    visits_sent = sum(p1 for i, (_, p0, p1) in enumerate(users) if i in chosen)
    visits_base = sum(p0 for i, (_, p0, p1) in enumerate(users) if i in chosen)
    return visits_sent - visits_base, visits_sent / BUDGET
idx = list(range(len(users)))
for name, key in [("random", lambda i: rng.random()),
                  ("response", lambda i: -users[i][2]),           # p(visit | sent)
                  ("uplift", lambda i: -(users[i][2] - users[i][1]))]:
    inc, resp = evaluate(sorted(idx, key=key))
    print(f"{name:9} incremental visits {inc:7.0f}   'response rate' {resp:.2f}")

VISIT_VALUE, CHANNEL_VALUE = 0.20, 4.0
uplift_k = [0.100, 0.060, 0.035, 0.020, 0.012, 0.007]
best, total = 0, 0.0
print("\nk  uplift  p_disable  net")
for k, u in enumerate(uplift_k, start=1):
    p_dis = 0.0005 * k
    net = u * VISIT_VALUE - p_dis * CHANNEL_VALUE
    print(f"{k}  {u:.3f}   {p_dis:.4f}   {net:+.4f}")
    if net > 0: best, total = k, total + net
print(f"optimal pushes/day: {best}, net value/user/day ${total:.4f}")

for seg in SEGMENTS:
    sent = ctrl = vs = vc = 0
    for s, p0, p1 in users:
        if s != seg: continue
        if rng.random() < 0.03: ctrl += 1; vc += rng.random() < p0
        else: sent += 1; vs += rng.random() < p1
    print(f"  {seg:8} estimated {vs / sent - vc / ctrl:+.3f}  (controls: {ctrl})")

What to look for: the response model with the highest "response rate" (it picks daily users) and the lowest incremental visits; the uplift model picking lapsing and weekly users with far more incremental visits than either; an optimum of three pushes a day at a $4 channel value, more at $1 and fewer at $10; and holdback estimates near the true segment uplifts (2, 8 and 10 points) but with visible noise in the smaller segments, which is why holdbacks run continuously and aggregate over days.

Work it out

A notification type is sent to 50 million users a week. Its A/B test showed +1.2% notification-attributed sessions. The randomised holdback shows visit probability within 24 hours of 0.41 when sent and 0.395 when held back. Push-disable probability per send is 0.03%, push channel value $4, and an incremental visit is worth $0.20. (a) What is the incremental value per send? (b) The expected channel cost per send? (c) Should it keep its share of the budget?

Answer

(a) Uplift = 0.41 − 0.395 = 0.015 visits per send × $0.20 = $0.003.

(b) 0.0003 × $4 = $0.0012.

(c) Net $0.0018 per send, positive but small: about $90,000 a week across 50 million sends. Whether it keeps its budget depends on λ: if other types' marginal candidates earn more than $0.0018 net, this type should yield to them for users near their limits. The attributed-sessions result is irrelevant: most of those sessions would have happened (39.5% visit anyway). Recommend making it digest-only or in-app-only for users already near their push limit, and checking per segment, since its uplift is probably concentrated in less active users.

Diagnose it

Two months after launching send-time optimisation, weekly actives in the treatment group are flat, notification opens are up 15%, and the share of pushes sent between 20:00 and 22:00 has risen from 25% to 60%. What went wrong?

Answer

The send-time model is probably optimising opens (a response measure) and has learned to send when users are already in the app or about to open it, the evening peak. Opens rise because users were going to look anyway; incremental visits do not. Concentrating pushes in two hours also competes with the user's own habit and with other notifications. Check uplift by send hour on holdback data: compare visit rates of held-back candidates whose chosen hour was 21:00 with sent ones. Fix: train the timing model on uplift by hour from randomised timing (randomly assign a share of delayable candidates to hours), cap the share of a user's notifications in any one hour, and evaluate on site-wide actives, not opens.
