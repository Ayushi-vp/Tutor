A customer opens a food-delivery app at 19:05 and sees "arrives 19:35–19:45". A rider requests a car and sees "driver arriving in 4 minutes". Behind those two numbers, the marketplace has made dozens of decisions that all depended on predicted travel times: which of twenty nearby drivers to send, whether to batch two orders on one courier, what price to quote for the trip, when to tell the restaurant to start cooking. The ETA, estimated time of arrival, is the quiet number underneath the whole business.

When it is wrong, the damage spreads. If pickup ETAs are optimistic, riders wait longer than promised and cancel. If delivery promises are set at the average time, a large share of orders arrive late, because trips that go wrong go very wrong, and late food generates refunds and lost customers. If the ETA used for pricing is biased in one city at Friday rush hour, fares are wrong there all evening. If dispatch compares drivers with noisy ETAs, it sends the wrong driver often enough to waste thousands of driver-hours a week. And the predictions are needed fast and often: every app refresh, every candidate driver in every matching round, every price quote. At a large company this is one of the highest-volume models in production; Uber has described its ETA model as its highest-QPS model.

The senior design question is to build the ETA system: combine a routing engine's physics-like estimate with a machine-learned correction, feed it real-time traffic, decide what to predict for each use (mean, median or a quantile), serve it at hundreds of thousands of predictions a second within a few milliseconds, evaluate it where decisions are made (by city, hour and trip type), and understand how the predictions feed back into dispatch, pricing and the data itself. Routing and the ride-hailing marketplace are covered in cd11 and cd5; this case is the prediction system that sits between them.

The lesson follows the interview: requirements and targets; estimates for prediction volume, GPS traffic, labels and the latency budget; the API and data; the architecture; deep dives on the routing-plus-residual design and real-time traffic, segment versus trip models, the choice of loss and target with MAE and quantile loss worked by hand, and fast serving; the evaluation plan by city and time with switchback tests; feedback effects including the winner's curse; failure modes; and ten times the scale. The real case is Uber's DeepETA, described in an engineering post and a paper.

What an ETA is made of

A trip's duration is the sum of several legs, each predicted separately: the driver's or courier's travel to the pickup point, the wait at pickup (finding the rider, a restaurant preparing food), the travel from pickup to drop-off, and the last stretch on foot (parking, finding the building, the lift). The travel legs are where maps help: a routing engine represents roads as a graph of road segments (stretches between intersections), each with a traversal time from current or typical speeds, finds the best path and adds up the times (cd11). The other legs, and the routing engine's systematic errors, are where machine learning helps.

The mental model: an experienced dispatcher correcting the map

Picture an old taxi dispatcher with a map and a radio. The map says the airport is 25 minutes away. The dispatcher knows better: at 17:30 on a Friday the bridge backs up, pickups at the terminal take an extra five minutes to find the passenger, and drivers coming from the north always take the ring road, which the map does not prefer. The dispatcher starts from the map's number and corrects it from experience. When a customer asks "when will it get here?", the dispatcher gives a time they are fairly sure about, not the average, because a broken promise is worse than a cautious one.

That is the design: the routing engine is the map, with live traffic painted on it; the residual model is the dispatcher's experience, learned from millions of completed trips; the choice of quantile is the dispatcher's caution, set differently for different listeners. The analogy breaks on speed and scale. The dispatcher answers a few calls an hour; the system answers hundreds of thousands of questions a second, most of them asked by other software (dispatch comparing drivers, pricing quoting fares), within a few milliseconds each.

One service, several answers

Different consumers need different numbers from the same prediction, which is the most important idea to bring into the interview early. Dispatch needs an unbiased expected pickup time to compare drivers; pricing needs a mean trip time robust to rare disasters; a customer promise needs a time that will be met nine times out of ten. So the ETA service returns a small distribution (for example the mean and the 50th and 90th percentiles) and each consumer uses the one that matches its costs.

A reader who stops here has the shape: live traffic from GPS into a routing engine; a residual model that corrects the engine's ETA with learned experience; several targets for several consumers; and a feedback loop from decisions to data that the evaluation must respect.

1 · Requirements

Functional. Predict durations for each leg type (driver to pickup, pickup wait, pickup to drop-off, courier to restaurant, restaurant to door) for rides and deliveries in every city served; accept single requests and many-to-many batches (all candidate drivers to one rider); return the mean and selected quantiles, plus the route used; support a fallback answer if the model is unavailable. Out of scope, said aloud: the routing engine's internals (cd11), the dispatch optimiser (cd5) and restaurant preparation-time prediction, which is a separate model this service consumes.

Accuracy targets. MAE improvement over the engine-only ETA in every city with enough trips, with no city, hour-of-week band or trip type getting worse; absolute bias below 30 seconds per city and hour band; P90 coverage between 88% and 92% per city and trip type.

Latency and throughput. ETA service p99 under 50 ms including the routing query; model inference p99 under 5 ms; 300,000 predictions a second at peak, with linear horizontal scaling.

Availability. 99.99%: dispatch stops without ETAs. If the model fails, the service returns the engine ETA with a historical correction table, flagged as degraded.

Freshness. Live traffic reflected in routes within 2 minutes of a slowdown; models retrained at least weekly and validated per city before deployment.

Clarifying questions. "Which consumers, and what does each lose from early versus late errors?" "Rides, deliveries or both, in how many cities?" "Do we own the routing engine?" "What latency does dispatch need for a batch of 50 candidates?" "Which signals exist: GPS frequency, restaurant prep times, building data?"

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Trips and labels. 20 million trips a day across rides and deliveries. Each produces two or three legs with observed durations: about 50 million labels a day. Eight weeks of training data is about 2.8 billion rows; a feature row of a few hundred bytes makes that roughly 1 TB, ordinary for a distributed training job.

Prediction volume. Dispatch dominates. Each ride request considers about 20 candidate drivers over about 3 matching rounds (re-dispatch when a driver declines): 60 pickup ETAs per trip, 1.2 billion a day. Add trip ETAs for price quotes (many quotes do not convert), app refreshes of pickup and arrival times, and delivery planning: roughly 2 billion predictions a day, about 23,000 a second on average. Demand peaks around meals and commutes, and batched matching computes many-to-many matrices in bursts, so plan for about 300,000 a second at peak.

Model compute. If one prediction costs about 50 microseconds of CPU (mostly embedding lookups and a shallow network, batched), 300,000 a second is 15 CPU-seconds per second: about 15 cores of pure inference, perhaps 100 with feature assembly, serialisation and headroom. Cheap. The constraint is latency, not cost: the model must answer inside the dispatch request's budget without a GPU round trip.

Traffic stream. With 1 million drivers and couriers online at peak sending a GPS point every 4 seconds, about 250,000 pings a second flow into map matching. A road graph of about 100 million segments worldwide has live observations on a few million at any moment; speeds are averaged per segment in 1 to 5-minute windows and written to the traffic store, roughly a few million updates a minute.

The latency budget. Shown below: the routing query is the largest piece; the model gets a small, fixed slice.

The insight to say out loud. ETA prediction is cheap per call and called constantly; the engineering is in the latency budget and in serving many targets from one model, and the science is in choosing the right target for each consumer and evaluating where decisions are made.

4 · High-level design

GPS pings stream through map matching into the traffic store. The routing engine answers path queries using live speeds where fresh and historical profiles elsewhere, producing a route and an engine ETA. The ETA service receives a request, issues the routing query (one-to-many for dispatch batches) and, in parallel, fetches features; it assembles one feature vector per origin, runs the residual model in-process for the batch, adds the predicted residuals to the engine ETAs, derives the requested targets, applies sanity bounds (no negative or absurd values; clamping) and returns. A fallback path returns engine ETA plus a correction from a table of average residuals by city, hour and leg type if the model or feature fetch times out. Offline, completed trips become labelled rows joined point-in-time with the features and the engine ETA served at request time; a training pipeline retrains on a rolling window, validates per city, hour band and leg type against the current model, and deploys through a canary.

Walk one request. A rider at 17:42 on a Thursday in a dense city requests a car. Dispatch sends one batch: 20 candidate drivers to the rider's location. The routing engine computes 20 routes in about 20 ms; for driver 7 the engine ETA is 312 seconds. Features for driver 7: origin and destination grid cells at three resolutions, hour and day, engine ETA and route distance, share of the route with live traffic data (85%) and the live-to-historical speed ratio (0.8, slower than usual), pickup location type (a large office building, known for slow pickups), leg type. The model predicts a residual of +36 seconds for the mean and quantile offsets, so mean 348, P50 335, P90 430 seconds. Dispatch ranks drivers by the mean (with the winner's-curse correction discussed later), picks driver 7, and the app shows the rider "6 min", rounding a value between P50 and the mean upward, since being early is fine and being late is not.

5 · Deep dive: routing engine plus residual, and real-time traffic

Why a residual. The routing engine is a physical model: roads, distances, speeds, turn costs. It is excellent at what it models and blind to what it does not: time spent finding the rider at a busy entrance, the slow last 200 metres through a car park, local drivers' preferred routes that differ from the engine's best path, systematic speed biases on particular roads. Predicting the residual, actual duration minus engine ETA, focuses the model on exactly those gaps. Uber calls this ETA post-processing and notes a practical advantage: new data sources and business needs are easier to absorb in the post-processing model than by refactoring the routing engine. It also gives a safe fallback, since the engine's ETA alone is a reasonable answer.

Live traffic. GPS points are noisy (tens of metres in cities, worse between tall buildings), so map matching finds the most likely sequence of road segments a vehicle drove, typically with a hidden Markov model over candidate segments near each point. From matched traversals, the stream computes each segment's travel time and aggregates it per window. Freshness and coverage are the issues: a segment observed by 12 vehicles in the last 3 minutes has a reliable live speed; one observed once 20 minutes ago does not. The engine blends live speed and the historical profile for that time of week with weights from observation count and age. Features for the residual model summarise how much of the route relied on live data and how far live speeds deviate from history, so the model learns when to distrust the engine.

6 · Deep dive: segment-level versus trip-level models

A segment-level model predicts each segment's traversal time (from live speed, history, time, weather, events); the ETA is the sum along the route. Strengths: it generalises to any route, including ones never driven; it supports routing itself (the engine needs per-segment weights); and errors can be traced to roads. Weaknesses: per-segment errors accumulate over hundreds of segments (if they are correlated, as congestion is, they do not cancel); and it cannot see what happens between segments and at the ends: waiting at lights depends on the turn, pickups depend on the building, deliveries on parking. Google's work on ETAs in Google Maps sits in between, using a graph neural network over "supersegments" (sequences of adjacent segments) so the model sees neighbouring traffic, and reported reducing negative ETA outcomes by over 40% in Sydney.

A trip-level model predicts the whole leg's duration or residual from trip features: origin, destination, time, engine ETA, route summary, leg type. Strengths: one prediction per trip with no error accumulation; it captures end effects and systematic engine biases; and it can be trained to whatever target and loss a consumer needs. Weakness: it depends on the route the engine chose and on the trip being similar to training data. The production design is a hybrid: segment-level traffic inside the routing engine, trip-level residual on top. That split also fits the organisation: the maps team owns the engine and its speeds, the ETA team owns the residual model and its targets.

7 · Deep dive: choosing the target: MAE, MSE and quantile loss

The loss decides the number. Train a model with squared error (MSE) and it predicts the conditional mean; with absolute error (MAE) it predicts the median; with pinball loss at level τ it predicts the τ-quantile. Pinball loss charges τ for each minute of under-prediction and (1 − τ) for each minute of over-prediction; at τ = 0.9, being a minute too low costs nine times as much as being a minute too high, so the model shifts up until only about 10% of outcomes exceed its prediction.

A worked example. Ten deliveries between the same restaurant and neighbourhood at the same hour took 14, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 23, 26 and 36 minutes. The distribution is right-skewed, as trip times almost always are (there is a floor, the free-flow time, and no ceiling on things going wrong).

Check the cells. The mean is 210 / 10 = 21 minutes; its absolute errors are 7, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, 0, 2, 5 and 15, which sum to 44, so its MAE is 4.4 minutes. The median (any value between 19 and 20; take 19.5) has errors 5.5, 3.5, 2.5, 1.5, 0.5, 0.5, 1.5, 3.5, 6.5 and 16.5, summing to 42, so MAE 4.2: the median wins on MAE, as theory says, and the mean wins on squared error. The P90 value, 26 minutes, has errors 12, 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5, 3, 0 and 10, summing to 70, MAE 7.0: the worst on average, yet the only one of the three that keeps its promise nine times in ten.

Choosing the quantile for a promise. A higher promise is met more often but quoted times grow, and customers order less when told 45 minutes instead of 35. If each extra quoted minute loses, say, 1% of conversions, and a late order costs a refund or goodwill credit plus some churn, the best quantile minimises expected cost per order; it is usually somewhere between P80 and P95, set per market and order type. Showing a range ("19:35–19:45", for example P50 to P90) communicates uncertainty and lets the promise sit at the upper end without looking slow.

Serving several targets from one model. One network with several output heads (mean, P50, P90) trained jointly, or a mean head plus quantile offsets, shares the expensive representation and costs little extra latency. Quantile heads trained separately can cross (a P90 below the P50 for some inputs), so outputs are post-processed to be monotone, or the model predicts non-negative gaps between successive quantiles. DeepETA took a related route: a single parameterised asymmetric Huber loss whose two parameters control robustness to outliers and the relative cost of under- versus over-prediction, so the same architecture can produce a robust mean for fares or a late-averse estimate elsewhere.

8 · Deep dive: p99 latency at 300,000 predictions a second

Mostly lookups. The fastest model is one that looks things up instead of computing them. Discretise every input: continuous features into quantile buckets (DeepETA found quantile buckets beat equal-width ones), categorical features directly, and locations into grid cells at several resolutions. Map each to an embedding row. With feature hashing, many grid cells share a fixed number of rows; DeepETA hashed each cell with several independent hash functions so collisions in one table are undone by the others. A prediction is then a few dozen O(1) lookups plus a shallow network: DeepETA reports hundreds of millions of parameters with only about 0.25% touched per prediction.

Cheap interactions. Features interact (rush hour matters downtown, not in suburbs). Self-attention over feature embeddings captures pairwise interactions but costs O(K²·d) for K features of dimension d; linear attention costs O(K·d²). With K = 40 and d = 8: 12,800 versus 2,560 operations, which is why DeepETA used a linear transformer.

Serving mechanics. Run inference in-process in the ETA service (or a sidecar) on CPUs to avoid network hops; batch all candidates of a dispatch request in one call; keep embedding tables in memory (a few GB) on every instance; precompute features that do not depend on the request; set a hard timeout with the fallback table; and load-test at peak with realistic batch sizes, since p99 under load, not average latency on a quiet machine, is the requirement.

9 · Evaluation plan

Offline. On recent held-out trips (the most recent week, never shuffled in time), report per city, hour-of-week band, leg type and distance band: MAE, mean signed error (bias), and for quantile heads coverage (share of actuals at or below the P90) and interval width. A new model must beat the current one in aggregate with a paired comparison on the same trips and must not degrade any cell with enough data beyond a tolerance; the worst cells are reviewed by hand. Special-day slices (holidays, storms, big events) are tracked separately.

Online. ETAs drive dispatch and pricing for everyone in a city, so user-level A/B tests leak: the treatment's dispatch choices change which drivers are available to control riders. Marketplaces use switchback experiments: each city alternates between treatment and control in randomised time slices (say 30 to 60 minutes), and analysis accounts for carry-over between adjacent slices. Metrics: accuracy on completed trips; rider conversion after seeing the ETA; cancellations; actual pickup wait; on-time delivery rate against the promise; driver utilisation. Guardrails: latency, fallback rate, and fare accuracy.

10 · Feedback loops and the winner's curse

The winner's curse. Dispatch picks the driver with the lowest predicted pickup ETA among, say, 20. If each prediction has independent noise with a standard deviation of a minute, the smallest of 20 noisy predictions is typically the one whose noise was most negative: the expected minimum of 20 standard normal draws is about −1.87. So the chosen driver's predicted ETA is optimistic by almost two minutes on average, even if the model is unbiased over all drivers. Riders see "3 min" and wait five. Defences: evaluate and calibrate on chosen assignments (the ETA shown to the rider comes from a model or correction trained on them), add features describing the selection (number of candidates, gap to the runner-up), and use prediction uncertainty in dispatch so a confident 4-minute estimate can beat a noisy 3.5-minute one.

Decisions shape data. Trips only happen when dispatch, pricing and the customer say yes. Areas and times where ETAs are long see fewer trips (customers decline), so the data under-represents the hard cases. Couriers may drive differently against a displayed promise. Logging the served predictions and decisions with every label, exploring a little in dispatch where it is cheap, weighting training data for selection, and monitoring bias in sparse cells keep the loop from drifting.

12 · What changes at 10× scale

At ten times the cities and trips, a single global model with location embeddings and segment bias layers (as DeepETA adopted) scales better than thousands of per-city models, but evaluation and gating per city become a large automated system in themselves. The traffic pipeline grows to millions of pings a second and becomes regionally partitioned. Dispatch moves toward batched, many-to-many matching over larger windows, increasing peak ETA volume further and making batch inference efficiency the main cost lever. And new products (multi-stop deliveries, scheduled rides) need distributions over longer horizons, pushing the service toward predicting full duration distributions rather than a few points.

Labels are defined by the consumer

"Actual duration" is less obvious than it looks. Driver-to-pickup ends when the driver arrives or when the rider gets in? Delivery ends when the courier arrives at the building or at the door? A courier who waits at the restaurant because the food is not ready inflates "courier to restaurant" with preparation time. Each leg needs a precise, instrumented start and end event, and contaminated labels (a driver who stopped for fuel, a trip cancelled mid-way) need filters, or the model learns to predict noise. Label definitions are versioned with the model, because changing them changes what MAE means.

Calibrating quantiles per cell

A P90 head can be well calibrated globally and wrong locally: 95% coverage in one suburb, 80% in a downtown core at rush hour. Since promises are made per cell, coverage is monitored per city, hour band and order type, and a lightweight post-hoc correction (shifting the quantile per cell from recent coverage, conformal-style) fixes residual miscalibration between retrains. This is the same idea as recalibrating a classifier's probabilities, applied to a time.

Uncertainty is a feature for dispatch

A point ETA hides how sure the model is. Two drivers both predicted at 4 minutes are not equal if one is on a highway with steady traffic and the other must cross a congested bridge. Exposing a spread (P90 − P50) lets dispatch prefer reliable assignments when the rider has been waiting, and lets delivery batching avoid pairing an order with an uncertain leg. It also helps with the winner's curse: penalising uncertainty when choosing the minimum reduces how often noise wins.

Bias adjustment by segment

Different trip types have different error distributions: deliveries versus rides, pickups versus drop-offs, short versus long trips, and different regions. DeepETA added a segment bias adjustment layer in its decoder, which adjusts the raw prediction per segment of the data, and reported it worked better than simply adding segment features; they chose it over a full multi-task decoder for latency reasons. The general lesson is that cheap per-segment output corrections can capture systematic differences without growing the model.

Route choice is part of the error

The engine's ETA assumes the driver takes the engine's route; many do not. Experienced drivers avoid a junction the engine likes, or follow a different navigation app. A residual model learns the average effect but not per driver. Predicting the route actually taken (or the probability of alternatives) and feeding that into the ETA is a further step, valuable mainly where alternatives differ greatly in time, such as river crossings.

When the world changes overnight

A bridge closes for months, a city changes its speed limits, a stadium opens. Historical profiles and learned embeddings now describe a world that no longer exists for those segments and cells. The routing engine absorbs map changes quickly; the residual model may keep correcting for an old pattern. Monitoring bias per cell catches it, and the remedies are faster retraining with recent data weighted more heavily, and manual overrides for known events. Robust systems treat "the map changed here" as an input event that triggers re-evaluation.

One global model or many local ones

Per-city models are easy to reason about and can be tuned locally, but thousands of them multiply training, validation and deployment work, and small cities have too little data for a good model. A single global model shares what is common (rush-hour effects, pickup behaviour at airports) and lets small cities borrow strength from large ones, with location embeddings and per-segment bias layers capturing local differences. Its risk is coupling: a change that helps the largest markets, which dominate the loss, can hurt small ones. The usual answer is a global model with a per-city evaluation gate, loss weighting so small cities are not drowned out, and the ability to pin a city to the previous version while a regression is investigated. DeepETA's stated goal of one model for every line of business worldwide is this choice, made explicitly.

How it fails in practice

The optimistic pickup. After a dispatch change increased candidates per request from 10 to 30, riders' complaints about late pickups rose although model MAE on all predictions was unchanged. With more candidates, the winner's curse grew: the minimum of 30 noisy estimates is more optimistic than the minimum of 10. Calibrating the rider-facing ETA on chosen assignments, with the candidate count as a feature, removed most of the gap.

The concert night. On a stadium event night, pickups near the venue took 12 minutes longer than predicted for two hours; the global dashboard barely moved. Per-cell bias alerts now page operations when a cell's recent residual mean departs from prediction by more than a threshold, and event calendars feed features and temporary corrections.

The silent traffic outage. A map-matching job fell behind during a deployment; the traffic store served speeds 25 minutes old during evening rush. ETAs were short across a city; on-time delivery fell. The routing engine now treats live speeds older than a threshold as missing (falling back to history), and ingestion lag is an alert with a dashboard next to ETA bias.

The mean in the promise. A delivery team displayed the mean prediction as the promised time to make quotes look fast; late orders rose to about a third. Switching the promise to a calibrated P85 with a range lowered conversion slightly and refunds substantially, a net gain worked out with exactly the expected-cost reasoning in section 7.

What to monitor

Per city, hour band and leg type: MAE, bias, P90 coverage, interval width, share of predictions in fallback; dispatch-chosen bias versus all-candidate bias; traffic freshness and coverage; model latency percentiles under load; on-time rate against promises; rider and customer complaints about timing.

Knobs people tune

Quantile used for each promise type; promise rounding; live-versus-historical blending weights and freshness thresholds; per-cell bias corrections; retraining window and recency weighting; timeout and fallback thresholds; batch sizes; uncertainty penalty in dispatch.

The real case: Uber's DeepETA

Uber described DeepETA in an engineering post (February 2022) and a paper. Its routing engine computes ETAs as sums of segment traversal times along the best path using map data and real-time traffic; DeepETA predicts the residual between that ETA and observed outcomes, an approach Uber calls ETA post-processing, using spatial and temporal features, real-time traffic information and the type of request. Uber had used very large gradient-boosted tree ensembles for this and moved to deep learning to keep scaling, with three requirements: a few milliseconds of latency, significantly better MAE than the tree model, and one model for all lines of business worldwide. After testing seven architectures, they chose an encoder with self-attention over feature embeddings, made fast with a linear transformer; all inputs are discretised and embedded (continuous features in quantile buckets), locations are encoded at multiple grid resolutions with multiple feature hashing, and only about 0.25% of the model's hundreds of millions of parameters are touched per prediction. A decoder with segment bias adjustment handles differences between deliveries and rides, pickups and drop-offs, short and long trips and regions; an asymmetric Huber loss with parameters for outlier robustness and asymmetry lets the same model serve different point estimates, such as a mean for fares or a late-averse estimate elsewhere. Uber described it as its highest-QPS model, served through its Michelangelo platform behind the routing front end, with automatic retraining and validation. The design maps onto every section of this lesson, which is why it is the reference answer to "how would you build ETAs?".

Goal: see what MSE, MAE and pinball loss each predict on skewed trip times, measure how often each would break a promise, and see the winner's curse in dispatch. Python 3.12, standard library only.

"""MSE vs MAE vs pinball for ETAs, quantile coverage, and the winner's curse. Python 3.12."""
import random, statistics

trips = [14, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 23, 26, 36]
def mse(c, xs): return sum((x - c) ** 2 for x in xs) / len(xs)
def mae(c, xs): return sum(abs(x - c) for x in xs) / len(xs)
def pinball(c, xs, tau): return sum(tau * (x - c) if x >= c else (1 - tau) * (c - x) for x in xs) / len(xs)
grid = [g / 10 for g in range(100, 400)]
for name, f in [("MSE", lambda c: mse(c, trips)), ("MAE", lambda c: mae(c, trips)),
                ("pinball 0.9", lambda c: pinball(c, trips, 0.9))]:
    best = min(grid, key=f)
    late = sum(x > best for x in trips) / len(trips)
    print(f"{name:12} best constant {best:5.1f} min  late if promised {late:.0%}  MAE {mae(best, trips):.2f}")

rng = random.Random(4)
big = [12 + rng.lognormvariate(2.0, 0.5) for _ in range(200_000)]  # floor + skewed delay
big.sort()
print(f"\nmean {statistics.fmean(big):.1f}  median {big[len(big)//2]:.1f}")
for tau in (0.5, 0.8, 0.9, 0.95):
    q = big[int(tau * len(big))]
    print(f"tau {tau:.2f}: quantile {q:5.1f} min, exceeded by {sum(x > q for x in big) / len(big):.1%}")
m = statistics.fmean(big)
print(f"promising the mean ({m:.1f}) is late {sum(x > m for x in big) / len(big):.0%} of the time")

N_CAND, NOISE, ROUNDS = 20, 60.0, 20_000
gaps = []
for _ in range(ROUNDS):
    true = [240 + rng.gauss(0, 30) for _ in range(N_CAND)]           # similar true pickup ETAs (s)
    pred = [t + rng.gauss(0, NOISE) for t in true]                   # unbiased but noisy
    i = min(range(N_CAND), key=pred.__getitem__)
    gaps.append(true[i] - pred[i])                                   # positive = optimistic
print(f"\nwinner's curse with {N_CAND} candidates: chosen driver arrives "
      f"{statistics.fmean(gaps):.0f} s later than predicted on average")

What to look for: each loss choosing a different constant from the same data, with the pinball-0.9 constant breaking the fewest promises and having the worst MAE; on the large sample, coverage close to 1 − τ for each quantile and promising the mean late roughly 40% of the time for this skew; and a winner's-curse gap of well over a minute with 20 candidates that grows with 50 and shrinks with 5, even though every individual prediction is unbiased.

Work it out

A delivery market promises at P80 today: 20% of orders are late. Each late order costs $3 in credits and churn. Moving to P90 would add 4 minutes to the average quoted time, and each quoted minute loses 0.8% of orders, each worth $2.50 in contribution. The market takes 100,000 orders a day at the current promise. (a) What does lateness cost today? (b) What would the change save in lateness and cost in conversions? (c) Should you switch?

Answer

(a) 20% × 100,000 × $3 = $60,000 a day.

(b) Conversions: 4 × 0.8% = 3.2% fewer orders, 3,200 × $2.50 = $8,000 a day lost. Remaining 96,800 orders at 10% late × $3 = $29,040, so lateness cost falls by about $31,000 a day.

(c) Net gain about $23,000 a day: switch, provided the P90 is calibrated per cell (otherwise the expected 10% may be 15% in some areas) and the conversion elasticity was measured for this market rather than assumed. Then check P85 and P95 the same way: the best quantile is where the marginal conversion loss equals the marginal lateness saving, and it varies by market.

Diagnose it

A new ETA model improves global MAE by 4% offline and in the switchback test. Two weeks after launch, on-time delivery is down 3 points in one large city during weekday dinner hours, and the city's operations team says promises there feel too tight. What do you check and change?

Answer

A global improvement can hide a local regression in bias or coverage. Check per-cell metrics for that city and hour band: bias (mean signed error) and P90 coverage for the new versus old model, by leg type. A likely finding is that the new model reduced MAE by moving predictions closer to the median, while its quantile head under-covers in that cell (for example 82% instead of 90%), perhaps because restaurant preparation-time features changed, a new traffic pattern emerged, or the city has fewer training rows relative to its complexity. Also check traffic freshness at those hours and any change in dispatch inputs. Change: add per-cell coverage to the launch gate (no cell may lose more than a set amount), apply a per-cell quantile correction from recent coverage immediately, and retrain with that city's recent data weighted more heavily.
