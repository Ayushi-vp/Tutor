A retail company has a data warehouse with 8,000 tables. The analytics team of 60 people writes SQL all day, and still the queue of requests from the rest of the company is three weeks long: a regional manager wants net revenue by store for the last fiscal quarter, a product manager wants the share of new customers who ordered twice within 30 days, a finance partner wants to know why returns rose in September. Each request is fifteen minutes of work for someone who knows which of the four "orders" tables is the certified one, that revenue must exclude refunds and tax, that the fiscal year starts in February, and that joining orders to the customer table without deduplicating it double-counts anyone who changed address.

The obvious idea is to let a language model write the SQL. A first prototype takes a week: paste a few table definitions into a prompt, ask the question, run what comes back. The demo is impressive. Then it meets the real warehouse. Asked for "revenue last quarter", it picks orders_raw instead of the certified fct_orders, uses calendar quarters, includes refunded orders, and returns a number 18% higher than the finance dashboard, formatted beautifully, with no hint that anything is wrong. Asked an open question by a curious intern, it runs SELECT DISTINCT over the 1.6-petabyte clickstream table and the warehouse bill for that one query is $10,000. Asked by a sales rep about "top accounts by salary of account manager", it happily joins the HR table, because the prototype's service account can read everything.

So the real design question is not "can a model write SQL" but "how do you build a system that answers business questions from a large, messy warehouse correctly, safely and affordably": one that finds the few relevant tables among thousands, uses the company's agreed definitions of its metrics, checks every query before it runs, respects each user's permissions, refuses to burn money, asks when a question is ambiguous, explains what it computed, and can prove how often it is right. Public benchmarks show why this is hard: on BIRD, a benchmark of large, noisy databases, GPT-4 reached 54.89% execution accuracy against 92.96% for human experts, and on Spider 2.0, built from enterprise warehouses with hundreds of columns per database, the best agent in the paper solved 21.3% of tasks.

The lesson follows the interview: requirements with accuracy, latency, cost and safety targets; estimates by hand for questions, tokens, model cost and warehouse cost; the API; the data that the agent needs about the warehouse; the high-level design; deep dives on schema retrieval, the semantic layer and query generation, validation and cost guards, and permissions; ambiguity handling; the evaluation plan, including why execution accuracy and answer correctness are different numbers; failure modes; and what changes at ten times the scale. The real cases are the BIRD and Spider 2.0 papers and Google BigQuery's documented cost controls.

What the system does

A text-to-SQL analytics agent takes a question in plain language, works out which data answers it, writes a query in the warehouse's SQL, runs it as the person who asked, and returns the result as a number, a table or a chart, with a sentence explaining what was computed and the SQL for anyone who wants to check. It is an agent rather than a single model call because it works in a loop: it can look up table descriptions, try a query, read the error or the result, fix the query, and ask the user a question when it cannot decide between two readings.

Three kinds of knowledge are involved, and keeping them apart is most of the design. The schema is what exists: tables, columns, types, how tables join (foreign keys, columns whose values point at another table's key). The semantics are what things mean: which table is certified, how net revenue is defined, when the fiscal year starts. The data is the actual rows, which the agent sees only through queries the user is allowed to run. A model knows SQL in general; it knows none of these three about your company, so they must be supplied at the right moment and in the right amount.

The mental model: a new analyst on their first week

Imagine hiring a capable analyst who knows SQL perfectly but has never seen your company. On day one you would not hand them a printout of 8,000 table definitions. You would give them the data catalogue to search, point them at the metric definitions the finance team signed off, tell them which tables are certified, and remind them to check the cost estimate before running anything huge. When a manager asks them something vague, a good new analyst asks one clarifying question. When their number disagrees with the dashboard, they check their joins. And they can only open the tables their own badge allows.

The agent is that analyst. Schema retrieval is searching the catalogue for the handful of relevant tables. The semantic layer is the signed-off metric definitions. Validation and dry runs are checking the query and its cost before pressing run. Clarifying questions are asking the manager what they meant. Running as the user is the badge. The analogy breaks in two places worth noticing. A human analyst learns from every mistake and remembers it; the agent repeats a mistake every time until someone changes its context, its tools or its examples. And a human who is unsure says so; a model produces fluent, confident SQL whether or not it is right, so uncertainty must be measured from outside (validation, result checks, evaluation) rather than expected from the model's tone.

Why it is harder than it looks

Benchmarks tell the story in three steps. On Spider (2018), 200 small databases averaging about 28 columns each, models now score above 90%. On BIRD (2023), 95 databases totalling 33.4 GB with dirty values and questions that need outside knowledge (that "POPLATEK TYDNE" means weekly issuance), GPT-4 scored 54.89% against 92.96% for humans. On Spider 2.0 (2024), taken from real enterprise warehouses averaging 812 columns, with dialects such as BigQuery and Snowflake and queries often over 100 lines, the best agent solved 21.3%. The jump in difficulty comes from exactly what your warehouse has: too many tables to show the model, names that do not say what they mean, metric definitions that live in people's heads, and values that need knowing. A reader who stops here has the shape: retrieve the right slice of schema and meaning, generate, check before running, run with the user's permissions, check the result, explain it, and measure accuracy honestly.

1 · Requirements

Functional. Users ask questions in chat (and from inside the business-intelligence tool) and get back an answer: a number, a table or a chart, a one-paragraph explanation of what was computed (which metric definition, which period, which filters), and the SQL. Follow-up questions keep context ("now split it by channel"). The agent asks a clarifying question when a question is ambiguous. Users can mark an answer wrong or right, and analysts can promote a verified question-and-query pair into the shared examples. Out of scope, said aloud: writing to the warehouse, building pipelines, and replacing certified dashboards; the agent answers ad-hoc questions.

Accuracy targets. On a golden set of analyst-verified questions: answer correctness at least 85% for questions covered by the semantic layer and at least 70% for others, against a baseline of whatever the prototype does. Schema-linking recall (the share of questions for which every table the reference query needs was retrieved) at least 95%. And a quality target that is easy to forget: the agent must say when it is unsure. An answer labelled "uncertified, computed from raw tables" that is wrong is much less harmful than one presented as fact.

Latency targets. First visible progress (the agent's restatement of the question or a clarifying question) within 3 seconds; a typical answer within 15 seconds at the median and 45 seconds at p95 (the 95th percentile, one question in twenty slower), most of which is warehouse execution time on large tables.

Cost targets. Model cost under $0.25 per question at the median; warehouse cost per question visible and capped: a default hard cap of 1 TiB scanned per query ($6.25 at BigQuery's on-demand rate), confirmation required above $5, and a per-user daily budget.

Safety targets. Zero queries executed with more privilege than the asking user has; zero write statements executed; every query auditable to a user. No table names or descriptions shown that the user could not see in the catalogue.

Clarifying questions to the interviewer. "Which warehouse and dialect?" (it changes dry runs and cost controls). "Is there a semantic layer or certified metric catalogue already?" "How do users authenticate to the warehouse today, and can we act on their behalf?" "Who are the users: analysts who read SQL or business users who never will?" "Is data residency a concern for sending schema and results to an external model provider?"

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Users and questions. 5,000 employees have access; at launch 1,500 use it weekly, asking about 15,000 questions a day across a working day. That is about 0.5 questions a second on average and perhaps 2 a second at the morning peak. Throughput is not the challenge; correctness, cost and permissions are.

The schema. 8,000 tables with an average of 30 columns is 240,000 columns. A compact description of one column (name, type, a short description) is about 12 tokens, and each table needs a description too, so the whole schema is roughly 240,000 × 12 + 8,000 × 60 ≈ 3.4 million tokens. At an assumed $3 per million input tokens, sending it once would cost about $10 per call, and it would not fit in most context windows anyway. So retrieval: the agent sees maybe 20 tables, each pruned to its most relevant columns, at about 400 tokens per table, so 8,000 tokens of schema per call.

Model cost. A typical question takes 3.2 model calls (plan and retrieve, generate, occasionally repair, then explain). Each call carries about 14,000 input tokens (instructions 2,000, schema 8,000, semantic-layer definitions 2,000, conversation and examples 2,000) and produces 500 output tokens. At assumed prices of $3 and $15 per million input and output tokens for a capable model, one call costs 14,000 × $3/10⁶ + 500 × $15/10⁶ = $0.042 + $0.0075 = $0.0495, and a question about $0.16. Fifteen thousand questions a day is about $2,400 a day, roughly $71,000 a month. Prompt caching of the stable instructions (ae9) trims perhaps 15% from that.

Warehouse cost. This is the number people forget. Suppose the agent executes 1.5 queries per question (retries, an exploratory count) and the median query scans 40 GB. At $6.25 per TiB, 40 GB costs 40/1,024 × $6.25 ≈ $0.24, so each question costs about $0.37 in warehouse charges, about $5,500 a day, more than twice the model bill. And the distribution has a long tail: one unfiltered scan of the 1,600 TiB clickstream table costs 1,600 × $6.25 = $10,000. The cost guard is not a nice-to-have; it is the difference between a useful tool and a budget incident.

Metadata index. One document per table (8,000) and per column (240,000), each with an embedding of 1,024 numbers in 32-bit floats: 248,000 × 4 KB ≈ 1 GB, a tiny vector index. Rebuilt nightly from the catalogue and query logs, with incremental updates when tables change.

Query logs. The warehouse already records every human-written query: say 300,000 a day. Mining 90 days of them gives the most valuable metadata the agent has: which tables are actually used, which are joined together and on which columns, which filters are common, and which tables are dead. This costs one batch job a night.

The insight to say out loud. The model bill is modest and predictable; the warehouse bill is larger and has a fat tail; and accuracy is capped by retrieval before the model writes a single token. Design effort goes where those three facts point.

Three choices matter. The request carries the user's identity, which the agent exchanges for warehouse credentials on that user's behalf (au12), so nothing in the API lets a caller choose a more powerful identity. Clarification is an event in the same stream, not an error, so the client can render options as buttons. And the answer carries certified and assumptions: whether it came from governed semantic-layer definitions, and what the agent assumed, so users can judge it.

4 · What the agent needs to know about the warehouse

The metadata index is built nightly. For each table: name, dataset, description, owner, certification status, row count, size in bytes, partition and clustering columns, freshness, and usage statistics from the query logs (queries per week, distinct users). For each column: name, type, description, and a profile: null share, distinct count, and for low-cardinality text columns the top values ("EMEA", "APAC", "AMER"). For pairs of tables: observed join columns and frequency, mined from logs, plus declared foreign keys. For each table and column a searchable text document and an embedding. Access control lists are copied from the catalogue so retrieval can filter by what each user can see.

The semantic layer is a governed catalogue of metrics and dimensions, often maintained by the analytics team in a tool such as dbt's semantic layer or Looker's modelling language. Each metric has a name, a description, the SQL expression and the table it is computed on, its allowed dimensions and default filters: net_revenue = SUM(order_amount - refund_amount - tax_amount) on fct_orders, excluding test accounts, by fiscal calendar. It can compile a request like "net_revenue by region where segment = enterprise for fiscal 2026-Q2" into correct SQL, including the joins.

The examples store holds verified question-and-query pairs, written or approved by analysts, embedded for retrieval. A few similar verified examples in the prompt help more than any instruction, because they carry the company's idioms: which table, which filters, which joins.

Conversation and preference memory holds the current conversation and a few durable preferences per user and team (this team means fiscal quarters; this user means active customers by "customers").

Traces record, per question: retrieved tables with scores, semantic-layer calls, every generated query with validation results, dry-run estimates, execution statistics (bytes, time, rows), the final answer and feedback. They feed evaluation and audit.

5 · High-level design

The request path is the loop in the primer diagram. A chat front end sends the question with the user's token. The orchestrator (a stateless service running the agent loop, hd7) first resolves conversation context and checks for ambiguity. It calls schema retrieval, which returns candidate tables, columns, join paths, relevant semantic-layer metrics and similar verified examples, all filtered by the user's catalogue permissions. The model decides between two routes: compose a semantic-layer query (preferred whenever the question maps onto governed metrics) or write raw SQL. Either way the resulting SQL goes to the validator, which parses it, applies policy, adds safeguards and gets a dry-run cost estimate. If the estimate is within budget, the query executes in the warehouse with the user's delegated credentials and a hard bytes cap. The result checker looks for signs of error (empty results, suspicious duplicates, totals that disagree with a known control figure). The explainer writes the answer, picks a chart type and lists assumptions. Off the path: the nightly metadata build, the evaluation runner, cost dashboards and the audit store.

6 · Deep dive: schema retrieval and schema linking

Schema linking means working out which tables and columns a question refers to. "Net revenue by region last quarter for enterprise customers" refers, in this warehouse, to fct_orders (amounts and refunds), dim_customer (segment), dim_store (region) and the fiscal calendar table dim_date. Nothing in the question says "fct" or "dim"; the word "region" appears as a column in eleven tables; and there are four tables with "orders" in their name, of which one is certified. Spider 2.0's error analysis found wrong schema linking to be the largest single category of failures (27.6% of errors in its analysis), with column linking especially hard when databases average more than 755 columns against about 54 in BIRD. This is the step that decides accuracy.

Step 1: build good documents. A table's searchable document is not just its name. It concatenates the name split into words (fct_orders becomes "fact orders"), the description, column names and descriptions, top values of categorical columns ("enterprise", "SMB", "consumer" in dim_customer.segment is how "enterprise customers" finds that table), and a summary of how the table is used: the five most common questions or query patterns from the logs. Usage is the strongest signal of all: a table queried by 300 people a week is far more likely to be right than an abandoned copy with a similar name.

Step 2: hybrid retrieval. Run lexical search (BM25 over the documents, which catches exact identifiers such as "SKU" or a product code) and vector search (which catches paraphrases: "income" for "revenue") and fuse the two rankings with reciprocal rank fusion (rec5). Then boost: certified tables up, deprecated tables down, tables used in similar verified examples up. Filter by the user's catalogue permissions before returning anything, so a table the user cannot see never appears in the prompt or in an error message.

Step 3: expand along joins. Retrieval finds the tables that match the question's words; the query also needs the tables that connect them. If fct_orders and dim_store are retrieved, the join path between them (perhaps via dim_store_hierarchy) must come along. The join graph mined from logs and declared keys gives the shortest frequently used paths, and the expander adds the intermediate tables.

Step 4: prune columns. A fact table may have 300 columns; the prompt gets the keys, the columns that matched, the partition column and the most used measures, perhaps 25, so the 20 tables fit in about 8,000 tokens.

Measuring it. For each golden question, the reference query names its tables. Table recall is the share of those tables that retrieval returned; full-set recall (the stricter number) is the share of questions for which all needed tables were returned, because one missing table makes the question unanswerable. A worked example: for "net revenue by region last quarter for enterprise customers", the reference needs four tables; retrieval at k = 10 returned fct_orders, dim_customer and dim_date but not dim_store, because the question's "region" matched eleven tables and the store dimension ranked twelfth. Table recall is 3/4 = 0.75; full-set recall for this question is 0. Across the golden set, full-set recall at k = 5, 10, 20 and 40 might come out at 62%, 81%, 93% and 97%. Two lessons follow. First, the generator cannot exceed these numbers, so retrieval improvements are the cheapest accuracy gains. Second, the misses are informative: here the fix was a better document for dim_store (its usage summary now says "region, territory, store-level reporting") and a join-path rule that pulls dim_store whenever fct_orders is grouped by a geographic word.

Agentic retrieval as a fallback. When the first retrieval looks weak (low scores, or the model says the needed column is missing), the agent can search the catalogue itself with a tool: "find tables with a column about refunds". This costs a model call and a second or two, so it is a fallback, not the default. Spider 2.0's agents did exactly this kind of exploration, and it is how a human analyst works when the first guess fails.

7 · Deep dive: the semantic layer and query generation

Why a semantic layer changes the problem. Without one, every question about revenue asks the model to re-derive revenue: which table, which columns, which exclusions, which calendar. Phrase the question twice and you may get two definitions. With one, "net revenue" is a named, governed object, and the model's job shrinks from "write correct SQL from scratch" to "pick the metric, the dimensions and the filters", a much easier problem with far fewer ways to be wrong. The semantic layer then compiles the request into SQL that the analytics team already trusts, including joins that avoid fan-out.

The routing decision. The model's first output is a structured plan: the metric or metrics, the dimensions, the filters, the time range, and whether the plan is expressible in the semantic layer. If it is, the agent calls the layer's query interface with the plan and receives SQL; the answer is marked certified. If not (a question about a table the layer does not cover, or an unusual calculation), the agent writes raw SQL, retrieves similar verified examples to imitate, and the answer is marked uncertified, with a nudge to an analyst to add the metric if the question is common. Over time the analytics team grows the layer where the logs show demand, and the certified share rises.

Prompting the generator. The raw-SQL prompt contains: the dialect and its idioms (date functions, how nested columns are unnested), the retrieved tables in a compact form (fct_orders(order_id PK, customer_id FK→dim_customer, store_id FK→dim_store, order_date DATE partition, order_amount NUMERIC, refund_amount NUMERIC, ...)), relevant metric definitions even if the query is raw, a few verified examples, the conversation, and rules: select only needed columns, always filter the partition column, never use SELECT *, qualify every column with its table. Asking the model to first write a short plan in words and then the SQL improves accuracy on multi-step questions, at the cost of some output tokens.

Values are part of the schema. BIRD's authors found that 70.1% of their questions needed knowledge of actual database values (that "center" is stored as pos = 'C'). The profile's top values solve the common case: the prompt shows that segment takes 'Enterprise', 'SMB' and 'Consumer', so the model writes = 'Enterprise', not = 'enterprise customers'. For high-cardinality columns (product names, cities), a value-lookup tool searches a precomputed index of distinct values rather than letting the model run SELECT DISTINCT on a huge table, which is both slow and expensive.

Self-correction, bounded. If validation or execution fails, the error goes back to the model with the query: "column region not found in fct_orders; did you mean dim_store.region?" Most syntax and naming errors are fixed in one repair. The loop is capped at two repairs, because each costs a model call and often a dry run, and a third attempt rarely succeeds where two failed; after that the agent says what it could not do.

8 · Deep dive: validation, dry runs and cost guards

Parse and policy. The query is parsed with a SQL parser for the warehouse's dialect (an open-source parser such as sqlglot handles many dialects). Parsing gives a tree that the validator can inspect reliably, unlike regular expressions over text. The checks: exactly one statement; it is a SELECT (or WITH ... SELECT), with no INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE, MERGE, CREATE, DROP, GRANT, scripting or external-table functions; every referenced table and column exists and is on the allow-list for agent use (some tables, such as raw security logs, are excluded entirely); and the user has access according to the catalogue. Write protection is enforced twice: by the validator and by giving the delegated credentials no write permission, so a parser bug cannot become a dropped table.

Safeguards added or required. On partitioned tables above a size threshold, a filter on the partition column is required; if missing, the query goes back to the model ("add a filter on order_date for the requested period"). Results returned to the user are capped (a LIMIT 10,000 wraps the final select) to protect the browser, but remember that LIMIT does not reduce scanned bytes: BigQuery's documentation states that for non-clustered tables a LIMIT clause won't reduce compute costs. A fan-out check looks at each join: if the joined table's key is not unique on the join column (known from the profile or declared keys), and the query sums a measure from the other side, the validator flags it, because that is exactly how revenue gets double-counted.

The dry run. BigQuery and most warehouses can validate and estimate a query without running it: submit the job with dryRun set to true and receive the bytes it would process. The estimate is free and takes well under a second. The agent converts it to dollars at the on-demand price, $6.25 per TiB in BigQuery's US pricing at the time of writing (the first TiB each month is free), and applies the tiers in the diagram: run silently under $0.50, run and show the cost up to $5, ask for confirmation above $5, refuse above the hard cap and suggest a narrower filter or a sampled table.

The hard cap travels with the query. When the query is submitted for real, it carries BigQuery's maximum bytes billed setting equal to the cap. The documentation describes the behaviour: the number of bytes the query will read is estimated before execution, and if it exceeds the limit the query fails without incurring a charge. This is the guard that holds even if the validator has a bug, the model ignored instructions, or the dry-run tier logic is wrong. One subtlety: for clustered tables the estimate is an upper bound, so a query can fail the cap even though it would actually have read less; the agent then reports that the query was refused and suggests a narrower range rather than silently raising the cap.

The blind spot: row-level security. BigQuery's documentation notes that dry runs on tables protected by row-level security always return 0 bytes, to prevent side-channel attacks (a precise byte count could leak how many rows exist that you cannot see). A naive cost guard reads "0 bytes", concludes "free", and runs the query. The design must treat 0 on an RLS table as "unknown" and estimate from metadata instead: the stored size of the referenced columns in the partitions the filter selects, an upper bound that keeps the tier logic safe, while the hard cap still protects the actual run.

Budgets above the query. A per-user daily budget (say $25) and a per-team monthly budget catch the slow leak that no single query triggers: an agent loop that retries, or a user who runs fifty $0.40 queries. Budget state is kept in the orchestrator, and the cost of each executed query is reconciled from the warehouse's job statistics (bytes actually billed), not from the estimate.

9 · Deep dive: permissions and row-level security

Run as the user. The agent receives the user's single-sign-on token and exchanges it for warehouse credentials scoped to that user, the token-exchange pattern from au12. Every query therefore runs with the user's grants. The warehouse then applies its own policies: row-level security, where a row access policy limits which rows a user sees (BigQuery's example uses SESSION_USER() so that people see only their own rows, or a group grant so a regional manager sees only their region), and column masking, where a sensitive column returns a masked value unless the user is entitled. Because enforcement happens inside the warehouse, the agent does not need to reimplement it, and it cannot get it wrong.

Why the agent should not have its own broad access. A shared service account with read access to everything is the easiest prototype and the most common real leak. It turns the agent into a confused deputy (au8): it acts on behalf of a user with powers the user does not have. Prompt injection makes this worse, because the agent reads table descriptions and cell values, and any of them can contain instructions. With delegated credentials, the worst outcome of a confused or manipulated agent is a query the user could have run themselves.

Metadata is data too. The existence of a table called layoffs_2026_q4_plan is itself sensitive. Schema retrieval filters by the user's catalogue permissions before ranking, the examples store only returns examples whose tables the user can access, and error messages never echo tables the user cannot see.

Caches respect entitlements. Caching results of identical queries saves warehouse money, but two users running the same SQL can legitimately get different results under row-level security. A result cache key therefore includes the user's entitlement fingerprint (their groups and policy versions), or caching is restricted to tables without row policies.

10 · Ambiguity and clarifying questions

"How many customers did we have last quarter?" has at least four readings: all customers or active ones, and fiscal or calendar quarter. A model will silently pick one. The agent instead runs an ambiguity check as part of planning: it lists the plausible interpretations of each vague term, using the semantic layer's definitions (which define "active customer" and the fiscal calendar) and the user's and team's saved preferences. Then it decides with a simple expected-cost rule. If the interpretations are cheap to compute (all four counts come from one small query), compute them; if the answers differ by less than, say, 2%, answer with the default reading and state it ("using fiscal Q2, May to July; calendar Q3 differs by 1%"); if they differ materially, ask one short question with buttons. If computing them is expensive, ask before running anything.

Two guardrails keep this from becoming annoying. A clarification rate above roughly 15% of questions is a sign the defaults or preferences are wrong; and each clarification's outcome is stored as a preference for that user or team, so the question is not asked again. Clarifications are evaluated like answers: the golden set includes ambiguous questions with the expected behaviour (ask, or answer with a stated assumption).

11 · Answers, explanations and charts

The explainer receives the question, the plan, the SQL and a summary of the result (the full result if small, the first rows and aggregate statistics if large). It writes a direct answer first ("Enterprise net revenue in fiscal Q2 was $48.2M, up 6% on Q1"), then the definition used and assumptions, then the SQL folded away. Numbers in the text are taken from the result by reference, not retyped by the model: the explainer is given named values and must cite them, and a checker verifies that every number in the answer appears in the result or is a simple computation from it (a percentage change), because models sometimes round or transpose digits. Chart selection is rule-based from the result's shape: one time column and one measure is a line chart, one category column with fewer than about 20 values and one measure is a bar chart, anything else is a table.

Result checks. Cheap signals catch many wrong answers before the user sees them: an empty result for a question that should have data; a total that disagrees with a known control figure (the semantic layer can provide last month's certified revenue); a sudden order-of-magnitude difference from the same question asked last week; more rows than distinct keys, the signature of fan-out. A failed check triggers one repair attempt or a visible warning.

12 · Evaluation plan

The golden set. A few hundred questions at first, growing to a few thousand: real questions from the analytics team's request queue and from logs, each with an analyst-verified reference query and answer, tagged by domain (finance, marketing, supply chain), difficulty, whether a semantic-layer metric applies, and whether the question is ambiguous (with the expected behaviour). Store reference queries, and recompute their results on every run against a frozen snapshot or current data, because answers go stale as data changes.

Three accuracy numbers. Schema-linking recall, from the retrieval step alone. Execution accuracy (EX), the metric Spider and BIRD use: does the generated query's result set match the reference query's? A fair comparison ignores row order (unless the question asks for an order), ignores column order and extra columns, and compares numbers with a small tolerance; Spider 2.0's evaluation scripts similarly focus on the essential columns because questions often do not specify which columns to return. Answer correctness: is the final stated answer right, judged against the reference answer by a validated judge or an analyst? The three disagree in instructive ways. A query can be EX-correct while the explanation misstates the period; it can be EX-wrong (an extra breakdown column the comparison did not tolerate) while the answer is fine; and when recall is the problem, EX and correctness fail together. Report all three per slice, with confidence intervals, since a 300-question set gives about ±5 points at 95% confidence.

Efficiency and cost. Bytes scanned per question against the reference query's bytes (BIRD's valid efficiency score rewards correct queries that also run fast relative to the reference, and its authors found that rewriting correct queries cut execution time by 77.75% on average in a small sample), model tokens per question, latency, repair rate.

Release gate. Every change to prompts, models, retrieval or the semantic-layer integration runs the golden set; a drop in answer correctness beyond the confidence interval on any major slice blocks release (hd12).

Online. Thumbs-down rate, follow-up corrections ("no, I meant net"), how often users open the SQL, how often analysts flag an answer, clarification rate and how often clarifications changed the answer, cost per answer, and a weekly sample of a hundred conversations reviewed by analysts, which also feeds new golden questions.

13 · Failure modes and trade-offs

14 · What changes at 10× scale

At 150,000 questions a day, model cost reaches about $700,000 a month and warehouse cost more. Three things change. Routing by difficulty: semantic-layer questions (pick a metric, dimensions, filters) go to a small, cheap model, and only raw-SQL questions use the large one, as in the gateway design (hd13). Caching and materialisation: the most common questions are answered from pre-aggregated tables the analytics team builds from the logs, so a "revenue by region last month" never touches the raw fact table again; result caches keyed by entitlement absorb repeated questions. Capacity pricing: at this volume, buying dedicated warehouse capacity (slots) instead of on-demand bytes makes cost predictable, and the cost guard switches from dollars to slot-time and queue position. Metadata grows too: with 50,000 tables across several warehouses, retrieval becomes two-stage (choose the domain or warehouse first, then tables), and the catalogue sync moves from nightly to event-driven.

Why recall caps accuracy, and why that changes where you invest

The accuracy of the whole system is roughly the product of stages: the probability that retrieval found every needed table, times the probability that the generator wrote correct SQL given the right tables, times the probability that the explanation states the result correctly. With 93% full-set recall, 85% generation accuracy given recall and 97% explanation accuracy, end-to-end correctness is about 0.93 × 0.85 × 0.97 ≈ 77%. Doubling the model's size might lift generation from 85% to 88%, worth about 2.5 points end to end; lifting recall from 93% to 98% with better table documents is worth about 4 points and costs nothing per call. Teams that measure only end-to-end accuracy tend to spend on the model; teams that measure each stage spend on the metadata.

Query logs are the best training data you already have

Every human-written query in the warehouse is a free, verified example of how your company asks for data: which tables, which joins, which filters, which definitions. Mining them gives table popularity, join paths, common filters and dead tables for retrieval, and, when paired with the question that prompted them (from tickets, notebooks or dashboard titles), candidate few-shot examples. The caveats: logs contain mistakes and one-off hacks, so weight by how many distinct users ran a pattern and prefer queries from certified dashboards; and logs reveal what people with access did, so examples must be filtered by the asking user's permissions like everything else.

Dialects and nested data are where generic SQL knowledge breaks

Spider 2.0 deliberately included BigQuery, Snowflake, DuckDB, SQLite, PostgreSQL and ClickHouse because each has its own functions and data types. Date arithmetic, string functions and especially nested and repeated columns (arrays of structs, common in event data, which must be unnested before aggregation) are where models trained mostly on generic SQL fail; Spider 2.0 reports noticeably lower success on tasks involving nested columns. The defence is a short dialect guide in the prompt with the idioms your warehouse needs, verified examples that use those idioms, and the parser catching invalid syntax before the warehouse does.

Execution accuracy has false positives and false negatives

Execution accuracy compares results on one database state, so two different queries can agree by coincidence: a query that forgets to exclude refunds returns the same answer as the reference on a snapshot where no refunds happened in the requested week. Spider's later test suites addressed this by running queries on several database variants built to distinguish them. In production, the equivalent is to run golden queries on two or three snapshots (different weeks) and to include questions designed so that the common mistakes change the answer. False negatives come from overly strict comparison (column order, extra columns, rounding), which is why the comparison must be defined carefully and spot-checked by analysts.

Semantic layers and agents push each other

An agent makes the gaps in a semantic layer visible: every uncertified raw-SQL answer is a question the layer could not express, with its frequency and the user's satisfaction attached. Ranking these by volume gives the analytics team a backlog driven by real demand. In the other direction, the semantic layer makes the agent cheaper and more accurate: questions that map to governed metrics need a smaller model, less schema context and no fan-out reasoning. Over a year, the healthy trajectory is a rising certified share, which is a better health metric for the programme than raw accuracy.

Clarification is a decision under uncertainty, not a style

Asking costs a turn of the user's attention; not asking risks a wrong number. The decision should compare the expected cost of each, which requires an estimate of how different the interpretations' answers are. Computing all interpretations when it is cheap converts an abstract ambiguity into a concrete number: "fiscal and calendar quarters differ by 14%" justifies a question, "they differ by 0.4%" does not. This also lets the agent show both numbers when asking, which is more useful than an abstract question and teaches users how the terms are defined.

Cost guards must be adversarially robust, not just careful

Users will try to get around refusals ("I really need the full history"), and the model will try to help them, for example by splitting one large scan into twelve monthly queries that each pass the per-query cap. Budgets therefore apply per question and per user per day, not only per query; the orchestrator sums the estimates of all queries issued for one question; and a refused query's alternatives are suggested by the system (narrower date range, sampled table, pre-aggregated table) rather than left to the model to engineer around the limit.

How it fails in practice

The double-counted quarter. In the first month, finance noticed that the agent's quarterly revenue was 18% higher than the dashboard. The trace showed the agent joining fct_orders to dim_customer_address, which has one row per address a customer ever had, to filter by region; customers who had moved were counted once per address. The fix was threefold: revenue became a semantic-layer metric so raw-SQL derivation stopped, the validator gained the fan-out check on non-unique join keys, and the question went into the golden set with a snapshot that contains movers.

The free query that wasn't. A team's tables moved under row-level security. The agent's dry runs on those tables started returning 0 bytes, the tier logic classified every query as free, and a week of exploratory queries on a large events table cost several thousand dollars before the weekly cost report caught it. The hard cap would have bounded each query, but it had been configured only for the confirmation tier. Now 0 bytes on a policy-protected table means "estimate from metadata", every query carries the hard cap regardless of tier, and cost is reconciled daily against the warehouse's billed bytes.

The confident stale column. After a schema migration renamed region to sales_region, the agent kept generating the old name for two days because the metadata index rebuilt only nightly and the verified examples still used it. Queries failed and repaired, but repair added latency and cost. The catalogue now pushes change events that update the index within minutes and mark examples that reference dropped columns for review.

What to monitor

Per question: latency by stage, model tokens, number of queries, bytes scanned and billed, repairs, clarifications, certified or not, feedback. Per day: answer-correctness on a sampled review, thumbs-down rate, cost per answer (model plus warehouse), share of queries refused or confirmed by the cost guard, share of queries with RLS-unknown estimates, recall on the golden set after each metadata rebuild. Safety: queries attempted on non-allowed tables (should be blocked, and a rising count suggests prompt injection or confused retrieval), write statements blocked, and permission errors from the warehouse (which mean retrieval offered a table the user cannot read).

Knobs people tune

The number of tables retrieved and columns kept; boosts for certification and usage; the number of verified examples in the prompt; repair attempts; cost tiers and the hard cap per user role; the materiality threshold for clarifications; which questions route to a small model; result-cache lifetime.

The real cases: BIRD, Spider 2.0 and BigQuery's cost controls

BIRD (Li et al., NeurIPS 2023) is the clearest public evidence of the gap between demo and reality: 12,751 question-SQL pairs over 95 databases totalling 33.4 GB across 37 domains, with dirty values, questions that need external knowledge (found in 70.1% of questions needing value illustration), and an efficiency metric. GPT-4 reached 54.89% execution accuracy against 92.96% for humans. Spider 2.0 (Lei et al., ICLR 2025) moved closer to enterprise reality: 632 tasks on BigQuery, Snowflake and other systems, databases averaging 812 columns, gold queries averaging 144 tokens; an o1-preview-based agent solved 21.3% of tasks, compared with 91.2% on Spider 1.0 and 73.0% on BIRD for methods based on GPT-4, and its error analysis put wrong schema linking first at 27.6% of errors. Benchmark numbers move quickly as models improve, so treat them as a picture of what makes the task hard (schema size, values, dialects, multi-step work), not as a forecast of your accuracy.

BigQuery's cost documentation, checked on 10 October 2026, supplies the mechanics of the cost guard: dry runs and the query validator estimate bytes before execution; the maximum bytes billed setting makes a query fail before running if its estimate exceeds the limit, with no charge; LIMIT does not reduce cost on non-clustered tables; estimates are upper bounds on clustered tables; and dry runs on tables with row-level security always return 0 bytes to prevent side channels. On-demand queries cost $6.25 per TiB in the US multi-region, with the first TiB each month free. Other warehouses have equivalents (statement timeouts, resource monitors, query cost estimates), but the design pattern is the same: estimate, tier, cap, reconcile.

Goal: build the schema-linking recall scorer from section 6, run it on two toy retrievers, and see why full-set recall (every needed table found) is the number that caps accuracy. Python 3.12, standard library only.

  1. Save as schema_recall.py and run python schema_recall.py.
  2. Read the first table: for each k, table recall and full-set recall for a name-only lexical retriever. Full-set recall is always lower, because one missing table fails a whole question.
  3. Read the second table: the same retriever over enriched documents (descriptions, top values, usage notes). Recall rises at every k with no change to the "model".
  4. Read the token column: context cost grows linearly with k while recall flattens. Pick the k you would ship and justify it.
  5. Add a question of your own whose words match none of the table names (for example "what share of shoppers came back within a month?") and see which documents it needs.
"""Schema-linking recall scorer. Python 3.12, stdlib only."""
import math, re
from collections import Counter

TABLES = {  # name: (enriched description, tokens in prompt)
  "fct_orders": ("orders amount refund tax order_date revenue sales certified", 420),
  "orders_raw": ("raw orders landing copy deprecated", 380),
  "dim_customer": ("customer segment enterprise smb consumer signup", 300),
  "dim_customer_address": ("customer address history moved city", 260),
  "dim_store": ("store region territory emea apac amer store-level reporting", 280),
  "dim_date": ("date fiscal quarter calendar month week", 200),
  "fct_returns": ("returns reason refund amount return_date", 320),
  "fct_sessions": ("web sessions visits clickstream device", 400),
  "dim_product": ("product sku category brand", 300),
  "fct_inventory": ("inventory stock level store sku daily", 350),
}
GOLDEN = [  # question, tables the reference query needs
  ("net revenue by region last quarter for enterprise customers",
   {"fct_orders", "dim_store", "dim_customer", "dim_date"}),
  ("refund amount by product category in september", {"fct_returns", "dim_product", "dim_date"}),
  ("stock level of sku 1123 in emea stores", {"fct_inventory", "dim_store"}),
  ("enterprise customers who signed up this fiscal year", {"dim_customer", "dim_date"}),
  ("web visits by device last week", {"fct_sessions", "dim_date"}),
  ("sales by brand for smb segment", {"fct_orders", "dim_product", "dim_customer"}),
]
def toks(s): return re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", s.lower().replace("_", " "))

def bm25_rank(q, docs, k1=1.2, b=0.75):
    N = len(docs); avg = sum(len(d) for d in docs.values()) / N
    df = Counter(t for d in docs.values() for t in set(d))
    def score(d):
        tf = Counter(d); s = 0.0
        for t in toks(q):
            if t in tf:
                idf = math.log(1 + (N - df[t] + 0.5) / (df[t] + 0.5))
                s += idf * tf[t] * (k1 + 1) / (tf[t] + k1 * (1 - b + b * len(d) / avg))
        return s
    return sorted(docs, key=lambda n: -score(docs[n]))

def evaluate(label, docs):
    print(f"\n{label}\n  k  table-recall  full-set-recall  avg-tokens")
    for k in (2, 3, 4, 6, 8):
        hit = need = full = tok = 0
        for q, gold in GOLDEN:
            top = bm25_rank(q, docs)[:k]
            got = gold & set(top)
            hit += len(got); need += len(gold); full += got == gold
            tok += sum(TABLES[t][1] for t in top)
        n = len(GOLDEN)
        print(f"{k:3} {hit/need:12.2f} {full/n:16.2f} {tok/n:11.0f}")

evaluate("Name-only documents", {n: toks(n) for n in TABLES})
evaluate("Enriched documents", {n: toks(n + " " + d) for n, (d, _) in TABLES.items()})

What to look for: full-set recall well below table recall at small k; a large jump from enriching the documents (words like "region" and "enterprise" live in descriptions and values, not table names); and the deprecated orders_raw competing with fct_orders under name-only search, which is why certification and usage boosts exist.

Estimate it

Your agent handles 15,000 questions a day. Each question uses 3.2 model calls of 14,000 input and 500 output tokens at $3 and $15 per million, and executes 1.5 queries with a median scan of 40 GB at $6.25 per TiB. (a) What are the daily model and warehouse costs? (b) A proposal adds 20 more tables to every prompt (8,000 more tokens) to raise full-set recall from 93% to 97%. What does it cost, and is it worth it? (c) What single control most limits the tail of warehouse cost?

Answer

(a) One call: 14,000 × $3/10⁶ + 500 × $15/10⁶ = $0.042 + $0.0075 = $0.0495; × 3.2 = $0.158 per question; × 15,000 ≈ $2,380 a day for the model. One query: 40/1,024 TiB × $6.25 ≈ $0.244; × 1.5 = $0.366 per question; × 15,000 ≈ $5,490 a day for the warehouse, more than twice the model.

(b) 8,000 extra input tokens × 3.2 calls × $3/10⁶ = $0.077 more per question, about $1,150 a day, roughly +48% on the model bill. Recall from 93% to 97% raises the ceiling by 4 points; with generation accuracy of 85% given recall, end-to-end correctness rises by about 0.04 × 0.85 ≈ 3.4 points, about 510 more correct answers a day, around $2.25 per extra correct answer. Whether that is worth it depends on what a wrong answer costs, but a cheaper route probably exists: improve the documents of the tables that are being missed (the misses are usually a few tables with poor descriptions), or retrieve 40 tables only when the first retrieval's scores are low.

(c) The hard per-query bytes cap (BigQuery's maximum bytes billed) submitted with every query, because it is enforced by the warehouse before execution regardless of what the model, validator or tier logic did. Partition-filter requirements and per-user daily budgets reduce the typical cost and slow leaks, but the cap is what makes a $10,000 scan impossible.

Diagnose it

After launch, analysts report that "customer count" answers are inconsistent: the same question asked on Monday and Thursday gives numbers 12% apart, while the underlying data barely changed. What do you check, and what do you change?

Answer

Pull both traces and diff them. Retrieval: did different tables come back (for example dim_customer one day and a marketing copy of customers the next, because the copy's description was edited)? Definition: did one query count all customers and the other active customers, or use different date logic? Ambiguity handling: did the agent assume differently because one user's saved preference applied? Non-determinism: at non-zero temperature, the generator can choose different but plausible queries. Changes: make "customer count" and "active customer count" governed semantic-layer metrics so the question compiles to one definition; run generation at temperature 0 for SQL; add certification boosts so copies rank lower; add the question with both readings to the golden set; and when the agent is not using a certified metric, say so in the answer so users know the number is a derivation.
