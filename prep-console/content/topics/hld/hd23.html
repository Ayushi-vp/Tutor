A bank's phone line takes a million calls a day. Most are routine: a balance, a lost card, a payment date, a branch's opening hours. Callers wait an average of six minutes for an agent, the contact centre costs a fortune, and the old menu system ("press 4 for card services") is hated. The bank wants a voice assistant that answers the phone, understands what callers say in their own words, looks up their account, does the routine things, and hands the rest to a human with a summary. The same assistant should also run inside the bank's mobile app.

The prototype works in a quiet office. It records the caller until they stop, sends the audio to a transcription service, sends the text to a language model, sends the reply to a speech synthesiser, and plays it. Each step takes about a second, so every reply arrives three or four seconds after the caller stops talking. Callers say "hello?" into the silence, then both talk at once. When the assistant is mid-sentence and the caller says "no, the other card", it keeps reading out the wrong card's details for ten seconds, because it cannot be interrupted. When a caller pauses to find their card, it decides they have finished and answers half a question. On a speakerphone it hears its own voice and interrupts itself. And at 9 a.m. on Monday, when 8,000 people call at once, the transcription vendor's rate limit returns errors and calls go silent.

Every one of these is a design problem, not a model problem. The senior question is to build a voice assistant that holds 10,000 simultaneous conversations, replies in about a second or less, can be interrupted naturally, takes turns without cutting people off, calls bank systems mid-conversation without dead air, works over both phone lines and the app, costs a few cents a minute at most, and can be shown to work well, with numbers. The two big architectural choices are a cascade of speech recognition, language model and speech synthesis, and a single speech-to-speech model, and the interview expects you to compare them honestly.

The lesson follows the interview: requirements with latency, quality, cost and safety targets; estimates by hand for concurrency, tokens, GPUs and cost per minute; the session API; the choice between cascade and speech-to-speech; the high-level design; deep dives on the per-stage latency budget, the barge-in state machine, transport over telephony and WebRTC, and tool calls mid-conversation; GPU sizing; evaluation; failure modes; and what changes at ten times the scale. The real cases are the published documentation of OpenAI's Realtime API, LiveKit's agent framework and Twilio's media streams, which show the exact mechanisms (voice activity detection settings, truncation on interruption, audio formats and buffer control) a production system has to get right. The theory of the latency budget itself is in mm7; this case is about running it as a system.

What the system does

A real-time voice assistant holds spoken conversations. For each call it runs a session: a long-lived process that receives the caller's audio as a continuous stream, decides when the caller has finished a turn, works out a reply (possibly calling tools such as "look up account" or "freeze card"), speaks the reply as a stream of audio, and keeps listening the whole time in case the caller interrupts. Unlike a chat request, which lasts a few seconds, a call lasts minutes, and audio flows in both directions continuously, so the design is about many concurrent stateful streams rather than individual requests.

The mental model: a simultaneous interpreter's booth

Picture a conference interpreter. They listen continuously, start working out the translation before the speaker finishes, begin speaking as soon as they have the first phrase, stop instantly if the speaker interrupts with a correction, and never leave long silences. Behind them, a team handles the logistics: microphones, headsets, booth scheduling for a hundred sessions in parallel.

The voice assistant is that interpreter plus that logistics team. Voice activity detection (VAD), a tiny model that labels each 20–30 ms audio frame as speech or not, is the interpreter's ear noticing someone is talking. Endpointing, deciding the caller has finished, is knowing when a sentence is over. Streaming speech recognition (ASR) is understanding words as they come. The language model is deciding what to say, and streaming speech synthesis (TTS) is speaking the first phrase while still composing the rest. Barge-in handling is stopping mid-sentence when interrupted. The logistics team is the media servers, telephony connections and GPU pools that make 10,000 booths work at once.

The analogy breaks in one important place: a human interpreter hears themselves without confusion, but the assistant's microphone can pick up its own voice (on speakerphones especially), and without echo cancellation it will interrupt itself. And unlike an interpreter, the assistant has no natural sense of when a pause means "I'm thinking" rather than "I'm done": that has to be built and tuned.

Three things that make voice different from chat

First, silence is the user interface. In chat, a user presses send; in speech, the system must infer the end of a turn from silence and meaning, and every millisecond it waits is added to every reply. Second, audio is consumed in real time: a reply can be generated faster than it is spoken, so the system always has buffered audio queued ahead of what the caller has heard, and an interruption must discard that buffer and correct the conversation history to what was actually heard. Third, calls are long-lived and stateful: a four-minute call pins resources for four minutes, peaks are sharp (Monday mornings), and a crashed process must not drop a live call. A reader who stops here has the shape: per-call streaming sessions, a turn-taking state machine at the centre, model stages that overlap rather than run in sequence, and capacity planned in concurrent calls rather than requests.

1 · Requirements

Functional. Answer inbound phone calls and in-app voice sessions; understand free speech in the bank's main languages; authenticate callers (knowledge questions or a one-time code sent to the app); answer questions from the help centre; call account tools (balance, recent transactions, freeze card, set up a payment plan) with the caller's permissions; hand off to a human agent with a summary when needed (hd15 covers handoff policy); allow interruption at any time; record transcripts for audit where the law allows.

Latency targets. Voice-to-voice latency, from the end of the caller's speech to the first audio of the reply as the caller hears it: p50 at most 1.0 s on the phone and 0.8 s in the app; p95 at most 1.6 s. Barge-in: agent audio stops within 200 ms of a confirmed interruption. No dead air longer than 1.5 s during tool calls.

Quality targets. Task success (the caller's goal achieved without a human) at least 65% for in-scope intents; premature endpoints (cutting the caller off) below 2% of turns; false barge-ins (stopping for a cough or "mm-hm") below 3% of agent turns; word error rate on numbers and names below agreed thresholds per language.

Scale and cost targets. 1 million calls a day, 10,000 simultaneous at peak; total cost including telephony at most $0.05 per minute; availability 99.95% for call handling (a failed assistant must fall back to the human queue, never to silence).

Safety targets. Authentication before any account data is spoken; spoken confirmation before money moves; no card numbers captured in transcripts (sensitive digits via keypad); recordings and transcripts retained and redacted per policy.

Clarifying questions. "Phone only, or app too?" "Which languages?" "Do we own the telephony or use a provider?" "Must the LLM be self-hosted for data reasons?" "What does a human handoff look like, and what is the human queue's capacity?"

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Concurrency. One million calls a day at an average of 4 minutes is 4 million call-minutes. Spread over the 1,440 minutes of a day, that is about 2,800 calls in progress on average (Little's law: concurrency = arrival rate × duration). Calls bunch into business hours, with a Monday-morning peak about three times the average, so roughly 8,300 at peak; the design is sized for 10,000 with headroom.

Audio bandwidth. Phone audio is G.711 μ-law: 8,000 samples a second at 8 bits, 64 kbit/s each way, plus packet overhead. 10,000 calls is about 1.3 Gbit/s of audio in both directions combined: unremarkable for a data centre, but it must stay inside one region close to the telephony provider, because every extra network hop adds latency.

LLM load. In a typical service call the agent speaks a turn about every 8 seconds, so 10,000 calls produce about 1,250 LLM requests a second. Each request carries the system prompt, tool definitions and the conversation so far, about 3,500 tokens, but with prefix caching (hd1) only the new part, roughly 300 tokens (the caller's latest words plus the agent's last turn and any tool result), needs fresh prefill. Spoken replies are short: about 40 output tokens. So the LLM fleet must handle about 375,000 prefill tokens and 50,000 decode tokens a second, with time to first token held near 250 ms.

ASR and TTS load. Every active call streams caller audio through VAD and ASR all the time (the caller might speak at any moment), so 10,000 ASR streams. The agent is speaking roughly 40% of the time, so about 4,000 TTS streams at peak, each of which must synthesise faster than real time.

Cost per minute, assuming self-hosted models. The GPU sizing in section 10 arrives at about 230 GPUs at peak. At an assumed $2.50 per GPU-hour, that is $575 an hour, during an hour in which 10,000 calls run for 60 minutes each: 600,000 call-minutes. GPU cost is therefore about $0.001 per call-minute at peak, perhaps $0.002 averaged over the day once idle off-peak capacity is counted. Telephony carriage (assume $0.010 a minute for this estimate; real rates depend on carrier, country and contract) is ten times larger. If the LLM is a hosted API instead, with the illustrative $3 and $15 per million input and output tokens and cached input at a tenth of the price, one turn costs about (300 + 3,200 × 0.1) × $3/10⁶ + 40 × $15/10⁶ ≈ $0.0019 + $0.0006 = $0.0025, and at 7.5 turns a minute about $0.018 a minute, more than everything else combined. Either way, the cost target of $0.05 a minute is met, but by a margin that depends far more on carriage and API pricing than on GPUs.

The insight to say out loud. This is a concurrency system, not a throughput system: 10,000 long-lived bidirectional streams, each with tight per-turn deadlines. The hard parts are turn-taking, the latency budget and graceful failure, not raw compute.

3 · The session API

# Phone: the telephony provider opens a WebSocket per call
WS /v1/calls/stream?call_sid=CA...          # inbound: base64 audio/x-mulaw @ 8 kHz, mono
  <- {"event":"media","media":{"payload":"..."}}        every 20 ms
  -> {"event":"media","media":{"payload":"..."}}        agent audio chunks
  -> {"event":"mark","mark":{"name":"turn12_chunk3"}}   provider echoes when played
  -> {"event":"clear"}                                  barge-in: drop buffered audio

# App: the client joins a WebRTC room; an agent worker joins the same room
POST /v1/sessions  { "channel": "app", "user_token": "...", "locale": "en-GB" }
  -> { "room": "rm_93", "token": "...", "media_url": "wss://media.region..." }

# Internal events every session emits (to tracing and evaluation)
turn.user_end_detected  { t_audio_end_ms, endpoint_delay_ms, transcript, confidence }
turn.agent_first_audio  { t_first_audio_ms, voice_to_voice_ms, stage_breakdown }
turn.barge_in           { confirmed: true, played_ms: 1840, truncated_chars: 112 }
tool.call / tool.result { name, latency_ms, idempotency_key, filler_spoken }

Two choices matter. The phone path speaks the telephony provider's protocol directly, including marks (the provider echoes a named marker when the audio before it has finished playing) and clear (empty the provider's playback buffer), because barge-in depends on both: Twilio's media-stream documentation describes exactly these two messages. And every turn emits timing events with a stage breakdown, because the latency budget is managed per turn from real measurements, not from component benchmarks.

4 · Cascade or speech-to-speech

A cascade connects separate models with text. Its strengths are control and visibility: transcripts exist for audit and evaluation; text guardrails and redaction run between recognition and the LLM and between the LLM and synthesis; any LLM can be used, including fine-tuned or self-hosted ones with full tool support; each stage can be swapped, scaled and measured independently. Its weakness is that each hand-off adds latency, and converting speech to text discards tone of voice: the LLM does not hear that the caller sounds upset, and the synthesiser must guess how to say things.

A speech-to-speech model takes audio in and produces audio out. Hosted examples include OpenAI's Realtime models; research models such as Moshi model the user's and the system's audio as parallel streams and report a theoretical latency of 160 ms. Strengths: fewer hops and therefore lower latency, prosody (tone, emphasis, hesitation) understood and produced, and turn detection built in (OpenAI's API offers server-side VAD and a semantic VAD mode). Weaknesses: less control between steps, transcripts produced alongside rather than as the source of truth, dependence on one vendor's model for both understanding and speaking, and usually a per-minute or per-audio-token price.

The design choice is per flow, behind one session layer. The bank routes authentication, payments and complaints (where exact transcripts, redaction and deterministic tool use matter) through the cascade, and open-ended help-centre conversations through speech-to-speech where it measures better on latency and satisfaction. The session layer, transport, tools and evaluation are shared, so moving a flow is a configuration change measured by an A/B test.

5 · High-level design

Edge. Phone calls arrive from carriers over SIP trunks (connections carrying calls between the phone network and software) to a telephony provider, which forwards audio over a WebSocket to the nearest region. App sessions use WebRTC to a media server (a selective forwarding unit, or SFU, such as LiveKit's) in the nearest region. Session workers are CPU processes, each running a few dozen call sessions: VAD, the turn state machine, orchestration of ASR, LLM, TTS and tools, and audio encoding. Model services are shared GPU pools reached over streaming gRPC: ASR, an LLM fleet (or a gateway to a hosted LLM, hd13), TTS, and for some flows a speech-to-speech endpoint. Tool services are the bank's APIs behind an authorisation layer that checks the authenticated caller's identity. State: per-call session state (transcript, task, tool results) checkpointed to a fast store after each turn; recordings and transcripts to object storage with retention rules; timing and quality events to the tracing and evaluation platform (hd12). Control plane: routing of calls to regions and workers, autoscaling, configuration of prompts, voices and turn-detection settings per flow.

Everything on a call's critical path lives in one region, ideally one availability zone: the media edge, the session worker and the GPU services. A cross-region hop adds tens of milliseconds to every turn, which the budget cannot afford.

6 · Deep dive: the latency budget, stage by stage

The baseline, stage by stage. Inbound carriage: the caller's audio crosses carriers and the telephony provider's jitter buffer (a small buffer that smooths uneven packet arrival), about 80 ms. Endpointing: the system waits for enough silence (and, with a turn detector, enough meaning) to decide the turn is over; 300 ms is an aggressive but common target. Defaults in frameworks are more conservative: LiveKit's documentation says the session waits between 0.5 and 3.0 seconds after speech by default, and its Silero VAD plugin defaults to 0.55 s of silence; OpenAI's server VAD example uses 500 ms. Getting to 300 ms is a tuning project, not a default. ASR finalisation: once the turn ends, the recogniser emits its final transcript, about 60 ms after the last audio. LLM time to first token: queueing plus prefill of the new tokens, about 250 ms. First phrase: TTS needs a few words before it can start speaking naturally, so the system waits for the first clause, about 100 ms of generation. TTS first audio: about 120 ms. Outbound carriage: about 80 ms. Total: about 990 ms at the median, just inside the phone target with no margin.

Where the time comes back. Semantic turn detection: a turn detector, a small model reading the partial transcript (and in some systems the audio), predicts whether the turn is complete. After a definite statement ("I'd like to freeze my card.") it can endpoint after 200 ms; after a trailing one ("My account number is, um...") it waits longer. OpenAI's semantic VAD mode works the same way, with an eagerness setting that tunes the maximum wait. Speculative generation: start the LLM on the partial transcript after, say, 150 ms of silence; if the turn is confirmed and the final transcript matches, the reply is already 150 ms along; if the caller keeps talking, discard it. This moves most of the LLM's time under the endpointing wait and brings the median to about 800 ms. Prefix caching keeps prefill to the new tokens. Short first sentences: instruct the model to open with a short clause ("Sure, let me check.") so TTS can start sooner. Co-location: VAD, ASR, LLM and TTS in the same zone as the media edge, with persistent streaming connections, so internal hops cost single-digit milliseconds. WebRTC: in the app, network carriage is about 40 ms each way instead of 80, saving another 80 ms.

Percentiles do not add. The p95 of each stage summed gives a pessimistic number, because stages rarely all hit their tail at once; mm7's lab shows stage p95s summing to about 1,510 ms against an end-to-end p95 of about 1,330 ms. The opposite error also happens: LLM queueing delay rises sharply as the fleet's utilisation rises, so the p95 at 9 a.m. on Monday can be twice that at 3 p.m. on Wednesday. Measure voice-to-voice latency end to end, per turn, at the media edge, and break it down by stage from timestamps, by hour and by load.

7 · Deep dive: the barge-in state machine

Detecting a candidate. VAD runs on the caller's audio continuously, including while the agent speaks. When it detects speech during the agent's turn, the session does not stop immediately: coughs, a door, a television and the caller's own backchannels ("mm-hm", "yeah", "OK", which mean "go on") would all stop the agent mid-sentence. Instead it enters the candidate state and ducks the agent's audio (lowers its volume, say to 30%) so the caller is not shouted over.

Confirming. The interruption is confirmed if speech continues for about 250 ms, or if ASR produces words that are not on the backchannel list. A word-based rule catches short real interruptions ("Stop.", "No.") that a duration rule would miss, while the duration rule catches interruptions in which ASR has not yet produced words. LiveKit's documentation describes a related feature, adaptive interruption handling, that distinguishes true interruptions from conversational backchannels, and a notion of false interruptions for this exact case. If the candidate speech ends quickly or is a backchannel, volume is restored and the agent continues.

Acting on a confirmed interruption. Three things, all needed. Stop the audio: the session stops sending audio, but the telephony provider has already buffered a second or more ahead, so the session sends Twilio's clear message, which empties the provider's audio buffer (Twilio then returns the outstanding marks). Over WebRTC, the agent's track is muted or its send buffer flushed. Cancel generation: cancel the LLM stream and the TTS stream so no more audio is produced and no more tokens are paid for. Truncate history: the LLM produced, say, three sentences, but the caller heard only the first one and a half. If the history keeps all three, the model believes it told the caller things they never heard ("as I mentioned, the fee is waived"). The session knows what was played from the marks: after each audio chunk it sends a mark named for that chunk, and the provider echoes it when that chunk finishes playing, so the last echoed mark gives the played position. OpenAI's Realtime documentation describes the same step for its API: over WebRTC and SIP the server truncates unplayed audio automatically, and over a WebSocket the client sends conversation.item.truncate with audio_end_ms, the number of milliseconds actually played.

Echo. If the caller is on a speakerphone or the app's loudspeaker, the agent's own voice comes back through the microphone. Browsers' WebRTC stacks include echo cancellation (AEC), which subtracts the known outgoing signal from the incoming one. On the phone you cannot rely on the far end, so the session raises the VAD threshold while the agent speaks (LiveKit's telephony example raises the server VAD threshold to 0.7 for noisy phone audio) and can compare incoming audio with its own recent output. Self-interruption shows up in metrics as barge-ins whose transcript matches the agent's own words.

Worked trace. At t = 0 the agent starts: "Your last three payments were forty pounds to..." At 1.6 s the caller says "No, the credit card." VAD fires at 1.65 s; the session ducks and enters the candidate state. ASR emits "no the" at 1.85 s: not a backchannel, so the interruption is confirmed at 1.85 s. The session sends clear; the last echoed mark corresponds to 1.70 s of played audio ("Your last three payments were forty pounds"), so the history is truncated there and the agent's turn is recorded as interrupted. The LLM and TTS streams are cancelled. The caller's full utterance ends at 2.6 s, endpointing confirms at 2.9 s, and the reply about the credit card starts at about 3.6 s. Audio stopped about 200 ms after the caller began speaking, within target.

8 · Deep dive: transport over telephony and WebRTC

Phone path. Calls reach the bank's numbers through carriers and a telephony provider. The provider bridges the call to software in one of two ways: a SIP trunk to the bank's own media servers (more control, more operations), or a media stream over a WebSocket, as Twilio offers. Twilio's documentation specifies the format: audio arrives in media messages as base64-encoded audio/x-mulaw at 8,000 Hz, one channel. That is G.711 μ-law, the 8-bit, 8 kHz, 64 kbit/s encoding of classic telephony. Two consequences. Recognition models must be trained or tuned for 8 kHz narrowband audio, because models trained on 16 kHz wideband lose accuracy on phone audio, especially on numbers and names; and TTS output must be resampled to 8 kHz and μ-law encoded, which flattens voice quality, so voices are chosen for intelligibility on the phone. Typical audio frames are 20 ms (160 bytes of μ-law each).

App path. The app uses WebRTC, the browser and mobile standard for real-time media over UDP: the Opus codec at up to 48 kHz, jitter buffers that adapt to network conditions, built-in echo cancellation, noise suppression and encryption. The app connects to a media server in the nearest region; the session worker joins the same room as another participant. WebRTC's UDP transport tolerates packet loss gracefully (Opus conceals a lost packet), whereas audio over a TCP WebSocket stalls on loss while TCP retransmits, so WebSockets carrying audio belong only between servers in the same data centre, never over mobile networks to users.

Region placement and failure. Phone numbers and app sessions are routed to the nearest healthy region; each region has its own media edge, session workers and GPU pools sized for its share of peak plus the share it would inherit if a neighbouring region failed. If no assistant capacity is available, the call routes to the human queue with a message, never to silence.

9 · Deep dive: tool calls mid-conversation

The dead-air problem. On the phone, more than about a second of silence makes callers think the line dropped. A tool such as "fetch last ten transactions" might take 300 ms or 3 seconds. The session predicts each tool's latency from recent history (a p90 per tool). If the prediction exceeds about 700 ms, the LLM is prompted (or a template is used) to speak a short filler first ("Let me pull up your recent transactions"), and the tool runs while the filler plays. If the tool is still running when the filler ends, a second filler or a soft processing sound follows at about 2 seconds, and a fallback at a hard timeout (say 6 seconds): "I'm having trouble reaching that system; I can transfer you or try again."

Keep listening. During a tool wait the caller may speak ("actually, just the credit card"). The state machine stays responsive: the new utterance is appended, and the tool result is used if still relevant or discarded if the caller changed the request.

State-changing tools. Freezing a card, setting up a payment plan or moving money follows the propose-confirm-execute pattern from hd15: the agent states exactly what it will do, the caller confirms ("yes"), and the tool executes with an idempotency key so that a retry after a timeout or a worker crash cannot execute twice. Numbers are read back digit by digit, because speech recognition errors on numbers are the most damaging kind.

Sensitive input. Card numbers or passwords spoken aloud end up in audio, transcripts and logs, which drags all of them into payment-card and security compliance scope. Systems commonly switch to keypad entry for these: the caller types digits on the keypad (the provider sends them as DTMF events, which Twilio's media streams expose as dtmf messages), and the session passes them to the tool without writing them to the transcript.

Two points deserve saying aloud. The LLM's capacity is set by the time-to-first-token target, not raw throughput: a fleet that is 95% busy queues requests, and queueing delay lands directly in the voice-to-voice latency. So LLM GPUs run well below full utilisation at peak. And because calls last minutes, scaling down means draining: a session worker stops accepting new calls and exits when its last call ends. GPU pools scale on a schedule (known daily and weekly peaks) plus queue depth and time to first token, with a warm minimum per region. Off-peak, spare GPUs can run batch work such as transcribing recordings for quality review.

11 · Evaluation plan

Per-turn metrics, from session events: voice-to-voice latency at p50 and p95 by channel, flow and hour, with the stage breakdown; premature endpoints (the caller resumes speaking within a second of the agent starting, a sign the agent cut them off); false barge-ins (agent stopped for noise, backchannel or its own echo); missed barge-ins (caller spoke over the agent for more than a second without the agent stopping); talk-over duration; dead-air gaps over 1.5 s.

Per-call metrics: task success (the intent was resolved without a human, verified by the tool log and absence of a repeat call within seven days); escalation rate and reason; authentication success; average handle time; post-call survey satisfaction where available.

Recognition quality: word error rate (WER, substitutions plus deletions plus insertions divided by reference words) on a weekly human-transcribed sample, broken out by channel, language and accent, with separate accuracy for numbers, dates and names, which matter most.

Offline regression with simulated callers. A suite of scripted scenarios (lost card, disputed payment, angry caller, caller who changes their mind, caller with a strong accent, noisy background, speakerphone echo) is played by an LLM acting as the caller through a TTS voice, with noise added, against each release. It measures task success, latency and interruption behaviour automatically. Simulated callers are more regular than real ones, so they gate releases but do not replace online measurement.

Online experiments. New turn detectors, voices, prompts or a switch between cascade and speech-to-speech for a flow are A/B tested by call, with task success as the primary metric and latency, premature endpoints, escalations and complaints as guardrails.

13 · What changes at 10× scale

At 10 million calls a day and 100,000 simultaneous calls, three things change. Multi-region by default: tens of thousands of GPUs cannot sit in one region, so each region carries its local traffic plus failover share, and telephony routing becomes region-aware. Model specialisation: the LLM bill or fleet is large enough to fine-tune a small model for the top intents (shorter prompts, faster first tokens) and to distil the turn detector per language. Capacity contracts and spot use: provisioned throughput from vendors or reserved GPUs cover the daily base, with burst capacity for peaks, and off-peak GPUs run transcription and evaluation jobs so the fleet's average utilisation rises. The session layer itself scales horizontally without change, which is the payoff of keeping it stateless apart from the per-turn checkpoint.

Turn-taking is a prediction problem with asymmetric costs

Endpointing decides, every few tens of milliseconds, whether the caller is done. Waiting too long costs latency on every turn; ending too early costs a broken turn, which is much worse: the agent answers half a question, the caller talks over it, barge-in fires, and the conversation loses several seconds and some trust. So the threshold should not sit where errors balance; it should sit where the expected cost is lowest, which tolerates a slightly slower median to keep premature endpoints rare. A turn detector improves the whole trade-off curve rather than moving along it: it knows that "I'd like to check my balance." is complete and "I'd like to check my, um" is not, so it can be fast on the first and patient on the second. LiveKit's documentation gives the same rationale for its turn-detector model, citing a user who says they need to think for a moment and then pauses.

Speculative generation is a cache of probable futures

Starting the LLM before the turn is confirmed is a bet that the partial transcript is the final one. When it wins, the reply is ready sooner; when it loses, the work is discarded. The cost is wasted prefill and decode on abandoned speculations, which at a 20–30% loss rate adds that much to LLM compute. Two refinements make it cheaper: speculate only after the turn detector's probability crosses a moderate threshold, and keep the speculation's prefix cache, because the final transcript usually extends the partial one, so even a "lost" speculation leaves useful cached prefill behind.

Interruptions corrupt state unless every layer agrees on what was heard

Four layers have their own idea of the conversation: the LLM's context, the TTS stream, the telephony provider's playback buffer and the caller's ears. A barge-in must bring the first three in line with the fourth. The playback buffer is the subtle one: audio sent is not audio played, because providers buffer ahead to smooth jitter. Marks solve this by turning "played" into an event; without them, sessions guess from send times and truncate in the wrong place, producing agents that skip or repeat half-sentences after interruptions. The same discipline applies to tools: a tool result that arrives after the caller changed the request must be checked against the current state before it is spoken.

Speech-to-speech changes where guardrails can live

In a cascade, text exists between every stage, so a redaction step can strip card numbers before the LLM sees them, a policy classifier can block an unsafe reply before it is spoken, and the transcript is the authoritative record. In a speech-to-speech model, the model hears audio and speaks audio; transcripts are produced alongside, not in between. Guardrails move to the edges: input policy enforced by what tools and data the model can reach, output checks run on the parallel transcript (which may lag the audio), and compliance records kept as audio plus transcript. For regulated flows this is often the deciding argument for keeping the cascade, independent of latency.

GPU utilisation fights latency, and calls make it worse

An LLM fleet serving chat can run hot because users tolerate an extra half-second; a voice fleet cannot, because queueing delay lands directly in voice-to-voice latency. Calls are also long, so the load cannot be shed quickly: a surge of new calls adds load that persists for minutes. The practical answers are priority scheduling (voice turns ahead of any batch work sharing the fleet), admission control at the call level (when capacity is exhausted, new calls go to the human queue or a callback offer, rather than degrading every existing call), and forecasting-driven scaling, because reactive autoscaling that takes several minutes to add GPUs is too slow for a Monday-morning ramp.

Narrowband audio changes model choices

Phone audio cuts frequencies above about 3.4 kHz, removing much of the information that distinguishes consonants such as f and s. Recognisers trained on wideband audio lose accuracy on it, most visibly on spelled names, postcodes and account numbers. Teams either fine-tune ASR on 8 kHz call audio, or use models trained on mixed bandwidths, and they add domain vocabulary (product names, branch names) as recognition hints. On the output side, synthetic voices that sound rich at 24 kHz can sound muffled at 8 kHz μ-law, so voices are evaluated on the phone channel, not in a studio.

How it fails in practice

The Monday-morning p95. The assistant met its latency targets in testing and at launch on a Wednesday. The next Monday at 9 a.m., p95 voice-to-voice latency rose from 1.4 s to 2.9 s and talk-over doubled. Traces showed the LLM fleet at 97% utilisation and time to first token queueing. Autoscaling was reactive and took eight minutes to add GPUs, by which time the ramp was over and callers had suffered. The fix was schedule-based pre-scaling from the weekly forecast, a lower utilisation target for the voice pool, and call-level admission control that offers callbacks when capacity is short.

The agent that talked to itself. In-app sessions on one Android model showed a false barge-in rate of 20%, against 2% elsewhere. Transcripts of the "interruptions" matched the agent's own words. That device's echo cancellation was disabled in the app's audio configuration for a speakerphone mode. Re-enabling the platform's echo cancellation and adding a check that flags barge-ins whose transcript matches recent agent output fixed it and gave a permanent detector for the next regression.

The phantom promise. Callers complained that the assistant claimed to have "already explained" a fee waiver they had never heard. Each case followed a barge-in. A refactor had moved truncation onto the send path rather than the played path, so after interruptions the history kept text that had been sent to the provider but never played. Restoring marks-based truncation and adding a simulated-caller scenario that interrupts at random points closed it.

What to monitor

Per turn: voice-to-voice latency by stage, endpointing delay, premature endpoints, false and missed barge-ins, dead-air gaps. Per call: task success, escalation, authentication failures, duration, survey score. Per service: concurrent sessions per worker, GPU utilisation and queue depth per pool, LLM time to first token, ASR and TTS real-time factor, tool latency and error rates. Business: cost per minute split into carriage, APIs and compute; calls routed to humans because of capacity.

Knobs people tune

VAD activation threshold and minimum silence per channel; turn-detector eagerness; barge-in confirmation duration and backchannel list; speculation threshold; filler trigger latency and phrases; TTS voice and speed for the phone channel; LLM utilisation target; regional capacity shares; admission-control thresholds.

The real cases: OpenAI Realtime, LiveKit and Twilio documentation

The mechanisms in this design are visible in vendors' public documentation, checked on 10 October 2026. OpenAI's Realtime API configures turn detection per session with either server_vad, which takes a threshold, prefix_padding_ms and silence_duration_ms (its example uses 0.5, 300 and 500), or semantic_vad, which scores the probability that the user has finished speaking from their words and has an eagerness setting (low, medium, high or auto, where auto equals medium). Its conversation guide describes interruption: over WebRTC and SIP the server knows how much audio has played and truncates automatically; over a WebSocket the client must stop playback and send conversation.item.truncate with audio_end_ms. LiveKit's Agents framework documents turn detection modes (a turn-detector model, a realtime model's own detection, VAD only, speech-to-text endpointing, or manual), default endpointing waits of 0.5 to 3.0 seconds, a requirement that VAD's minimum silence be at least 250 ms when using its audio turn detector, Silero VAD defaults (0.55 s minimum silence, activation threshold 0.5) and a telephony configuration with a higher VAD threshold and a 400 ms silence window. Twilio's media streams specify phone audio as audio/x-mulaw at 8,000 Hz mono, provide mark messages that report when audio has finished playing, and a clear message that empties the playback buffer. Defaults and product names change quickly in this area; the stable lesson is the set of mechanisms every production voice system needs: tunable endpointing, interruption with truncation to played audio, and explicit buffer control.

Goal: build a voice latency budget calculator that samples each stage from a skewed distribution, shows end-to-end percentiles for phone and app, shows that summed stage p95s overstate the end-to-end p95, measures the effect of speculation, and sizes GPUs for N concurrent calls. Python 3.12, standard library only.

  1. Save as voice_budget.py and run python voice_budget.py.
  2. Read the stage table: each stage's p50 and p95, then the sum of p95s against the simulated end-to-end p95.
  3. Compare the three scenarios (phone, phone with speculation, app with speculation). Speculation should move the median by roughly the overlap, not the whole LLM time.
  4. Change ENDPOINT_MS to 550 (a common default) and see how far the phone p50 moves: the turn detector, not the model, is the biggest lever.
  5. Change CALLS and the per-GPU capacities in the sizing block and read the GPU count and cost per minute.
"""Voice latency budget and GPU sizing. Python 3.12, stdlib only."""
import math, random, statistics as st
rng = random.Random(42)
ENDPOINT_MS = 300
def lognorm(median, p95):                 # sampler with given median and p95
    sigma = math.log(p95 / median) / 1.645
    return lambda: median * math.exp(rng.gauss(0, sigma))
def stages(channel):
    net = (80, 130) if channel == "phone" else (40, 80)
    return {"net_in": lognorm(*net), "endpoint": lognorm(ENDPOINT_MS, ENDPOINT_MS * 1.6),
            "asr_final": lognorm(60, 120), "llm_ttft": lognorm(250, 520),
            "first_phrase": lognorm(100, 180), "tts_first": lognorm(120, 220),
            "net_out": lognorm(*net)}
def pct(xs, p): xs = sorted(xs); return xs[int(p / 100 * (len(xs) - 1))]
def simulate(channel, speculate, n=20_000):
    S = stages(channel); totals = []; per = {k: [] for k in S}
    for _ in range(n):
        v = {k: f() for k, f in S.items()}
        for k in v: per[k].append(v[k])
        llm = v["llm_ttft"]
        if speculate:                     # LLM starts at 150 ms of silence on the partial
            head_start = max(0.0, v["endpoint"] + v["asr_final"] - 150)
            llm = max(0.0, llm - head_start)
        totals.append(v["net_in"] + v["endpoint"] + v["asr_final"] + llm
                      + v["first_phrase"] + v["tts_first"] + v["net_out"])
    return per, totals
per, tot = simulate("phone", False)
print(f"{'stage':13} {'p50':>6} {'p95':>6}")
for k, xs in per.items(): print(f"{k:13} {pct(xs,50):6.0f} {pct(xs,95):6.0f}")
print(f"sum of stage p95s: {sum(pct(xs,95) for xs in per.values()):.0f} ms  "
      f"vs end-to-end p95: {pct(tot,95):.0f} ms\n")
for label, ch, spec in [("phone", "phone", False), ("phone+spec", "phone", True),
                        ("app+spec", "app", True)]:
    _, t = simulate(ch, spec)
    print(f"{label:11} p50 {pct(t,50):5.0f} ms   p95 {pct(t,95):5.0f} ms")

CALLS, TURN_S, NEW_TOK, OUT_TOK = 10_000, 8, 300, 40
DEC_PER_GPU, PRE_PER_GPU, UTIL = 2_100, 25_000, 0.65
ASR_PER_GPU, TTS_PER_GPU, TALK_SHARE, GPU_HR = 150, 40, 0.4, 2.50
req = CALLS / TURN_S
llm = (req * OUT_TOK / DEC_PER_GPU + req * NEW_TOK / PRE_PER_GPU) / UTIL
asr = CALLS / ASR_PER_GPU; tts = CALLS * TALK_SHARE / TTS_PER_GPU
total = math.ceil(llm) + math.ceil(asr) + math.ceil(tts)
print(f"\n{req:.0f} LLM req/s -> LLM {llm:.0f}, ASR {asr:.0f}, TTS {tts:.0f} GPUs = {total}")
print(f"GPU cost per call-minute at peak: ${total * GPU_HR / (CALLS * 60):.4f}")

What to look for: the sum of stage p95s well above the simulated end-to-end p95 (tails rarely coincide); a phone p50 near one second without speculation and noticeably lower with it; the endpointing stage dominating; and a GPU cost per minute around a tenth of a cent, small next to an assumed $0.010 per minute of carriage.

Estimate it

A retailer expects 300,000 calls a day averaging 3 minutes, with a peak hour carrying 12% of the day's calls. Agent turns come every 9 seconds, need 250 new prefill tokens and 45 output tokens, and the agent speaks 35% of the time. Use the lesson's per-GPU assumptions (2,100 decode and 25,000 prefill tokens a second per GPU at 65% utilisation, 150 ASR streams and 40 TTS streams per GPU). (a) Peak concurrency? (b) GPUs per service? (c) GPU cost per call-minute at $2.50 an hour?

Answer

(a) The peak hour has 0.12 × 300,000 = 36,000 calls, an arrival rate of 10 a second; with 180-second calls, Little's law gives about 1,800 concurrent calls.

(b) LLM requests: 1,800 / 9 = 200 a second. Decode: 200 × 45 = 9,000 tok/s ÷ 2,100 ≈ 4.3 GPUs; prefill: 200 × 250 = 50,000 tok/s ÷ 25,000 = 2 GPUs; total 6.3 ÷ 0.65 ≈ 10 LLM GPUs. ASR: 1,800 ÷ 150 = 12. TTS: 1,800 × 0.35 = 630 streams ÷ 40 ≈ 16. About 38 GPUs at peak, plus a warm minimum and failover share for a second region.

(c) 38 × $2.50 = $95 an hour for 1,800 × 60 = 108,000 call-minutes: about $0.0009 per minute at peak. Off-peak the same GPUs serve fewer calls unless scaled down, so the daily average is perhaps two to three times higher, still far below typical carriage rates.

Diagnose it

After a release, the share of calls where callers repeat themselves ("as I was saying...") doubles, voice-to-voice p50 improves by 120 ms, and false barge-ins are unchanged. What happened, and what do you check?

Answer

Faster replies plus more repeats points to premature endpoints: the release made the agent end turns sooner, so it answers before callers finish and they have to repeat. Check the release diff for endpointing changes (a lower silence threshold, a higher turn-detector eagerness, a new detector model); then look at the premature-endpoint metric (caller speech resuming within a second of the agent starting) by flow and channel, and listen to a sample. Phone calls with long account numbers or callers who pause mid-sentence will be hit hardest. Roll back the endpointing change or make it channel- and flow-specific, and add a regression scenario with mid-sentence pauses to the simulated-caller suite. The lesson: the latency metric improved because quality got worse, which is why premature endpoints must be a guardrail on every latency change.
