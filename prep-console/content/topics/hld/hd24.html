A design company launches a text-to-image feature inside its editor: type "a watercolour fox reading a newspaper in a café, soft morning light" and get four images to choose from. The beta is a hit. Within a month, five million requests a day arrive, each for four images. The team had sized the GPU fleet from a benchmark of one image at a time, and they discover four things in quick succession.

First, the bill. Each request burns several GPU-seconds, and at twenty million images a day the fleet costs more than the rest of the company's infrastructure combined. Second, the queue. On Tuesday afternoon an influencer posts a prompt template and traffic triples in ten minutes; every request waits behind thousands of others, the editor shows a spinner for a minute, and users give up and press "generate" again, which makes the queue longer. Third, safety. Someone discovers that an innocent-sounding prompt with a deliberate misspelling produces sexual images of a real celebrity, and a journalist publishes examples. The prompt filter missed it, and nothing checked the images. Fourth, provenance. A generated image of a fake news event circulates on social media, and the company is asked whether it came from their product. They cannot say: the file had been screenshotted and its metadata was gone.

Each of these is a system design problem. The senior question is to design the service end to end: the request flow from prompt to stored image; what an image actually costs in GPU-seconds and how steps, resolution, guidance, batching and distilled few-step models change it; how many GPUs are needed and how to size the queue so waits stay short; prompt and output safety; provenance with signed metadata and watermarks; caching and variations; abuse and rate limits; and how to evaluate quality. The diffusion maths is in mm5; this case is about running it as a product.

The lesson follows the interview: requirements with latency, cost, safety and quality targets; estimates by hand for GPU-seconds, GPU-hours and cost; the API; the high-level design; deep dives on GPU cost per image, fleet sizing with queueing theory, safety, provenance, and caching and variations; abuse controls; evaluation; failure modes; and what changes at ten times the scale. The real cases are the SDXL and Adversarial Diffusion Distillation papers for model cost, Hugging Face's diffusion serving guide for practical speed levers, and the C2PA specification for provenance.

What the service does

A text-to-image service turns a written prompt into images. Under the hood, a latent diffusion model (mm5) starts from random noise in a compressed latent space (for a 1,024 × 1,024 image, a grid of 128 × 128 positions with 4 channels), and a large neural network called the denoiser removes a little noise at each of many steps, steered by an encoding of the prompt. A VAE decoder then turns the final latent into pixels. The service wraps that model with everything a product needs: accounts and limits, safety checks, a queue, a GPU fleet, storage and delivery, provenance and evaluation.

The mental model: a photo lab with very expensive printers

Picture a busy photo lab. Customers hand in orders at a counter; a clerk checks each order against the shop's rules (no prints of other people's copyrighted photos, nothing illegal); orders go into a queue; a room of very expensive printers works through the queue, each print taking a known amount of printer time that depends on size and quality setting; a quality inspector looks at every print before it goes out; each print is stamped on the back with the lab's name and date; and the manager watches the queue to decide how many printers to run.

The service is that lab. The counter clerk is admission: authentication, rate limits and the prompt safety check. The printers are GPUs, and printer time is GPU-seconds, the unit of cost. Size and quality settings are resolution, steps and guidance. The inspector is the output safety classifier. The stamp is provenance: a signed C2PA manifest and an invisible watermark. The manager is capacity planning and queueing. The analogy breaks in two useful places. A printer copies what it is given, while the model invents content, so the inspector matters far more than in a photo lab: the order can look innocent and the output still be unacceptable. And a stamp on the back of a print is lost if someone photocopies the front; image metadata is lost the same way when someone takes a screenshot, which is why provenance needs a mark in the pixels too.

Three facts that shape the design

First, cost is GPU time, and it is large: unlike a text request that might cost a fraction of a cent, a high-quality image can take more than a GPU-second, and products ask for four at a time. Second, the work is long and uniform enough to queue: each job takes seconds, so waits come from too few GPUs or from bursts, and queueing theory predicts them well. Third, the product can create harmful content, so safety and provenance are part of the main path, not add-ons. A reader who stops here has the shape: admission and prompt checks, tiered queues feeding a pooled GPU fleet, output inspection, provenance stamping, storage, and a ledger of GPU-seconds that turns into the bill.

1 · Requirements

Functional. Generate images from a text prompt in several aspect ratios; four images per request by default; two tiers, fast (drafts, free users) and quality (final renders, paid users); variations of a chosen image (new seeds, subtle changes), upscaling and inpainting as follow-on operations; a gallery of the user's results; content policy enforcement; provenance on every delivered image; and an admin path for trust-and-safety review and appeals. Out of scope, said aloud: training the model and video generation.

Latency targets. Fast tier: p50 under 2 s, p95 under 4 s from request to all four images visible. Quality tier: p50 under 8 s and p95 under 15 s, with a low-resolution preview within 3 s. Queue wait itself: p99 under 1 s at normal peak.

Cost targets. GPU cost per delivered image under $0.001 on average across tiers; GPU utilisation above 75% averaged over the day (idle GPUs are the main waste).

Safety targets. Prompt-level block recall at least 95% on the red-team set for the most severe categories, with over-blocking under 1% on a benign prompt set; output-level recall at least 99% for sexual content involving minors (zero tolerance, with mandatory reporting where the law requires it, for example to NCMEC in the United States) and at least 97% for other severe categories; every delivered image carries a C2PA manifest and a watermark.

Quality targets. A new model replaces the old one only if it is preferred in a human side-by-side on the fixed prompt set and does not regress prompt adherence or safety.

Clarifying questions. "Which resolutions and how many images per request?" "Free and paid tiers?" "Can users upload images (image-to-image, inpainting)? That changes the abuse surface." "Self-hosted models or an API?" "Which jurisdictions, for safety reporting and provenance labelling rules?"

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Traffic. 5 million requests a day at four images each is 20 million images a day: about 58 requests and 231 images a second on average. With a daily peak about twice the average, 116 requests a second (463 images a second) at peak.

GPU-seconds per image. Work it from the model. Assume a 2–3-billion-parameter latent diffusion model (SDXL's UNet has 2.6 billion parameters) at 1,024 × 1,024, with classifier-free guidance, in reduced precision on a current data-centre GPU. Measure, do not trust these constants, but suppose one denoising step costs 40 ms per image when four images are batched together (guidance's two evaluations included). Thirty steps is 1.2 s; text encoding is a few tens of milliseconds; the VAE decoder about 0.1 s per image. So about 1.3 GPU-seconds per quality image, and a four-image request occupies one GPU for about 5.2 seconds.

A distilled few-step model changes the arithmetic. Adversarial Diffusion Distillation (the method behind SDXL Turbo) samples in 1–4 steps and does not use classifier-free guidance at inference, which halves the evaluations per step. Four steps at about 20 ms each is 0.08 s, plus 0.1 s of decoding: about 0.2 GPU-seconds per fast image, 0.8 s per four-image request. Notice that decoding is now half the cost; on the fast tier, the decoder and the safety models become worth optimising.

GPU-hours and money. All quality: 20 million × 1.3 = 26 million GPU-seconds a day = 7,200 GPU-hours, about 300 GPUs busy on average. At an assumed $2.50 per GPU-hour, about $18,000 a day, $540,000 a month, $0.0009 per image. With 70% of images on the fast tier: 0.3 × 1.3 + 0.7 × 0.2 = 0.53 GPU-seconds per image, 10.6 million GPU-seconds, about 2,900 GPU-hours and $7,400 a day. The tier mix is the biggest cost lever in the system.

Storage and delivery. A 1,024 × 1,024 image as a high-quality JPEG or WebP is roughly 300 KB. Twenty million a day is 6 TB a day. Keeping unselected images for 30 days and selected ones indefinitely, with most users choosing one of four, the 30-day working set is about 180 TB plus a growing archive: ordinary object storage behind a CDN.

Safety models. A prompt classifier (a small text model, a few milliseconds on CPU or a shared GPU) on 58 requests a second, and an image classifier on every image (say 10 ms of GPU each) at 231 images a second: about 2.3 GPU-seconds of classification per second, roughly three GPUs. Cheap relative to generation, so there is no excuse to skip it.

The insight to say out loud. Every decision in this design is a multiplier on GPU-seconds: steps, guidance, resolution, images per request, tier mix, variations versus fresh generations, and how busy the fleet can safely run. Get those right and the bill halves; get queueing wrong and users wait regardless of how many GPUs you buy.

Three choices matter. Generation is asynchronous: a job ID comes back immediately and progress streams as events, because a 5–15 second synchronous HTTP call is fragile on mobile networks and makes retries dangerous. An idempotency key stops a retried request from generating and charging twice, which matters because users press the button again when waiting. And the response reports GPU-seconds and the model version, so billing, analytics and evaluation see exactly what ran.

4 · Data model

Jobs: job ID, user, tier, prompt (original and rewritten), parameters (resolution, steps, guidance scale, seeds, model version, scheduler), status, timestamps per stage, GPU-seconds, safety decisions with scores. Images: image ID, job, seed, storage key, perceptual hash, watermark ID, manifest ID, safety scores, selected by the user or not, retention class. Manifests: a repository of issued C2PA manifests keyed by watermark ID and image hash, so a stripped copy can be traced. Accounts: credits, limits, risk score, strike history. Ledger: one row per job with GPU-seconds by stage, for cost and capacity analysis. Prompts and images are personal data and possibly sensitive; retention is limited and access to them is logged.

5 · High-level design

The flow is the primer diagram. Admission (stateless API servers) authenticates, applies rate limits and credits, deduplicates by idempotency key and runs the prompt safety step: rules for known-bad terms and a classifier for disguised requests. An optional prompt rewriter, a small language model, expands short prompts into detailed ones the image model follows better ("a fox" becomes a paragraph about composition, lighting and style), applies style presets, and is itself checked by the safety classifier, since a rewriter can introduce problems too. Jobs enter per-tier queues in a durable queue service. GPU workers, each owning one GPU with the model loaded, pull jobs, form small batches (up to four images of the same resolution and model), run text encoding, the denoising loop and decoding, and stream a preview from an intermediate step for the quality tier. Images go to output safety (image classifiers for nudity, violence, minors and real-person likeness, plus hash matching against known abusive content for uploaded inputs and outputs), then provenance (watermark embedding, then C2PA signing), then object storage with CDN URLs. A scheduler watches queue depth and wait times and scales workers; a review service handles blocked items, appeals and reports; the ledger feeds billing and capacity planning.

6 · Deep dive: GPU-seconds per image

The formula. GPU-seconds per image ≈ steps × (time per denoiser evaluation) × (evaluations per step) + text encoding + decoding + safety. Each term has a lever.

Steps. Full models are typically sampled with 20–50 steps using a deterministic solver (DDIM-style samplers, mm5). Fewer steps save linearly but eventually lose detail. Distilled models change the regime: Latent Consistency Models and Adversarial Diffusion Distillation train students that produce good images in 1–4 steps. ADD's paper reports that four steps match SDXL at 512 × 512 in their evaluation, and the student does not use classifier-free guidance at inference. The cost is some loss of diversity and fine control, which is why many products use the distilled model for drafts and the full model for finals.

Guidance. Classifier-free guidance (CFG) evaluates the denoiser twice per step, with and without the prompt, and extrapolates. It is the reason prompts are followed closely, and it doubles the per-step cost. Models with guidance distilled into them, or few-step students without guidance, save that factor of two.

Resolution. The denoiser's cost grows with the number of latent positions: 1,024 × 1,024 has four times the positions of 512 × 512. Convolutions scale roughly linearly with positions, while self-attention layers scale with the square of positions in each attention block, so the total grows somewhat faster than four times. This is why products generate at a base resolution and upscale with a separate, cheaper model, rather than generating huge images directly.

Batching. Running four images together uses the GPU more fully than one at a time, especially at low resolution where a single image does not saturate the hardware; at high resolution the gain is smaller because one image already keeps the GPU busy. Batching also adds waiting: a worker that waits to fill a batch delays the first job. Here the natural batch is the user's own four images, which arrive together, so batching costs no extra wait.

Precision and kernels. Hugging Face's diffusion guide lists the standard levers: load weights in bfloat16 or float16 instead of float32, use scaled dot-product attention (which picks a FlashAttention-style kernel where available), and compile the model with torch.compile. Each is a configuration choice, and together they commonly cut time per step substantially compared with a naive float32 pipeline. Quantised weights and caching parts of the computation between adjacent steps go further, at some quality risk that must be evaluated.

7 · Deep dive: fleet sizing and queueing

Offered load. Little's law gives the average number of GPUs busy: arrival rate × GPU-seconds per job. At peak, 116 quality requests a second × 5.2 s = 603 GPUs of offered load. With exactly 603 GPUs, the queue grows without limit, because any random burst can never be worked off. The question is how many more are needed to keep waits short.

Erlang C. For random (Poisson) arrivals and c identical servers, the Erlang C formula gives the probability that an arriving job finds all servers busy and must wait, P(wait). Given that it waits, the wait is roughly exponential with rate (c − a) / S, where a is the offered load and S the service time, so the probability of waiting longer than t is P(wait) × e−(c − a)t/S. The striking thing is how hot a large pool can run: at 91% utilisation, only 1.3% of jobs wait at all. This is the economy of scale of pooling: with hundreds of servers, the chance that all of them are busy at once is small even when each is busy most of the time.

Fragmentation costs real GPUs. Split the same 660 GPUs into six regional pools of 110, each carrying a sixth of the traffic, and the p99 wait rises from 27 ms to about 1.8 s; to bring the split fleet under the 1 s target needs roughly 690 GPUs. Every split (by region, model version, tenant, resolution) has this cost, so the design keeps one pool per model per region, routes jobs across availability zones, and lets the quality tier borrow idle fast-tier GPUs that have both models loaded.

Where the model lies. Erlang C assumes random arrivals and memoryless service times. Real traffic is burstier: a viral prompt can triple arrivals within minutes, and Monday mornings ramp faster than autoscaling. Service times have tails (large resolutions, upscales). Batching windows and model loading add delay that the formula ignores. So the formula sets the order of magnitude, and a discrete-event simulation driven by real arrival traces sets the final number. In practice teams provision about 10–20% above the Erlang answer for the forecast peak, keep warm spare capacity, and protect latency with admission control.

Admission control and graceful degradation. When the predicted wait for a new job exceeds the tier's limit, accepting it only makes every job late. The scheduler instead degrades in a fixed order: free-tier requests drop to two images, then move to the fast model, then receive a "busy, try again in a minute" response with a retry time; paid quality jobs are protected longest. This keeps the fleet out of the regime where waits grow without bound.

Scaling up quickly. A new GPU worker is useless until its model is loaded: several gigabytes of weights. Keeping weights on local disks of standby nodes, using pre-built container images with the model baked in, and keeping a warm pool of idle-but-loaded workers (paid for, as insurance) bring scale-up from many minutes to tens of seconds.

8 · Deep dive: prompt and output safety

Before the GPU: prompt checks. A rule layer blocks prompts with terms that are never acceptable and normalises tricks (misspellings, spacing, homoglyphs, translations). A text classifier scores categories: sexual content, sexual content involving minors, violence and gore, hate, self-harm, real-person likeness in sexual or defamatory contexts, and brand or copyrighted-character misuse if policy restricts it. Thresholds per category are chosen from precision and recall on labelled prompt sets, with the asymmetry of hd6: for the most severe categories, recall comes first and some over-blocking is accepted. If the rewriter is used, its output is checked too.

After the GPU: output checks. Every generated image is classified for nudity, sexual content, violence, apparent minors and the likeness of known public figures. The combination matters: nudity plus an apparent minor is the highest severity and is blocked and escalated regardless of any score elsewhere. For services that accept uploaded images (image-to-image, inpainting), inputs are hash-matched against databases of known child sexual abuse material and other known abusive images before processing, and outputs are hash-matched too; in the United States, providers who become aware of apparent child sexual abuse material are legally required to report it to NCMEC. Blocked outputs are not delivered; the user sees a policy message; the job, prompt, scores and images are retained in a restricted store for review and legal obligations.

Account-level signals. Determined abusers probe: they try dozens of variants until one passes. Each block adds to a per-account risk score; above a threshold, the account gets slower limits, stricter thresholds, mandatory human review of outputs, or suspension. Velocity on a named real person (many generations of one celebrity's name with suggestive terms) triggers review even if each image passed.

Human review and appeals. A small team reviews samples of blocked and passed items (to measure precision and recall), high-severity escalations and user appeals. Reviewer exposure to disturbing content is limited by policy, with blurring by default and wellbeing support.

9 · Deep dive: provenance with C2PA and watermarks

The manifest. C2PA, the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity's specification (version 2.3 is current at the time of writing; the concepts here are stable across 2.x), defines a manifest: a set of assertions (statements about the asset), wrapped in a claim that is digitally signed by the claim generator, here the image service, using an X.509 certificate. For a generated image, the key assertion is c2pa.actions starting with a c2pa.created action whose digitalSourceType is the IPTC value http://cv.iptc.org/newscodes/digitalsourcetype/trainedAlgorithmicMedia, which declares that the media was created by a trained generative model. The manifest is tied to the file by a hard binding, a cryptographic hash of the asset's bytes, so any change to the file invalidates the signature check. Manifests are embedded in the file (in JPEG, in a container format called JUMBF) and can also be stored externally.

Why metadata is not enough. Many platforms strip metadata on upload, and a screenshot creates a new file with none. The specification addresses this with soft bindings: content identifiers that survive some changes, either an invisible watermark embedded in the pixels or a fingerprint computed from the content. The specification's actions include c2pa.watermarked.bound for a watermark inserted to create a soft binding, and it calls a manifest that can be rediscovered through a soft binding in a manifest repository a durable Content Credential. So the service embeds a watermark carrying an identifier, records that identifier in the manifest, and stores the manifest in a repository; a verifier holding a stripped copy detects the watermark and looks the manifest up.

The order of operations. Watermark first, then sign: the hard binding hashes the final bytes, so embedding a watermark after signing would break the binding. Safety before both, so blocked images are never stamped or stored for delivery. Signing keys live in a hardware security module; the signing service is on the critical path, so it is replicated and its latency (a few milliseconds) budgeted.

Limits to state honestly. Invisible watermarks can be weakened by heavy cropping, resizing, re-encoding, rotation and deliberate removal attacks; detection is probabilistic and has false positives. A missing manifest or watermark proves nothing, because other generators may add neither. Provenance therefore supports, rather than replaces, detection classifiers and policy, and the service publishes a detection endpoint for trust-and-safety partners, with documented error rates.

10 · Caching and variations

Exact caching stores results keyed by everything that determines the output: model version, scheduler, steps, guidance scale, resolution, normalised prompt (after rewriting), seed and batch configuration. It helps in specific cases: featured or template prompts shown on a landing page, re-downloads, and identical requests from many users during a viral trend (where serving the cached set, or a cached set with fresh seeds generated in the background, protects the fleet). It does not help in general, because most prompts are unique and users expect fresh images. Note the determinism caveat: different GPU types and batch compositions can change outputs slightly even with the same seed, so a cache hit is "the images this configuration produced", not a mathematical guarantee.

Cheaper intermediate products. Text-encoder outputs for a prompt can be cached and reused for variations, upscales and retries, saving a few tens of milliseconds per job. More valuable is the structure of variations. A new-seed variation reuses the prompt encoding and costs a full generation otherwise. A subtle variation uses image-to-image: noise the chosen image to strength 0.3 and denoise from there, running only about 30% of the steps (9 of 30), about a third of the cost of a fresh generation, with results that keep the composition. Upscaling uses a dedicated model that is much cheaper per output pixel than generating at the higher resolution. Product design that steers users toward variations and upscales of a chosen image, rather than repeated fresh generations, lowers GPU-seconds per satisfied user.

11 · Abuse and rate limits

Limits are enforced at admission, with a shared counter store (cd12). Concurrency: one or two jobs in flight per user, which stops a script from filling the queue. Credits: a daily allowance per tier, consumed per image weighted by GPU-seconds (a quality image costs more credits than a fast one). Risk-based throttling: new accounts, accounts with recent blocks and traffic from suspicious networks get lower limits and stricter thresholds. Free tier on the cheap model, which also limits the damage of mass-created accounts. Velocity detection on prompt hashes and named targets catches coordinated campaigns. Every limit returns a clear error with a retry time, so honest clients behave well and the system can tell retries from attacks.

12 · Evaluation plan

Offline quality. A fixed, versioned prompt set (a few thousand prompts) by category: people, animals, text in images, counting, spatial relations, styles, edge cases. For each model: prompt adherence with automated checks in the style of GenEval, which uses object detectors to verify object presence, co-occurrence, counts, colours and positions; alignment scores such as CLIPScore, used with care because they reward keyword matching and miss counts and relations; human side-by-side preference on a sample, randomised left and right, judged on adherence and overall quality; and FID only as a coarse distribution check, since it does not measure prompt following.

Offline safety. A red-team prompt set per category, refreshed with real attack patterns from blocked logs, measuring block recall at prompt and output stages; a benign set (including medical, artistic nudity and historical violence where policy allows) measuring over-blocking; output-classifier precision and recall on a labelled image set, especially for apparent minors.

Cost and latency gates. GPU-seconds per image and p95 latency on the production pipeline at production batch sizes. A model that is 3% preferred but 40% more expensive is a business decision, not an automatic win.

Online. A/B tests by user with the share of requests where at least one image is saved, downloaded or used in a design as the primary metric; regenerate rate (a sign of dissatisfaction), time to images, abandonment while waiting, report rate and appeal overturn rate as guardrails.

14 · What changes at 10× scale

At 200 million images a day, the fleet is several thousand GPUs and the economics change. Model efficiency becomes the main project: distillation of the quality model to fewer steps, quantisation, step caching and custom kernels each save hundreds of GPUs. Capacity strategy: reserved capacity covers the daily base; peaks run on burst capacity in several regions, and global routing sends free-tier work to whichever region has spare GPUs, since a few hundred milliseconds of extra network time is invisible next to generation time. Safety at scale: classifiers run on dedicated pools, human review is sampled statistically rather than exhaustive, and abuse detection becomes a graph problem across accounts, payment methods and devices. Provenance volume (200 million signatures a day) requires a horizontally scaled signing service, which is cheap but must never be the reason images are delivered unsigned.

Why large pools can run hot

The intuition that servers must stay at 60–70% utilisation comes from small systems. Waiting happens when all servers are busy at once, and with many servers the number busy fluctuates around the mean by roughly the square root of the mean. A pool with an offered load of 600 GPUs fluctuates by about ±25, so 660 GPUs (two to three standard deviations of headroom) is rarely saturated; a pool with a load of 100 fluctuates by about ±10, so it needs proportionally more headroom. This "square-root staffing" rule, borrowed from call centres, says spare capacity should grow with the square root of the load, not proportionally, which is exactly why consolidation saves so many GPUs and fragmentation wastes them.

Distillation moves cost from inference to training, and changes the product

A few-step student is expensive to train once and cheap forever after, which pays off quickly at millions of images a day. But distillation changes behaviour: adversarial objectives sharpen images and can reduce diversity across seeds, and dropping guidance removes a knob some users rely on (strong prompt adherence at high guidance scales). Products therefore expose tiers rather than silently swapping models, and evaluate diversity explicitly (how different are four images from one prompt?) along with preference.

Decoder and safety costs matter once denoising is cheap

With 30 guided steps, the VAE decoder and safety classifiers are a few per cent of the cost and nobody looks at them. With a four-step student, the decoder can be half of the GPU time per image, and the safety classifier a noticeable share. Optimising them (a lighter decoder, decoding at a lower resolution for previews, batching classifiers across jobs) becomes worthwhile. This is a general pattern: when one stage is optimised by an order of magnitude, the next stage becomes the bottleneck, and profiling has to be repeated.

Safety thresholds interact with tiers and with each other

A prompt classifier tuned for high recall blocks some benign prompts; an output classifier after it sees only what passed, so its measured precision and recall depend on the prompt threshold. Changing one threshold changes the other's operating point, and evaluating each in isolation misleads. The right evaluation runs the full chain on labelled prompt sets and measures end-to-end delivered-harm rate and end-to-end over-blocking, then tunes thresholds jointly. Tiers complicate it further: a paid professional tier may allow artistic nudity that the free tier blocks, so thresholds are per policy profile, and the profile is part of the job record.

Provenance is a trust system, not a feature flag

A C2PA signature is only as meaningful as the trust placed in the signer's certificate and in what the signer claims. A service that signs "created by a trained model" for every image is making an honest claim; one that signs edited real photographs as unedited would be misusing the system. Verifiers check the certificate chain against trust lists, the hard binding against the file and the assertions for consistency. For the service, this means key management (keys in hardware modules, rotation, revocation plans), careful claims (record the model and the operation, not more), and a public explanation of what its credentials mean.

Prompt rewriting is both a quality lever and a risk

Short prompts produce generic images; a language model that expands them into detailed descriptions usually produces images users prefer, and it is a natural place to apply style presets and policy (for example, never adding real names). But the rewriter can introduce content the user did not ask for, bias the output (adding demographic details), or be manipulated into producing disallowed descriptions. So its output passes the same prompt safety check, the user can see and edit the expanded prompt, and its effect is measured in A/B tests like any model change.

Cost per satisfied user, not cost per image

GPU-seconds per image is the engineering metric, but the business pays for outcomes: a user who finds an image they keep. A change that halves the cost per image but doubles the number of regenerations before a user is satisfied saves nothing. So the cost metric that guides product decisions is GPU-seconds per saved or used image, measured in A/B tests. It explains several design choices that look odd from the infrastructure side: offering four images at once (more chance one is right, fewer rounds), investing in prompt rewriting (better first attempts), and making subtle variations prominent (a cheap path from "almost" to "yes"). It also explains why a slightly more expensive model can lower total cost if users settle on its first batch more often.

How it fails in practice

The viral template. A creator shared a prompt template, and requests tripled within ten minutes. Admission control did not exist, so every request was accepted, queue waits climbed past a minute, users retried, and the retries made it worse. The fleet was saturated for an hour while autoscaling, which waited for new nodes to download model weights, added capacity slowly. Afterwards: admission control that degrades free requests first, weights baked into node images with a warm pool, and an automatic exact cache for any prompt template exceeding a request-rate threshold.

The misspelled celebrity. A prompt using a deliberately misspelled celebrity name plus suggestive terms passed the prompt classifier, and the output classifier at the time checked nudity but not real-person likeness, so sexualised images of a real person were delivered and circulated. The fixes: a likeness classifier on outputs combined with the nudity score, normalisation of misspellings in the prompt layer, velocity detection on named people, and the attack added to the red-team set.

The unsigned week. A deployment changed the order of post-processing so that images were resized for the CDN after signing. Every manifest's hard binding failed verification for a week before a partner reported it. Signing is now the last step, a verification check runs on a sample of delivered files continuously, and failures page the on-call engineer.

What to monitor

Per tier: queue depth, wait percentiles, GPU-seconds per job by stage, GPU utilisation, admission rejections and degradations. Fleet: warm spare capacity, scale-up time, worker failures, model versions in service. Safety: prompt and output block rates by category, account strikes, review backlog, appeal overturn rate. Provenance: signing latency and errors, sampled verification failures, watermark detection rate on sampled outputs after common transformations. Product: save and download rate, regenerate rate, time to images, abandonment.

Knobs people tune

Steps and guidance per tier; default resolution and images per request; batch size; fleet size and spare-capacity margin; admission thresholds and degradation order; safety thresholds per category and policy profile; watermark strength; credit weights per operation; cache triggers for viral templates.

The real cases: SDXL, ADD, Diffusers and C2PA

The SDXL report (Podell et al., 2023) describes the kind of model a quality tier serves: a UNet backbone about three times larger than earlier Stable Diffusion versions, 2.6 billion parameters, two text encoders, training around 1,024 × 1,024 pixels with multiple aspect ratios, and an optional refinement stage. Adversarial Diffusion Distillation (Sauer et al., 2023), the method behind SDXL Turbo, shows the fast-tier alternative: 1–4 sampling steps, no classifier-free guidance at inference, outperforming earlier few-step methods in a single step and matching SDXL in four steps at 512 × 512 in the authors' evaluation. Hugging Face's Diffusers documentation on accelerating inference, checked on 10 October 2026, lists the serving levers every pipeline should start from: bfloat16 or float16 weights, scaled dot-product attention with its automatic choice of efficient kernels, and torch.compile, demonstrated on an SDXL pipeline. The C2PA specification (version 2.3 at the time of writing) defines the manifest, the c2pa.created action with digitalSourceType trainedAlgorithmicMedia for generated media, hard bindings by hash, soft bindings by watermark or fingerprint, the c2pa.watermarked.bound action, and the idea of a durable Content Credential that can be rediscovered through a soft binding. The GPU-seconds and fleet numbers in this lesson are worked from stated assumptions; the method (measure the pipeline, compute offered load, size with Erlang C, validate by simulation) is what transfers.

Goal: build a discrete-event GPU queue simulator, check it against the Erlang C numbers in section 7, see how much worse split pools and traffic bursts are, and find the fleet size that meets a 1-second p99 wait. Python 3.12, standard library only.

  1. Save as gpu_queue.py and run python gpu_queue.py.
  2. Read the first table: one pool, Poisson arrivals at 116 requests a second, 5.2 GPU-seconds each, for several fleet sizes. The p99 waits should be close to the Erlang C table (the service times here vary a little, so not identical).
  3. Read the second table: the same total GPUs as six independent pools. Waits are far longer at the same utilisation.
  4. Read the third table: one pool with a burst (arrivals tripled for 60 seconds). Find how many GPUs keep p99 under 1 s, and compare with how many you would rather protect with admission control.
  5. Change SERVICE_S to 0.8 (the fast tier) and rerun.
"""GPU queue simulator: FCFS, c identical GPUs. Python 3.12, stdlib only."""
import heapq, math, random
RATE, SERVICE_S, SIM_S = 116.0, 5.2, 900          # requests/s, GPU-s per request, seconds
def arrivals(rate, seconds, rng, burst=None):
    t, out = 0.0, []
    while t < seconds:
        r = rate * (3 if burst and burst[0] <= t < burst[1] else 1)
        t += rng.expovariate(r); out.append(t)
    return out
def run(times, c, rng):
    free = [0.0] * c; heapq.heapify(free); waits = []; busy = 0.0
    for t in times:                                 # FCFS: earliest-free GPU takes the job
        f = heapq.heappop(free); start = max(t, f)
        s = SERVICE_S * math.exp(rng.gauss(0, 0.15) - 0.15**2 / 2)
        waits.append(start - t); busy += s; heapq.heappush(free, start + s)
    waits.sort(); n = len(waits)
    q = lambda p: waits[min(n - 1, int(p * n))]
    return q(0.5), q(0.95), q(0.99), busy / (c * times[-1])
def table(title, c_list, pools=1, burst=None):
    print(f"\n{title}\n  GPUs   util    p50     p95     p99   (seconds of wait)")
    for c in c_list:
        rng = random.Random(1); res = []
        for _ in range(pools):                     # each pool gets 1/pools of the traffic
            res.append(run(arrivals(RATE / pools, SIM_S, rng, burst), c // pools, rng))
        p50, p95, p99 = (max(r[i] for r in res) for i in range(3))
        util = sum(r[3] for r in res) / pools
        print(f"  {c:5} {util:5.1%} {p50:7.2f} {p95:7.2f} {p99:7.2f}")
table("One pool, Poisson", [610, 620, 640, 660, 700])
table("Six pools, same total (worst pool shown)", [612, 630, 642, 660, 690], pools=6)
table("One pool, 3x burst for 60 s", [660, 800, 1000, 1400], burst=(300, 360))
print(f"\nOffered load = {RATE * SERVICE_S:.0f} GPUs")

What to look for: waits near zero at 660 GPUs in one pool but seconds of p99 wait at the same total split six ways; and in the burst table, that riding out a tripling with capacity alone needs far more GPUs than the steady state, which is why admission control and degradation are cheaper than buying for the spike.

Estimate it

Your product moves from four images per request to two by default, and from 30 to 20 guided steps on the quality tier, keeping 5 million requests a day, a peak of twice the average, 40 ms per guided step per image and 0.1 s decoding per image. (a) GPU-seconds per request and offered load at peak? (b) Daily GPU-hours and cost at $2.50 an hour, all quality? (c) Roughly how many GPUs at peak for a sub-second p99 wait in one pool?

Answer

(a) Per image: 20 × 0.04 + 0.1 = 0.9 GPU-seconds; per two-image request 1.8 GPU-seconds. Peak arrivals: 5,000,000 / 86,400 × 2 ≈ 116 requests a second, so offered load = 116 × 1.8 ≈ 209 GPUs.

(b) 10 million images × 0.9 = 9 million GPU-seconds = 2,500 GPU-hours a day, about $6,250 a day, a third of the original $18,000 (half from fewer images, the rest from fewer steps).

(c) With an offered load near 209, square-root staffing suggests headroom of a few times √209 ≈ 14, so about 230–240 GPUs keeps waits short in one pool (Erlang C or the lab's simulator gives the exact figure), plus 10–20% for burstiness. Check that the product change does not raise regenerations: if users now generate twice as often to see four options, the saving disappears, which is why such changes are A/B tested on cost per satisfied user, not cost per request.

Diagnose it

After a model update, output-level blocks for "sexual content" fall from 0.8% to 0.3% of images, prompt-level blocks are unchanged, and user reports of explicit images rise threefold. What happened, and what do you check?

Answer

Fewer blocks plus more reports means the output classifier is missing more, not that the model is safer. Likely causes: the new model's images differ in style or distribution from what the output classifier was trained on (a classic distribution shift), so its scores dropped below threshold for images people still find explicit; or the post-processing order changed and the classifier now sees a downscaled preview. Check: run the labelled image set and a sample of reported images through the classifier and compare score distributions between old and new model outputs; confirm which image the classifier receives; review a stratified sample of passed images by hand. Fixes: recalibrate or retrain the classifier on the new model's outputs before full rollout, temporarily lower the threshold, and add "output-classifier recall on the new model's images" to the model release gate so a model update cannot ship without its safety classifier being re-validated.
