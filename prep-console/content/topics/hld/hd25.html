A shopper sees a jacket on someone in a café and wants one like it. Typing a description fails: "green jacket with big pockets and a hood" returns ten thousand jackets, none quite right. A marketplace with a billion product images could find it from a photo, if it had visual search. A second shopper photographs their living room and wants the lamp in the corner, the rug and the side table, each one matched to products for sale: "shop the look".

A first attempt is simple: take a pretrained image model, compute a feature vector for every catalogue image, and return the nearest vectors to the query photo's vector. In testing with catalogue photos as queries it looks magical. With real photos it falls apart. The photo of the café jacket returns pictures of cafés, because the model encodes the whole scene. A phone photo in dim light of a white sneaker returns grey sneakers on white backgrounds, because the catalogue's studio lighting dominates. Nearest-neighbour search over a billion vectors of 2,048 floats needs 8 TB of memory and takes seconds per query. Items that sold an hour ago keep appearing, because the index is rebuilt weekly. And when the team upgrades the image model, results become random, because the new query vectors are compared with old catalogue vectors.

The senior design question is to build visual search properly: detection and cropping so queries are about objects, not scenes; embedding models trained for the gap between street photos and catalogue shots; queries that mix image and text ("this, but in red"); an index that fits a billion vectors in memory with product quantisation, worked out by hand; freshness for a catalogue that changes by millions of listings a day; re-ranking with business signals so the first result is something you can actually buy; a latency budget of a few hundred milliseconds; and metrics that separate "the index works" from "the results are right" from "people buy things".

The lesson follows the interview: requirements; estimates for traffic, compute and especially index memory; the API; the high-level design; deep dives on detection and embeddings, product quantisation and index sizing, multimodal queries, freshness and model upgrades, and re-ranking; the latency budget; evaluation; failure modes; and what changes at ten times the scale. The real cases are eBay's and Pinterest's published visual search systems, and the Faiss library's documentation for index choices.

What the system does

Visual search takes an image as the query and returns catalogue items that look like what is in it. The core trick, used by every system of this kind, is an embedding: a model turns each image (or each object cut out of an image) into a vector of a few hundred numbers, trained so that pictures of the same or similar products land close together. Searching then becomes finding the nearest vectors among a billion, which an approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN) index does in milliseconds by examining only a tiny fraction of them. Around that core sit a detector that finds objects in the query, a ranking stage that brings in price, stock and quality, and pipelines that keep a fast-changing catalogue searchable.

The mental model: a librarian who remembers faces

Imagine a vast warehouse of products and a librarian who, instead of reading labels, has memorised a compact "face" for every item: a short description in their own private code that captures shape, colour, pattern and style. When you hand them a photo, they first look at what you are pointing at (not the café behind it), form the same kind of compact face for it, and walk straight to the aisle where similar faces are stored, comparing only the items there. Then, from the twenty closest, they pick out the ones actually in stock, at sensible prices, from reliable sellers.

The detector is looking at what you are pointing at. The embedding is the compact face. The IVF index is the aisles: vectors are grouped into clusters and only the nearest clusters are searched. Product quantisation is the librarian's shorthand, which lets a billion faces fit in memory at the cost of some precision. Refinement is taking a closer look at the top few with the full picture. Re-ranking is preferring what you can actually buy. The analogy breaks in one useful place: the librarian's code is learned from data, and if it was learned mostly from studio photos it will describe your dim phone photo badly, no matter how good the aisle system is. Index quality and embedding quality are separate problems.

Three facts that shape the design

First, the query and the catalogue look different: phone photos of objects in the world against studio images on white, so the embedding must be trained for that gap and the detector must isolate objects. Second, memory decides the index: a billion vectors is the scale where uncompressed storage costs terabytes of RAM, so compression and its accuracy loss are the central trade-off. Third, the catalogue never stops changing: on a marketplace, listings appear and sell every minute (eBay's paper calls this volatile inventory), so the index must absorb changes without full rebuilds. A reader who stops here has the shape: detect, embed, search a compressed sharded index with a fresh delta and tombstones, refine, re-rank with business signals, and evaluate the index, the embedding and the business outcome separately.

1 · Requirements

Functional. Search by photo (camera or upload) across a catalogue of 300 million active listings with about 1 billion images; detect multiple objects in a photo and let the user choose one or browse all ("shop the look"); refine with text ("in red", "under $50") and filters (category, price, size, location); "more like this" from any listing image; results show available listings only. Out of scope, said aloud: visual search inside video, and duplicate-listing detection for trust and safety (related, but a separate pipeline).

Latency targets. Server-side p50 under 150 ms and p95 under 300 ms from image received to results returned; the client resizes the photo to about 512 pixels on the long side before upload so upload time stays small.

Freshness targets. New listings searchable within 5 minutes; sold or removed listings gone from results within 1 minute.

Quality targets. On labelled street-to-shop pairs, exact-product recall@10 (the right product among the top ten) at least 60% in fashion and home; on labelled similarity judgements, precision@10 at least 80%; online, a measurable lift in purchases per search over text search for visual categories.

Cost targets. Index serving in RAM affordable per replica (well under a terabyte); re-embedding the full catalogue achievable within a day.

Clarifying questions. "How many images and how fast does the catalogue change?" "Which categories matter most?" "Is there a text search we can blend with?" "Do sellers upload studio photos or phone photos?" "What does success mean: clicks, purchases, or fewer failed text searches?"

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Traffic. 20 million visual searches a day is about 230 a second on average; with an evening peak about three times the average, 700 queries a second. Each query may yield several crops, but the user usually searches one; assume 1.3 embeddings and index searches per query on average.

Query compute. Detection at about 25 ms and embedding at about 10 ms per crop on a GPU (batched across concurrent queries) is roughly 40 ms of GPU time per query, so 700 × 0.04 ≈ 28 GPUs busy at peak, about 40 with headroom. Small next to the index serving, but enough to need batching and autoscaling.

Index memory, the estimate that shapes the design. One billion vectors at 256 dimensions:

The HNSW figure uses the Faiss wiki's formula, (d × 4 + M × 2 × 4) bytes per vector. The choice is IVF-PQ with 64-byte codes: 72 GB plus about 1 GB of IVF centroids (a million lists × 256 dimensions × 4 bytes) fits comfortably in one large server's memory per replica, where HNSW at 1.28 TB would need a dozen. The cost of compression is accuracy, recovered by refining the top candidates with float16 vectors: 512 GB in total, kept on local NVMe SSDs across the shards.

Ingestion. 10 million new or changed listing images a day is about 116 a second; detection plus embedding at about 35 ms of GPU each is about 4 GPUs. A full re-embed when the model changes is the big one: 1 billion images × about 3 ms each (embedding only, crops cached) = 3 million GPU-seconds, about 830 GPU-hours, a day on 35 GPUs. That number decides how often you can afford to change models.

The insight to say out loud. Compute is modest; memory and freshness are the hard constraints, and embedding quality decides whether any of it is useful. Product quantisation makes a billion vectors fit; the delta index and tombstones make it fresh; the training data makes it right.

4 · Data model

Catalogue images: image ID, listing ID, URL, detected objects with boxes and labels, embedding per object for the current model version, perceptual hash for near-duplicate detection, predicted attributes (category, colour, pattern, material), timestamps. Listings (from the marketplace): status, price, seller, location, attributes the seller entered, engagement statistics. Index versions: model version, codebooks, list centroids, shard layout, build time, the set of image IDs included. Tombstones: image IDs removed since the last build, replicated to every shard. Query logs: query embedding (or a hash of it), crops, results shown, clicks and purchases, for training and evaluation, with retention limits because user photos are personal data.

5 · High-level design

The query path: an API layer receives the image and runs decode and resize; the detection service (GPU, batched) returns boxes; the selected crop goes to the embedding service (GPU, batched), which returns a normalised 256-dimensional vector; the search broker fans out to all index shards in parallel; each shard searches its main IVF-PQ index and its delta index, removes tombstoned and filtered-out items, refines its top candidates with float16 vectors from local SSD, and returns its best 200; the broker merges to the top 500; the re-ranker fetches business features from a feature store and scores the candidates with a learning-to-rank model; a diversity step removes near-duplicates; results return. The ingestion path listens to listing events, fetches images, runs the same detection and embedding models in batch, writes vectors to the shards' delta indexes and attributes to the feature store, and sends deletions to the tombstone set. A nightly index builder produces a new main index per shard, and a model lifecycle pipeline handles re-embedding when the embedding model changes.

6 · Deep dive: detection, cropping and embeddings

Detection first. A user's photo of an outfit contains several products and a lot of background. An object detector (mm10) returns a box, label and confidence for each product it finds. Pinterest's visual discovery paper describes the progression from whole-image features to detected objects, and the payoff: by indexing objects rather than whole images, Lens could find objects within images; their SSD-based detector ran at 290 × 290 pixels in 93 ms on an older AWS GPU (an NVIDIA GRID K520), which shows that detection fits an interactive budget even on modest hardware. Crops get a margin of about 10% so the embedding sees the object's edges, and very small or low-confidence detections are dropped.

Index objects, not just images. The same detector runs on catalogue images at ingestion, so a listing photo of a model wearing a jacket, jeans and boots yields three searchable objects, each mapped back to the listing (and flagged as primary or secondary, since the listing sells the jacket, not the boots). Query crops then match catalogue crops: like with like.

Embeddings that bridge the domain gap. A generic pretrained model encodes everything in a photo: lighting, background, camera angle. What matters is the product's identity and style. The embedding model is therefore fine-tuned with metric learning: training pairs of a user photo (or crop) and the matching catalogue image are pulled together, and other products pushed apart, with a contrastive loss of the kind CLIP and two-tower models use (mm2, rec4). Hard negatives (same category and colour, different product) teach the fine distinctions that decide exact-match search. Pairs come from several sources: user uploads followed by a purchase, sellers' multiple photos of one item, and augmentation that puts catalogue images on varied backgrounds with varied lighting. Category matters: the features that distinguish two sofas differ from those that distinguish two watches, so a shared backbone with category-specific heads, or category-specific models for the biggest categories, is common.

eBay's design as a contrast. eBay's 2017 system used one modified ResNet-50 with two output streams: one predicts the listing's category, the other produces a 4,096-bit binary hash (a sigmoid layer thresholded at 0.5), with both trained to predict category so the hash carries semantic information. Search was limited to the top predicted categories, then ranked by Hamming distance between binary hashes (the count of differing bits), then re-ranked by agreement between predicted aspects (colour, brand, style) and the listing's aspects. Each hash is 512 bytes, keeping storage under 100 GB for 200 million images. It shows the same three ideas this design uses (narrow the search, compress the vectors, re-rank with attributes) in a different form.

7 · Deep dive: product quantisation and index sizing for 1B vectors

Product quantisation, step by step. Take the 256-dimensional embedding and split it into m = 64 sub-vectors of 4 dimensions each. For each of the 64 sub-spaces, run k-means on a training sample to learn a codebook of 256 centroids. To encode a vector, replace each sub-vector with the index (0–255, one byte) of its nearest centroid: 64 bytes instead of 1,024. The codebooks themselves are tiny: 64 × 256 × 4 floats = 256 KB. To search, use asymmetric distance computation (ADC), introduced in Jégou, Douze and Schmid's 2011 paper: the query stays uncompressed; compute a table of distances from each query sub-vector to each of that sub-space's 256 centroids (16,384 numbers), and then the approximate distance to any database vector is the sum of 64 table lookups indexed by its codes. No database vector is ever decompressed.

IVF on top. Scanning a billion codes per query, even at 64 lookups each, is too slow. An inverted file (IVF) first clusters the vectors into lists with a coarse quantiser; each vector is stored in the list of its nearest centroid (in practice PQ encodes the residual, the vector minus its list centroid, which is more accurate). A query finds its nearest nprobe lists and scans only those. The Faiss wiki's guidance for 100 million to 1 billion vectors is IVF1048576_HNSW32: about a million lists (220), with the list centroids themselves searched by a small HNSW graph because a million centroids is too many to compare exhaustively. With a billion vectors in a million lists, each list holds about 1,000 vectors; probing 64 lists scans about 64,000 codes, roughly 4 MB of data and a couple of milliseconds of CPU in total, split across shards.

Refinement. PQ distances are approximate, so the true nearest neighbour may rank 30th. Each shard takes its top 1,000 by PQ distance and recomputes exact distances with float16 vectors (512 bytes each) read from local SSD: 1,000 random reads, issued in parallel, take a few milliseconds on NVMe. The Faiss paper calls this pattern refining with a second, more accurate index. It typically recovers most of the recall lost to compression.

Sharding and replication. The 72 GB index plus delta and tombstones is split into four shards by vector (each holds a quarter of the catalogue), about 19 GB each, so each shard scans a quarter of the probed codes and a failure affects a quarter of the catalogue rather than all of it. Every query fans out to all four shards; the broker waits for all, with a timeout after which it returns results from the shards that answered (a partial result is better than none). Each shard is replicated three times across availability zones. At 700 queries a second with 1.3 searches each, each shard handles about 900 searches a second, spread over three replicas: comfortable for a many-core server, leaving headroom for a zone loss.

Tuning the knobs. nprobe, the number of candidates refined and the PQ code size trade recall against latency and memory. They are tuned by measuring ANN recall@100 against exact search on a sample of real queries (exact search over a billion vectors is slow but fine offline on a sample), choosing the cheapest setting that keeps ANN recall above, say, 95%.

8 · Deep dive: multimodal queries

"This dress, but in red", "similar sofa under $500", "this lamp in brass": users want to modify a visual query with words. Three approaches, in order of effort.

Attribute filters from text. Parse the text for attributes the catalogue knows (colour, material, price, size) and apply them as filters on the visual search: search by the dress image within colour = red. Precise when the attribute is reliable (seller-entered or predicted with high confidence), useless for vague modifiers ("more casual"). Filters interact with ANN indexes: a selective filter can leave too few results among the probed lists, so the shard increases nprobe or falls back to scanning the filtered subset when it is small (the filtered-search problem in detail is in hd26).

Embedding arithmetic in a joint space. If the image embedding lives in a joint image-text space (a CLIP-style model, mm2), combine the normalised image vector with a weighted text vector for "red dress" and search with the sum. It needs no new model and handles vaguer modifiers, but it is approximate: the sum can drift toward "anything red". The weight is tuned on labelled examples.

Composed retrieval models. Train a model on triplets (reference image, modification text, target image) to produce one query vector from both inputs. It handles the modification properly but needs triplet data, which is scarce; marketplaces can mine it from sessions where a user searched with an image and then clicked a variant.

A practical design uses the attribute route for clear structured modifiers, embedding arithmetic for the rest, and measures both on a labelled set of modified queries.

9 · Deep dive: freshness and model upgrades

Additions. When a listing is created, the ingestion pipeline fetches its images, detects and embeds them in micro-batches (a few seconds of batching for GPU efficiency), and inserts the vectors into the owning shard's delta index: a small in-memory index (flat or HNSW, since it holds at most a day's additions, about 10 million vectors across shards, 2.5 million per shard) searched in parallel with the main index on every query. Its results merge with the main index's before refinement. New listings are searchable within minutes.

Deletions. Sold, ended or removed listings must disappear quickly, because showing sold items is the most visible freshness failure. IVF-PQ lists are packed arrays that are expensive to edit, so deletions go to a tombstone set (a hash set or bitmap of image IDs) replicated to every shard and checked when candidates are collected. A deletion takes effect within seconds. Items that change (new price, new photo) are a deletion plus an addition.

Rebuilds. Nightly, an index builder produces a new main index per shard from the full current catalogue: assign vectors to lists, encode with the current codebooks, write the files, and swap them in atomically on each replica, after which the delta and tombstones are reset. Codebooks and list centroids are retrained occasionally (monthly, or when monitoring shows the distribution has drifted, for example after a new category launch), since the Faiss paper notes that techniques fitted to the data distribution, including IVF and PQ, degrade when the distribution changes significantly.

Model upgrades. A new embedding model produces vectors in a new space; mixing them with the old index produces nonsense. The upgrade is a migration: re-embed the catalogue in batch (about 830 GPU-hours), train new codebooks, build a complete new index version alongside the old, dual-write new listings to both, evaluate offline, A/B test with query embedding and index switched together by version, then cut over and keep the old version briefly for rollback. Because this is expensive, teams batch model improvements into a few upgrades a year, or train new models to be backward compatible with old embeddings so they can be mixed during a gradual migration.

10 · Deep dive: re-ranking with business signals

Retrieval returns 500 candidates ordered by visual distance. Ranking decides what the user sees, and visual similarity is only one input. The re-ranker is a learning-to-rank model (hd9), often gradient-boosted trees for speed, trained on logged searches with clicks and purchases as labels, using features: visual similarity (after refinement), category agreement between the query's predicted category and the listing's, attribute agreement (predicted colour, pattern and brand against the listing's), price and price relative to the category median, in-stock status and quantity, seller rating and return rate, shipping location and estimated delivery time, listing freshness, and historical click-through and conversion for the listing. eBay's paper describes a hand-weighted version of attribute agreement: predicted aspects that match the listing's aspects earn reward points, with larger weights for size, brand and price. Learned ranking generalises that idea.

Diversity. Marketplaces have many near-identical listings (a seller lists the same item in ten sizes with the same photo; several sellers use the manufacturer's image). Without a diversity step, the top ten can be ten copies of one product. A perceptual hash groups near-duplicate images, and the ranker keeps the best listing from each group (or groups them in the interface).

Position bias and feedback loops. Training on clicks teaches the ranker what was shown, not what was best. Logged position features at training time (set to a constant at serving), occasional randomisation of lower positions and labelled evaluation sets counter the loop, as in hd9.

12 · Evaluation plan

Three layers, measured separately. Index fidelity: ANN recall@100 of the production index against exact search with the same embedding, on a sample of real query embeddings; this catches index regressions (bad codebooks, wrong nprobe) independently of model quality. Relevance: on labelled sets, exact-product recall@10 for street-to-shop pairs (did the purchased product appear?), and similar-product precision@10 from human raters judging "would this satisfy the shopper?", by category, by query type (camera photo, screenshot, catalogue image) and by detected object size. Business impact: online A/B tests by user, with purchases or add-to-cart per visual search as the primary metric, and click-through, zero-click rate, follow-up text searches (a sign the visual search failed), latency and complaint rate as guardrails.

Report by category. Visual search helps some categories far more than others. Pinterest reported that adding visual features to Related Pins increased engagement by 3.2% across 5 million query Pins and 4.0% when expanded to 100 million, with the largest gains in visual categories (art 8.8%, tattoos 8.0%, illustrations 7.9%) and the smallest in text-driven ones (quotes 2.0%, fitness planning 0.2%). An average hides where the system works and where it does not.

Freshness metrics. Time from listing creation to searchable (p50, p99), share of results that are sold or removed at click time (should be near zero), delta index size before rebuild.

14 · What changes at 10× scale

At 10 billion images, IVF-PQ at 72 bytes per vector is 720 GB per replica, so the index spreads over many more shards, or moves to a disk-resident graph index such as DiskANN (hd26), which keeps compressed vectors in RAM and the graph and full vectors on SSD. Re-embedding becomes a week of hundreds of GPUs, so backward-compatible embedding training becomes essential and model upgrades rarer. Query traffic at 7,000 a second makes GPU batching for detection and embedding a serious fleet, and caching embeddings for repeated query images (screenshots of popular products are shared widely) pays off. And multimodal queries are likely to shift toward a single joint model for images and text, so text search and visual search can share one index and one ranker.

Why PQ works: dimensions are not equally informative, and codebooks adapt

Product quantisation assumes each sub-vector can be summarised by one of 256 representatives without losing much. That holds when the sub-spaces capture roughly independent, balanced parts of the variance. When variance is concentrated in a few dimensions, some codebooks waste their 256 slots on noise while others are starved. OPQ (optimised product quantisation) learns a rotation of the vector space before splitting it, spreading variance evenly across sub-spaces; the Faiss paper describes it as a preprocessing step that makes PQ more accurate when the data's variance sits in a few components. For embeddings from models trained with normalised outputs, the gain is often modest; for raw CNN features it can be large. It is cheap to try and should be measured on recall.

Residual encoding is why IVF-PQ beats PQ alone

Encoding a vector directly with PQ spends the codebooks on describing where the vector is in the whole space. In IVF-PQ, the coarse quantiser already says which of a million regions the vector is in, so PQ encodes only the residual, the small offset from the region's centroid. Residuals are smaller and more uniform than raw vectors, so the same 64 bytes describe them more accurately. This is also why more lists improve accuracy as well as speed, up to the point where the coarse search itself becomes the bottleneck, which is where the HNSW coarse quantiser comes in.

Detection quality silently caps search quality

If the detector misses the object the user cares about, or boxes it badly, the best embedding cannot help, and the failure looks like bad search rather than bad detection. Detection must therefore be evaluated on user photos specifically (not catalogue images, where objects are centred and isolated), with recall of the products users actually tapped, and the interface should let users adjust the crop. A useful diagnostic is to compare search success when users accept the automatic crop against when they adjust it: a large gap means the detector, not the embedding, is the bottleneck.

Binary codes versus PQ is a speed-accuracy-simplicity choice

eBay's 4,096-bit binary hashes compare with Hamming distance, which modern CPUs compute with a few instructions per 64 bits, and need no codebooks, so they are simple and very fast. But at 512 bytes per image they are eight times larger than 64-byte PQ codes, and binary codes generally preserve distances less finely for the same size. eBay compensated by searching only within the top predicted categories, which shrinks the scan dramatically. PQ with refinement gives better accuracy per byte; binary codes give simplicity and speed. Newer quantisers such as RaBitQ, documented in the Faiss wiki at about one bit per dimension plus a small overhead, revisit the binary idea with better accuracy.

Embedding versioning is a data-management problem

Every stored embedding is tied to a model version, and everything downstream (indexes, caches, deduplication, recommendation features built on the same embeddings) inherits that version. Teams that treat embeddings as plain columns discover during the first upgrade that a dozen systems quietly depend on the old space. The fix is organisational as much as technical: an embedding registry recording which systems consume which version, dual-writing during migrations, and deprecation dates. Backward-compatible training (adding a loss that keeps new embeddings comparable to old ones) trades a little accuracy for the ability to upgrade incrementally.

Visual search and text search should share a ranker

Users move between modes: photo, then text refinement, then filters. If visual search and text search have different rankers and different notions of relevance, results jump around confusingly. Sharing the ranking stage (the same learning-to-rank model with query-type features) and the same candidate features means business rules, diversity and personalisation behave consistently, and improvements to the ranker benefit both. Joint image-text embedding models make the convergence natural: a single index can serve image queries, text queries and combined ones.

Narrowing the search is a recall bet

Every shortcut that searches less (probing fewer IVF lists, searching only the top predicted categories as eBay did, filtering by attributes parsed from text) is a bet that the right answer lies inside the narrowed space. When the bet loses, the item is not ranked low; it is absent, and no ranker can recover it. The defence is to measure each narrowing step's own recall on labelled data (how often is the purchased product inside the top three predicted categories?) and to hedge: search the top few categories rather than one, raise nprobe for queries whose nearest lists are almost equally close, and fall back to a wider search when the narrowed one returns weak scores.

How it fails in practice

The half-migrated model. During an embedding-model upgrade, the query service was deployed with the new model a day before the index swap completed on one of four shards. Results from that shard were essentially random and, merged with the others, quietly degraded quality for 25% of candidates. No error fired. The fix: every shard reports its index version, the broker rejects shards whose version differs from the query embedding's, and dashboards show version per shard.

Sold-out first results. After a traffic peak, the tombstone replication stream lagged by twenty minutes, and the top results for popular products were listings that had sold out. Click-to-dead-listing rate jumped from 0.1% to 4%. Tombstone lag is now an alerting metric with a one-minute threshold, and the re-ranker also checks live availability from the listing service for the top twenty results.

The background bias. A new category (home décor) launched with an embedding fine-tuned mostly on fashion pairs. Searches for lamps returned lamps photographed in similar rooms rather than similar lamps. Offline ANN recall was excellent; relevance on a newly labelled décor set was poor. Fine-tuning with décor street-to-shop pairs and object-level crops fixed it, and category-level relevance became part of the release gate.

What to monitor

Query path: latency by stage and by shard, timeouts and partial results, GPU batch sizes and queueing, detection counts per image. Index: ANN recall on a fixed query sample after every rebuild, delta size, tombstone count and replication lag, memory per shard, version per shard. Quality: daily sampled relevance judgements by category, click-through, purchases per search, follow-up text searches, click-to-dead-listing rate. Ingestion: time to searchable, embedding failures, backlog.

Knobs people tune

nprobe and refinement depth per category; PQ code size; delta index size before forced rebuild; tombstone check strategy; detector confidence threshold and crop margin; weight of text in embedding arithmetic; re-ranker features and diversity thresholds; GPU batch window.

The real cases: eBay and Pinterest

eBay's "Visual Search at eBay" (Yang et al., KDD 2017) describes a system built for a marketplace whose inventory is volatile: items are listed and sold every minute. A single modified ResNet-50 predicts category and produces a 4,096-bit binary semantic hash in one forward pass; search is limited to the top predicted categories, ranked by Hamming distance and re-ranked by aspect agreement (with more weight on size, brand and price); each hash takes 512 bytes, keeping storage under 100 GB for 200 million images. It powered visual search in eBay ShopBot and Close5. Pinterest's "Visual Discovery at Pinterest" (Zhai et al., WWW 2017) traces Related Pins (2014), Similar Looks (2015), Flashlight (2016) and Lens (2017); applies object detection to billions of images so that objects rather than whole images are indexed; reports an SSD detector at 290 × 290 in 93 ms on an NVIDIA GRID K520; and measures engagement gains from visual features of 3.2% on 5 million query Pins and 4.0% on 100 million, strongest in visual categories. Both papers predate today's joint image-text models, but their architecture (detect, embed, compress, narrow, re-rank with attributes, measure engagement by category) is the template this design follows. The index arithmetic uses the Faiss wiki's published memory formula for HNSW, its PQ code sizes and its recommendation of an IVF with about a million lists for 100 million to 1 billion vectors.

Goal: build the index memory calculator behind section 2: bytes per vector and total memory for flat, float16, HNSW, binary and IVF-PQ options; shard and replica counts for a given server size; and the GPU-hours of a full re-embed. Python 3.12, standard library only.

  1. Save as pq_memory.py and run python pq_memory.py.
  2. Read the memory table for 1 billion 256-dimensional vectors and confirm the lesson's numbers: 1,024 GB float32, 1,280 GB HNSW at M = 32, and about 73 GB for PQ64 with IDs once the coarse centroids (about 1 GB) are added.
  3. Read the shard plan: how many 256 GB servers each option needs per replica, and in total with three replicas.
  4. Change D to 768 (a typical text-embedding size) and see HNSW grow to over 3 TB while PQ64 stays at 72 GB: PQ's size depends on the code length, not the dimension.
  5. Change N to 10 billion and read the re-embed estimate; then halve MS_PER_IMAGE to see why faster embedding models make upgrades practical.
"""Vector index memory, shard and re-embed calculator. Python 3.12, stdlib only."""
import math
N, D = 1_000_000_000, 256            # vectors, dimensions
ID_BYTES, RAM_GB, USABLE, REPLICAS = 8, 256, 0.7, 3
NLIST, MS_PER_IMAGE, GPUS = 1 << 20, 3.0, 35

def options(d):
    return {
        "float32 flat":       4 * d,
        "float16 flat":       2 * d,
        "SQ8 (1 byte/dim)":   d,
        "HNSW M=32 (fp32)":   4 * d + 32 * 2 * 4,      # Faiss wiki: d*4 + M*2*4
        "binary 4096-bit":    4096 // 8,
        "IVF-PQ32 + id":      32 + ID_BYTES,
        "IVF-PQ64 + id":      64 + ID_BYTES,
        "IVF-PQ64 + id + fp16 on SSD": 64 + ID_BYTES,    # RAM only; SSD listed below
    }
print(f"N = {N:,} vectors, d = {D}\n")
print(f"{'option':30} {'B/vec':>7} {'RAM GB':>9} {'servers/replica':>16} {'total':>6}")
for name, b in options(D).items():
    gb = N * b / 1e9
    if "IVF" in name: gb += NLIST * D * 4 / 1e9          # coarse centroids
    per = math.ceil(gb / (RAM_GB * USABLE))
    print(f"{name:30} {b:7} {gb:9,.0f} {per:16} {per * REPLICAS:6}")
print(f"\nfp16 vectors for refinement on SSD: {N * 2 * D / 1e9:,.0f} GB")
pq_m = 64; sub = D // pq_m
print(f"PQ{pq_m}: {pq_m} sub-vectors of {sub} dims, codebooks "
      f"{pq_m * 256 * sub * 4 / 1024:.0f} KB, lookup table {pq_m * 256:,} entries per query")
per_list = N / NLIST
for nprobe in (16, 64, 256):
    print(f"nprobe {nprobe:4}: scans ~{nprobe * per_list:,.0f} codes "
          f"(~{nprobe * per_list * pq_m / 1e6:.1f} MB) per query")
gpu_h = N * MS_PER_IMAGE / 1000 / 3600
print(f"\nFull re-embed: {gpu_h:,.0f} GPU-hours = {gpu_h / GPUS:.1f} h on {GPUS} GPUs")

What to look for: a sixteen-fold gap between float32 and PQ64 and nearly eighteen-fold against HNSW; PQ memory independent of dimension; the scan size growing linearly with nprobe (which is why recall and latency trade off); and a re-embed measured in hundreds of GPU-hours, the hidden cost of every embedding-model change.

Estimate it

A furniture marketplace has 200 million images, embeds them at 512 dimensions, and serves 300 visual queries a second at peak. (a) Memory for float32, HNSW at M = 16, and IVF-PQ with 32-byte codes plus 8-byte IDs? (b) Which would you choose for servers with 256 GB of RAM, and how would you shard and replicate? (c) How long would a re-embed take on 20 GPUs at 4 ms per image?

Answer

(a) float32: 512 × 4 = 2,048 bytes × 200 million = 410 GB. HNSW at M = 16: 2,048 + 16 × 2 × 4 = 2,176 bytes, 435 GB. IVF-PQ32 + ID: 40 bytes, 8 GB (plus centroids: with 262,144 lists, the Faiss suggestion for 10–100 million scaled up, about 0.5 GB).

(b) IVF-PQ fits on one server per replica with plenty of room; with 512 dimensions, 32-byte codes are aggressive compression (16 dimensions per byte-code), so use 64-byte codes (72 bytes with ID, about 14 GB) and refine the top 500–1,000 with float16 vectors (1,024 bytes each, 205 GB in total) on local SSD. Two shards for parallelism and fault isolation, three replicas each across zones; at 300 queries a second each shard replica handles about 100 searches a second, very light. HNSW in RAM (435 GB) would need at least two servers per replica just for memory and would only pay off if latency or recall targets demanded it.

(c) 200 million × 4 ms = 800,000 GPU-seconds ≈ 222 GPU-hours ÷ 20 GPUs ≈ 11 hours, an overnight job, so model upgrades are practical a few times a year with a versioned index switch.

Diagnose it

After a nightly rebuild, purchases per visual search drop 6% in all categories at once. Latency, detection counts and the embedding model version are unchanged. What do you check?

Answer

A drop everywhere that starts with a rebuild, with the embedding unchanged, points at the index rather than the model. Check ANN recall@100 against exact search on the fixed query sample for the new build versus the previous one: if it fell (say from 96% to 85%), the index is the cause. Then find why: codebooks or list centroids retrained on a bad sample (for example a sample dominated by one new category), a configuration change in nprobe or refinement depth, a shard built from a partial catalogue (check vector counts per shard against the catalogue), or residuals encoded against the wrong centroids after a mismatched training step. Roll back to the previous build (keep it for exactly this reason), fix the build, and add an automatic gate: a rebuild is not swapped in unless its recall on the fixed sample is within a point of the previous build and its vector counts match the catalogue.
