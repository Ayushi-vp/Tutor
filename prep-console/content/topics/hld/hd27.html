An AI company trains its own chat models. Each training cycle needs three kinds of human data. Supervised fine-tuning needs thousands of demonstrations: prompts with ideal responses written or carefully edited by people. Preference tuning needs tens of thousands of comparisons: two model responses to the same prompt and a judgement of which is better. And evaluation needs labels for golden sets and for checking automated judges. For the first year this ran on spreadsheets and a contractor marketplace: a researcher wrote instructions in a document, exported prompts to a CSV, contractors filled in columns, and someone merged the files.

Then the questions started. Why did the reward model trained on March's comparisons get worse than the one trained on February's? (Half of March's labels came from a new vendor team that had read an older version of the instructions, and nobody could tell which labels were which.) Why do two labellers disagree on 40% of comparisons? (Some pairs are genuinely close, some instructions are ambiguous, and one labeller always picked the longer response.) Why did the legal team find customers' phone numbers in a training dataset? (Prompts were sampled from production logs without scrubbing.) Why did the model's score on the company's main evaluation set jump eight points overnight? (Evaluation prompts had leaked into a preference batch.) And what does a usable label actually cost, now that a third of the budget goes to relabelling?

The senior design question is to build the platform that makes this a reliable production process: task design and versioned guidelines; labeller pools and routing by skill; quality control with gold questions, agreement statistics and adjudication; active learning to decide what is worth labelling; interfaces for collecting preferences well; PII handling; throughput and cost per usable label; and clean, versioned datasets flowing into training and evaluation pipelines. Agreement statistics and labelling operations as a measurement discipline are taught in ev5; preference optimisation in lm23. This case is about the system that runs them at scale.

The lesson follows the interview: requirements with quality, throughput, cost and privacy targets; estimates for labels, hours, people and money; the API and data model; the high-level design; deep dives on task design and guidelines, routing, quality control with worked agreement statistics, active learning, preference-collection interfaces, PII, and cost per usable label; feeding results into training and evaluation; the platform's own evaluation; failure modes; and what changes at ten times the scale. The real cases are the published labelling programmes behind InstructGPT and Llama 2, and a production tool's documentation of how it measures agreement.

What the platform does

A data-labelling and human-feedback platform takes raw material (prompts, documents, model responses) and produces labelled datasets: demonstrations, preference pairs, safety labels and evaluation labels. Around that core it does what a factory's quality and logistics departments do: defines each task precisely, recruits and qualifies the right workers, sends each item to someone able to do it, checks the work, resolves disagreements, measures quality and cost, protects sensitive material, and delivers versioned, documented output to the teams that use it.

The mental model: a clinical trial's measurement process

Think of how a clinical trial measures outcomes. A protocol defines exactly what counts as "improved", with examples. Assessors are trained and certified before they rate anyone. Some patients are assessed by two people independently, so the trial can report how reliable the ratings are. Known reference cases are slipped in to check that assessors still apply the protocol. Disagreements go to a senior clinician. Every rating is recorded with who made it, when, and under which protocol version, and patient identities are protected throughout.

The platform is that measurement process for model training data. Guidelines are the protocol. Qualification and calibration are assessor training. Overlap labelling and agreement statistics measure reliability. Gold questions are the reference cases. Adjudication is the senior clinician. Lineage is the audit trail, and PII scrubbing is patient confidentiality. The analogy breaks in two places worth noticing. In a trial the outcome usually has a true value; in preference labelling, many comparisons are genuinely matters of judgement, so disagreement is partly signal, not only error. And a trial's protocol is fixed in advance, while the platform's guidelines evolve every few weeks as models and products change, so every label must carry the version it was made under.

Three facts that shape the design

First, labels are measurements with error, and the platform must quantify that error and carry it with the data, because downstream models learn whatever the labels say, including their noise. Second, human time is the expensive, slow resource: every design choice (what to label, how many labellers per item, how much gold and review) is a trade between data quality and human hours. Third, the data is sensitive in two directions: prompts may contain users' personal data, and labelled datasets are crown jewels whose leakage into evaluation, or out of the company, causes real harm. None of these is solved by a better labelling tool alone; they are solved by a process with measurements at every step, which the tool then enforces. A reader who stops here has the shape: select, scrub, build tasks under versioned guidelines, route by skill, label in a secure workspace, check with gold and overlap, adjudicate disagreements, and publish versioned datasets with lineage, in a loop driven by each new model.

1 · Requirements

Functional. Researchers define task types (preference comparison, ranking, SFT demonstration writing or editing, safety classification, evaluation rubric scoring, span or box annotation) with a guideline and an interface; submit batches of items; choose pools, overlap rate and gold share; monitor progress and quality; and export datasets. Labellers see a queue, complete tasks in a secure workspace, see feedback on gold and adjudicated items, and can flag unclear guidelines. Adjudicators resolve disagreements. Administrators manage pools, qualifications, payments and access. Out of scope, said aloud: training the models, and recruiting the vendors' workforce.

Quality targets. Per task type, an agreed reliability floor before data is used: for example Krippendorff's alpha at least 0.67 for categorical safety labels (the threshold Krippendorff suggests for tentative conclusions, with 0.8 for reliable data), gold accuracy at least 90% per active labeller, and for preference pairs a measured agreement reported by strength bucket. Every label traceable to its labeller (pseudonymously), guideline version and quality statistics.

Throughput targets. Weekly: 20,000 usable preference pairs, 3,000 SFT demonstrations, 10,000 evaluation labels and 5,000 safety labels; turnaround for a 2,000-item evaluation batch under 72 hours.

Cost targets. Published cost per usable label per task type, tracked weekly; total spend within budget, with the split between labelling, quality control and adjudication visible.

Privacy and safety targets. No raw personal data visible to labellers; no data leaves the secure workspace; erasure requests applied to every dataset version within 30 days; exposure limits and wellbeing support for labellers handling harmful content.

Clarifying questions. "Which task types, and which matter most?" "In-house labellers, vendors, or both?" "Where do prompts come from, and what consent covers them?" "How fast must data reach training after a model checkpoint?" "Who owns guidelines: researchers or a dedicated data team?"

An SFT demonstration takes about 25 minutes (research, writing, self-review). An evaluation label against a rubric about 1.5 minutes; a safety label about 1 minute. Roughly $140,000 a week, $7 million a year: a budget line that justifies a platform team, and where a 20% improvement in cost per usable label pays for engineers.

Data volume. Small by infrastructure standards: tens of thousands of tasks a week, each a few kilobytes of text plus metadata. The platform is not a big-data problem; it is a workflow, quality and security problem.

The insight to say out loud. The constraint is human hours and the quality of each hour. Every mechanism in the design either raises the information per labelled item (active learning, good interfaces, clear guidelines) or protects the reliability of each label (qualification, gold, overlap, adjudication), and both are measured in cost per usable label.

4 · High-level design

The pipeline is the primer diagram. Ingestion accepts items from researchers, from sampling jobs that run the latest model checkpoints on prompt sets, and from production logs where consent allows, passing everything through PII scrubbing. The selector applies active-learning policies to choose which items are labelled. The task builder renders items under a task type and guideline version. The router assigns tasks to labellers by pool, skill and trust score, inserting gold questions and overlap duplicates. Labellers work in a secure workspace, a browser application in a locked-down environment with no copy, paste or download, watermarking of screens and full access logs. Quality control runs continuously: gold scoring, agreement on overlap items, speed and pattern checks, a reliability model. Disagreements and flagged items go to adjudication. Accepted labels go to the dataset service, which builds versioned snapshots with lineage and exports them to training (via the fine-tuning platform, hd21) and to evaluation (hd12), with a contamination guard deduplicating training exports against evaluation sets. Dashboards show progress, quality and cost per batch, task type, pool and labeller.

5 · Deep dive: task design and guidelines

A task type is a measurement instrument. Its guideline defines what each answer means, with positive and negative examples, edge cases and tie-breaking rules. For preference comparisons, the guideline says what "better" means: InstructGPT's labellers were asked to prioritise helpfulness to the user while weighing truthfulness and harmlessness, and Llama 2 separated helpfulness and safety into two task types with different guidelines, so each could be specific. A vague guideline ("pick the better response") produces labels that encode each labeller's personal taste.

Calibration before production. A new guideline (or a major revision) starts with a calibration round: 50–100 items labelled by everyone in the pool, agreement computed, disagreements discussed in a session with the guideline owner, the guideline revised, and the round repeated until agreement reaches the target. Only then do production batches start. The calibration items become the first gold questions.

Versioning. Guidelines change as models and products change: a new policy on medical advice, a new definition of "concise". Each change gets a version, and every task records the version it was presented under. Datasets can then be filtered by version, analyses can compare labels across versions, and a bad revision can be rolled back. Without versioning, a mid-month guideline change silently splits a dataset into two incompatible halves, as in the opening story.

Task size and fatigue. Long tasks produce worse labels late in a session. Tasks are sized to a few minutes, sessions are capped, and quality is tracked by time into session, so fatigue shows up as a measurable pattern.

6 · Deep dive: labeller pools and routing

Pools by skill. Generalists by language; domain experts (software engineering, mathematics, medicine, law, finance) for tasks that need expertise to judge correctness; a safety pool trained on harmful-content policies, with wellbeing support; and adjudicators, the most experienced labellers and in-house specialists. InstructGPT's paper describes selecting its roughly 40 contractors with a screening test that measured agreement with researchers on sensitive prompts, which is the same idea: qualify before access.

Qualification and trust. Each pool has a qualification test drawn from gold items; passing grants access to a task type at a probationary trust level. A labeller's trust score is a running estimate from gold accuracy, agreement with peers on overlap items and adjudication outcomes, weighted toward recent work. It drives routing (hard and high-value tasks go to high-trust labellers), the review rate of their work (low trust means more of it is reviewed) and, for vendors, payment bonuses.

The router's rules. Match the task's required skills to qualified pools. Insert gold questions at the configured share, indistinguishable from normal tasks. Assign overlap copies to randomly chosen pairs of qualified labellers, never the same person twice, so agreement estimates are unbiased. Respect exposure limits: a labeller in the safety pool sees at most a set number of graphic items per day. Balance queues across pools and time zones to meet due dates. Prefer giving a labeller a run of tasks of one type (context switching costs time) without letting one labeller dominate a batch (which would bias it toward their judgement).

7 · Deep dive: quality control and agreement statistics

Gold questions. Hidden items with answers agreed by experts, mixed into queues at about 5%. For preference tasks, good gold items are pairs where one response is clearly worse for a reason the guideline names (a factual error, an unsafe instruction, ignoring the question), because close pairs make poor tests. Each labeller's gold accuracy is tracked over a sliding window; a drop below the threshold triggers review of their recent work and reduced routing. Gold items wear out (labellers learn them), so the set is refreshed from adjudicated items.

Overlap and agreement. A fraction of items (20% here) is labelled by two labellers independently. Agreement is computed per task type, per guideline version, per labeller pair and per strength bucket. For two labellers with categorical answers, Cohen's kappa corrects for chance (ev5 derives it). Work an example from overlap data: on 200 preference pairs, labeller 1 chose A 120 times and labeller 2 chose A 110 times; both chose A on 90 and both B on 60, so they agreed on 150. Observed agreement po = 0.75. Chance agreement, from each labeller's base rates, pe = 0.60 × 0.55 + 0.40 × 0.45 = 0.51. Kappa = (0.75 − 0.51) / (1 − 0.51) ≈ 0.49. For comparison, InstructGPT reported raw agreement of 72.6 ± 1.5% among its training labellers and 77.3 ± 1.3% for held-out labellers, and the earlier summarisation work found researchers agreeing with each other 73 ± 4% of the time: preference judgements are genuinely hard to agree on.

Many labellers and missing labels. When items have different numbers of labels (some three, some two, because a labeller skipped or a task expired), Krippendorff's alpha applies: α = 1 − Do / De, observed disagreement over the disagreement expected by chance. A small worked case with nominal labels A and B: eight items, labelled AAA, ABA, BB, AA, BBB, BA, AA and BBA. Count ordered pairs of labels within each item, each weighted by 1 / (labels in the item − 1): this gives AA 8, BB 6, AB 3 and BA 3, a total of 20 pairable values, 11 A and 9 B. Observed disagreement Do = 6 / 20 = 0.30. Expected disagreement from the pooled values De = 2 × 11 × 9 / (20 × 19) = 198 / 380 ≈ 0.52. So α = 1 − 0.30 / 0.52 ≈ 0.42, well below the 0.667 that Krippendorff suggests as the floor for tentative conclusions. The platform computes these statistics continuously; the lab does the same in code.

What the numbers mean for preference data. Low agreement on preferences is partly noise and partly real: some pairs are close, and reasonable people differ. The platform therefore reports agreement by preference strength. If labellers agree strongly on pairs marked "significantly better" and near chance on "negligibly better", the data is behaving as expected, and close pairs can be down-weighted or excluded from training. Llama 2's authors used the strength rating directly, adding a margin to the reward-model loss that is larger for clearly separable pairs, and report that it improved accuracy on separable pairs. If agreement is low even on supposedly clear pairs, the guideline is the problem.

Speed and pattern checks. Tasks completed far faster than reading time allows, labellers who pick the left response 70% of the time when order is randomised, or rationales copied between tasks are flagged automatically for review.

Reliability models. With overlap data, a model such as Dawid–Skene estimates each labeller's confusion pattern and each item's most likely true label jointly, weighting reliable labellers more. It is most useful for categorical tasks (safety labels, rubric scores) and as a diagnostic for preferences.

Adjudication. Disagreements on overlap items go to an adjudicator with more expertise, who records the final label and a reason. A random sample of agreed items is also adjudicated, because two labellers can share a misconception. Adjudicated items feed back as gold questions and as guideline examples. Label Studio's documentation shows how a production tool frames the measurement side: agreement per question and overall, pairwise or consensus methods, exact match for categories, and overlap measures such as IoU for boxes and span overlap for text.

8 · Deep dive: active learning, choosing what to label

Why selection matters. A random sample of prompts with responses from the current model contains many pairs where both responses are fine and nearly identical, and many prompts the model already handles well. Labelling them costs the same as labelling the informative ones. Active learning chooses items expected to teach the model most per label.

Policies, combined. On-policy sampling: generate responses from the latest model checkpoint, since a reward model must score what the current policy produces; Llama 2's authors collected preference data in weekly batches using the latest model for exactly this reason, noting that reward-model accuracy degrades quickly when not exposed to the new distribution. Uncertainty sampling: prefer pairs where the current reward model's predicted preference probability is near 0.5, or where an ensemble of reward models (or a reward model and an LLM judge) disagree. Diversity: cluster prompt embeddings (ml12) and sample across clusters, so the batch is not all one topic. Targeted: prompts from known weaknesses found by evaluation (hd12) and red-teaming. Random slice: keep 20–30% of each batch randomly sampled, so agreement, coverage and reward-model accuracy can be measured without selection bias, and so the training data does not drift entirely toward hard edge cases.

Generating good pairs. Two responses from the same model at the same temperature are often near-identical. Pairs become more informative when responses come from different checkpoints or model variants, different temperatures or different prompts to the model (a concise and a detailed instruction); Llama 2 sampled the two responses from two different model variants with varied temperature to maximise diversity. Rankings of several responses give more comparisons per prompt: InstructGPT presented K = 4 to 9 responses to rank, which yields K(K − 1) / 2 comparisons from one prompt reading, 36 for K = 9.

Deduplication and exclusions. Near-duplicate prompts are removed, and anything within a similarity threshold of an evaluation item is excluded before labelling, not only before export.

10 · Deep dive: preference-collection interfaces

The interface shapes the data. Its design rules, each with a reason:

  • Randomised order, hidden identity. Left-right position and model names bias choices; order is randomised per task and recorded, so position bias can be measured and corrected.
  • Strength, not just choice. A short scale (Llama 2 used significantly better, better, slightly better, negligibly better or unsure) separates close calls from clear ones, which training and agreement analysis both use.
  • Escape hatches. "Both unsafe", "both bad" and "cannot judge (needs expertise)" options prevent forced choices that pollute the data; Llama 2's safety stage recorded whether the preferred response was safe and the other not (18% of its safety data), both safe (47%) or both unsafe (35%).
  • Per-criterion ratings where needed. Separate ratings for helpfulness, factual accuracy and safety let datasets for different objectives be built from one task, at the cost of time per task.
  • Rationale. A one-line reason helps adjudication, guideline revision and catching careless work, and costs about 20 seconds.
  • Edit to improve. Letting labellers edit the better response turns a comparison into an SFT demonstration, which is often cheaper than writing from scratch.
  • Context and tools. Multi-turn context, the ability to run code (for coding tasks) or search a reference (for factual tasks), keyboard shortcuts and a clear view of long responses, because a labeller who cannot check correctness will judge style.

11 · Deep dive: PII and data protection

Sourcing. Prompts come from researchers, red-teamers, synthetic generation, and production logs only where users consented or the legal basis and contracts allow; enterprise customers' data is typically excluded by contract. The source and consent basis are stored with every item, so a later change (a user withdraws consent) can be enforced.

Scrubbing before humans. Pattern detectors (emails, phone numbers, card numbers, national identifiers, addresses) and a named-entity model replace personal data with consistent typed placeholders ("[PERSON_1] asked [PERSON_2] about [ORDER_ID]") before any task is created. Items the scrubber is unsure about go to a restricted pool or are dropped. Scrubbing recall is measured on a labelled sample, like any classifier.

Secure workspace. Labellers, especially vendor staff, work in a locked-down browser environment: no copy, paste, download or screenshots where the platform can prevent them, visible watermarks with the labeller's pseudonym to deter photographing screens, access only to assigned tasks, and full access logs. Data stays in the company's environment, in the required region.

Retention and erasure. Raw items are kept only as long as needed; datasets keep scrubbed text. Lineage makes erasure possible: an erasure request finds every item derived from the user's data, removes it from future dataset versions and flags past versions (and models trained on them) according to policy.

Labeller wellbeing. Safety tasks expose people to disturbing content. Exposure limits per day, blurred previews by default, opt-in pools, rotation and access to support are part of the design, not an afterthought, and are tracked like any other operational metric.

12 · Feeding training and evaluation

Versioned datasets. An export produces an immutable snapshot with a manifest. Each record carries: item ID, prompt and responses, the model checkpoints that produced the responses, the final label and strength, all individual labels with labeller pseudonyms, the guideline version, agreement statistics for its batch, adjudication outcome, and source and consent basis. Training jobs (hd21) reference a snapshot ID, so every model can be traced to exactly the labels it learned from.

Filters at export. Researchers choose what to train on: exclude negligible-strength pairs, exclude items flagged both bad, require a minimum batch agreement, select guideline versions. These choices are recorded in the manifest.

The contamination guard. Evaluation sets are frozen, hashed and stored separately. Every training export is deduplicated against all evaluation items by exact hash and by embedding similarity above a threshold, and the platform refuses to route evaluation items into training queues. A sudden jump in a model's score on an old evaluation set, with no matching jump on a fresh one, is the alarm that a leak happened anyway.

Closing the loop. Each new checkpoint is sampled for the next round of preference collection; reward-model accuracy on a held-out set of fresh human preferences is measured after every batch, which tells the platform whether its data is still teaching the model something.

13 · Evaluating the platform itself

Label quality: gold accuracy by labeller and pool, agreement by task type, guideline version and strength bucket, adjudication overturn rates (how often adjudicators change agreed labels), scrubbing recall. Throughput: hours completed versus planned by pool, batch turnaround, queue age. Cost: cost per usable label by task type, the share of spend on quality control and adjudication. Downstream value: reward-model accuracy on fresh held-out preferences, and offline evaluation gains of models trained on new batches versus without them; a batch that does not move these is a signal to change selection policies. People: labeller retention, exposure counts for safety pools, and survey feedback on guideline clarity.

15 · What changes at 10× scale

At 200,000 preference pairs a week, three changes. AI assistance in the loop: an LLM judge pre-labels items, and humans review where the judge is uncertain or disagrees with a second judge, with the judge validated against human labels on a fixed set (hd12); this cuts human hours sharply but makes judge validation part of the platform. Multiple vendors with comparable quality metrics and gold sets, routed by measured cost per usable label and turnaround. Stronger statistics: with millions of labels, labeller-reliability models, per-labeller bias correction and automatic guideline-ambiguity detection (clusters of items with persistent disagreement) become standard tools. The fundamentals stay: versioned guidelines, measured agreement, lineage and the contamination guard.

Disagreement is data, not just noise

Treating every disagreement as error and forcing a single "true" label throws away information. For subjective judgements, the distribution of labels is itself the most honest answer: if three of five labellers prefer A, the preference is real but weak. Some training methods use soft labels (the proportion preferring each response) or margins proportional to strength, and evaluation can report how often a model agrees with the majority on clear items versus close ones. The platform supports this by keeping every individual label in the dataset, not only the adjudicated one, so downstream teams can choose how to aggregate.

Chance correction depends on prevalence, so report the split

Kappa and alpha subtract chance agreement computed from how often each category is used. When one category dominates (95% of items are "safe"), chance agreement is very high, and even good labellers get low kappa, the so-called prevalence paradox that ev5 works through. The fix is not a different statistic but fuller reporting: raw agreement, the category distribution, and the chance-corrected number together, plus agreement on the rare category specifically, which is usually the one that matters (for safety labels, agreement on what is unsafe).

Gold questions measure attention, not judgement

Gold items must have clear answers, so they test whether a labeller is paying attention and applying the guideline to unambiguous cases. They say little about judgement on hard, close cases, which is where most of the value and most of the disagreement lies. That is why gold accuracy is combined with agreement on overlap items and adjudication outcomes in the trust score, and why adjudicators review a sample of hard agreed items: those are the only signals about quality on difficult cases.

Selection bias is the price of active learning

Choosing uncertain items makes training data more informative, but it makes the labelled set unrepresentative: agreement measured on hard items looks worse than on typical ones, and a reward model trained only on edge cases can become miscalibrated on ordinary responses. Keeping a random slice in every batch solves both: it gives unbiased estimates of agreement and coverage, and it anchors training on the typical distribution. Importance weights can correct estimates computed on the selected items, but the random slice is simpler and more robust.

Reward hacking starts in the data

Reward models learn what labellers reward, including their biases: longer answers, confident tone, lists, flattery. A policy optimised against such a reward model amplifies the bias. The platform can measure these biases directly (preference rate for the longer response on pairs whose content is otherwise equivalent; position bias) and counter them: guidelines that tell labellers to judge content over length, length-controlled pairs, and analysis of strength by length difference. The cheapest place to stop reward hacking is before the label is recorded.

AI-assisted labelling changes what humans are for

As LLM judges improve, the efficient design uses them to pre-label and to flag, with humans concentrating on items where judges are uncertain, disagree with each other or touch high-stakes categories, and on a random sample to keep measuring judge accuracy. This raises the expertise needed from humans (the remaining items are the hard ones) and makes judge validation a first-class platform concern. It also creates a subtle risk: if humans see the judge's label, they anchor on it, so assisted tasks hide the pre-label on the sample used to measure judge quality.

Labeller incentives shape label quality

Paying per task rewards speed; paying per hour rewards nothing in particular; neither rewards care on hard items. Vendors and platforms that tie bonuses to gold accuracy and adjudication outcomes, cap daily task counts and pay more for expert pools get better data, but every incentive can be gamed: labellers learn to recognise gold items (so gold sets rotate), or avoid hard tasks (so routing assigns them rather than letting labellers choose). The platform measures incentive effects like any other change, for example comparing agreement and gold accuracy before and after a payment change, because labelling quality is an outcome of the whole system, including how people are paid and treated, not only of the interface and the guideline. Reporting quality by vendor, pool and payment scheme keeps that visible.

How it fails in practice

The silent guideline split. Midway through a month, the definition of "concise" in the helpfulness guideline changed. Half the batch was labelled under each version, and the resulting reward model learned contradictory preferences about length, so the next policy's answers became erratic in length. Because tasks recorded only the guideline name, not the version, the two halves could not be separated. Every task now records the version, exports can filter by it, and a guideline change opens a new task type version rather than editing the old one in place.

The left-hand labeller. A vendor labeller chose the left response 78% of the time over two weeks, while order was randomised. Gold accuracy was acceptable because gold pairs were easy. A position-bias check per labeller flagged it; their recent labels were removed and relabelled, and position bias is now part of the trust score.

The leaked evaluation set. An internal evaluation set's prompts were included in a prompt pool used for preference sampling, because both had been imported from the same spreadsheet. The next model jumped eight points on that evaluation set and showed no gain on a fresh one. The contamination guard (deduplication against all evaluation items at selection and export) was built after this, and evaluation sets now live in a separate project that training queues cannot read.

What to monitor

Per batch: progress against plan, agreement by strength and category, gold accuracy, rejection rate, unusable share, adjudication backlog, cost per usable label. Per labeller: gold accuracy, agreement with peers, speed, position bias, trust score trend, exposure counts. Per guideline version: agreement trend and the items with persistent disagreement (candidates for clarification). Pipeline: scrubbing recall on audits, contamination-guard hits, time from checkpoint to labelled data. Downstream: reward-model accuracy on fresh held-out preferences after each batch.

Knobs people tune

Gold share and overlap rate by task type and trust level; trust thresholds for access and review; selection mix (uncertain, diverse, targeted, random); pair generation settings (which checkpoints, temperatures); strength-based export filters; adjudication policy (all disagreements, clear-strength only, sample of agreed items); exposure limits; session length.

The real cases: InstructGPT, Llama 2 and Label Studio

InstructGPT's paper (Ouyang et al., 2022) documents a labelling programme at the scale of a research project: about 40 contractors hired through Upwork and Scale AI, selected by a screening test measuring sensitivity to harmful content and agreement with researchers; an SFT dataset of about 13,000 training prompts, a reward-model dataset of 33,000 and a PPO dataset of 31,000; rankings of K = 4 to 9 responses per prompt to extract many comparisons from one reading; and inter-annotator agreement of 72.6 ± 1.5% among training labellers and 77.3 ± 1.3% for held-out labellers who produced no training data, against 73 ± 4% between researchers in the earlier summarisation work. Llama 2's paper (Touvron et al., 2023) shows the industrial version: binary comparisons with a four-level strength scale, separate helpfulness and safety guidelines, safety bins of 18%, 47% and 35%, weekly batches collected from the latest model to keep the reward model on-distribution (14 batches), over a million comparisons (1,418,091 in its Meta reward-modelling data), and a margin in the reward-model loss driven by the strength rating. Label Studio's agreement documentation, checked on 10 October 2026, shows how a production tool measures label quality per question and overall, with pairwise and consensus methods and task-appropriate similarity measures. The cost figures in this lesson are worked from stated assumptions; the method (funnel the raw label through gold, overlap, rejection, usability and adjudication to a cost per usable label) is what transfers.

"""Agreement and cost toolkit for a labelling platform. Python 3.12, stdlib only."""
import random
from collections import Counter
from itertools import permutations
rng = random.Random(3)

def cohen_kappa(a, b):
    n = len(a); po = sum(x == y for x, y in zip(a, b)) / n
    ca, cb = Counter(a), Counter(b)
    pe = sum(ca[k] / n * cb[k] / n for k in set(a) | set(b))
    return po, pe, (po - pe) / (1 - pe)

def kripp_alpha_nominal(units):
    """units: list of label lists; None = missing. Returns alpha."""
    o = Counter()
    for u in units:
        v = [x for x in u if x is not None]
        if len(v) < 2: continue
        for i, j in permutations(range(len(v)), 2):
            o[(v[i], v[j])] += 1 / (len(v) - 1)
    nc = Counter()
    for (c, _), w in o.items(): nc[c] += w
    n = sum(nc.values())
    do = sum(w for (c, k), w in o.items() if c != k) / n
    de = sum(nc[c] * nc[k] for c in nc for k in nc if c != k) / (n * (n - 1))
    return 1 - do / de

a = ["A"] * 90 + ["A"] * 30 + ["B"] * 20 + ["B"] * 60          # labeller 1
b = ["A"] * 90 + ["B"] * 30 + ["A"] * 20 + ["B"] * 60          # labeller 2
po, pe, k = cohen_kappa(a, b)
print(f"Cohen: observed {po:.2f}  chance {pe:.2f}  kappa {k:.2f}")
small = [list("AAA"), list("ABA"), ["B", "B", None], ["A", "A", None], list("BBB"),
         ["B", "A", None], ["A", None, "A"], list("BBA")]
print(f"Krippendorff alpha, 8-item example: {kripp_alpha_nominal(small):.3f}")

# Simulated batch: 400 items, 3 labellers (one careless, one left-biased), some missing
truth = [rng.random() < 0.6 for _ in range(400)]                 # True = A better
gold = set(rng.sample(range(400), 20))
def label(t, kind, left_is_a):
    if kind == "careless" and rng.random() < 0.45: choice_left = rng.random() < 0.5
    elif kind == "biased" and rng.random() < 0.30: choice_left = True
    else:
        correct = rng.random() < 0.85
        a_better = t if correct else not t
        choice_left = a_better == left_is_a
    return "A" if choice_left == left_is_a else "B", choice_left
kinds = {"L1": "good", "L2": "careless", "L3": "biased"}
units, stats = [], {n: Counter() for n in kinds}
for i, t in enumerate(truth):
    row = []
    for name, kind in kinds.items():
        if rng.random() < 0.15: row.append(None); continue        # task expired / skipped
        left_is_a = rng.random() < 0.5
        lab, left = label(t, kind, left_is_a)
        row.append(lab); stats[name]["n"] += 1; stats[name]["left"] += left
        if i in gold: stats[name]["gold"] += 1; stats[name]["gold_ok"] += (lab == "A") == t
    units.append(row)
alpha = kripp_alpha_nominal(units)
boots = sorted(kripp_alpha_nominal([rng.choice(units) for _ in units]) for _ in range(500))
print(f"\nBatch alpha {alpha:.3f}  (95% bootstrap {boots[12]:.3f}-{boots[487]:.3f})")
print(f"{'labeller':8} {'gold acc':>8} {'left %':>7}")
for name, s in stats.items():
    print(f"{name:8} {s['gold_ok'] / max(1, s['gold']):8.2f} {s['left'] / s['n']:7.0%}")

def cost_per_usable(rate=24, minutes=3, reject=0.06, unusable=0.10, overlap=0.2,
                    gold=0.05, disagree=0.25, adj_cost=7.5):
    items = 1000 / ((1 - reject) * (1 - unusable))
    tasks = items * (1 + overlap) / (1 - gold)
    total = tasks * rate * minutes / 60 + items * overlap * disagree * adj_cost
    return total / 1000
print(f"\nCost per usable label: ${cost_per_usable():.2f} (raw ${24 * 3 / 60:.2f})")

What to look for: kappa well below raw agreement; a batch alpha pulled down by the careless labeller, with an interval that shows how uncertain one batch's estimate is; the biased labeller exposed by the left-choice rate rather than by gold accuracy; and a cost per usable label close to double the raw figure.

Estimate it

A team needs 5,000 usable code-review preference pairs a week from software-engineering experts at $70 an hour, 6 minutes per pair. Expect 8% rejected labels, 15% unusable items (both responses wrong), 25% overlap, 5% gold, disagreement on 30% of overlap items, and adjudication by a staff engineer at $120 an hour taking 10 minutes. (a) Cost per usable label? (b) Expert hours per week? (c) Which two changes would you test first to cut cost?

Answer

(a) Raw cost: 6 minutes at $70 an hour is $7.00. Items per 1,000 usable: 1,000 / (0.92 × 0.85) ≈ 1,279. Labels with 25% overlap: 1,598. Tasks with 5% gold: 1,598 / 0.95 ≈ 1,683, costing 1,683 × $7.00 ≈ $11,780. Adjudication: 1,279 × 0.25 × 0.30 ≈ 96 items × $20 = $1,920. Total ≈ $13,700 per 1,000, about $13.70 per usable label, nearly twice the raw $7.

(b) For 5,000 usable labels: about 8,415 tasks × 0.1 hours ≈ 840 expert labelling hours, plus about 480 adjudications × 1/6 hour ≈ 80 staff-engineer hours. At 30 productive hours a week, about 28 experts plus part of three senior engineers: the expert pool is the bottleneck.

(c) The unusable share (15%, both responses wrong) suggests the pairs are badly generated: sampling responses from stronger or more varied checkpoints, or filtering out prompts where the model fails completely, would cut wasted expert time. And the 30% disagreement rate suggests guideline ambiguity: a calibration round on the disagreeing items would lower both adjudication cost and rejection. Lowering overlap to 15% for high-trust experts is a third option once agreement is established.

Diagnose it

Reward-model accuracy on fresh held-out preferences has been flat for three weekly batches, although each batch passed its quality checks (gold accuracy 93%, agreement unchanged). What do you investigate?

Answer

Quality checks say the labels are reliable; flat accuracy says they are not informative. Check distribution: are responses being sampled from the latest checkpoint, or from a stale one (a sampling job pinned to an old model is common)? Check selection: has the uncertainty-sampling share dropped, or is the batch dominated by easy pairs where both responses are good (look at the strength distribution: a rising share of "negligibly better" means little signal)? Check coverage: are prompts concentrated in a few clusters the reward model already handles? Check export filters: did a filter change start excluding the informative close-but-clear pairs? Fixes follow the cause: repoint sampling to the current checkpoint, rebalance the selector toward uncertain and under-covered clusters, generate more varied pairs, and add reward-model accuracy on fresh data as a per-batch gate so an uninformative batch is caught the week it happens.
