A video app has 100 million videos and 50 million people opening it every day. Each person will look at perhaps twenty videos in a session. The home screen has room for about ten at a time. Which ten? A reverse-chronological feed of followed creators works for a while, then fails: most users follow few creators, most good videos come from creators they have never heard of, and a creator who uploads ten times a day drowns everyone else. A "most popular" feed is the same for everyone and stale within hours. The app needs to choose, for each person, at each moment, the handful of items they are most likely to value, out of millions, in under a quarter of a second.

The first attempt usually trains one model to predict whether a user will click an item, and scores every item. Two problems arrive at once. The arithmetic does not work: even a small model costing 0.4 milliseconds per item would need 20 million × 0.4 ms = 8,000 CPU-seconds for one request. And the objective is wrong: a model optimised for clicks learns that exaggerated thumbnails get clicks, and the feed fills with videos people abandon after five seconds. Then a third, slower problem appears: because the model only learns from what it showed, new videos never get shown, never get data, and never get shown.

Recommendation system design is how large platforms answer these three problems: a funnel of increasingly expensive models over decreasing numbers of candidates, objectives that measure value rather than clicks, and deliberate handling of the feedback loop. Interviewers use this question because it tests ML judgement (what to predict, how to evaluate) and systems judgement (what runs where within a latency budget) at the same time.

This lesson follows the interview: requirements with quality, latency, cost and safety targets; estimates (request rate, ranking compute, embedding index size, feature-store reads, logging volume, a p99 latency budget); the API and data model; the architecture; deep dives on two-tower retrieval, multi-task ranking, real-time features, feedback loops and exploration, and cold start; the evaluation plan; failure modes; YouTube's 2016 deep recommendation paper as the real case; and a lab that measures how large the candidate set must be.

The problem in one sentence

Given a user and a moment, order millions of items by how much this user would value seeing each one now, and show the top dozen, in well under a second. Three words in that sentence need care. "Value" is not clicks; it is some mix of time spent, satisfaction, likes and shares, minus skips and regret. "Now" means the system must use what the user did thirty seconds ago, not just last week. "Millions" means no expensive model can look at every item.

The mental model: a hiring funnel

Think of hiring for one role from a million applicants. A recruiter does not interview everyone. A keyword search over CVs picks a few thousand (cheap, crude, fast). A recruiter screen picks a few hundred (moderate cost). Full interviews with the hiring team rank those (expensive, accurate). A final committee balances the shortlist (no two hires from the same team, someone for the night shift). A recommender works the same way. Candidate generation uses cheap methods to pull roughly a thousand plausible items from millions. A pre-ranker, a light model, trims them to a few hundred. The ranker, the most accurate and expensive model, scores those using rich features about the user, the item and their relationship. Re-ranking adjusts the ordered list for diversity, freshness and rules, and the top dozen are shown. The analogy breaks in one place: hiring picks once, while a recommender picks again on every scroll and learns from every reaction, so its choices change its own future data.

Two kinds of data feed this. Features describe the user (history, preferences, device, time of day), the item (topic, length, creator, age, recent engagement) and their relationship (how often this user watched this creator). Labels come from what users did with what they were shown: each impression, one item shown to one user in one position, becomes a training row labelled with clicked or not, watch time, liked, skipped. That is the feedback loop: the system's choices create its own training data, which is why it needs deliberate exploration, a theme this lesson returns to.

What does "real-time" add? Two things. The request itself is real-time: the whole funnel runs while the user waits, inside a budget of a couple of hundred milliseconds, so every stage has a deadline and a fallback. And the inputs are fresh: what the user did in the last minute, and how an item has performed in the last hour, flow into features within seconds through streaming jobs. The models themselves may be retrained only daily; it is the features and the candidate indexes that keep the feed current. Keeping those two meanings apart avoids a common interview mistake, proposing to retrain a large model every few seconds.

A reader who stops here has the right picture: a funnel of models from cheap to expensive, features about user, item and their relationship, labels from impressions, and a loop in which recommendations shape future data. The rest makes each piece concrete.

1 · Requirements

Functional. A personalised home feed for a video app: on open and on each scroll, return the next page of about 20 items for this user. The feed reflects the user's actions within the session (watch three cooking videos and the next page leans that way). New uploads can appear in feeds within minutes. Users can say "not interested", which takes effect immediately. Out of scope, said aloud: search ranking, notifications, ads ranking and the "up next" panel, which reuse the same components with different objectives.

Quality targets. The product goal is long-term satisfaction, measured online by daily active users' watch time per day, return rate after 7 days and surveyed satisfaction. Offline: retrieval recall@1,000 of the item the user watched next of at least 60% on time-split data, and ranker normalised entropy below the current production model's. Every launch passes an A/B test with guardrails.

Latency targets. Server-side p99 under 250 ms for a feed page, p50 under 100 ms. The client prefetches the next page while the user scrolls, so perceived latency is lower still.

Cost target. Serving cost per thousand feed requests, tracked and budgeted; a model change that improves quality but doubles ranking cost needs a business case.

Safety targets. Policy-violating content is never recommended; borderline content is demoted and capped per session; age-appropriate settings for teen accounts are enforced at retrieval; no single topic dominates a session beyond a cap; creators' exposure is monitored for runaway concentration. Personal data used for recommendations follows user privacy settings, including a non-personalised option where regulation requires one.

Clarifying questions. "What is the primary metric the business wants to move?" decides the objective. "How fresh must new items be?" decides whether item embeddings are batch or streaming. "Logged-out users?" means a non-personalised path. "What is the corpus growth rate?" sizes the index.

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Requests. 50 million daily active users × 10 feed pages per day = 500 million requests per day. Average: 500M ÷ 86,400 ≈ 5,800 per second; with a 3× evening peak, about 17,000 requests per second.

Corpus and retrieval index. 100 million items in total, of which 20 million are eligible (not removed, not too old, available in the region). A two-tower item embedding of 128 dimensions in float32 is 512 bytes, so 20M × 512 B = 10.2 GB of vectors, 2.6 GB in int8, plus a graph index of about 3 GB. That fits in one machine's memory, so the index is replicated rather than sharded: each replica handles perhaps 2,000 searches per second, so 17,000 ÷ 2,000 ≈ 9 replicas, 12 with headroom.

Ranking compute. Assume the heavy ranker costs 0.4 ms of one CPU core per item: a multi-task neural network of a few million multiply-adds plus feature transforms and embedding lookups. Ranking 300 items costs 300 × 0.4 = 120 ms of CPU per request. At 17,000 requests per second: 17,000 × 0.12 = 2,040 cores, about 64 servers of 32 cores before headroom, 80 with it. If the ranker scored all 1,500 candidates, that would be 10,200 cores, 5× more. The pre-ranker at 20 µs per item over 1,500 items costs 30 ms of CPU per request, 510 cores. Many companies run the heavy ranker on GPUs to afford bigger models; the arithmetic is the same, only the cost per item changes.

Feature reads. Per request: one user feature row (profile, the last 50 item IDs watched, topic affinities: about 4 KB) and features for 1,500 candidates for the pre-ranker and 300 for the ranker (about 1 KB per item). Fetching 300 KB of item features per request from a remote store would be 17,000 × 300 KB = 5.1 GB/s. But item features are the same for every user, and engagement is concentrated: the most popular 1 million items typically cover most impressions. So ranker hosts keep an in-process cache of hot item features (1M × 1 KB = 1 GB), refreshed every minute, and fetch misses (say 10%) in one batched multi-get. Remote reads become 17,000 user rows per second plus 17,000 batched multi-gets of about 30 keys each, about 510,000 keys per second: a modest key-value cluster.

Embedding tables in the ranker. ID embeddings for 100 million items at 64 dimensions in float32 are 100M × 256 B = 25.6 GB; per-user ID embeddings for 500 million registered users would be 128 GB. Tables this size are why recommendation models are dominated by memory, not arithmetic (Meta's DLRM paper is built around this), and why systems hash IDs into smaller tables, share embeddings, or keep user embeddings in the feature store instead of the model.

Logging and training data. 500M requests × 20 items shown = 10 billion impressions per day. Logging the served features with each impression (about 300 features, roughly 1.5 KB compressed) is 15 TB per day. Thirty days of training data is 450 TB. Down-sampling non-engaged impressions to 10% (and re-weighting them in training) cuts that by about 80%. This volume is why training pipelines run on distributed batch processing (sc18) and why the training window is a deliberate choice.

The insight to say out loud. The serving fleet is dominated by ranking compute and the data platform is dominated by impression logs. Retrieval is the cheapest stage, and that is by design.

3 · The latency budget

The deadline rule is worth stating. If the follows source misses its 30 ms deadline, the request proceeds with the other sources' candidates; the page is slightly less personalised for one request, not slower. Every stage has a fallback: if the ranker times out, serve the pre-ranker's order; if features are missing, use defaults the model saw during training (and log the miss).

The request_id ties every client event back to the exact serving decision (which model version, which candidates, which features), which is what makes training data and debugging possible. The cursor carries the IDs already shown in this session so the next page does not repeat them.

5 · Data model

Online stores. User features: a key-value row per user (profile fields, recent item IDs, topic and creator affinities, counters) updated by batch jobs daily and by streaming jobs within seconds. Item features: per item (metadata, content embeddings, creator, age, engagement counters over 1 hour, 1 day, 7 days). Item embedding index: the ANN index of item-tower vectors with eligibility metadata. Co-visitation table: for each item, its top 100 co-watched items, recomputed daily.

Logs. Impression log: request ID, user, item, position, candidate source, model versions and the feature values used for ranking. Event log: client events keyed by request ID. A join job produces training examples: (served features, labels such as clicked, watch seconds, liked, skipped), once each impression's outcome window (say 1 hour) has closed.

6 · High-level design

A feed request hits the feed service, which loads the user's feature row and the session state, then calls the candidate sources in parallel. The two-tower source runs the user tower on fresh features (including the last few items watched this session) and searches the ANN index. The co-visitation source looks up items co-watched with the user's last few watches. The follows source pulls recent uploads from creators the user follows. A trending and fresh-items source adds popular items in the user's region and an exploration sample of new uploads. The union, about 1,500 items, is de-duplicated and filtered (already seen, blocked creators, ineligible content). The pre-ranker scores all of them and keeps 300. The ranker fetches rich features and scores them with a multi-task model. The re-ranker computes final scores with the value model, applies diversity and business rules, and returns 20 items, which are hydrated with titles and thumbnails. The impression log records everything.

Offline, a training pipeline joins impressions with events into labelled examples and retrains the ranker daily (or continuously) and the two-tower model daily or weekly. A batch job recomputes item embeddings and co-visitation tables; a streaming job embeds new uploads within minutes and updates counters and user session features within seconds. Models go through offline evaluation, then shadow scoring, then an A/B test, then rollout.

7 · Deep dive: two-tower retrieval

The retrieval model must score millions of items in milliseconds, which forces one structural constraint: the item's representation cannot depend on the user. A two-tower model has a user tower (input: user features and recent history; output: a 128-dimension vector u) and an item tower (input: item ID embedding, content embeddings of title, thumbnail and audio, creator, age; output: a 128-dimension vector v). The score is the dot product u·v. Because v does not depend on the user, all 20 million item vectors are computed in a batch job (and for new items, by a streaming job) and loaded into an ANN index. At request time, one user-tower forward pass (a few milliseconds) and one ANN search (about 15 ms) return the top 600 items.

Training. The training data is (user context, item the user engaged with) pairs. The model should give the engaged item a higher score than other items, which is a softmax over the whole corpus, too expensive with 20 million classes. YouTube's 2016 paper framed retrieval as exactly this "extreme multiclass classification" and trained it by sampling several thousand negative classes per example, which it reported as more than 100× faster than a full softmax. A cheaper variant is in-batch negatives: in a batch of 4,096 (user, item) pairs, each user's positive item is contrasted with the other 4,095 items in the batch. The catch is that popular items appear in batches far more often, so they are used as negatives far more often, and the model learns to under-score them. The logQ correction (Yi et al., 2019) subtracts the log of each item's sampling probability from its score during training, which makes the sampled loss an unbiased estimate of the full softmax. At serving the correction is not applied.

What the user tower sees is the design's freshness lever. If it receives the last 50 items the user watched, including ones from 30 seconds ago (from the streaming session store), then each new watch moves u and the next page's candidates shift. The item index can be hours old for established items without much harm; the user vector must be current.

Why several sources. No single retriever covers everything. Two-tower embeddings capture broad taste but blur specific intent ("more from this exact series"); co-visitation captures specific sequences; follows capture explicit subscriptions; trending captures what is happening now; the fresh-items source guarantees new uploads a chance. The union is the candidate set, and each source's contribution is a measurable quantity: how often items it alone contributed ended up in the shown 20 and were engaged with.

8 · Deep dive: ranking

The ranker scores 300 items with everything the retrieval stage could not use: cross features that depend on both user and item (how many of this creator's videos the user finished, the similarity between the item's topic and the user's last session, the time since the user last saw this creator), item engagement counters, context (device, time of day, network), plus ID embeddings. YouTube's paper describes "hundreds of features" split roughly evenly between categorical and continuous. Categorical features become embeddings; continuous ones are normalised (the paper uses quantile normalisation and also feeds powers of the normalised values).

What to predict. A single click head learns to love clickbait. YouTube's ranker instead predicted expected watch time with a trick: logistic regression where each clicked impression is weighted by its watch time and each unclicked impression has weight 1. The learned odds then approximate the expected watch time per impression (the paper derives this: odds ≈ E[T](1 + P), close to E[T] when click probability P is small), and the serving layer uses exp(logit) as the predicted watch time. Modern rankers go further with multi-task heads: P(click), E[watch time], P(like), P(share), P(comment), P(skip within 3 seconds), P(not interested). They share lower layers (learning a common representation) and have separate small heads, often with gating so different tasks can use different mixtures of the shared layers (the multi-gate mixture-of-experts design in Zhao et al., 2019).

The value model combines heads into one ordering score, for example: score = E[watch seconds] + 20 × P(like) + 40 × P(share) − 60 × P(not interested). The weights encode product priorities and are tuned with online experiments against the long-term metric. For the combination to be meaningful, each head must be calibrated (ml15): a predicted 0.1 must mean 10%. Calibration also lets each head be monitored against reality: if P(like) averages 0.05 but the observed like rate is 0.03, something has drifted.

Re-ranking. A list of individually best items can be bad as a set: ten videos from one creator, or five near-identical news clips. The re-ranker builds the page greedily, picking at each slot the item with the best score after penalties for similarity to items already chosen (a maximal marginal relevance style rule), and enforces rules: at most two items per creator per page, a cap on borderline content, insertion of an exploration slot. Integrity filters run again here as a final guarantee.

9 · Deep dive: real-time features and freshness

Freshness has three layers with different speeds. Session features (the items watched or skipped in the last minutes) must update within seconds: client events are batched to the events API every few seconds, written to a log (in1), and a stream processor (sc18) updates the user's session row in the key-value store; the next feed request reads it. Item counters (views, likes, skips in the last hour) update in seconds to a minute, so a video going viral is noticed while it is still rising. Models and embeddings update more slowly: the ranker retrained daily or incrementally every hour, the two-tower model daily, item embeddings for new uploads computed by the streaming pipeline within minutes using the latest item tower. Most of the "real-time" in a real-time recommender is in features.

The trap is training/serving skew (ml14). If the training pipeline computes "likes in the last hour" from complete, de-duplicated batch data, but serving reads a streaming counter that lags by a minute and double-counts retries, the model learns a relationship that does not hold online. The robust pattern is log-and-train: the ranker logs the exact feature values it used for every impression, and training examples are built from those logs joined with later outcomes. The same values are used in both places by construction. The cost is storage (the 15 TB per day estimated above) and slower feature iteration, because a new feature has no history until it has been logged for a while; backfilling it for training needs point-in-time correct computation from raw events, the problem a feature store specialises in (the subject of a later design case).

10 · Deep dive: feedback loops, position bias and exploration

Three biases come from the system observing only its own outputs. Exposure bias: items never shown have no labels, so the model cannot learn they are good. YouTube's paper addresses this partly by generating training examples from all watches, including those found through search or embedded on other sites, "rather than just watches on the recommendations we produce", so discoveries made elsewhere propagate. Position bias: users click higher positions more regardless of quality. If the model ignores position, it learns "items the old model ranked first are good", entrenching the old ranking. The fix is to give position to the model only in a separate shallow tower during training (its contribution is added to the main logit) and to remove it at serving; then the main model learns quality conditional on position. Popularity bias: popular items get more impressions, more data, more confident scores, and more impressions.

Exploration spends a small share of traffic to learn. Options: an exploration slot on each page filled from new or uncertain items; boosting items whose predicted value is uncertain (an upper-confidence-bound idea); guaranteed initial exposure for every new upload to a small, well-matched audience. Log the probability with which each exploration item was chosen; that propensity makes unbiased offline evaluation possible later (an off-policy estimator re-weights logged outcomes by how likely the new policy would have been to show them). Exploration costs a little engagement now for a better model and a healthier creator ecosystem later, which is why it needs a long-term holdout to justify it.

Cold start is the extreme case. A new item has no ID-embedding history, so its item-tower vector must come from content (title and description embeddings, thumbnail and audio embeddings, creator features); it is inserted into the index within minutes and given an exploration budget. A new user has no history, so the first pages use context (region, language, device), popular and diverse items, and then the user tower updates after every action; the first session is effectively a fast interactive survey.

11 · Evaluation plan

Offline, retrieval. Split by time (train on days 1-28, evaluate on day 29) to avoid leaking the future. For each evaluation impression with engagement, check whether the engaged item was in the candidate set: recall@K for K = 100, 500, 1,000, per source and for the union. Also measure each source's unique contribution.

Offline, ranking. Per head: log loss, normalised entropy (log loss relative to predicting the average rate), AUC, and calibration (predicted versus observed rate in buckets). Slice by user cohort (new, casual, heavy), by item age and by country, because averages hide regressions on new items. YouTube's paper used a "weighted, per-user loss": for pairs of clicked and unclicked impressions on the same page, the fraction of watch time mispredicted when the unclicked item scored higher, which is a watch-time-weighted ranking metric closer to the product goal than AUC.

Online. A/B test every model change (in10). Primary metric: watch time per daily active user or a satisfaction-weighted engagement metric. Guardrails: 7-day return rate, "not interested" rate, share of impressions from new creators, topic diversity per session, prevalence of borderline content (from sampled human review), p99 latency and serving cost. Run important changes long enough to see novelty effects fade, and keep a small long-term holdout on an old model to measure cumulative effects.

12 · Failure modes and trade-offs

A feature pipeline breaks (a counter stuck at zero): the model receives values outside its training distribution and quietly ranks badly. Monitor feature distributions against training statistics and fall back to defaults with an alert. An index refresh fails: new items stop appearing; alert on the age of the newest item in the index. A feedback runaway: a small group of creators' share of impressions grows week over week; guardrail metrics catch it if someone watches them. A ranker timeout under load: degrade to the pre-ranker's order rather than failing the page.

Trade-offs to volunteer: candidate count (quality against ranking cost, as the lab shows: diminishing returns past about a thousand), model size (accuracy against CPU or GPU cost per item), freshness (streaming complexity against staleness), exploration (short-term engagement against long-term learning), and objective weights (which product goal wins). Each is decided by measurement, and each should be stated with the metric that would change your mind.

Why the two towers cannot interact

The dot-product structure is not a modelling preference; it is what makes ANN search possible. An ANN index answers "which stored vectors have the largest dot product with this query vector", so the score must factor into a user part and an item part. Any feature that depends on both (has this user watched this creator before?) breaks the factorisation. Late-interaction designs, which keep several vectors per item and take the maximum similarity, recover some expressiveness at higher index cost. The standard division of labour is: retrieval captures broad compatibility with factorised scores, ranking captures specifics with cross features. A candidate who proposes putting cross features into the retrieval model has not understood why it is fast.

Negative sampling decides what the retriever learns

The items used as negatives define the question the model answers. Random negatives teach "is this item plausible for anyone like this user?", which is easy and leads to blurry retrieval. In-batch negatives are mostly popular items, so the model learns to discriminate among popular items, at the cost of popularity bias that the logQ correction removes. Hard negatives (items shown to the user but not engaged with) teach fine distinctions but are biased by what the old system showed, and too many make training unstable. Production systems mix them, and the mix is a hyperparameter evaluated by recall@K offline and by online tests.

The surrogate problem

YouTube's paper makes a point that interviewers like: recommendation "often involves solving a surrogate problem and transferring the result to a particular context". The model predicts a label available in logs (next watch, click, watch time), and the hope is that it transfers to the goal (long-term satisfaction). The paper found that the choice of surrogate had "an outsized importance on performance in A/B testing but is very difficult to measure with offline experiments". Two of its concrete choices: predict the user's next watch from history up to that point, rather than a randomly held-out watch from the middle, because the latter leaks future information; and generate a fixed number of training examples per user so a small group of very active users does not dominate the loss.

Example age and non-stationarity

Popularity changes over time, and a model trained on several weeks of data learns the average popularity over the window. A video uploaded yesterday that is surging looks average. YouTube's fix was to feed the age of each training example as a feature and set it to zero (or slightly negative) at serving, telling the model "predict for the end of the training window". The paper reports this let the model represent time-dependent popularity and increased watch time dramatically on recently uploaded videos in A/B tests. The general lesson: when the world drifts within your training window, give the model a way to know when each example happened.

Embedding tables dominate memory

A ranker's dense layers might be tens of megabytes; its embedding tables, one row per item ID, user ID, creator ID and categorical value, can be hundreds of gigabytes. Serving them needs either sharded parameter servers (a network hop per lookup batch), hashing many IDs into a smaller table (accepting collisions), or moving user embeddings into the feature store as precomputed vectors. Training them needs model-parallel embedding sharding across GPUs with all-to-all communication, the main design point of Meta's DLRM. ByteDance's Monolith adds collisionless hash tables with expiry and frequency filtering, so rarely seen IDs do not consume memory, and streams parameter updates from training to serving within minutes.

Slate effects

Pointwise ranking scores each item independently, but users experience a page. Two great cooking videos side by side are worth less than one cooking video and one music video, because the second cooking video's value depends on the first. Re-ranking with similarity penalties is the practical approximation; slate-aware models that score a whole page exist but are expensive. In an interview, naming the gap between pointwise scores and page value, and explaining the greedy diversity re-ranker as the pragmatic answer, is enough.

Long-term value versus short-term engagement

Optimising next-hour engagement can harm next-month retention: sensational content wins clicks today and fatigue later. Systems address this by adding heads for negative signals (dislike, "not interested", uninstall risk), surveying users about satisfaction and training a head to predict survey answers, and judging launches on long-running holdouts. This is also where integrity and well-being constraints enter as hard limits rather than weights, because some harms should not be traded for engagement at any exchange rate.

How it fails in practice

The broken counter. A schema change upstream made the "views in the last hour" counter report zero for a region. Nothing crashed; the ranker treated every item there as unpopular and the feed tilted toward stale content. Detection: per-feature distribution monitors comparing serving values with the training distribution (share of zeros, mean, quantiles) per region, alerting within minutes.

The stale index. The embedding-refresh job failed for two days, so new uploads were missing from two-tower retrieval and only reached users through the follows source. Detection: alert on the age of the newest item in the index and on the share of impressions from items under 24 hours old.

Offline win, online loss. A new ranker improved offline AUC and lost watch time in the A/B test. Causes found in practice: the model learned to exploit position more strongly; the gain was concentrated on items that the re-ranker's rules suppressed anyway; or the new model was better at predicting clicks and worse at predicting watch time. Treat offline metrics as a filter, never a verdict.

Rabbit holes and concentration. Engagement-optimised loops can narrow sessions to one topic or a few creators. Guardrail metrics on topic diversity and creator concentration, diversity rules in re-ranking, and integrity demotions for borderline content are standard.

Metrics and dashboards

Serving: latency per stage, timeouts per candidate source, fallback rates, candidate counts per source, cache hit rate for item features, ranking CPU or GPU per request. Model health: prediction means versus observed rates per head (calibration drift), feature missing rates and distribution shift, index freshness. Product: engagement and satisfaction metrics with guardrails, per cohort.

The real case: YouTube's deep recommendation paper

Covington, Adams and Sargin's 2016 RecSys paper describes the system that recommended videos to "more than a billion users" and made the two-stage funnel standard. Candidate generation narrows the corpus of "millions" of videos to "hundreds"; ranking orders those, and "dozens" are shown. The models had "approximately one billion parameters" and were "trained on hundreds of billions of examples". Candidate generation was posed as extreme multiclass classification over millions of videos, trained with several thousand sampled negatives per example, "more than 100 times speedup over traditional softmax", and served by nearest-neighbour search in dot-product space within "tens of milliseconds"; the authors found A/B results "not particularly sensitive" to the choice of nearest-neighbour algorithm. Ranking used hundreds of features and predicted expected watch time with watch-time-weighted logistic regression, because "ranking by click-through rate often promotes deceptive videos that the user does not complete". In their ranking experiments the 1024 → 512 → 256 ReLU tower gave the best watch-time-weighted pairwise loss, 34.6% against 41.6% with no hidden layer, while staying within the serving CPU budget; weighting positive and negative examples equally instead of by watch time increased the loss by 4.1%. The paper also states plainly that "live A/B results are not always correlated with offline experiments". The interview lessons: the funnel is about cost, the label is a design decision, CPU budget constrains model size, and online tests decide.

Goal: see the funnel's central trade-off: a cheap but noisy retriever picks K candidates from 200,000 items, an accurate ranker orders them, and you measure how final quality depends on K. NumPy (from the existing gpt_env venv) on Python 3.12; runs in about 12 seconds.

The script invents ground truth: each user and item has a latent taste vector and each item a quality bias. The two-tower retriever sees noisy copies of the vectors and only a weak quality signal (it cannot use cross features). The ranker sees the truth plus a little noise but costs one call per item. "Final quality" is the true value of the 10 items shown divided by the best possible 10.

"""A recommendation funnel in miniature: a cheap two-tower retriever picks K
candidates out of 200,000 items, an accurate (expensive) ranker orders them.
How big must K be? NumPy only (Python 3.12)."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N_ITEMS, N_USERS, D = 200_000, 200, 32

# Ground truth: users and items have latent tastes; items also have a quality bias.
U = rng.normal(size=(N_USERS, D)) / np.sqrt(D)
V = rng.normal(size=(N_ITEMS, D))
quality = rng.normal(scale=0.5, size=N_ITEMS)
def true_score(u_idx, items):                       # what the user would really enjoy
    return U[u_idx] @ V[items].T + quality[items]

# Two-tower retriever: separate user and item embeddings, compared by dot product.
# It is cheap and precomputable, but its embeddings are noisy and it cannot see
# the quality bias well (no cross features), so it is a rough first cut.
U_tower = U + rng.normal(scale=0.15, size=U.shape)
V_tower = V + rng.normal(scale=0.6, size=V.shape)
q_tower = 0.3 * quality                               # a weak popularity/quality signal

# Heavy ranker: sees much richer features, so its error is small -- but it costs
# one model evaluation per (user, item) pair.
def ranker(u_idx, items):
    return true_score(u_idx, items) + rng.normal(scale=0.15, size=len(items))

TOP = 10
results = {k: [] for k in (50, 200, 1000, 5000, 20000)}
for u in range(N_USERS):
    all_true = true_score(u, np.arange(N_ITEMS))
    ideal = np.sort(all_true)[-TOP:].sum()            # best possible 10 for this user
    true_top = set(np.argpartition(all_true, -TOP)[-TOP:])
    retr = U_tower[u] @ V_tower.T + q_tower
    order = np.argsort(-retr)
    for k in results:
        cand = order[:k]                              # candidate generation
        shown = cand[np.argsort(-ranker(u, cand))[:TOP]]  # ranking
        recall = len(true_top & set(cand)) / TOP
        results[k].append((recall, true_score(u, shown).sum() / ideal))

print(f"{'K':>6} {'recall@K of true top-10':>24} {'final quality':>14} {'ranker calls':>13}")
for k, rows in results.items():
    r, q = np.mean(rows, axis=0)
    print(f"{k:>6} {r:>24.2f} {q:>14.3f} {k:>13,}")
print(f"(ranking every item would cost {N_ITEMS:,} calls per request)")
# Try: make the retriever worse (V_tower noise 1.0) or better (0.3) and watch how
# large K must be; that is the trade-off between retrieval quality and ranking cost.

Expected output: roughly 0.83 quality at K = 50, 0.90 at 200, 0.95 at 1,000, 0.98 at 5,000 and 0.99 at 20,000, while recall of the true top 10 climbs from about 0.14 to 0.93. Final quality is high even when recall is modest, because the ranker only needs good items in the candidate set, not the exact best ones. That is why production funnels settle around a thousand candidates.

Work it out

Your feed serves 10M daily active users, 20 sessions each, 30 items per session. Candidate generation returns 800 items per request; the ranking model scores each in 0.4 ms on CPU.

(a) What is the ranking QPS and the CPU cost? (b) The ranker needs 60 features per item, fetched from Redis. What is the read pattern? (c) A junior proposes ranking 8,000 candidates instead of 800 "for better quality". Evaluate.

Answer

(a) 10M × 20 = 200M sessions/day. Assuming a request per session, that is 200M ÷ 86,400 ≈ 2,300 requests/s average, and with a 3× peak factor about 7,000 rps. Each request scores 800 items × 0.4 ms = 320 ms of CPU per request. At 7,000 rps that is 2,240 CPU-seconds per second: roughly 2,240 cores, or about 70 machines of 32 cores, before any headroom. Ranking is the dominant infrastructure cost, which is exactly why the funnel exists, and why a pre-ranker cutting 800 to 300 would save about 60%.

(b) Naively 800 × 60 = 48,000 individual Redis reads per request, which at even 0.2 ms each would be 9.6 s. The design must be: one pipelined multi-get of 800 item-feature blobs (one key per item holding all its features; items are shared across users, so these cache extremely well in-process), plus one read of the user's feature row, which is the same for all 800 items. That is two round trips, not 48,000. Recognising that user features are per request and item features are per item is the key insight.

(c) 10× the candidates is 10× the ranking CPU: about 22,400 cores. The lab shows the return: quality rises only a few points beyond about a thousand candidates, because the extra 7,200 items are mostly ones retrieval already judged unlikely. If quality matters, improve retrieval recall (better two-tower model, more sources) or add a pre-ranker; measure the change with offline recall@K and an A/B test. Spending 10× compute on the stage with diminishing returns is the wrong lever.

Diagnose it

A new ranker version improved offline AUC on the click head by 0.6% and normalised entropy by 0.4%. In a two-week A/B test, clicks per user rose 1.5%, watch time per user fell 0.8%, and "not interested" taps rose 6%. What happened, and what do you do?

Answer

What happened. The model got better at predicting clicks and the value model let that dominate: the feed shifted toward items that attract clicks but are not watched or are actively disliked. The rise in "not interested" is the tell: users are seeing more items they reject. Offline click metrics measured a surrogate, and the surrogate diverged from the goal.

What to do. Do not ship. Check each head's offline metrics and calibration, not just the click head: if the watch-time head regressed or became miscalibrated, its weight in the value model is now wrong. Re-run with the value model re-tuned, or with the click head's weight reduced, and evaluate offline with a watch-time-weighted metric (like YouTube's weighted per-user loss) as well as AUC. Make "not interested" rate and watch time explicit launch guardrails so a click win cannot pass alone.
