At 02:14 on a Saturday, a script starts trying stolen card numbers on a small online shop: $1 payments, one every two seconds, each from a different card. Most are declined by the issuing banks; the ones that succeed are now known to work and are sold on. By 03:00 the same cards are buying $1,800 laptops at a large electronics retailer. Six weeks later, the real cardholders notice and dispute the charges. The retailer loses the laptops, the payment amount and a dispute fee on each one, and if its dispute rate stays high, its card-network standing is at risk.

Blocking everything suspicious is not the answer either. A customer buying a $1,800 laptop at 3 am from a new device while travelling looks suspicious too. Block her and the retailer loses a sale, she may never come back, and she tells her friends. For most merchants, the money lost to declining good customers is comparable to, or larger than, the money lost to fraud itself. So a fraud system has two kinds of mistakes with very different costs, positives that are about one in a thousand, a decision deadline of a fraction of a second inside the payment flow, labels that arrive weeks later, no labels at all for the payments it blocks, and an adversary who changes tactics when blocked.

That combination is why interviewers like this question. It tests whether you know that accuracy is meaningless here, whether you can turn costs into a decision rule, whether you can build behavioural features that are correct both online and in training, and whether you understand what you cannot observe.

This lesson walks the interview: requirements with quality, latency, cost and safety targets; estimates for transaction rate, counter-store size and reads, training data and label volumes, and the latency budget; the API and data model; the architecture; deep dives on features, the model, the decision layer, delayed and missing labels, and adversarial drift; the evaluation plan; failure modes; Stripe Radar's published design as the real case; and a lab that sweeps thresholds by cost and shows why calibration matters.

What the system decides

When a customer pays by card online, the merchant's payment processor sends an authorisation request through the card network to the issuer, the customer's bank, which approves or declines it within a second or two (cd9). Before or alongside that request, the processor's fraud system decides what to do with the payment: allow it, step it up (ask the cardholder to authenticate with their bank through 3-D Secure, an approval in their banking app or a one-time code), send it to manual review by an analyst, or block it. Weeks later, if the real cardholder never authorised it, they dispute it and their bank reverses the payment: a chargeback. The merchant loses the goods, the money and a fee.

The system has two kinds of mistakes. A missed fraud costs the amount plus fees. A false decline, a good payment blocked, costs the lost sale and the customer's goodwill. Fraud is rare, on the order of 1 in 1,000 payments. So among a thousand payments, a system that blocks five might catch the one fraud and wrongly block four good customers, and whether that is a good trade depends entirely on the amounts.

The mental model: a bank's night guard with a ledger

Picture a guard at a door who must decide, in a glance, whether each visitor is legitimate. One visitor tells the guard little. What tells the guard a lot is the ledger: this badge was used at six other doors in the last ten minutes; this face has come in with eleven different badges this week; this door has had twenty failed attempts since midnight. Fraud detection is mostly about that ledger: velocity features, counts and sums of recent activity per card, device, IP address, email and merchant over time windows, and network features that link entities across the whole platform. A large processor sees most cards before; Stripe's documentation says there is a 92% chance it has already seen a card a business encounters. The analogy breaks in one place: the guard learns whether a visitor was a thief only weeks later, and never learns anything about the visitors turned away.

Why use machine learning at all, rather than a long list of rules? Rules are excellent at known patterns and at policy, but fraud signals are weak individually and strong in combination: a slightly unusual amount, a new device, a card used at two other merchants this hour, a shipping country that differs from the card's. Writing rules for every combination explodes, and every rule has a threshold someone must maintain. A model learns how those weak signals combine and outputs one probability, and rules then handle what models are bad at: reacting in minutes and encoding business policy.

The pieces, in one paragraph: a scoring service builds features from the payment and from a counter store kept fresh by a stream of payment events, runs a model, and hands a calibrated score to a decision layer that combines it with rules and costs to pick an action. Outcomes trickle back as labels over weeks, and training reconstructs exactly what the features looked like at the moment of each payment. Hold that picture; the rest of the lesson adds numbers and mechanisms.

1 · Requirements

Functional. Score every card payment a processor handles for its merchants, before authorisation, and return an action: allow, request 3-D Secure, send to review, or block, plus a risk score and reasons. Merchants can add their own rules ("block if amount > $1,000 and the card's country differs from the shipping country") and allow and block lists. Analysts get a review queue. Disputes, early fraud warnings and review verdicts flow back as labels. Out of scope, said aloud: merchant onboarding risk (fraudulent merchants), anti-money-laundering monitoring and account-takeover detection on login, which reuse much of the same platform.

Quality targets. State both sides of the trade. Keep the false-decline rate below 0.1% of legitimate payments (the figure Stripe publishes for Radar) and maximise fraud caught subject to that, measured in dollars as well as counts. Keep merchants' dispute rates under the card networks' monitoring thresholds. Probabilities must be calibrated, so expected-cost decisions are meaningful.

Latency targets. p99 under 100 ms for the score and decision, because it sits inside the checkout and authorisation path; a timeout falls back to a rules-only decision rather than failing the payment.

Cost target. The objective is total cost: fraud losses + chargeback fees + false-decline cost + review cost + infrastructure. Infrastructure is small next to the first three.

Safety and compliance targets. Card numbers never appear in features or logs in the clear (they are tokenised, as payment-card security standards require); every decision is explainable; features that act as proxies for protected characteristics are excluded; decisions and model versions are auditable for years.

Clarifying questions. "Who bears the fraud loss, the merchant or us?" (it changes the cost function). "What actions can we take besides block?" "How quickly do labels arrive and from where?" "Is the decision made before or after the issuer's?"

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Transactions. Assume 20 million card payments per day: an average of 20,000,000 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 230 per second. Peaks are sharp (Black Friday, ticket sales, flash promotions), so design for 3,000 per second, about 13× the average.

Fraud volume. At 1 in 1,000, 20,000 fraudulent payments a day. If the average fraudulent payment is $150 (fraud skews toward larger, resellable purchases), unmitigated fraud is $3 million a day. A system that catches 80% of fraud dollars saves $2.4 million a day; one that wrongly blocks 0.1% of the 19.98 million good payments declines about 20,000 customers a day. Both numbers matter, and they are about the same size in count.

Velocity counters. Track six entity types per payment (card, device fingerprint, IP address, email, merchant, card BIN, the first digits identifying the issuing bank) over four windows (10 minutes, 1 hour, 24 hours, 30 days), with count, sum and a distinct-count sketch (for example "distinct cards seen on this device") per window. Store one key per entity holding all its windows, so a payment reads six keys and updates six keys. At 3,000 payments per second: 18,000 reads and 18,000 writes per second, comfortable for a small in-memory cluster. Memory: if about 300 million entities are active within 30 days at about 200 bytes each (counters plus small sketches), that is 60 GB; shard across a few nodes with replicas, and expire keys after the longest window.

Training data. 90 days × 20 million = 1.8 billion payments containing 1.8 million frauds. Keeping all positives and 1 in 50 negatives gives 36 million negatives: about 38 million rows. At 1,000 features of about 4 bytes, that is 152 GB, a comfortable single-machine or small-cluster training set. Negatives are weighted by 50 (or the model re-calibrated) so probabilities reflect the true base rate.

Label arrival. Chargebacks arrive weeks to months after the payment (card-network rules give cardholders months to dispute). If the median delay is about 25 days, then a week after a payment only a few percent of its eventual fraud labels exist, and at 30 days perhaps 60%; the lab prints such a maturity curve. So the newest data that can be evaluated reliably is roughly 60-90 days old. That shapes everything about monitoring.

Review capacity. 50 analysts × 7 productive hours × 60 reviews per hour (one a minute) = 21,000 reviews a day. That is about 0.1% of payments, so review is reserved for high-value ambiguous cases, and the decision layer must know the queue's capacity.

3 · The latency budget

One subtlety in the budget: the counter update for the current payment happens after the response, and the stream processor that maintains counters lags by a second or two. A card-testing script firing 10 payments in two seconds would see stale counts. So the scoring service adds the current payment to the counts it read (and can keep a short in-process buffer of the last few seconds per hot entity), so the feature value reflects everything up to this payment.

4 · API

POST /v1/risk/evaluate            (internal, called by the payments service)
  {payment_id, merchant_id, amount, currency, card_token, bin, country,
   device_fingerprint?, ip, email_hash?, shipping?, customer_id?, ts}
  -> {action: allow | three_ds | review | block, risk_score: 0-99,
      reasons: ["card used at 6 merchants in 1h", ...], model_version, rule_ids}

POST /v1/risk/labels             {payment_id, label: fraud | not_fraud,
                                  source: dispute | early_warning | review | merchant, ts}
POST /v1/rules                   {merchant_id?, expression, action, priority}  # merchant rules
GET  /v1/review/queue            # analyst UI

Two details: the request carries tokens and hashes, never raw card numbers or emails; and every label carries its source and time, because labels from different sources have different reliability and arrive at different delays, and training must know when each label became known.

5 · Data model

Decision log (append-only, kept for years): payment ID, time, every feature value used, score, model version, rules fired, action, and whether the payment was in the randomised holdout. Labels: payment ID, label, source, time received. Counter store: key = (entity type, entity ID), value = windowed counts, sums and sketches with a last-updated time. Entity profiles: slower-changing per-entity facts (account age, typical spending, countries seen), recomputed daily. Rules: versioned expressions with owners and expiry dates, because rules written during an attack should not live forever.

The decision log is the single most important table. It makes training data free of training/serving skew (train on what was served), makes every decision auditable and explainable after the fact, and is the input to the counterfactual estimates in section 9.

6 · High-level design

A payment arrives at the payments service, which calls the scoring service synchronously. The scoring service tokenises sensitive fields, issues parallel reads to the counter store, the entity-profile store and a network lookup service (which answers questions such as "how many distinct cards has this device used across all merchants this week?"), assembles the feature vector, runs the model, and passes the calibrated probability to the decision layer. The decision layer computes the expected cost of each action, evaluates platform and merchant rules (which can override), checks review-queue capacity, and returns the action. After responding, the service emits a payment event to the log, which drives the stream processor that updates counters, and writes the decision log.

Offline, a label service collects disputes, early warnings from issuers, review verdicts and merchant reports. A training pipeline builds examples from the decision log (for payments already scored by the current feature set) or by replaying raw events with point-in-time correct feature computation (for new features), joins labels that are mature, trains, calibrates on recent mature data, and evaluates against the current model. A new model is first run in shadow mode, scoring live traffic without acting, then promoted gradually.

7 · Deep dive: features

Payment features come with the request: amount, currency, merchant category, card country versus IP country versus shipping country, time of day, whether the customer is logged in. They help, but alone they are weak: a fraudulent $300 order looks like a legitimate one.

Velocity features carry most of the signal. Examples: payments and declines on this card in the last 10 minutes; distinct merchants for this card today; distinct cards used from this device in the last hour; total amount from this IP today; failed attempts at this merchant in the last 10 minutes. Stripe's post mentions exactly these kinds of signals, "payment velocity", "unusual volume on a card", whether the cardholder's name matches the email, and "the number of cards previously associated with an IP address". Card testing, for instance, is invisible in any single $1 payment and obvious in "40 distinct cards from this device in 10 minutes".

How a counter works: each window is stored as a ring of small time buckets (for a 1-hour window, sixty 1-minute buckets); an increment adds to the current bucket; a read sums the live buckets. Distinct counts use a small sketch such as HyperLogLog (in9) rather than a set. Updates arrive from the stream processor, keyed by payment ID so that a replayed event does not count twice.

Network features come from the platform's scale. A card that is new to this merchant may have paid at 200 other merchants without trouble; a device that is new to this merchant may have been linked to 30 disputed payments elsewhere. These are computed as entity profiles and graph aggregates (cards per device, devices per card, disputes per linked entity), updated by batch and streaming jobs. This is the main advantage a large processor has over a single merchant's in-house system.

Point-in-time correctness. When building training data for a payment made on 3 March at 14:02:11, every feature must be computed from data available at 14:02:11, not later. The classic leak: computing "disputes linked to this device" today, including disputes filed in April about the March payment itself. The model learns that the label predicts the label, scores beautifully offline and fails online. The decision log avoids this for features that were served; new features require replaying events with as-of semantics (the subject of the feature-store design case).

8 · Deep dive: the model and imbalance

Start with gradient-boosted trees (ml10): they handle mixed numeric and categorical features, missing values and odd scales with little preprocessing, train in minutes on tens of millions of rows, and are easy to inspect. As data grows into billions of examples and features include high-cardinality identifiers best represented as learned embeddings, deep networks overtake them. Stripe's post traces exactly this path: from logistic regression to a "Wide & Deep" ensemble of XGBoost and a deep network, to a DNN-only model with a multi-branch architecture inspired by ResNeXt. Removing XGBoost cut training time by over 85%, to under two hours, which let the team iterate faster and train on 10× more data, with continued gains.

Imbalance. With 0.1% positives, train on all positives and a sample of negatives, and correct for the sampling. If negatives are kept with probability 1/50, either give each sampled negative a weight of 50, or train unweighted and then convert: the model's odds are inflated by 50, so true odds = model odds ÷ 50. Then calibrate on recent mature data with an unsampled distribution (isotonic regression or a temperature on the logit, ml15), and check calibration by score bucket. The lab shows why this matters: with calibrated probabilities, an amount-aware rule beats the best fixed threshold; with uncorrected probabilities from 50/50-style training, the same rule blocks far too much and costs over ten times more.

Metrics for model selection. PR-AUC (area under the precision-recall curve) compares models across thresholds and, unlike ROC-AUC, is sensitive to performance on the rare class. At the operating point, report recall at a fixed false-positive rate (for example, at 0.1% of good payments blocked) and dollar-weighted recall, because catching many small frauds and missing the large ones is a bad model.

9 · Deep dive: the decision layer

The model produces P(fraud). The decision is a separate step, and it should minimise expected cost. For a payment of amount A with fraud probability p: allowing costs p × (A + chargeback fee); blocking costs (1 − p) × (margin × A + false-decline friction). Block when the first exceeds the second. Worked example with a $15 fee, 3% margin and $8 friction: for A = $40, allowing costs p × 55 and blocking costs (1 − p) × 9.2, so block when p > 9.2 ÷ 64.2 ≈ 14%. For A = $2,000, allowing costs p × 2,015 and blocking costs (1 − p) × 68, so block when p > 3.3%. The threshold falls as the amount rises. A single fixed threshold is a poor approximation of this rule, and the lab measures the difference.

More than two actions. Step-up authentication costs a little friction (a few percent of good customers abandon at a challenge) and, when it succeeds, shifts fraud liability to the issuer, so it is the right action for a wide middle band of scores. Manual review costs analyst time and delays the order, so it suits high-value, ambiguous payments, and it is capacity-limited: if the queue is full, the next-best action applies. The decision layer computes the expected cost of each action and picks the minimum, then applies rules.

Rules. Rules are written in a small expression language over features and the score, versioned, tested against recent traffic before activation ("this rule would have blocked 412 payments yesterday, 37 of them later disputed"), and given an owner and expiry. Merchants can write their own within limits. Rules respond in minutes, which the model cannot.

10 · Deep dive: delayed and missing labels

Delay. A fraud label is usually a dispute, filed when the cardholder notices, often weeks later. Some signals arrive sooner: issuers send early fraud warnings, analysts' review verdicts arrive in hours, and merchants report fraud they find. Use them in tiers: fast, noisier labels for monitoring and rapid rules; mature labels for training and formal evaluation. When training, exclude recent data whose labels are not mature (or the model learns that recent payments are rarely fraud), or model the delay explicitly by weighting recent negatives as "probably negative so far".

Missing labels for blocked payments. If the system blocks the 0.5% riskiest payments, it never learns which of them were good customers. Over time two problems compound: the model is trained only on allowed payments, so it never sees outcomes in its own high-score region, and the measured fraud rate falls because the obvious fraud is blocked, which makes the next model look worse than it is. The standard remedy is a small counterfactual holdout: for a random fraction of payments, the decision is not taken from the model (or a more lenient threshold applies), with amount caps to limit the cost. Outcomes in the holdout are unbiased; weighting them by the inverse of their selection probability gives estimates of true precision and recall. Stripe's documentation says that "for a small subset of payments, Stripe modifies the reported risk score so we can measure the performance of our models and obtain data for subsequent model development", which is this idea in production.

11 · Deep dive: adversarial drift

Fraud is not a stationary pattern; it is people adapting to the system. When a rule blocks a card-testing pattern at $1, testers move to $3; when device fingerprints are used, they rotate devices. Three time scales of response: rules in minutes (analysts watching dashboards of declines and early warnings by merchant, BIN and device), retraining on a regular cadence (weekly or more often; Stripe's under-two-hour training time makes frequent retraining practical), and new features over weeks, when an attack exposes a blind spot. Monitoring watches for distribution shift in features and scores by segment (merchant, country, BIN range), and alarms on spikes of early fraud warnings for a merchant even when the score distribution looks normal, because a new attack often looks normal to the old model.

12 · Evaluation plan

Offline. Train on data up to day T − 90, evaluate on payments from days T − 90 to T − 60 (mature labels), never with random splits that put a card's future payments into training. Report PR-AUC, recall at a 0.1% false-positive rate, dollar-weighted recall, calibration by score bucket, and the total-cost estimate at the chosen policy, all sliced by merchant category, country and amount band. Use the holdout's unbiased labels to check that offline gains are not artefacts of selection bias.

Shadow and online. Run the candidate model in shadow on live traffic for a week: compare score distributions, how many decisions would change, and the early-warning rate among payments the new model would block and the old one allowed. Then ramp gradually (1%, 10%, 50%) with automatic rollback if block rate, early-warning rate or merchant complaints move beyond limits. Final judgement waits for the labels: a launch review at 60-90 days compares fraud and false-decline costs.

Fairness and explanation checks. Compare false-decline rates across customer segments that should not matter (countries within a merchant's market, card types) and investigate gaps; verify that reasons shown to merchants match the actual top attributions.

13 · Failure modes and trade-offs

A counter store outage removes the strongest features; the model sees defaults and scores everything as low risk. Fall back to stricter rules and alert. A feature pipeline bug (a counter stops updating) is subtler: monitor feature freshness and distributions. A calibration drift after a merchant mix change moves the effective threshold silently; recalibrate on a schedule. A label pipeline break (dispute feed stops) makes the fraud rate look wonderful. A runaway rule written during an incident keeps blocking good customers months later: rules need expiry.

Trade-offs to volunteer: false declines against fraud losses (a business decision expressed as costs), model complexity against latency and iteration speed, holdout size against information, retraining frequency against stability, and merchant-configurable rules against consistency. State the cost model first; most of the answers follow from it.

Why PR curves, not ROC curves

The ROC curve plots recall against the false-positive rate, the fraction of negatives flagged. With 999 negatives per positive, a false-positive rate of 1% sounds tiny but means 10 good customers blocked for every fraud, and moves the ROC curve only slightly. Two models with nearly identical ROC-AUC can differ by a factor of two in precision at the operating point. The precision-recall curve exposes this directly, because precision counts false positives against true positives. Saito and Rehmsmeier's 2015 paper is the standard citation. In practice, report precision and recall at the operating point and recall at a fixed false-positive rate, since that is what the business experiences.

Calibration is what makes costs usable

The expected-cost rule multiplies a probability by money. If the model says 5% and the true rate in that bucket is 0.5%, every decision is wrong by a factor of ten in expected value. Downsampling negatives inflates odds by exactly the sampling factor, which is easy to correct; harder miscalibration comes from distribution shift (a new merchant mix) and from model families that are overconfident (deep networks without a temperature correction, as Guo et al. showed). Calibrate on recent mature data, monitor predicted against observed fraud rate per score bucket, and recalibrate more often than you retrain: a calibration layer is cheap to refit.

Selection bias compounds across model generations

Each model blocks the payments it finds riskiest, so the next model's training data lacks outcomes for those payments. If the old model was wrong about a region (say, it over-blocked a legitimate travel pattern), no data ever arrives to correct it, and the new model inherits the error. Inverse-propensity weighting of holdout data corrects estimates in expectation, but the holdout's variance is high because it is small. Practical systems combine a holdout with "reject inference" heuristics, such as treating payments blocked by a rule that was later found faulty as unlabelled rather than fraudulent, and periodically relaxing thresholds in small, controlled experiments.

Entity resolution and graph features

Fraudsters reuse infrastructure: devices, IP ranges, email patterns, shipping addresses, card ranges bought together. Linking payments through shared entities builds a graph in which fraud clusters. Useful features are simple aggregates over that graph ("disputes among cards that share a device with this card in the last 30 days") rather than heavy graph neural networks, because they are cheap to serve and easy to explain. The engineering challenge is keeping them point-in-time correct: a graph aggregate computed today includes edges and disputes that did not exist at payment time, the exact leak that inflates offline results.

Adversarial evaluation

Offline metrics assume tomorrow's fraud resembles last quarter's. A stronger evaluation simulates adaptation: hold out entire fraud rings or attack types and test whether the model catches them (does it generalise, or did it memorise identifiers?), and measure how quickly performance decays on data further in the future. A model that relies heavily on identity features (specific BINs, IP ranges) often decays fastest, because those are what attackers rotate first.

Training with labels that are still arriving

If the training set includes payments from the last few weeks, many of their frauds have not been reported yet, so they appear as negatives. The model learns that "recent-looking" payments are safe, and that bias is worst on exactly the newest attack patterns. Three ways to handle it: exclude data younger than the maturity window (simple, but the model is always 60-90 days behind); include recent data but down-weight its negatives by the probability that a fraud label would already have arrived at that age, estimated from the maturity curve; or train a second, fast model on early-arriving proxy labels (issuer warnings, review verdicts) for the newest weeks and blend it with the mature-label model. The second option is a small change with a large effect on how quickly the model learns a new pattern.

The economics of step-up authentication

Step-up authentication sits between allowing and blocking, and its cost structure is different from both. A challenge costs friction: some fraction of good customers abandon at the extra step. It also changes who pays for fraud: an authenticated payment that later turns out fraudulent is usually the issuer's loss rather than the merchant's. So the expected cost of a challenge is roughly (1 − p) × abandonment rate × lost margin plus p × (fraud that passes authentication anyway) × the merchant's residual loss. For a wide band of middle scores this beats both alternatives, which is why mature systems widen that band rather than move the block threshold. In regions where regulation requires strong authentication for most online payments, the fraud model's job shifts toward choosing which payments may be exempted, the same expected-cost logic in reverse.

Latency engineering for the tail

The 100 ms p99 is set by the slowest dependency. Techniques: one key per entity so each entity is one read; hedged reads (send a second request to a replica if the first has not answered in, say, 5 ms); feature defaults when a dependency times out, provided the model saw such defaults in training (otherwise a missing feature becomes an out-of-distribution input); and models compiled to efficient native code. Trees are cheap; a large DNN on CPU is several milliseconds, still affordable. The design rule is that a missing feature degrades a decision but never blocks a payment flow.

How it fails in practice

The invisible attack. Account takeover with real customers' saved cards and devices looks legitimate on payment and device features. Losses rise at one merchant while scores look normal. Detection comes from early fraud warnings and customer complaints by merchant; the fix needs new signals from login behaviour, not a retrained payment model.

The over-eager rule. During a card-testing attack an analyst ships "block all payments under $2 from new devices". The attack ends in a day; the rule blocks legitimate low-value subscriptions for three months. Rules need owners, expiry dates and a weekly report of their block counts and later dispute rates.

The broken label feed. A change in the dispute file format silently drops half of the labels. The measured fraud rate halves, the next model trains on "clean" fraud-labelled-as-good data, and recall falls. Monitor label volume per source against payment volume.

The holiday shift. Peak-season traffic has more new customers, more gift shipping addresses and more large orders: legitimate patterns that look like fraud. False declines spike exactly when they are most expensive. Prepare with seasonal features, recalibration on last year's season and temporarily relaxed thresholds for trusted segments.

What to monitor

Block, challenge and review rates by merchant, country and BIN, against the same hour last week; score distribution by segment; early-fraud-warning rate; review queue length and age; feature freshness and missing rates; scoring latency per dependency; calibration (predicted versus observed fraud rate for mature cohorts by score bucket); label volume by source.

The real case: Stripe Radar

Stripe's 2023 engineering post "How we built it: Stripe Radar" describes a system that decides whether to allow each payment "in less than 100 milliseconds", where fraud is "on the order of 1 out of every 1,000 payments", and which "incorrectly blocks just 0.1%" of legitimate payments. Each decision "assesses more than 1,000 characteristics of a potential transaction", including payment velocity, unusual volume on a card, whether the cardholder's name matches the email, and the number of cards previously associated with an IP address. The models evolved from logistic regression to a "Wide & Deep" ensemble of an XGBoost model and a deep neural network, and then to a DNN-only model with a multi-branch architecture inspired by ResNeXt. Removing XGBoost reduced training time "by over 85% (to less than two hours)", although simply removing it without other changes would have cost 1.5% recall. Faster training let the team increase training data tenfold "and still found significant model improvements". Merchants see "risk insights" showing which features contributed to a decline. Stripe's documentation adds the network effect (a 92% chance a business's card has been seen before on Stripe), a 0-99 risk score with 65 and 75 as the default elevated and high-risk levels, and the statement that scores are modified for a small subset of payments to measure model performance. The interview lessons: the latency budget is real, the false-decline rate is a first-class target, model choice followed data scale and iteration speed, and measurement requires deliberately not acting on some decisions.

"""Fraud decisions under imbalance: why accuracy lies, how to pick a threshold by
cost, why the threshold should depend on the amount, and how label delay hides
the truth. NumPy only (Python 3.12)."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
N, FRAUD_RATE = 2_000_000, 0.001                   # 1 in 1,000 payments is fraud
y = rng.random(N) < FRAUD_RATE
amount = rng.lognormal(mean=3.8, sigma=1.0, size=N)  # median ~ $45
amount[y] *= 1.8                                     # fraudsters skew to bigger tickets
# A decent model: its raw score x is higher for fraud, with overlap.
M1, M0, S = 2.0, -2.5, 1.0
x = np.where(y, rng.normal(M1, S, N), rng.normal(M0, S, N))
# Calibrated probability = Bayes' rule on the two score distributions and the 0.1% prior.
prior = np.log(FRAUD_RATE / (1 - FRAUD_RATE))
p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(prior + (M1 - M0) / S**2 * x - (M1**2 - M0**2) / (2 * S**2))))
CALIBRATED = True
if not CALIBRATED:          # a model trained on 50/50 down-sampled data, never re-calibrated
    p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(np.log(p / (1 - p)) - prior)))

CHARGEBACK_FEE, FP_COST = 15.0, 8.0                # $ per missed fraud, $ friction per wrong block
MARGIN = 0.03                                      # merchant margin lost on a blocked good sale

def cost(block):
    missed = ~block & y
    false_pos = block & ~y
    return (amount[missed].sum() + CHARGEBACK_FEE * missed.sum()
            + (MARGIN * amount[false_pos]).sum() + FP_COST * false_pos.sum())

print(f"payments {N:,}, fraud {y.sum():,}; 'never block' accuracy = {1 - y.mean():.4f}")
print(f"{'threshold':>9} {'precision':>9} {'recall':>7} {'blocked good %':>14} {'cost $':>12}")
best = None
for t in [0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9]:
    b = p >= t
    tp = (b & y).sum(); prec = tp / max(b.sum(), 1); rec = tp / y.sum()
    c = cost(b)
    best = min(best or (c, t), (c, t))
    print(f"{t:>9} {prec:>9.2f} {rec:>7.2f} {100 * (b & ~y).sum() / (~y).sum():>14.3f} {c:>12,.0f}")
print(f"cheapest fixed threshold: {best[1]} (${best[0]:,.0f})")

# Value-aware rule: block when expected fraud loss exceeds expected cost of blocking.
loss_if_allow = p * (amount + CHARGEBACK_FEE)
loss_if_block = (1 - p) * (MARGIN * amount + FP_COST)
print(f"amount-aware rule cost: ${cost(loss_if_allow > loss_if_block):,.0f}")

# Label delay: chargebacks arrive weeks later. Assume a lognormal delay, median 25 days.
delay = rng.lognormal(mean=np.log(25), sigma=0.6, size=y.sum())
for day in (7, 14, 30, 60, 90):
    print(f"day {day:>2}: {np.mean(delay <= day):5.0%} of this week's fraud is labelled so far")

Expected output: best fixed threshold around 0.05-0.1 at about $46,000; amount-aware rule about $40,600; uncalibrated amount-aware rule about $750,000; about 2% of fraud labelled after a week and 60% after 30 days. The costs are invented, but the shapes are the lesson: decide by cost, use calibrated probabilities, and do not evaluate on immature labels.

Work it out

A payments platform sees 5M transactions/day, 0.12% fraudulent, average value £85. A blocked legitimate customer costs an estimated £40 in support and churn. Your model at threshold 0.5 gives precision 0.62 and recall 0.71.

(a) What does a day cost at this threshold? (b) Should you move the threshold to precision 0.45 / recall 0.86? (c) What does this tell you about the objective?

Answer

Set up. Fraud per day: 5,000,000 × 0.0012 = 6,000.

(a) At recall 0.71: caught 4,260, missed 1,740. Missed-fraud loss = 1,740 × £85 = £147,900. With precision 0.62, blocks total 4,260 ÷ 0.62 = 6,871, so false positives = 6,871 − 4,260 = 2,611, costing 2,611 × £40 = £104,440. Total ≈ £252,340/day.

(b) At recall 0.86, precision 0.45: caught 5,160, missed 840 → £71,400. Blocks = 5,160 ÷ 0.45 = 11,467, false positives = 6,307 → £252,280. Total ≈ £323,680/day, about £71k/day worse. Do not move it. Catching 900 more frauds costs 3,696 more wrongly blocked customers, and at these prices that trade is bad.

(c) What this really shows. First, the optimum is wherever the marginal fraud caught equals the marginal false-positive cost: sweep the full curve rather than comparing two points. Second, the two cost terms are the same order of magnitude, so neither precision nor recall alone is the objective; total cost is. Third, the average value hides the real lever: if fraud concentrates in high-value payments, an amount-aware rule (block when p × amount exceeds (1 − p) × £40) beats any single threshold, and a middle action such as 3-D Secure for medium scores can cut both costs at once.

Diagnose it

Two weeks after a new model launched, the dashboard shows fraud rate on allowed payments down 40% versus the old model's launch period, and the team wants to celebrate. Block rate is up 25%. What do you check before believing it?

Answer

Label maturity. Two weeks after payment, only a small fraction of eventual chargebacks has arrived; comparing with the old model's period at the same age is the only fair comparison, and the formal answer waits 60-90 days. Look at early fraud warnings, which arrive sooner, as the leading indicator.

Selection effects. A 25% higher block rate removes payments from the allowed pool, so the fraud rate among allowed payments falls almost automatically. The question is what those extra blocks cost: estimate false declines from the randomised holdout (what fraction of holdout payments in the newly blocked score band turned out good) and from customer complaints and retries.

Total cost. Put both sides in money: fraud dollars avoided against false-decline cost for the extra blocks. If the new model mainly shifted the threshold, a better model would have done the same at the old block rate; compare the two models at equal block rates on holdout data before concluding anything.
