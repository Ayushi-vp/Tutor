A social platform receives 100 million new posts, comments, images and videos a day. Somewhere in that stream, every day, are a few known abuse images being re-uploaded, a coordinated harassment campaign against one person, a livestream of something dangerous, spam that links to scams, a teenager posting about self-harm, a news photo of war that is graphic but newsworthy, and a joke that uses a slur in a way its community considers reclaimed. The platform's policies say what to do with each, and the policies differ by country, by surface (a public post or a private message) and by the user's age. They change every few weeks as new harms appear and regulators ask for more.

The first version is usually a keyword list and a report button. Keywords are evaded within hours (a zero for an "o", a space inside a word, text written into an image) and they flag innocent uses. Reports arrive after the damage is done and depend on who happens to see what. The second version trains a classifier per policy and sends everything it flags to a team of moderators, and the review queue becomes three times larger than the team can clear. Someone raises the threshold to make the queue fit, recall drops, and nobody can tell by how much, because the system only ever looks at what it flagged.

Content moderation design is about running expensive judgement only where it is needed, keeping policy separate from models so it can change weekly, using human reviewers well and humanely, and measuring the harm that gets through rather than the work that gets done. Interviewers like it because it combines a cost-driven cascade, rare-class machine learning, human-in-the-loop systems and measurement under adversarial pressure.

This lesson follows the interview: requirements with quality, latency, cost and safety targets; estimates for volumes per modality, compute per stage and reviewer capacity; the API and data model; the architecture; deep dives on the cascade and its thresholds, hash matching, attribute models with a policy layer, the LLM judge, the human review system, and data for rare categories; the evaluation plan; failure modes; OpenAI's published moderation work and Meta's PDQ hashing as the real case; and a lab that computes the cost and recall of a cascade.

What the pipeline decides

For each item, the pipeline outputs an action under the policy that applies: allow; label (a warning screen over graphic content); restrict (keep it up but out of recommendations, or hide it from minors); remove; or escalate (to specialist teams, to law enforcement where the law requires it). It also acts on accounts: warnings, feature limits, suspensions. Some decisions must happen before an item is visible (known abuse imagery, content from accounts already under restriction); most can happen shortly after posting, and some only after user reports or appeals.

Two facts shape the design. Violations are rare: OpenAI's moderation paper observed self-harm in 0.04% of sampled prompts and threatening hate in 0.017%, and platform-wide rates of most categories are a fraction of a percent. And judgement is expensive at the high end: a human reviewer costs on the order of dimes per item and can handle a few hundred items in a shift, while a small classifier costs a tiny fraction of a cent. So the system is built as a cascade: cheap checks on everything, expensive checks on the few items the cheap checks are unsure about.

The mental model: airport security

Every passenger walks through a metal detector (cheap, fast, catches the obvious). A watch list catches known people instantly. Bags that look odd on the X-ray go to a second, slower scan. A few passengers get a hand search by a trained officer. Nobody hand-searches everyone, because it would cost too much and stop the airport, and nobody relies only on the metal detector, because it misses too much. Rules on what is forbidden change; the scanners do not need rebuilding when they do, because officers apply the new rules to what the scanners show. The analogy breaks in a useful place: at an airport, every missed item is a security failure, while in moderation the harm of a missed item depends on how many people see it, which is why reach drives priorities.

Follow one image through the figure. A user uploads a photo. Its PDQ hash is computed and compared with the hash bank: no match. A cheap image classifier scores it 0.31 for graphic violence and near zero for everything else, above the 0.2 gate for that attribute, so the post goes live but is queued for stage 2. Twenty seconds later the policy-aware model reads the photo, its caption ("aftermath of the protest downtown") and the policy for graphic content, and decides it is newsworthy violence that should carry a warning screen rather than be removed; the policy layer applies the label. No human sees it unless someone reports it. If the model had been unsure, the item would have gone to a reviewer queue with a deadline set by severity and the post's growing view count.

Two more ideas complete the picture. Models score attributes that change slowly (is there nudity? a weapon? a threat against a person? a slur?), and a separate policy layer turns those scores, plus context such as region, surface and the viewer's age, into actions; when policy changes, the layer changes and the models do not. And success is measured by prevalence, the share of all content views that were of violating content, estimated by labelling random samples, because the system's own flags can never reveal what it missed.

1 · Requirements

Functional. Evaluate every new post, comment, image, video and profile change against the platform's policies; take actions on content and accounts; accept user reports; run human review with tooling; handle appeals; report to authorities where the law requires; let policy teams change policies and see the impact before launch. Categories include child safety, terrorism and violent extremism, hate speech, harassment, violence and graphic content, adult content, self-harm, spam and scams. Out of scope, said aloud: legal takedown requests workflows and misinformation fact-checking partnerships, which reuse the review tooling.

Quality targets. Known violating media (in hash banks) is blocked before it is visible, essentially 100%. For each category: prevalence below a target set by the policy team and trending down; precision of automated removals above 95% (audited weekly), because wrongful removals hurt users and trust; appeal overturn rate below a target per category.

Latency targets. Synchronous checks at posting time (hash match and cheap classifiers) within 150 ms at p99 so posting stays fast. Asynchronous model review within a minute. Human review deadlines by severity: the most severe categories within an hour, high severity within 24 hours, others within 72. Live video is checked continuously, with frame samples every few seconds.

Cost target. A budget per million items, dominated by human review; the design should minimise reviewer hours per unit of prevented harm.

Safety targets for the system itself. Moderator wellbeing protections (blurring, greyscale, exposure limits); no leakage of reported content or reporter identity; audit trails for every action; fairness across languages and dialects measured, not assumed.

Clarifying questions. "Which categories must be blocked before posting, and which can be removed after?" "Which languages and regions?" "What is the reviewer headcount and budget?" "Do we act on private messages, and with what privacy constraints?"

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Volume. 100 million items per day: 80 million text items, 18 million images and 2 million videos averaging 60 seconds. Average 100M ÷ 86,400 ≈ 1,160 items per second; with a 2× peak, about 2,500 items per second.

Stage 0: hashing. Every image and sampled video frame gets a perceptual hash. Meta's PDQ hash is 256 bits (32 bytes). A bank of 10 million known violating hashes is 320 MB, small enough to hold in memory on every matching server. Matching means finding bank hashes within Hamming distance 31 (PDQ's recommended starting threshold); with indexing tricks this costs microseconds per lookup. Video: 2M videos × 60 seconds, sampling one frame per second, is 120 million frame hashes a day, plus whole-video hashes.

Stage 1: cheap classifiers. A distilled text model of about 66 million parameters reading 128 tokens costs about 2 × 66M × 128 ≈ 17 GFLOPs per item; 80M items is 1.4 × 1018 FLOPs a day, about 1.6 × 1013 per second, a fraction of one GPU or a few hundred CPU cores. An image encoder of the CLIP ViT-B/16 class costs roughly 17-18 GFLOPs per image; 18M images plus 120M video frames is about 2.4 × 1018 FLOPs a day, a few GPUs. The cheap stage is cheap: under ten GPUs for everything.

Stage 2: policy-aware model. Suppose the cheap stage passes 5% of items (5 million a day) as possibly violating. A multimodal model of about 8 billion parameters reads a policy excerpt (cached, since it is the same for every item in a category) plus about 1,100 tokens of item content (text plus image tokens) and writes a short decision: 5M × 1,100 × 16 GFLOPs ≈ 8.8 × 1019 FLOPs a day, about 1015 per second, two H100s at 50% of dense BF16 on average and four or five at peak. Running it on all 100 million items instead would need about twenty times as many, roughly 40-80 GPUs. At API prices of, say, $0.15 per million input tokens, 5.5 billion tokens a day is about $825; on everything it would be about $16,500 a day.

Stage 3: humans. Suppose 0.4% of items reach review: model-uncertain items, user reports (deduplicated per item) and appeals, about 400,000 per day. At an average of 40 seconds per decision and 6.5 productive hours per shift (with wellbeing breaks), one reviewer handles 6.5 × 3,600 ÷ 40 = 585 items a day, so the queue needs about 680 reviewers working every day, around 1,000 headcount with weekends and leave. At about $0.20 per decision, that is $80,000 a day, around $29 million a year: more than all the compute combined by two orders of magnitude.

The insight to say out loud. The cascade is not mainly about saving GPU money; the GPUs are cheap. It is about spending reviewer time only where machines are uncertain and where the harm is greatest, because reviewer time is both the most expensive and the scarcest resource, and the most taxing for the people involved.

The simulation endpoint deserves mention: a policy change is backtested against labelled recent data before launch, the moderation equivalent of a dry run, so the policy team sees how many decisions would change and in which categories.

5 · Data model

Items (ID, author, surface, region, created time, media references, hashes). Signals: per item, per model, per attribute: score and model version, append-only. Decisions: per item, the action, policy version, which stage decided, reviewer ID if human, reasons, time; append-only so the history of an item is auditable. Hash banks: hash, type (PDQ, video), category, source (internal or shared bank), added time. Review tasks: item, reason, severity, reach estimate, deadline, queue, assigned reviewer, status. Labels: from review, from audits and from the prevalence sample, kept separate because they come from different sampling processes and must not be mixed when estimating rates.

6 · High-level design

When an item is posted, the posting service calls the synchronous moderation service: it computes media hashes and checks banks, runs cheap text and image classifiers, and asks the policy layer for a decision. Most items are allowed immediately; known violating media is blocked; some posts are held. Every item, whatever the synchronous result, is written to a moderation log (in1). Asynchronous workers consume the log: heavier classifiers (video, audio transcription, OCR of text in images), the policy-aware large model for items above a gate, and account-level signals (a new account posting the same link 200 times). The policy layer re-evaluates with the new signals and may remove, restrict or create a review task. User reports and appeals also create review tasks. The review system routes tasks to reviewers by language, category and priority, records decisions, and feeds them back as labels. A measurement pipeline independently samples views for prevalence labelling. A training pipeline uses labels from review, active learning and audits to retrain models, which ship through offline evaluation and shadow mode.

7 · Deep dive: the cascade and its thresholds

Every stage has two thresholds, which split its score range into three bands: above the action threshold, act automatically; between the action and escalation thresholds, pass to the next stage; below the escalation threshold, allow and stop. The cheap stage typically only has the lower threshold (it rarely acts alone except on the most confident scores); the policy model has both; humans decide.

The gate caps recall. Anything the cheap stage allows is never seen by a later stage (unless reported). So the cheap gate's threshold sets the ceiling on the system's recall, and it is chosen for high recall, not precision. The lab shows this: with its synthetic classifiers, a gate that passes 22% of items to the expensive model keeps 99.3% of violations; tightening it to pass 6% keeps 95.2%. The cost saving from tightening is real, but the recall loss is permanent.

Thresholds are cost decisions. As in fraud detection (hd4), set them where the marginal cost of a wrong action equals the marginal cost of a missed violation, which differs by category: for child safety the cost of a miss is enormous and thresholds are set for recall, accepting more review; for low-severity spam, precision matters more. Calibrated scores per category (ml15) make thresholds interpretable and stable across model versions. In the lab, raising the automatic-removal threshold from 0.9 to 0.97 nearly eliminates wrong automatic removals but sends 80% more items to humans, a trade the policy team must decide per category.

Score bands and reach. An item in the uncertain band with a few views can wait for review; one in the same band that is going viral cannot. The policy layer can apply a reach limit (keep it out of recommendations and search) while it waits, an action whose cost is low if the item turns out fine.

8 · Deep dive: hash matching

A cryptographic hash (such as SHA-256) changes completely if one pixel changes, so it only finds byte-identical copies. A perceptual hash summarises what an image looks like, so resized, recompressed or slightly edited copies produce nearly the same hash. Meta's open-source PDQ algorithm converts the image to luminance, downsamples it, computes a two-dimensional discrete cosine transform (the same transform JPEG uses), keeps a 16 × 16 block of low-frequency coefficients, and sets each of the 256 bits by whether that coefficient is above the median. Two hashes are compared by Hamming distance, the number of differing bits. Meta's documentation reports that random pairs of unrelated images average about 128 (half the bits), and recommends treating distances of 31 or less as a match and discarding hashes with a quality score of 49 or below (featureless images such as solid colours, which would match each other spuriously).

Finding all bank hashes within distance 31 of a query, among millions, needs an index. A classic trick is multi-index hashing: split each 256-bit hash into chunks; by the pigeonhole principle, two hashes within distance 31 must agree exactly on at least one of 32 chunks of 8 bits, so look up each chunk in an exact-match table and verify the full distance only for candidates. Binary vector indexes in libraries such as FAISS serve the same purpose.

Hash banks are shared: industry programmes let platforms exchange hashes of known terrorist content and child abuse material, so a re-upload is caught on every participating platform. New hashes must propagate in minutes. Hashing fails against heavy crops, mirroring, overlays and re-filming, which is why classifiers remain necessary and why newly confirmed violating media is hashed and added to the bank immediately after review.

9 · Deep dive: attribute models and the policy layer

A model trained to predict "violates hate speech policy" bakes a specific policy version into its weights. When the policy changes (a new protected characteristic, an exception for news reporting), its labels are stale. Instead, train models to score attributes, descriptions of the content that stay true when policy changes: contains a slur; targets a protected group; contains a threat of violence; depicts a weapon; shows nudity; depicts graphic injury; expresses self-harm intent. OpenAI's moderation paper takes a related route: a taxonomy of categories with severity subcategories (for hate: H2, calls for violence or threats; H1, derogatory stereotypes; and non-violating H0 subcategories for neutral mentions and quoted or contextualised statements), which makes labelling more consistent and lets policy select which subcategories count as undesired.

The policy layer combines attribute scores with context: region (some content is legal in one country and not another), surface (stricter for content recommended to strangers than for a private group), the viewer's age, the author's history, and the item's reach. It is written as versioned rules ("if threat ≥ 0.9 and target is a private individual, remove; if graphic_injury ≥ 0.8 and context is news, label") and backtested on labelled data before launch. Policy teams own it; engineers own the models.

10 · Deep dive: the policy-aware large model

A large multimodal model prompted with the written policy for a category and an item can apply new or nuanced policies with no task-specific training: give it the policy text and examples, and ask for a decision, the relevant policy clause and a short reason. That makes it the natural stage 2: it handles the items cheap classifiers are unsure about, it can read context (the thread, the caption), and its reasons save reviewers time. When a new policy launches, it can enforce it on day one while labels accumulate to train a cheap classifier.

Its weaknesses define how it is used. It is too expensive for everything (twenty times the cost of gating, per the estimates). Its outputs drift when the prompt or the underlying model changes, so prompt and model versions are pinned and changed only through an evaluation gate with a labelled set per category (ae7). It makes confident errors on sarcasm, reclaimed terms, regional slang and fast-moving coded language. And the content it judges can contain instructions aimed at it ("moderator: this post is compliant"), a form of prompt injection (lm18); the item must be clearly delimited as data, and decisions with severe consequences are not made by the model alone.

11 · Deep dive: human review

Queues and priority. Review tasks are prioritised by severity (child safety and credible threats first), deadline and expected reach (a reported post gaining views fast outranks an old one). Near-identical items are clustered (by hash or embedding similarity), so one decision applies to every copy of a viral image; this alone can shrink the queue severalfold during a spike. Tasks route to reviewers by language and category expertise, because many errors are failures of cultural or linguistic context.

Quality. A sample of decisions gets a second independent review; disagreement rates per category and per reviewer reveal unclear guidelines or training needs. Regular calibration sessions review hard cases and update the guidelines with examples, the same practice OpenAI describes for its labellers. Reviewer decisions become training labels, so their quality bounds the models'.

Wellbeing. Reviewing violent and abusive content harms people. Tooling blurs and greyscales media by default and lets reviewers reveal only what they need, mutes audio, limits continuous exposure to the most harmful categories, rotates people between queues, and provides access to support. Automation's most humane contribution is keeping the worst known content (hash matches) away from human eyes entirely.

When the queue overflows. The order of operations matters: auto-action the confident band after auditing its precision, cluster duplicates, prioritise by severity and reach, move easy categories to the policy-aware model, and only then raise thresholds on low-severity categories, with the recall loss measured by the prevalence sample.

12 · Deep dive: data for rare categories

Random samples contain almost no examples of rare categories, so labelling random content wastes budget. Active learning chooses what to label. OpenAI's pipeline combined three samplers: random samples (to keep the data representative and calibration honest), samples with high model scores for each category (likely positives), and uncertainty sampling (scores closest to 0.5). They report this captured up to 22× more undesired samples than random sampling for rare events and improved rare-category performance by up to 10×. For a category with no data at all, they generated synthetic examples with a language model from templates, labelled them, and used them to start the loop; they also found that large amounts of noisy synthetic data hurt once good labelled data existed.

Label quality. Measuring labeller accuracy on random items is meaningless when 99.9% are negative (everyone agrees on the negatives). OpenAI's audits instead drew, per category, ten items labelled undesired and ten with high model scores, and computed F1 against auditors' labels. They also found mislabels by cross-scoring: train on half the data, score the other half, and re-label disagreements if a sampled audit shows more than 30% of them are mislabelled.

13 · Evaluation plan

Prevalence. Sample content views uniformly (each view equally likely), have trained labellers judge the viewed content against current policy, and estimate the share of views that were violating, with confidence intervals, per category, surface and region. Meta defines prevalence exactly this way: estimated views of violating content divided by estimated total views. It measures harm experienced, including everything the system missed.

Per-stage metrics. For each classifier, precision and recall at its thresholds on a labelled evaluation set that includes random samples (for recall) and active-learning samples (for coverage of rare cases), sliced by language and region. For automated actions, weekly precision audits. For humans, agreement rates and appeal overturn rates.

Operational metrics. Proactive rate (the share of actioned content found before any report), time to action, views before action, queue age by severity. Before any model or policy change: offline evaluation on labelled sets, shadow mode on live traffic, then staged rollout with prevalence and overturn rates as guardrails.

Fairness. False-positive rates per dialect and language on dialect-labelled sets, counterfactual templates that swap identity terms, and appeal overturn rates by region. OpenAI found an early model classified the bare phrase "black women." as hateful, and fixed it with curated templates contrasting identity groups with random objects.

14 · Failure modes and trade-offs

Evasion waves: a new misspelling or image-of-text trick spreads; cheap classifiers miss it; reports rise. Response: normalisation and OCR, targeted rules, hashes, active learning on the new pattern. Over-removal after a model update: a new classifier flags a benign trending phrase; precision audits and appeal rates spike. Response: rollback, then targeted data. Queue collapse during a crisis: a violent event produces a flood of re-uploads; clustering and fast hashing of the original media are the main defence, plus temporary reach limits on new uploads matching the event's embedding. Policy drift between layers: guidelines for reviewers and the policy layer's rules diverge, so humans and machines make different decisions on the same content; keep one source of truth for policy with versioning.

The trade-offs: recall against wrongful removals (per category, by severity), the cheap gate's recall against stage-2 cost, automation against human judgement, speed against accuracy (reach limits as the middle ground), global consistency against local context, and privacy (private messages) against safety.

Why recall measurement needs an independent sample

Every quantity the pipeline produces about itself, flags, removals, review outcomes, is conditioned on the pipeline having noticed the item. Estimating recall from those numbers is circular. The prevalence sample breaks the circle: views are sampled with known probabilities, independent of any model score, so the share of violating views among them estimates the true rate, and the share of those that the system had already actioned estimates recall. The statistical challenge is rarity: if prevalence is 0.05%, a sample of 100,000 views contains about 50 violating ones, giving a wide confidence interval. Stratified sampling (more samples from surfaces or content types with higher risk, re-weighted in the estimate) narrows it. Treat the prevalence sample as permanent infrastructure with its own labelling budget.

Cascades and correlated errors

A cascade is only as good as the independence of its stages' errors. If the cheap classifier and the large model both miss the same kind of content (say, both rely on the words in the text and the violation is in the image), adding the second stage adds cost without recall. Diversity helps: different modalities, different training data, an LLM reading policy text versus a classifier trained on labels. The lab's large-model score is deliberately correlated with the cheap score (as in real systems where both see similar inputs); in a real evaluation, check recall of the second stage specifically on items the first stage scored low, from the prevalence sample.

Multi-task attribute models

Training one shared encoder with many attribute heads (nudity, violence, weapons, slurs, threats) is cheaper to serve than a model per attribute: one forward pass gives every score. Heads for rare attributes benefit from representation learned on common ones. The cost is coupling: retraining for one attribute changes the shared layers and can shift every other head's scores, so every head's calibration and thresholds must be re-checked on each release, and thresholds should be expressed in calibrated probabilities rather than raw scores.

Context changes meaning

"I will kill you" is a threat in a reply to a stranger and a joke between friends in a game chat; a graphic photo is evidence in a news report and gore in a harassment campaign; a slur is an attack from an outsider and reclaimed language inside a community. OpenAI's taxonomy includes explicit non-violating subcategories (contextualised violence, quoted hate speech for commentary) for this reason. Models that see only the item fail on context; the remedy is passing context (the thread, the relationship, the surface) to models that can use it, mostly the large model and humans, and designing labelling guidelines that say how context changes the label.

Adversarial dynamics and the value of speed

Evaders probe the system, find what passes and share it. The half-life of an evasion technique depends on how fast the platform responds: hours if hashes, normalisation rules and targeted classifiers can ship quickly, weeks if every fix waits for a retraining cycle. That is the case for layered defences with different speeds, the same argument as rules alongside models in fraud detection. Red-teaming before each model release, as OpenAI describes, finds some evasions before adversaries do; for example, it found models over-triggering on a token common in short academic tweet datasets.

Deduplication is the cheapest capacity

Abuse is repetitive: spam campaigns post the same text with small variations, harassment brigades copy the same message, violating images circulate as near-copies. Clustering items by hash, by text shingles (overlapping word sequences) or by embedding similarity lets one decision apply to a whole cluster, and lets the system act on new members of a known-bad cluster automatically. During a spike, this is often worth more than any model improvement. The risk is over-propagation: a cluster formed by loose similarity can sweep in innocent items, so propagated actions use tighter similarity thresholds than clustering for review, and propagated removals are audited separately.

Legal and regulatory constraints shape the design

Some categories carry legal duties: in many jurisdictions, platforms must report child sexual abuse material to designated authorities and preserve evidence, and regulations increasingly require statements of reasons for removals, appeal processes, risk assessments and transparency reports. These requirements turn into concrete design features: decision logs with reasons, appeal workflows with deadlines, data retention rules per category, and measurement pipelines that can produce audited numbers. Designing them in from the start is far cheaper than retrofitting.

How it fails in practice

The crisis flood. A violent event is livestreamed; within an hour, thousands of re-uploads appear, edited, cropped and re-filmed to evade hashes. Queues explode. What works: hashing confirmed copies immediately and sharing hashes across platforms, clustering near-duplicates by embedding similarity so one review decision covers a cluster, temporarily tightening thresholds for uploads that resemble the original, and limiting reach on new uploads in the affected topic until reviewed.

The silent recall drop. A model update improved precision and the queue shrank, and everyone was pleased. Prevalence rose over the next month. The new model's gate threshold had been tuned on a labelled set without enough examples of a new evasion pattern. Only the prevalence sample could show it; every internal flag-based metric looked better.

Over-enforcement on a language. A classifier trained mostly on one dialect flags common expressions in another as abusive. Appeals in that region rise and overturn rates are high. Detection: per-language appeal overturn rates and per-dialect evaluation sets; fix with dialect-aware labellers and data.

LLM judge drift. The provider updates the model behind an API alias and the policy model's removal rate on harassment changes by 30% overnight. Pin model versions, run the labelled evaluation set daily against the production configuration, and alert on decision-rate changes per category.

Reviewer backlog at the weekend. Review capacity follows working shifts in each reviewer location; reports follow user activity, which peaks at weekends and evenings. A design that only balances daily totals will breach severity deadlines every Saturday night. Plan capacity per hour and per language, and let the policy layer apply temporary reach limits to high-reach items waiting in a backlog.

What to monitor

Per category and region: decision volumes by stage and action, score distributions, automated-action precision from audits, queue length and age by severity, time to action, views before action, proactive rate, appeal volume and overturn rate, prevalence with confidence intervals, and reviewer exposure and agreement metrics.

The real case: OpenAI's moderation system and Meta's PDQ

OpenAI's paper "A Holistic Approach to Undesired Content Detection in the Real World" (Markov et al., AAAI 2023) describes the classifier behind its moderation endpoint. It covers a taxonomy of five top-level categories (sexual, hate, violence, self-harm, harassment) with severity subcategories, and a model that predicts eight categories. The data lessons are the most transferable. Rarity: among sampled prompts, self-harm appeared in 0.04% and threatening hate in 0.017%. Active learning, combining random, high-score and uncertainty sampling, captured up to 22× more undesired samples and improved rare-category performance by up to 10×. Label quality needed concrete instructions, regular calibration sessions and audits designed for rare classes. Curated synthetic templates contrasting identity groups with random objects raised hateful-content AUPRC from 0.417 to 0.551 with 69,000 examples and reduced false positives on identity terms, while large amounts of noisy synthetic data hurt. Red-teaming found over-triggering on tokens such as "#". Meta's open-source PDQ hashing, published with its ThreatExchange tools, shows the cheapest stage in detail: a 256-bit perceptual hash compared by Hamming distance, with random pairs averaging about 128 bits apart, a recommended match threshold of 31 and a quality cutoff of 49 to discard featureless images. Together they show both ends of the cascade: hashing for the known, careful data work for the rare and new.

"""A moderation cascade: cheap classifier -> expensive model -> humans.
Compare cost, recall and human load against running the expensive model on
everything. NumPy only (Python 3.12). Prices are illustrative assumptions."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
N = 1_000_000                 # one day's sample of posts (scale results by 100 for 100M/day)
PREV = 0.01                   # 1% violate policy
bad = rng.random(N) < PREV

def scores(sep):              # a classifier: higher sep = better separation
    z = np.where(bad, rng.normal(sep, 1, N), rng.normal(0, 1, N))
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(z - sep / 2) * 1.7))
cheap = scores(3.2)                                    # small model, CPU
big = 0.3 * cheap + 0.7 * scores(4.5)                  # large model: correlated but much sharper

COST = {"cheap": 0.000002, "big": 0.0006, "human": 0.20}   # $ per item

def run(t_cheap, t_auto, t_review, use_cascade=True):
    if use_cascade:
        to_big = cheap >= t_cheap                      # stage 1 gate
    else:
        to_big = np.ones(N, bool)
    s = np.where(to_big, big, 0.0)
    auto = s >= t_auto                                 # confident: act automatically
    review = (s >= t_review) & ~auto                   # uncertain: send to humans
    removed = auto | (review & bad)                    # humans are assumed accurate
    cost = (N * COST["cheap"] * use_cascade + to_big.sum() * COST["big"]
            + review.sum() * COST["human"])
    return dict(big_calls=int(to_big.sum()), human=int(review.sum()),
                recall=(removed & bad).sum() / bad.sum(),
                wrong_auto=int((auto & ~bad).sum()), cost=cost)

print(f"posts {N:,}, violating {bad.sum():,}")
print(f"{'setting':34} {'big calls':>10} {'human':>7} {'recall':>7} {'wrong auto':>10} {'$/day':>8}")
for name, args in [
    ("big model on everything", (0, 0.9, 0.5, False)),
    ("cascade, gate 0.05", (0.05, 0.9, 0.5)),
    ("cascade, gate 0.20", (0.20, 0.9, 0.5)),
    ("cascade, gate 0.50", (0.50, 0.9, 0.5)),
    ("gate 0.20, auto-remove >= 0.97", (0.20, 0.97, 0.5)),
    ("gate 0.20, review band 0.3-0.9", (0.20, 0.9, 0.3)),
]:
    r = run(*args)
    print(f"{name:34} {r['big_calls']:>10,} {r['human']:>7,} {r['recall']:>7.3f}"
          f" {r['wrong_auto']:>10,} {r['cost']:>8,.0f}")

# The cheap gate's recall caps the whole cascade: anything it drops is never seen again.
for t in (0.05, 0.2, 0.5):
    print(f"gate {t}: passes {np.mean(cheap >= t):.1%} of posts, keeps {np.mean(cheap[bad] >= t):.1%} of violations")

Expected output: the big model on everything costs about $2,100 a day for this sample with recall about 0.995; a gate at 0.2 cuts expensive calls by 78% and cost to about $1,100 with recall about 0.99; a gate at 0.5 is cheaper again but drops recall to about 0.95, because the gate kept only 95% of violations. Raising auto-removal to 0.97 removes the last wrongful automatic action but nearly doubles human reviews. Every threshold is a policy decision with a price.

Work it out

A platform takes 8M posts/day. Hash matching catches 0.4% instantly. Classifiers flag 3% of the rest for review. You have 120 moderators working 7 productive hours a day at 45 seconds per item.

(a) Does the queue clear? (b) If not, what are your options, and which is best? (c) The safety team wants recall raised, which would flag 5%. What do you say?

Answer

(a) After hashing, 8,000,000 × 0.996 = 7,968,000 posts reach the classifiers. 3% flagged = 239,040 items/day. Human capacity: 120 × 7 × 3,600 ÷ 45 = 67,200 items/day. The queue receives 3.6× what it can process, so it does not clear: it grows by about 172,000 items a day and the backlog becomes unbounded within a week.

(b) The options, from worst to best:

  • Hire 3.6× the moderators. Roughly 310 more people. Real, slow and expensive, but it is the honest baseline everything else is compared against.
  • Raise the flagging threshold until volume fits capacity. This works arithmetically and is the wrong first move: it silently reduces recall and you will not notice what you stopped catching.
  • Auto-action the confident tail. The score distribution is not uniform: if the top 20% of flags are 97% precise in an audit, remove them automatically and never spend a human on them. That frees about 48,000 review slots for the genuinely ambiguous cases.
  • Cluster and prioritise. Deduplicate near-identical items so one decision covers many, and sort the queue by severity and reach so the items that matter most are reviewed first; let low-severity, low-reach items expire into a reach-limited state rather than wait.
  • Add a policy-aware model stage before humans for categories where it is accurate, so humans see only what it is unsure about.

(c) Flagging 5% is 398,400 items a day, 5.9× capacity. Raising recall at the gate without changing the downstream stages just converts recall into backlog, and an unreviewed flag protects nobody. Agree on the goal, then fund it: show the safety team the cost per additional violation caught, propose doing it for the high-severity categories first (where a miss is most costly), and pair it with the auto-action and clustering steps so the human load stays within capacity. Measure the result with the prevalence sample, not with the flag count.

Design it

A new policy launches in two weeks: content that promotes a newly banned category of dangerous "challenge" videos must be removed. There are no training labels. What do you ship on launch day, and how do you get to a cheap classifier?

Answer

Launch day. Write the policy text with examples and counter-examples (news coverage of the challenge is allowed; promoting or demonstrating it is not). Route candidate items to the policy-aware multimodal model: select candidates with cheap signals that already exist (video and caption embeddings similar to known examples, hashtags, audio fingerprints of the challenge's soundtrack). Remove only above a conservative confidence and send the rest to a trained reviewer queue. Hash every confirmed video for re-upload blocking. Backtest the prompt on a few hundred hand-labelled items before launch.

Weeks after. Reviewer decisions and the model's high-confidence calls become labels; active learning adds uncertain items. Once there are a few thousand clean labels, train a cheap attribute head ("depicts dangerous challenge") on the shared video encoder, calibrate it, and move the policy model behind it as stage 2. Track prevalence for the new category with a targeted sample, because the general prevalence sample is too coarse for a new, rare category.
