A shopper types "nike pegasus 40 womens size 8" into a marketplace's search box. The marketplace has 120 million listings from a million sellers. Among them are about forty listings of exactly that shoe in that size, a few hundred of the same shoe in other sizes, thousands of other running shoes, and millions of things that merely contain the word "nike". The shopper will look at the first four or five results on a phone, maybe scroll to the tenth, and decide within seconds whether this site has what they want. If the first screen shows a men's Pegasus 39, a phone case with a swoosh and a sponsored sock, they leave. That single query is the whole product.

Now multiply it by 300 million searches a day, in dozens of languages, with misspellings ("pegasos"), vague queries ("gift for runner"), queries that are really product codes ("DV3854-001"), and queries whose best answer changes hourly as stock sells out and prices change. A search team lives between two kinds of failure. The visible kind is a bad first page: wrong items, missing items, an empty result. The invisible kind is worse: the ranking slowly learns from its own clicks that whatever it showed first is what people want, sellers who game titles rise, and nobody notices because every dashboard is relative to last week.

The "search ranking system" design question asks you to build the machine that picks those first few results. It is a favourite senior interview because it tests almost everything at once: classic information retrieval (inverted indexes and BM25), modern embeddings, the economics of a multi-stage funnel under a latency budget, learning to rank and its metrics, the statistics of biased click data, and the operational reality of an index that changes every second. It also tests judgment, because the interviewer will push on what you would trade away when you cannot have everything.

This lesson follows the interview. It starts with requirements that include quality, latency, cost and safety targets; does the estimates by hand (queries per second, candidates per stage, features per request, the p99 budget split by stage, index and log sizes, CPU counts, the cost of evaluating a release); defines the API and data model; draws the architecture; then goes deep on four places where systems really break: query understanding, the funnel's recall, learning to rank with NDCG and MRR worked by hand, and position bias with click models. It closes with the evaluation plan, failure modes, Airbnb's two papers on moving search ranking to deep learning, and a lab that computes NDCG and trains a tiny LambdaRank model.

The theory of each piece already has a home in the Search & recsys track: BM25 in rec2, hybrid search in rec5, ranking models in rec6, learning to rank in rec7, metrics in rec8, biases in rec10. Here the subject is the system: how those pieces fit, what they cost, and how they fail together.

The problem in one sentence

Given a query, a user and a moment, return a short ordered list of listings that maximises the chance the user finds and buys what they want, within a quarter of a second, from a catalogue of 120 million that changes constantly. Each part of that sentence becomes a requirement: "short ordered list" means only the top few positions matter; "the user" means personalisation and context; "a moment" means stock, price and freshness; "a quarter of a second" means a budget; "120 million" means you cannot score everything with your best model.

The mental model: a library with a fast clerk and a slow expert

Picture a vast library and a patron at the desk asking for "a beginner book on sourdough". First a reference librarian interprets the request: fixes the spelling, notes that "beginner" matters and that "sourdough" belongs under baking (query understanding). Then a fast clerk runs to the catalogue cards and pulls every card mentioning sourdough or bread starters, plus cards the librarian's intuition says are similar, a cartful of a thousand or so (retrieval). An assistant flips through the cart and discards the obviously unpromising ones in seconds each (the first-pass ranker). Finally an expert reads the remaining few hundred carefully, considering the patron's level, what is in stock and what similar patrons liked, and orders them (the main ranker). The head librarian looks over the final stack, makes sure it is not ten copies of the same book and that the reserved items are excluded, and hands over the top dozen (re-ranking and rules).

The analogy carries three lessons. The expert never sees books the clerk did not pull, so the clerk's job is completeness, not precision. Time is spent unequally: microseconds per book for the assistant, much more per book for the expert. And the library learns: the head librarian notes which books patrons actually borrowed, and that record trains everyone next time. Where the analogy breaks is the learning: patrons mostly borrow books from the top of the stack they were handed, so the record says as much about the librarian's ordering as about the books. Untangling that is the position-bias problem, and it is the hardest part of the lesson.

A reader who stops here has the right picture: interpret the query without losing anything, retrieve broadly from two kinds of index, rank in two passes of increasing cost, assemble the page under rules, and learn from logs that are biased by the system's own choices. The rest of the lesson puts numbers on each box and shows where it breaks.

One distinction to hold onto from the start: search differs from the home-feed recommender of hd3 because the user has told you what they want. Relevance to the query is a hard constraint that personalisation may only refine, never override; a feed can show anything interesting, while a search for running shoes that returns a great jacket has failed.

1 · Requirements

Functional. Users search the marketplace with free text plus optional filters (category, price range, size, condition, shipping location) and get a paginated, ranked list of listings. Results are personalised lightly (the user's location, size history and past categories) and respect hard constraints: only in-stock listings that ship to the user, nothing the seller or the law has blocked. The system logs every search and what was shown for training and analysis. Out of scope, said aloud so the interviewer can object: typeahead (in11), sponsored-listing auctions (hd10 covers ads), and the catalogue system itself.

Quality targets. These need to be measurable, so tie each to a metric the evaluation plan will compute. Offline, NDCG@10 on a human-judged evaluation set must not regress by more than 0.5 points at launch, and known-item queries (a model number, an exact title) must find the item at rank 1 at least 90% of the time (MRR above 0.9 on that slice). Online, the goal is purchases per search; zero-result rate under 2% of searches; abandonment (a search with no engagement) not up by more than 0.2 points in any experiment.

Latency targets. Server-side p99 of 250 ms for the first page and p50 near 100 ms, because each 100 ms of delay measurably reduces engagement in e-commerce search. Later pages may be slower but are cheaper because candidates can be cached from the first page.

Freshness targets. A price or stock change visible in search within 60 seconds (showing an out-of-stock item at rank 1 wastes the most valuable slot); a new listing searchable within 5 minutes; ranking models retrained at least weekly, with features as fresh as hd8's online store allows.

Cost target. Serving cost per thousand searches budgeted, say under a cent of compute, which in practice means CPU ranking for the bulk of the traffic, with any GPU model justified by measured lift.

Safety and fairness targets. Prohibited items never appear (a hard filter enforced after ranking, not a model output); queries with sensitive intent (self-harm, weapons) follow a policy; no single seller takes more than a set share of the first page for broad queries; adult content excluded unless the user opted in; personal data in logs governed (ae5).

Clarifying questions. "Is the business goal purchases, revenue or long-term retention?" (it decides the label and the value formula). "How are results displayed: a list or a grid, mobile or desktop?" (it decides how many positions matter and the shape of position bias). "Do sellers pay for placement in organic results?" (they must not, or the system is an ads system). "How much query traffic is head versus tail?" (it decides caching and where an expensive model can run).

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Traffic. 300 million searches a day is 300,000,000 / 86,400 ≈ 3,500 queries per second on average. Shopping traffic peaks in the evening and during sales at about three times the average, so design for 10,000 QPS at peak. Query frequency follows a power law: the most frequent 1% of distinct queries make up a large share of traffic, and most distinct queries are seen once a day or less. That shape will matter for caching and for where expensive models can run.

Candidates per stage. Lexical retrieval returns its top 1,000 by BM25 after filters; dense retrieval returns its top 500 by embedding similarity; after removing duplicates the union is about 1,300. The first-pass ranker keeps 300; the main ranker orders those and passes the top 100 to re-ranking; the first page shows 48 in a grid. On a phone, about 4 to 6 are visible before scrolling.

The lexical index. 120 million listings with about 1.5 KB of indexed text each (title, attributes, a description excerpt) is 180 GB of text. A compressed inverted index with positions is typically a fraction of the text size, call it 60 GB, plus about 200 bytes per listing of filterable numeric fields (price, stock, category, shipping regions) stored column-wise for fast filtering, 24 GB. About 85 GB in total. Split into 8 shards of about 11 GB, each fits in a node's memory with room for the operating system's page cache. Every query must visit all 8 shards because listings are partitioned by document, not by term (rec2 explains why document partitioning wins for this workload).

Lexical serving capacity. If one shard replica answers about 800 top-1,000 queries per second within its 30 ms deadline (WAND skipping helps a lot on common terms), 10,000 QPS needs 13 replicas of each shard. Add headroom for a lost zone and deployments: 16 replicas × 8 shards = 128 search nodes. Replication here is for throughput first and availability second.

The dense index. 120 million listings × 256-dimensional vectors × 2 bytes (16-bit floats) = 61 GB of vectors, plus an HNSW graph (an approximate nearest-neighbour index that links each vector to a few dozen neighbours, rec4) of roughly 32 neighbour IDs × 4 bytes × 2 layers' worth ≈ 256 bytes per vector, another 31 GB. Split into 4 shards of about 23 GB; at a few milliseconds per search each replica handles a couple of thousand QPS, so about 6 replicas per shard, 24 nodes. Quantising vectors to 8 bits would halve the vector memory at a small recall cost.

Ranking compute. The first-pass ranker is a gradient-boosted tree model, about 300 trees of depth 6 over 40 features. Evaluating it costs a few microseconds per item; at 1,300 items that is about 5 ms of CPU per query, so 10,000 QPS × 5 ms = 50 cores busy. The main ranker is a neural network with about 2 million dense weights, so roughly 4 million floating-point operations per item. A CPU core sustaining around 30 billion operations per second on batched matrix work takes about 0.13 ms per item; call it 150 µs with overhead. 300 items × 150 µs = 45 ms of CPU per query, spread over several cores to keep latency down. 10,000 × 45 ms = 450 cores busy, about 900 cores provisioned at 50% target utilisation. In raw arithmetic the main ranker needs 10,000 × 300 × 4 MFLOP = 12 TFLOP/s, which a single modern inference GPU could supply, but feature handling, batching across requests and redundancy make CPU the simpler choice at this model size. The interview point: compute grows with candidates × cost per item, so the funnel's widths are the main cost knobs.

Features per request. The first pass reads about 40 features per candidate, mostly precomputed listing features (price, rating, sales velocity) plus query-listing match scores computed during retrieval: 1,300 × 40 = 52,000 values. The main ranker reads about 300 features for each of 300 candidates, 90,000 values, about 360 KB of floats. Query features (category prediction, embedding, length) are computed once per query; user features are one read from the online store (hd8). Item features are the bulk: 120 million listings × 200 features × 4 bytes = 96 GB, too big for every ranking host, so hosts cache the hot few million listings locally (searches concentrate on popular and in-stock items) and fetch misses in one batched multi-get.

Logs. Logging 300 features for each of the 48 displayed results is 48 × 300 × 4 bytes ≈ 58 KB per search, or 17 TB a day uncompressed. That is affordable for a large company but wasteful, so log IDs, scores and positions for all searches (about 1 KB each, 300 GB a day) and full feature vectors for every search with engagement plus a 10% random sample of the rest. Engagement events (clicks, add-to-cart, purchases) are small: a few hundred million a day.

Evaluation cost per release. The human-judged set is 3,000 queries stratified by frequency band and category, with 40 judged listings per query: 120,000 judgments. With three raters per judgment at an assumed $0.15 each, building it once costs about $54,000; refreshing 10% a month costs about $5,000. An offline run of a candidate model over the judged set and one day of held-out logs (300 million searches, subsampled to 10 million) is a few hundred CPU-hours, tens of dollars. An interleaving test of a few days and an A/B test of two weeks are the real cost, measured in traffic and time, not money.

The insight to say out loud. The expensive things are not where beginners expect. The index fits in a modest cluster; the models are small. The cost drivers are fan-out (every query touches 8 lexical and 4 dense shards, so the slowest of 12 servers sets latency), feature movement, and, above all, the traffic that experiments consume.

3 · The latency budget

Two rules make a budget like this real rather than decorative. Each stage receives its deadline from the request ("you have until t + 120 ms") and returns its best partial answer when time runs out, rather than every service having its own timeout that does not know how much of the request's time is left. And every stage has a degraded mode: retrieval without the slow shard, ranking with the first-pass order, results without personalisation. Degraded pages are counted and alerted on, because a system that silently serves first-pass order 5% of the time has lost quality nobody sees in the latency graph.

5 · Data model

Listing document (search index): ID, analysed text fields (title, brand, attributes, description excerpt), numeric and keyword fields for filters (price, stock, category path, shipping regions, seller ID), and static ranking signals (quality score, sales velocity) as doc values. Listing embedding: ID, vector, model version. Impression log: request ID, timestamp, user or anonymous ID, raw and rewritten query, query-understanding outputs, model versions, candidate IDs with their stage scores, displayed positions, sampled feature vectors. Event log: request ID, listing ID, position, event type, timestamp. Judgments: query, listing, grade 0-3, rater, guideline version, date. Propensities: layout, device, position, estimated examination probability, estimation date. Training examples: query groups built by joining impressions to events within attribution windows (a click within 30 minutes, a purchase within 24 hours), with graded labels and IPW weights.

The judgments table deserves care. Relevance guidelines (what is a 3, a 2, a 1, a 0 for "nike pegasus 40 womens"?) are versioned, because changing the guideline changes every NDCG number computed afterwards. A grade-3 "exact" listing matches every stated attribute; a grade-2 "substitute" misses one (a different size); grade-1 "related" is the right kind of thing (another women's running shoe); grade-0 is irrelevant.

6 · High-level design

A search gateway authenticates, applies rate limits and assigns the request to experiment variants (in10). The query-understanding service normalises the text, corrects spelling, predicts category and attributes, generates expansions and computes the query embedding; results for frequent queries are cached for minutes. The retrieval broker fans the structured query out to the 8 lexical shards and the 4 ANN shards in parallel, each returning its top candidates after applying hard filters (in stock, ships to the user), then merges and de-duplicates them. The ranking service fetches features (local cache, then the online feature store), runs the first-pass model, then the main model on the survivors. The page builder re-ranks the top 100 for diversity and seller caps, blends sponsored listings from the ads system at reserved slots, enforces policy filters, and hydrates titles, prices and images from the listing store.

Offline, a change-data-capture stream from the catalogue database feeds the indexer, which applies partial updates (price, stock) to the index within seconds and full re-indexes nightly; an embedding job computes vectors for new and changed listings and pushes them to the ANN shards. A joiner attaches events to impressions and writes training examples. Training pipelines produce candidate models; an offline evaluator computes metrics on the judged set and held-out logs; models that pass are registered and launched through interleaving and A/B tests. A propensity job estimates position bias weekly from randomised traffic.

7 · Deep dive: query understanding without killing recall

Query understanding is where the system decides what the user meant, and it is the stage with the most leverage and the most dangerous failures, because its mistakes happen before retrieval. rec5 teaches the techniques; the system questions are how its outputs are used and how its errors are contained.

Walk the example. "nike pegasos 40 womens size 8" is lowercased and tokenised. Spelling correction proposes "pegasus" because the noisy-channel score (how likely the typo is, times how common the corrected query is in logs and the catalogue) is high and "pegasos" matches almost no listings. A category classifier predicts running shoes with probability 0.97. An attribute extractor, a small sequence-labelling model, tags "nike" as brand, "pegasus 40" as model, "womens" as gender and "size 8" as size. An expansion step adds "pegasus 40" as a phrase and "women's" as a variant spelling. A text encoder produces the query embedding for dense retrieval. Each step takes a few milliseconds on CPU, and the whole result for a frequent query is cached.

The design decision is what to do with each output. Every prediction can be used in one of three ways, from safest to most aggressive: as a ranking feature (the ranker sees "predicted category = running shoes, listing category = running shoes" and learns how much to trust it), as a retrieval boost (BM25 scores of matching listings are multiplied up, but non-matching listings can still be retrieved), or as a hard filter (non-matching listings are excluded). The cost of a mistake rises in the same order. A classifier that is right 95% of the time is excellent as a feature and dangerous as a filter: on one query in twenty, a filter removes the correct listings and the ranker cannot bring them back.

So the rule is: features by default, boosts when the signal is strong, hard filters only above a high confidence and for attributes users expect to be enforced (an explicit size filter the user clicked, yes; a size inferred from text, as a boost). And always pair a filter with query relaxation: if fewer than, say, 20 results survive, drop the least confident constraint and retrieve again, and tell the interface so it can show "showing results for size 8 and similar". The same applies to spelling: correct automatically only when the original query returns few results and the correction is confident; otherwise search for both and show "did you mean". A spelling corrector that rewrites a real but rare brand name ("Hoka" to "Hook") is a classic incident, and it shows up in metrics as a rise in reformulations for that query.

Two more system concerns. Query understanding has its own models and its own versions, so its outputs are logged with each search and its changes go through experiments like ranker changes. And it is a natural place for an LLM: rewriting vague tail queries ("gift for someone who runs marathons") into structured ones is valuable and rare enough to afford a model call, especially if rewrites are generated offline for queries seen before and cached (rec14 covers LLMs in search).

8 · Deep dive: the funnel and its recall

Every cut in the funnel is a bet that the items discarded would not have made the final page. The metric that checks the bet is stage recall: of the items the final ranker would place in the top 48 if it could score an unlimited candidate set, what share survives this stage? You cannot afford to compute that on every request, but you can on a sample. For 0.1% of searches, a shadow path retrieves 5,000 candidates instead of 1,300, scores all of them with the main ranker offline, and records which of its top 48 the production funnel kept. If first-pass recall is 92%, then 8% of the page the main ranker wanted was thrown away by the cheap model, and widening the first pass from 300 to 400 or improving the first-pass model may be worth more than any change to the main ranker.

The same lens explains why lexical and dense retrieval are both kept. Measured on the judged set, lexical retrieval might find 85% of grade-3 listings within its top 1,000 and dense retrieval 70% within its top 500, but their union finds 95%, because their failures are different: lexical misses "sneakers" when the listing says "trainers"; dense misses "DV3854-001" because a product code means nothing to an embedding trained on text similarity. The broker does not need to calibrate their scores against each other (rec5 explains why that is fragile); it passes both scores as features and lets the first-pass ranker learn how to combine them.

Recall losses multiply. If retrieval keeps 95% of what matters, the first pass 92% and re-ranking's business rules 99%, the final page holds about 0.95 × 0.92 × 0.99 ≈ 87% of what an all-seeing system would show. Each stage owner sees a respectable number; the product sees 13% lost. That is why stage recall is reported as a chain on one dashboard and why changes to an early stage are evaluated end to end.

9 · Deep dive: learning to rank inside the system

What a training example is. In learning to rank, the unit is a query group: all candidates scored for one search, each with features and a label. rec7 teaches the losses; here the questions are where labels come from and how metrics guide choices. From logs, a purchase becomes grade 3, add-to-cart 2, a click 1, and a result shown above a clicked result but skipped becomes 0, the "skip-above" heuristic that follows from the cascade model below. Results below the last click carry little information and are often dropped or down-weighted, because the user may never have seen them. Human judgments supply the same 0-3 scale for the evaluation set.

Metrics, worked on a toy ranking. One query, "wireless earbuds for running". The judged pool has grades {3, 3, 2, 2, 1} among its best five, so the ideal top 5 is [3, 3, 2, 2, 1]. Three rankers return these grades in their top 5:

Here is how A's DCG is computed. With exponential gain 2grade − 1, the gains are 7, 3, 0, 1, 3. The discounts 1 / log2(rank + 1) are 1, 0.631, 0.5, 0.431, 0.387. Multiply and sum: 7 + 1.89 + 0 + 0.43 + 1.16 = 10.48. The ideal list gives 7 + 4.42 + 1.5 + 1.29 + 0.39 = 14.60, so NDCG@5 = 10.48 / 14.60 = 0.718. MRR, if "relevant" means grade 2 or higher, is 1 over the rank of the first relevant result: 1 for A, 1/2 for B and C.

The table teaches three things. C has a clearly better set of five than A (two grade-3s and two grade-2s against one of each) yet ties with A on NDCG, because its rank 1 is irrelevant and rank 1 carries the most weight. MRR says A is twice as good as C, because it only looks at the first relevant result; it suits known-item queries where one answer matters and is blind to everything after it. And B, which buries both perfect results below a weak one, loses on both. When you choose an offline metric, you are choosing which of these behaviours you reward; for a shopping grid where users scan several results, NDCG@10 with graded labels is the usual primary, MRR the metric for the known-item slice.

Pointwise, pairwise, listwise as system choices. A pointwise model predicts each item's label on its own, for example P(purchase | query, user, listing) with log loss. It ignores that only order within a query matters, but its outputs are probabilities, which the page builder needs to blend organic results with ads or to compute expected revenue. A pairwise model learns from pairs within a query (this one should be above that one); RankNet turns a score difference into a probability and LambdaRank weights each pair by how much swapping it would change NDCG. A listwise model optimises a loss over the whole list, such as a softmax over the candidates' scores with the purchased item as the target. In this design the first-pass ranker is LambdaMART (boosted trees trained on LambdaRank gradients), strong and cheap on dense features; the main ranker is a multi-task network with pointwise heads for click, add-to-cart and purchase whose calibrated outputs are combined into a value, optionally with a listwise term to sharpen ordering.

Why the ΔNDCG weighting matters, with numbers. Take ranker B. Swapping ranks 1 and 2 (grades 1 and 3) changes DCG by (7 − 1) × (1 − 0.631) = 2.21, so ΔNDCG = 2.21 / 14.60 = 0.152. Take ranker A and swap ranks 3 and 5 (grades 0 and 2): (3 − 0) × (0.5 − 0.387) = 0.34, ΔNDCG = 0.023. Plain RankNet treats both mis-ordered pairs alike (if their score gaps are equal); LambdaRank pushes the first about 6.5 times harder. That is the whole idea in one comparison: spend the model's capacity where users look.

The pair explosion. A page of 48 results has 48 × 47 / 2 = 1,128 pairs, most between two unclicked results with the same label, which carry no information. Training uses only pairs with different labels and samples among them, weighting toward pairs that involve the top positions. At 120 million query groups with engagement per day, even a few useful pairs each produce billions of pairs a month, so the training pipeline subsamples by query (keeping head queries from swamping the tail) as well as by pair.

10 · Deep dive: position bias and click models

The labels above came from clicks and purchases, and those are biased by where items were shown. A listing at position 1 is seen by nearly everyone; one at position 8 on a phone is seen only by those who scroll. A click model is a small probabilistic story about how users interact with a results page, used to separate "was it seen?" from "was it good?".

The examination hypothesis underlies most of them: a result is clicked only if it is examined and found attractive. The position-based model (PBM) assumes the examination probability depends only on the position k, written θk, and attractiveness only on the query-listing pair, γ: P(click at k) = θk × γ. The cascade model (Craswell et al., 2008) assumes the user reads strictly top to bottom, clicks the first attractive result and stops, so P(click at k) = γk × the product over earlier ranks of (1 − γi): a click at rank 3 tells you ranks 1 and 2 were examined and rejected, which is where skip-above negatives come from. Later models such as the dynamic Bayesian network model (Chapelle and Zhang, 2009) add the possibility of continuing after a click if the user was not satisfied, which fits shopping, where people open several listings.

Estimating propensities. The clean method is a small randomised intervention. For about 0.5% of searches, shuffle the top 8 results before display. Within that slice, relevance is independent of position, so the click rate at each position, divided by the click rate at position 1, estimates θk. Suppose shuffled traffic shows click rates of 12.0%, 7.8%, 6.0%, 4.8% and 4.2% at positions 1 to 5: the propensities are 1, 0.65, 0.50, 0.40 and 0.35. The intervention costs something: those users see a worse order, perhaps a few percent fewer purchases in the slice, which is a few hundredths of a percent overall. A gentler design swaps only adjacent pairs (positions k and k+1), which hurts less and still identifies the ratios between neighbours. A cheaper method uses natural variation: over months, different ranker versions placed the same listing for the same query at different positions, and comparing its click rates across positions estimates the curve without a deliberate experiment (Agarwal et al., 2019, call this intervention harvesting).

Using them. With inverse propensity weighting, each clicked example's weight in the loss is 1/θk, so the training objective estimates what clicks would look like if every position were examined equally (Joachims et al., 2017, rec10). In the figure, listing Y's 3% raw click rate at position 5 becomes 8.6%, close to X's 9%. Two practical safeguards: clip small propensities (a θ of 0.05 multiplies clicks by 20, and a handful of noisy clicks then dominate), and estimate curves per device and layout. A different and widely used alternative trains the model with position as an input feature, so it can explain away the position effect during training, then sets position to a fixed value at serving time. Airbnb's version of this, with dropout so the model does not lean on position too heavily, is the real case in this lesson.

Beyond position. Clicks are also biased by presentation: a listing with a better photo, a "bestseller" badge or a lower displayed price draws clicks independent of fit (attractiveness bias), and users click higher results more when they trust the engine (trust bias). Purchases are much less biased than clicks, which is why the label mapping puts most weight on them, at the cost of being rarer and arriving later.

11 · Evaluation plan

Offline, per stage. Retrieval: recall@1,000 of grade-3 and grade-2 listings on the judged set, by frequency band (head, torso, tail) and by category. First pass: stage recall from the shadow sample. Main ranker: NDCG@10 on the judged set; NDCG@10 on one held-out day of logs with IPW-corrected labels; log loss and calibration of each pointwise head; MRR on known-item queries. Every number is reported with a confidence interval by bootstrapping over queries (ev7), because a 0.3-point NDCG change on 3,000 queries can be noise.

Online. Interleaving first (rec13): merge the candidate ranker's results with production's for a small share of traffic for a few days, and drop candidates that lose. Then an A/B test of about two weeks on a few percent of users. Primary metric: purchases per search (or per searching user, if search sessions change in length). Secondaries that explain the primary: click-through rate on the first page, add-to-cart rate, revenue per search, position of the first click.

Guardrails, which block a launch even if the primary metric improves: p99 latency, zero-result rate, abandonment rate, reformulation rate (users rewriting the query right away is a strong dissatisfaction signal), seller-exposure concentration on the first page, complaint and report rate, and prohibited-content incidents (which must be zero).

Release gate. A new model ships only if offline NDCG on the judged set does not regress beyond its interval on any frequency band, known-item MRR stays above 0.9, latency in a load test fits the budget, and the A/B test is positive on the primary metric with no guardrail breached. These gates are automated so that a busy week does not erode them.

The trade-offs to name: wider funnel stages (more recall) against latency and cores; personalisation against cacheability (a personalised page cannot be cached across users, so personalise in the ranker on top of cached candidates); clicks (plentiful, biased, fast) against purchases (rare, less biased, delayed) as labels; exploration (randomised slots that cost a little now) against learning (labels for listings the model has never shown); and relevance against revenue, which belongs in an explicit, experiment-tuned value formula rather than hidden inside a model.

Choosing the label is choosing the product

The mapping from engagement to grades (purchase 3, cart 2, click 1) looks like a detail and is the single most consequential choice in the system. Clicks alone reward attractive thumbnails and low headline prices, and a ranker trained on them drifts toward clickbait. Purchases alone are 20-50 times rarer and arrive hours later, so tail queries have almost no positive labels. Revenue-weighted labels push expensive items up and can hurt conversion. Most teams settle on graded labels with purchases dominant, then check the result against the human-judged set so a label change that games engagement shows up as a relevance drop. Interviewers like candidates who say the label definition is versioned and changed through experiments like a model.

Why boosted trees refuse to die in search

LambdaMART on a few hundred well-engineered features is hard to beat, and Airbnb's first neural ranker, a single hidden layer of 32 units, was only "booking neutral" against the trees. Trees handle unnormalised features, missing values and monotone relationships (price, rating) gracefully, train in hours on CPUs and are easy to inspect. Neural rankers pull ahead when there is enough data to learn embeddings of high-cardinality inputs (query terms, categories, locations), when there are many related objectives to share representations across, and when raw sequences such as recent views can be fed directly. A common production shape keeps trees as the first pass and a neural network as the main ranker, or feeds tree outputs into the network as features.

Query-dependent and query-independent features

Features split into query-independent (a listing's quality score, sales velocity, price), query-dependent (BM25 score of the title, embedding similarity, whether the predicted category matches) and user-dependent (distance to the user, size match with past purchases). Query-independent features can be precomputed and stored in the index; query-dependent ones must be computed per request and dominate cost; user-dependent ones break caching. Knowing which is which drives the architecture: static signals are pushed into retrieval so the first pass can use them, query-dependent signals are computed once in retrieval and passed along rather than recomputed, and personalisation is applied late on a cached candidate set.

Head, torso and tail need different machinery

A few thousand head queries carry a large share of traffic and have abundant labels; their results can be cached for a minute and even hand-reviewed. Tail queries, each rare, together carry a large share of traffic too and have almost no labels. They benefit most from query understanding (rewriting to a head-like form), dense retrieval and models that generalise through features rather than memorising query IDs. Offline metrics must be stratified by band, because an overall NDCG gain is often a head-query gain that hides a tail regression. Rewriting tail queries with an LLM offline and caching the rewrites is one of the most cost-effective uses of an LLM in search.

Position bias in a grid, and why one curve is not enough

On a desktop grid of four columns, examination falls by row and also varies by column (left columns are seen first in left-to-right languages); on a phone list it falls by row only but much faster after the first screen. A single θk curve averaged across layouts over-corrects some positions and under-corrects others. Propensities are estimated per layout and device, and the position feature, when used, includes row and column. When the interface changes (bigger images, a new badge), propensities change too, so they are re-estimated after every major interface release.

Distilling an expensive re-ranker

A cross-encoder that reads the query and listing text together is clearly more accurate than features on hard queries, but at tens of milliseconds of GPU per query for 30 candidates it cannot run on every search at 10,000 QPS within cost. Two patterns capture most of the gain. Run it offline on frequent query-listing pairs and store its score as a feature, refreshed as listings change. Or use it as a teacher: generate scores on millions of sampled pairs and train the main ranker (or the query encoder for dense retrieval) to match them, so the student learns the teacher's judgments within the CPU budget. Either way, measure agreement between teacher and human labels before trusting it.

Index freshness against index efficiency

Inverted indexes are fastest when immutable and merged into large segments; prices and stock change constantly. Lucene-family engines handle this with near-real-time refresh: new and updated documents go to small new segments that become searchable within about a second, and deletes are marked rather than removed until segments merge. Very frequent updates (a price that changes every minute) churn segments and slow queries, so volatile fields are often kept outside the inverted index, in a fast key-value store or doc-value columns updated in place, and checked at filter or hydration time. The rule: index what you search on, look up what changes.

How it fails in practice

The corrector that ate a brand. A spelling model trained on general text starts correcting a newly popular brand name to a common word. Searches for the brand return irrelevant results, reformulations for that query triple, and nobody notices for days because aggregate zero-result rate barely moves. The defences are a dictionary built from the catalogue (brand and model names are never corrected), confidence thresholds, and alerts on per-query reformulation spikes for frequent queries.

The silent default. A feature pipeline for "sales in the last 7 days" fails for one region; the online store expires the values and the ranker sees the default 0, which in training meant "new listing". Established listings in that region drop in rank, conversion falls a few percent there, and the latency dashboard is green. Monitoring default rates per feature and per region catches it in minutes; training with the same default semantics that serving uses limits the damage.

The offline win that lost. A team improves offline NDCG by 2% on click-derived labels, and the A/B test is negative. The new model had learned features correlated with position under the old ranker (for example a "historical CTR" feature that was itself position-biased), so it reproduced the old ranking more faithfully and scored well on labels created by that ranking. The fixes are IPW-corrected labels for offline evaluation, a human-judged set as a second opinion, and position-debiased historical CTR features.

The out-of-stock first row. During a sale, stock changes faster than the index refresh; the first row fills with sold-out items. Users click, fail to buy and abandon. An availability check at hydration (drop and backfill items that sold out since retrieval) and a stock-velocity feature in the ranker fix it.

Metrics and dashboards

Per stage: latency percentiles, timeout and partial-result rates, candidates returned, stage recall from the shadow sample. Quality: zero-result rate, abandonment, reformulation rate, click-through and purchases per search, all by frequency band, category, device and locale. Models: feature default rates, prediction distribution shifts, calibration of the pointwise heads, offline-online prediction parity on logged requests. Index: refresh lag, update queue depth, document counts per shard. Exploration: share of traffic in randomised slices and the estimated propensity curves over time.

Knobs people tune

Candidate counts per stage (the cost and recall dial), the BM25 field weights and the lexical/dense mix, query-understanding confidence thresholds and relaxation rules, cache TTLs for head queries, the value formula's weights on click, cart and purchase, the propensity clip value, seller caps and diversity strength in re-ranking, and the share of traffic devoted to exploration.

The real case: Airbnb search ranking

Airbnb's KDD 2019 paper "Applying Deep Learning to Airbnb Search" (Haldar et al.) is an unusually honest account of moving a production search ranker from trees to neural networks. The starting point was a gradient-boosted tree model whose gains had plateaued. The first neural network to reach production was deliberately simple, a single hidden layer of 32 fully connected ReLU units trained with an L2 regression loss where booked listings had utility 1.0 and others 0.0, and it was booking-neutral against the trees; its value was proving the pipeline. The breakthrough was LambdaRank: they moved to pairs of {booked listing, not-booked listing} from the same search, minimised the cross-entropy of the score difference, and weighted each pair's loss by the change in NDCG from swapping the two, so improving a booked listing from position 2 to 1 got priority over moving one from 10 to 9. Offline they measured NDCG with the booked listing as relevance 1 and everything else 0. After an intermediate model that fed GBDT leaf indices and a factorisation machine's prediction into the network, they simplified to a deep network with two hidden layers (127 and 83 units) over 195 input features, trained on ten times more data; at 1.7 billion training pairs the gap between training and test NDCG closed.

The paper's failures are as instructive as its successes. Listing-ID embeddings overfit, because even the most popular listing can be booked at most 365 times a year, too little data per ID. A multi-task model predicting long views alongside bookings raised long views but left bookings neutral. Dropout hurt offline metrics slightly. Engineering mattered as much as modelling: switching the training input pipeline from CSV and feed_dict to Protobufs and TensorFlow's Dataset API gave a 17x training speedup and took GPU utilisation from about 25% to about 90%, and the team wrote its own Java scoring library to meet serving latency.

The 2020 follow-up, "Improving Deep Learning for Airbnb Search", tackled position bias. Rather than building a separate propensity model, they added the listing's position as a feature, mirroring the position-based model's assumption that the network's output is relevance times a position-bias term. Naively, this cut test NDCG (with position set to 0) by about 1.3%, because the network leaned on position. Regularising the position feature with dropout, setting it to 0 for a fraction of training examples, balanced the two, and a rate of 0.15 was chosen by sweeping. At scoring time the position feature is set to 0. In an online A/B test this gave +0.7% bookings and +1.8% revenue; the authors attribute the revenue lift to removing a bias toward cheaper listings that earlier models had baked into the training data, a feedback loop of exactly the kind this lesson warns about.

Goal: compute NDCG@k and MRR by hand-checkable code, then train a tiny linear ranker with LambdaRank gradients and watch NDCG rise. Python 3.12, standard library only; runs in a second or two.

  1. Save the script as lambdarank_toy.py and run python lambdarank_toy.py.
  2. Check the first three lines against the table in section 9: NDCG@5 of 0.718, 0.611 and 0.710, MRR of 1, 0.5 and 0.5.
  3. Watch mean NDCG@5 over the 200 synthetic queries rise during training. The hidden relevance depends mostly on text match, a little on rating, and negatively on price; the learned weights should recover those signs.
  4. Set USE_DELTA = False to train plain RankNet (every mis-ordered pair weighted equally) and compare the final NDCG@5. LambdaRank usually ends higher on the top-heavy metric because it spends its gradient at the top.
  5. Break it: give the popularity feature a large weight in the hidden relevance and see whether the model learns it; then think about what that feature would do in a live feedback loop.
"""NDCG@k, MRR and a tiny LambdaRank on synthetic queries. Python 3.12, stdlib only."""
import math, random

def dcg(grades, k):
    return sum((2 ** g - 1) / math.log2(i + 2) for i, g in enumerate(grades[:k]))

def ndcg(grades, k, pool=None):
    ideal = dcg(sorted(pool if pool else grades, reverse=True), k)
    return dcg(grades, k) / ideal if ideal else 0.0

def rr(grades, rel=2):
    return next((1 / (i + 1) for i, g in enumerate(grades) if g >= rel), 0.0)

pool = [3, 3, 2, 2, 1]                                   # best five judged grades
for name, g in [("A", [3, 2, 0, 1, 2]), ("B", [1, 3, 3, 0, 0]), ("C", [0, 3, 3, 2, 2])]:
    print(f"{name} {g}  NDCG@5={ndcg(g, 5, pool):.3f}  RR={rr(g):.2f}")

rng = random.Random(7)
TRUE_W = [2.0, 0.6, -0.8]                                # text match, rating, price (hidden)
def make_query(n=10):
    docs = []
    for _ in range(n):
        x = [rng.random(), rng.random(), rng.random()]
        rel = sum(w * v for w, v in zip(TRUE_W, x)) + rng.gauss(0, 0.2)
        docs.append((x, rel))
    cut = sorted(r for _, r in docs)                     # turn relevance into grades 0-3
    grade = lambda r: sum(r >= cut[int(q * n)] for q in (0.4, 0.7, 0.9))
    return [(x, grade(r)) for x, r in docs]
queries = [make_query() for _ in range(200)]

def score(w, x): return sum(a * b for a, b in zip(w, x))
def mean_ndcg(w, k=5):
    tot = 0.0
    for q in queries:
        order = sorted(q, key=lambda d: -score(w, d[0]))
        tot += ndcg([g for _, g in order], k)
    return tot / len(queries)

USE_DELTA = True
LR = 0.05 if USE_DELTA else 0.005      # |ΔNDCG| shrinks lambdas ~10x, so RankNet needs a smaller step
w = [0.0, 0.0, 0.0]
for epoch in range(31):
    grad = [0.0, 0.0, 0.0]
    for q in queries:
        order = sorted(q, key=lambda d: -score(w, d[0]))
        idcg = dcg(sorted((g for _, g in q), reverse=True), len(q))
        for i, (xi, gi) in enumerate(order):
            for j, (xj, gj) in enumerate(order):
                if gi <= gj:
                    continue                             # only pairs where i should be above j
                rho = 1 / (1 + math.exp(score(w, xi) - score(w, xj)))   # RankNet gradient size
                disc = lambda p: 1 / math.log2(p + 2)
                delta = abs((2 ** gi - 2 ** gj) * (disc(i) - disc(j))) / idcg if USE_DELTA else 1.0
                lam = rho * delta
                for f in range(3):
                    grad[f] += lam * (xi[f] - xj[f])     # push i up, j down
    w = [a + LR * g / len(queries) for a, g in zip(w, grad)]
    if epoch % 5 == 0:
        print(f"epoch {epoch:2d}  mean NDCG@5={mean_ndcg(w):.3f}  w={[round(v, 2) for v in w]}")

What to look for: mean NDCG@5 starts near the value of a random order (with all weights 0 every score ties and the sort keeps input order) and climbs over the epochs; the weight on text match becomes the largest and the price weight turns negative. The inner loop is exactly the per-pair lambda of section 9: the RankNet term rho times |ΔNDCG|.

Work it out

Your marketplace serves 6,000 searches per second at peak. Retrieval returns 2,000 candidates; you are proposing a first-pass GBDT costing 4 µs per item and keeping 400, then a main ranker costing 200 µs of CPU per item. The p99 budget for both ranking stages together is 70 ms, and the main ranker can be split across 8 cores per request. (a) How many cores does ranking need at peak, at 50% target utilisation? (b) Does the main ranker fit the latency budget? (c) Product wants the main ranker to see 800 candidates "for better recall". What does it cost, and what would you measure before agreeing?

Answer

(a) Cores. First pass: 2,000 × 4 µs = 8 ms of CPU per search; 6,000 × 8 ms = 48 cores busy. Main ranker: 400 × 200 µs = 80 ms of CPU per search; 6,000 × 80 ms = 480 cores busy. Total 528 cores busy, about 1,060 provisioned at 50% utilisation. The main ranker is 90% of the cost.

(b) Latency. The first pass takes about 8 ms on one core (or less split). The main ranker's 80 ms of CPU split across 8 cores is about 10 ms of wall time plus coordination overhead, say 15 ms at p99 when cores are contended. Together about 25 ms, inside the 70 ms budget, with room for feature fetching if it is not already counted separately.

(c) 800 candidates. Main-ranker CPU doubles to 160 ms per search: 960 cores busy instead of 480, so the main ranker's provisioned fleet goes from about 960 cores to about 1,920, and its wall time to about 25-30 ms. Before agreeing, measure first-pass stage recall with the shadow method: score 2,000 candidates with the main ranker on a sample and check how many of its top 48 fall at first-pass ranks 401-800. If that share is 1%, the doubling buys almost nothing; improving the first-pass model (better features, distillation from the main ranker) is cheaper. If it is 10%, run an A/B test of 800 against 400 and weigh the purchase lift against roughly 1,000 extra cores.

Diagnose it

A week after a query-understanding release that added an attribute extractor (colour, size, gender) used as hard filters, overall purchases per search are down 0.4%, zero-result rate is up from 1.6% to 2.3%, and the ranking model has not changed. Offline, the extractor's accuracy on its test set is 96%. What happened, and what do you change?

Answer

Mistakes became filters. At 96% per-attribute accuracy, a query with three extracted attributes has about a 1 − 0.96³ ≈ 11.5% chance that at least one is wrong, and every wrong attribute used as a hard filter removes the right listings before retrieval. Queries like "black and white sneakers" (two colours), "toddler 8" (a size system) or brand names that look like colours ("Rose" as a brand) are typical victims. Zero results rise for the most affected queries; others still return results but worse ones, which shows up as fewer purchases.

Confirm it by slicing zero-result and reformulation rates by whether filters were applied, and sampling queries with filters and zero results for manual review. Check how the extractor's test set was built: if it over-represents clean head queries, 96% overstates accuracy on real traffic.

Change: use extracted attributes as boosts and ranking features by default; enforce as filters only above a high confidence threshold and only for attributes the user typed unambiguously; add query relaxation (if fewer than 20 results survive, drop the least confident filter and retry); and gate future query-understanding releases on zero-result and reformulation guardrails in an A/B test, not on offline accuracy.
