A product team believes that showing 16 cards in a homepage carousel instead of 12 will make people click more. They could just ship it and look at the click graph — but traffic changes every day for a hundred reasons: news, seasons, a marketing email, another team's launch. A rise or a fall after the change proves nothing. The only reliable way to know is a controlled experiment: at the same time, randomly show half the users the old carousel and half the new one, and compare. Randomisation makes the two groups alike in every way except the change, so a difference beyond chance is caused by the change.

That idea is simple. Doing it for a large company is not. Hundreds of teams want to run experiments at once on the same users. Every experiment needs users split consistently (no one should flip between variants), independently of other experiments, and in exactly the proportions configured. Billions of events must be joined to the right experiment and variant and turned into dozens of metrics with correct confidence intervals. And the statistics are full of traps that make a useless change look like a winner: stopping as soon as the result looks good, testing a hundred metrics and celebrating the five that "won", analysing users who never saw the change, or — the subtle one — a data pipeline that quietly drops different users from each group.

That last trap is what happened to the 16-card carousel at Microsoft. The experiment showed a significant drop in engagement; the team nearly abandoned the idea. A platform check flagged that the treatment group had fewer users than it should have. The cause turned out to be a bot filter that removed the most enthusiastic treatment users because they clicked so much; once fixed, the result flipped and the feature shipped. An experimentation platform's job is as much to protect teams from such errors as to compute p-values.

This lesson designs the platform in interview order: requirements, estimates, API, data model, architecture, then deep dives into assignment and bucketing, overlapping experiments, exposure logging, the metrics pipeline, sample size and power worked by hand, variance reduction, peeking and sequential testing, and sample ratio mismatch. It ends with the Microsoft SRM paper and a lab that computes sample sizes, runs an SRM check and shows how peeking inflates false positives.

The mental model: a clinical trial run by software

A drug trial assigns patients at random to the new drug or a placebo, decides in advance how many patients and which outcome will be measured, checks that the two groups really got the treatments they were assigned, and only then compares outcomes. An A/B test is the same: units (usually users) are randomly assigned to variants (control A, treatment B); the primary metric and sample size are decided up front; the platform checks that assignment and logging worked; and only then is the difference tested for significance.

The statistics answer one question: if the change did nothing, how surprising would this difference be? That probability is the p-value. If it's below a threshold α (usually 5%), we call the result significant — accepting that, when there's truly no effect, we'll be wrong 5% of the time (a false positive). Separately, power is the chance of detecting a real effect of a given size; experiments are usually sized for 80%. Small effects need large samples: the difference has to stand out from the noise, and noise only shrinks with the square root of the number of users.

Where the analogy breaks: a clinical trial has a handful of carefully monitored patients; a platform has millions of users, hundreds of concurrent trials on the same people, and a data pipeline between what happened and what's analysed. Most real failures come from that pipeline and from human impatience, not from the formulas.

A tiny worked example

Suppose 10,000 users see the old carousel and 500 click (5.0%), and 10,000 see the new one and 540 click (5.4%). Is 0.4 points real? Each group's rate has a standard error of about √(0.05 × 0.95 ÷ 10,000) ≈ 0.0022, so the difference has a standard error of about √2 × 0.0022 ≈ 0.0031. The observed difference, 0.004, is 1.3 standard errors — a p-value of about 0.2. Twenty percent of the time, two identical carousels would differ this much by chance, so this is not evidence. With 100,000 users per group and the same rates, the standard error shrinks by √10 to about 0.001, the difference is about 4 standard errors, and the p-value is about 0.00004 — now it is evidence. Same effect, ten times the users, a completely different conclusion. That is why sample size is planned before the experiment starts, and why "it looks better" on a small sample means nothing.

The platform's pieces

The core loop is: configure → assign → expose → log → compute → check → decide. Every box is ordinary data engineering; the value of the platform is that it gets the experiment-specific details right once, for everyone.

1 · Requirements

Functional. Teams create experiments with variants, traffic allocation, targeting (country, platform, new users), a primary metric, guardrail metrics and a planned duration. The product gets each user's variant for each running experiment quickly and consistently. Exposures are logged. A scorecard shows, per metric, the difference between variants with confidence intervals, after data-quality checks. Experiments can be ramped up (1% → 10% → 50%), paused or stopped automatically when guardrails are breached. A catalogue of past experiments supports learning.

Non-functional. Assignment adds no meaningful latency (in-process, microseconds) and keeps working if the platform is down. Assignment is deterministic and independent across experiments. Hundreds of experiments run concurrently. Scorecards update at least daily, guardrails within an hour. Trustworthiness: correct variance estimates, SRM detection, A/A tests passing at the expected 5% rate.

Clarifying questions. "What is the randomisation unit — user, device, session, or something coarser such as a seller or city?" (Marketplaces where users interact need cluster randomisation.) "Which platforms — server, web, mobile with offline use?" "What metrics matter, and how are they defined?" "Is there an existing event log, or does the platform own logging?"

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Scenario: 50 million daily active users, 200 concurrent experiments, 5 billion product events a day, 100 metrics in the standard catalogue.

Assignment. Each request evaluates the running experiments that apply to it — say 20 — by hashing; a SHA-256 or MurmurHash of a short string takes well under a microsecond, so 20 evaluations cost tens of microseconds in-process. No network call, so no throughput limit to plan for. The configuration pushed to every server and app: 200 experiments × a few KB = about 1 MB.

Exposure events. If the average user is exposed to 10 experiments a day, that's 50M × 10 = 500 million exposures/day, about 5,800/s on average. Deduplicated to first exposure per user per experiment, the exposure table grows by 200 experiments × users exposed per day.

Metric computation. 5 billion events/day aggregated per user per day per metric: 50M users × 100 metrics = 5 billion user-metric values per day — a big but routine batch job (a few hundred GB of input, a few tens of GB of output in columnar form). For each experiment, join its exposed users with their metric values and compute per variant: count, sum and sum of squares (plus cross-products for ratio metrics and CUPED). Those sufficient statistics are a few numbers per (experiment, variant, metric, day): 200 × 2 × 100 × 3 values a day — tiny. The heavy work is the join, not the statistics.

Sample sizes (worked in the deep dive): detecting a 2% relative lift on a 5% conversion rate needs about 745,000 users per variant. If 2 million eligible users a day are split 50/50, that's under a day of traffic — but experiments run at least a full week to cover weekly cycles. If only 10% of users trigger the feature, the same lift needs the same 745,000 triggered users per variant, about 7.5 days.

Experiment capacity. With independent overlapping experiments, traffic isn't divided: every user can be in all 200 at once. Only mutually exclusive layers divide traffic, which is why the default is overlap.

4 · Data model

  • experiments(key, layer, salt, unit, variants and bucket ranges, targeting, metrics, status, start/stop times, version) — versioned; any change to allocation creates a new version, because analysis must know exactly who was eligible when.
  • exposures(experiment, version, unit_id, variant, first_exposure_ts, attributes at exposure) — first exposure per unit per experiment version.
  • events — the existing product event log.
  • metric_daily(unit_id, date, metric, value) — per-unit daily metric values computed once and shared by all experiments.
  • scorecard_stats(experiment, variant, metric, date, n, sum, sum_sq, sum_x, sum_xy, …) — sufficient statistics from which any test can be computed.

5 · High-level architecture

The config service stores experiments and pushes compiled configuration (layers, salts, bucket ranges, targeting rules) to server SDKs and mobile apps; clients cache it and keep working with the last version if the service is unreachable. SDKs assign by hashing and log exposures into the event pipeline (the in1 log, then a warehouse). Daily metric jobs compute per-unit metric values from product events; experiment jobs join each experiment's exposures to those values and produce sufficient statistics per variant; the analysis engine runs checks and tests and renders scorecards. A near-real-time path computes a few guardrails (errors, latency, crashes, revenue) per variant per hour from the stream to stop harmful experiments during ramp-up.

6 · Deep dive: assignment and bucketing

The assignment function must satisfy three requirements the Microsoft paper states explicitly: users must be equally likely to see each variant (for a 50/50 split); repeat assignments of a user must be consistent; and assignment in one experiment must be uncorrelated with assignment in any other. A hash does all three without storing anything: bucket = hash(salt + ":" + user_id) mod 1000; the experiment maps buckets 0–499 to control and 500–999 to treatment. The same input always produces the same bucket (consistency). A good hash spreads users uniformly (equal likelihood). And a different salt per experiment makes the bucket in one experiment unrelated to the bucket in another (independence) — the lab checks that the fraction of users in treatment for both of two experiments is 0.250, as independence predicts.

The details bite. Microsoft's assignment service used "a hash function to randomize users into one thousand buckets, each bucket representing 0.1 percent of users"; a bug assigned control one bucket too few, so a 50/50 test was really 49.9/50 — caught only because an A/A test showed an SRM. Salts must be unique per experiment: the paper notes that with 365 possible seeds, only about 23 experiments are needed for a 50% chance that two share a seed (the birthday problem), which would correlate their assignments. And user IDs must be stable — if a user's ID changes when they log in, they can land in a different variant mid-experiment.

Ramp-ups use the same mechanism: at 10% traffic, buckets 0–49 are control and 50–99 treatment, the rest are not in the experiment; ramping to 50% extends the ranges without moving anyone already assigned, so users don't switch variants.

7 · Deep dive: overlapping experiments and layers

If each user could be in only one experiment, 200 concurrent experiments would each get 0.5% of traffic — far too little for power. Google's 2010 paper on its overlapping experiment infrastructure describes the alternative: split parameters into layers; experiments in the same layer are mutually exclusive (each user is in at most one), while each layer assigns users independently of other layers, so a user is in one experiment per layer. Experiments on the search results ranking and on the page's colour scheme can overlap on the same users; two experiments that both change the ranking go in the same layer. Domains carve out traffic for experiments that need exclusivity across layers.

Why is overlap safe? Because of independence: in experiment A, both control and treatment contain the same proportion of B's control and treatment users, so whatever B does affects A's two groups equally and cancels in the comparison. It adds noise, not bias. What overlap can't hide is an interaction — A's effect depends on B's variant (a new button colour that clashes with B's new background). Platforms test pairs of overlapping experiments for interaction effects on key metrics; in practice they are uncommon, and layers are reserved for experiments that touch the same component.

8 · Deep dive: exposure, triggering and the metrics pipeline

Assign a user to an experiment on the checkout page and most assigned users never reach checkout. Analysing all assigned users dilutes the effect: if 10% reach checkout and the change lifts their conversion by 3%, the average lift over all assigned users is 0.3%, and since required sample size scales with 1/δ², detecting 0.3% needs about 100 times as many users as detecting 3% among the triggered users. So log an exposure at the point where the variants actually diverge, and analyse triggered users. The trap: the trigger must be logged identically in both variants. If treatment logs exposure when its new component renders and control has no component to render, the groups are defined differently. Control must log a counterfactual exposure at the same point ("would have seen the new carousel").

The pipeline then works in two stages. Per-unit metrics: from product events, compute each user's value of each metric per day — clicks, sessions, revenue, latency percentiles of their requests — once, shared by all experiments. Per-experiment aggregation: join the experiment's triggered users (with variant and first exposure time) to their metric values from the exposure onward, and compute sufficient statistics per variant. Because user-level values are the unit of analysis, variance calculations are correct for user-randomised experiments. Ratio metrics such as clicks per session need the delta method, because the denominator varies by user; treating sessions as independent samples underestimates variance and produces false positives.

Two data rules prevent biases: compute user attributes used for filtering (bot status, segments) from pre-exposure data or at first exposure and freeze them — the Microsoft paper cites Booking.com doing exactly this — and never filter users based on post-treatment behaviour.

9 · Deep dive: sample size, power and the maths by hand

For a conversion metric with baseline rate p, the variance per user is p(1 − p). To detect an absolute difference δ with a two-sided test at significance α and power 1 − β, each variant needs about

n = 2 × (z_(1−α/2) + z_(1−β))² × p(1 − p) / δ²

With α = 0.05, z0.975 = 1.96; with 80% power, z0.80 = 0.84; (1.96 + 0.84)² = 7.84. A 5% baseline and a 2% relative lift means δ = 0.05 × 0.02 = 0.001. So n = 2 × 7.84 × 0.05 × 0.95 ÷ 0.001² = 2 × 7.84 × 0.0475 ÷ 0.000001 ≈ 745,000 users per variant (the lab prints 745,644). Kohavi's rule of thumb 16σ²/δ² gives 16 × 0.0475 ÷ 0.000001 = 760,000 — the same ballpark.

The formula explains every lever. Halve the detectable effect and you need four times the users (δ is squared). Higher-variance metrics need more users (revenue per user, with its long tail, is far noisier than conversion). Detecting a 10% lift needs about 30,000 per variant; a 2% lift on a 30% baseline only 92,000 (higher baseline, larger absolute δ). Triggering raises the effect size among analysed users. And variance reduction shrinks σ² directly.

CUPED (Controlled-experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data), from Microsoft's experimentation team, is the most widely used method. For each user, take a covariate X measured before the experiment — usually the same metric in the previous weeks — and analyse Yadj = Y − θ(X − mean(X)), with θ = cov(X, Y)/var(X). Since X is measured before treatment, it can't be affected by it, so the adjusted metric has the same expected difference between variants but less variance: the variance falls by a factor of (1 − ρ²), where ρ is the correlation between X and Y. Applied at Bing, the paper reports variance reduction of about 50%, "equivalent to doubling our traffic or halving the time".

10 · Deep dive: peeking and sequential testing

Teams look at results every day. If they stop as soon as p < 0.05, they inflate the false-positive rate: each look is another chance for random fluctuations to cross the line, and the fluctuations wander a lot early on. The lab runs 400 A/A experiments — no real difference — checked daily for 20 days: 24% of them crossed p < 0.05 at some point, versus 5% when tested once at the end. A quarter of "wins" would be pure noise.

Two ways out. Fixed horizon: compute the sample size and duration in advance (rounding up to whole weeks to cover weekday and weekend behaviour), and make the scorecard hide p-values — or label them clearly as not decision-ready — until then. Sequential testing: methods designed for repeated looks. Group-sequential designs spend the 5% α across a few planned interim analyses with strict early thresholds (the O'Brien-Fleming style boundary in the figure); always-valid p-values and confidence sequences (Johari, Pekelis and Walsh) stay valid under continuous monitoring at the cost of some power. Stopping for harm is different: guardrail breaches can stop an experiment at any time, because the claim "don't ship this" doesn't need a controlled false-positive rate on a win.

Two more statistical hygiene rules. Multiple comparisons: 100 metrics at α = 0.05 give about 5 false "wins" in a null experiment; designate primary metrics, and use corrections (Bonferroni, or false-discovery-rate control) for broad claims. Novelty and primacy effects: users react to change itself; look at effects by days since first exposure and prefer experiments long enough for the effect to stabilise.

11 · Deep dive: sample ratio mismatch

If an experiment is configured 50/50 and 1,637,070 users are analysed, you expect about 818,535 in each group. Random variation alone gives a standard deviation of √(1,637,070 × 0.25) ≈ 640 users. Microsoft's paper quotes an experiment with 821,588 versus 815,482: a difference of 3,053 from the expectation in each arm, about 4.8 standard deviations. The chi-square statistic is Σ (observed − expected)²/expected = 2 × 3,053² ÷ 818,535 ≈ 22.8, and with one degree of freedom the p-value is about 1.8 × 10−6 — "less than 1 in 500k", as the paper says (the lab computes 1 in about 549,000). That's a sample ratio mismatch (SRM).

An SRM means the groups are no longer comparable: something removed or added users differently by variant, and the removed users are almost never random. A new feature that crashes for some users removes them from treatment — and they were likely to be unhappy users, so treatment looks better. A slow treatment page loses impatient users before the exposure fires. A redirect in one variant loses users to the redirect. A bot filter that uses post-treatment behaviour removes different users in each arm. The paper's taxonomy groups causes by stage: assignment, execution, logging, analysis (data "cooking") and experimenter error.

So the platform runs a chi-square SRM test on every experiment, every day, before showing anything else, with a strict threshold (p < 0.0005 is common, to avoid false alarms across thousands of experiments), and blocks or prominently flags the scorecard when it fails. It also runs the test within segments (browser, platform, country, day) because an SRM concentrated in one segment points straight at the cause. And it runs A/A tests continuously: if more than about 5% of A/A tests are significant, or A/A tests show SRMs, the platform itself is broken.

12 · From scorecard to decision

A scorecard is not a decision. A typical rule set: the SRM check must pass; no guardrail may be significantly worse; the primary metric must be significantly better at the planned horizon (or by the sequential rule); and secondary metrics must tell a consistent story. Then a human reviews the size of the effect against the cost of the change — a statistically significant 0.1% lift may not be worth a more complex codebase — and checks for heterogeneous effects (a win overall that hurts one platform or country). Recording each decision with its reasoning in the experiment catalogue turns individual tests into institutional knowledge: what has been tried, what worked, and by how much.

13 · Failure modes and trade-offs

Assignment bugs (wrong bucket ranges, reused salts, unstable IDs) — caught by A/A tests and SRM. Logging differences between variants — new telemetry added only in treatment, or exposure logged at different points — caught by SRM and by checking event counts per variant. Interference between units — in a marketplace, treating some buyers changes prices for everyone; randomise by cluster or by time (switchback experiments). Harmful experiments — a bug that tanks revenue; staged ramp-up with real-time guardrails and automatic shutdown. Pipeline delays or failures — scorecards silently missing days; show data freshness on every scorecard. Human misuse — peeking, metric fishing, rerunning until it wins; product design that hides decision p-values until the planned end and records the primary metric before the start.

The trade-offs to state in the close: overlapping experiments for power against small interaction risks; triggered analysis for sensitivity against the risk of biased triggering; fixed horizons for simplicity against sequential methods for flexibility; strict SRM thresholds against false alarms; and speed of learning against the discipline that makes results trustworthy.

Choosing metrics: the overall evaluation criterion

Short experiments can only measure short-term behaviour, but businesses care about long-term outcomes. Experimentation teams therefore define an overall evaluation criterion — a metric or small set of metrics that is measurable in one or two weeks, sensitive enough to move, and believed to predict long-term value (sessions per user rather than revenue per day, for a search engine, because users returning more often is what drives long-term revenue). Bing's "puzzling outcomes" paper shows why the choice matters: a bug that made search results much worse increased distinct queries per user by over 10% and revenue per user by over 30% — users had to search more, and clicked more ads, to find what they wanted. Optimising the wrong short-term metric rewards degrading the product. Guardrails protect the things that must not get worse, and metric definitions belong in a central, versioned catalogue so every experiment computes them identically.

Network effects and cluster randomisation

Independence between units is an assumption, and some products break it. In a ride-hailing marketplace, giving treatment riders a discount pulls drivers away from control riders; in a social network, a new sharing feature for treatment users changes what control users see. Effects then leak between groups and the measured difference is biased, usually toward zero. Remedies change the randomisation unit: randomise whole cities or time slots ("switchback" experiments that alternate the whole market between variants every hour), or clusters of densely connected users in a social graph. Each reduces the number of independent units, raising variance, so these experiments need longer durations and careful analysis at the cluster level.

Bayesian versus frequentist presentation

Some platforms present results as "probability that treatment is better" and "expected loss" instead of p-values. These Bayesian summaries are easier for people to read, but they don't remove the underlying issues: with uninformative priors they behave much like frequentist tests, peeking still inflates errors unless the decision rule accounts for it, and SRMs and logging bugs invalidate them just the same. The platform choice matters less than consistency, clear decision rules, and data-quality gates.

Experimenting with ML and LLM systems

Model changes are increasingly what gets tested: a new ranking model, a different prompt, a cheaper LLM. Offline evaluation (held-out datasets, LLM-as-judge rubrics) decides which candidates are worth an online test, but only an online experiment measures what users actually do. Three specifics: cost is a guardrail — a variant that improves satisfaction by doubling tokens per request may not be worth it, so track cost per successful task; latency interacts with quality — a slower but better model can lose on engagement because users wait; and outputs are non-deterministic, which adds variance, so plan for larger samples and use variance reduction. Assign by user or conversation so a user doesn't flip between personalities mid-session, and log the model and prompt version with every request so the analysis can join on it.

Holdouts and cumulative impact

Each experiment measures one change against the current product. A year of individually "winning" experiments doesn't necessarily add up to the sum of their measured lifts: winners are overestimated, effects interact, and novelty fades. A long-term holdout — a small fraction of users (say 1–5%) kept on the product as it was at the start of the quarter or year, excluded from all launches — measures the cumulative effect of everything shipped. It costs those users the improvements and costs the business some revenue, so it's kept small and time-limited, but it's the only honest answer to "how much did all our experimentation actually move the metric?"

Building trust in the platform itself

A platform earns trust by continuously testing itself. Run A/A experiments permanently and check that about 5% are significant on each metric — more means variance is underestimated, less means it's overestimated. Check that SRM rates are low and investigate each one. Re-run notable experiments ("replications") to see whether big wins hold. Publish a running record of shipped experiments' measured effects compared with their effects after launch, since winners tend to be overestimated (the "winner's curse": the experiments that crossed the threshold are disproportionately those where noise added to the effect).

The real case: the MSN carousel and sample ratio mismatch (Microsoft, KDD 2019)

Fabijan and colleagues at Microsoft, with Lukas Vermeer of Booking.com, open their paper with a real experiment. A product team at MSN increased the number of rotating cards in the homepage carousel from 12 to 16. Related experiments had shown more cards led to more clicks, and the experiment "had enough statistical power to detect very small changes". Instead it "revealed a significant decrease in engagement with the cards. Users seeing more cards appeared to click less."

The platform also flagged a sample ratio mismatch: the treatment group "had less users in the analysis compared to what was configured and expected". A deep dive found the reason in the data processing ("cooking") phase: "the most engaged users in the treatment group were being classified as computer bots and removed from the experiment analysis by an algorithm that scans for bot activity". Treatment users engaged with 16 cards so much that they crossed the bot detector's engagement threshold. Removing exactly the most engaged treatment users made treatment look worse. "After accounting for the bot classification results, the results were flipped and the correct decision to ship the feature was made."

The paper's broader findings make SRM checks a must-have feature. About 6% of experiments at Microsoft exhibited an SRM — "a product running ten thousand experiments in a year can expect to see at least one SRM per day" — and LinkedIn reported about 10% in triggered analyses. It gives the numeric example used in this lesson (821,588 versus 815,482, less than 1 in 500,000 by chance), describes an A/A test whose SRM revealed an assignment bug that gave control one bucket too few out of a thousand, explains how reused hash seeds correlate experiments, and classifies SRM causes into a taxonomy from assignment to analysis. Its prevention advice is concrete: compute attributes such as bot status at first exposure and freeze them, as Booking.com does, so no post-treatment data is used to filter users.

The case generalises into the platform design of this lesson. The bug was not in the statistics — the test correctly found that the analysed treatment users clicked less. It was in which users were analysed. Only a check that doesn't depend on the metric at all — "are the groups the sizes they should be?" — could catch it. That is why SRM sits first in the analysis engine, ahead of any significance result, and why the most useful thing a platform team can do after an SRM is publish the root cause, so the next team that builds a bot filter, a redirect or a new logging path knows the trap.

What goes wrong, and what to watch

SRM rate across experiments and per segment — a rise means a platform or logging regression. A/A false-positive rate per metric — should be about 5%. Pipeline freshness and completeness — missing days or late data per variant. Guardrail breaches during ramps, and the time to automatic shutdown. Experiment hygiene: share of experiments that stop before their planned duration, change allocation mid-run, or have no pre-registered primary metric. Config push health — the fraction of clients on the latest configuration, since stale clients assign by old rules.

Goal: compute sample sizes by formula, check deterministic and independent hash bucketing, run the chi-square SRM test on the Microsoft paper's numbers, and measure how much daily peeking inflates false positives in A/A tests. Python 3.12, standard library only, about 4 seconds.

"""A/B testing maths: sample size, hash bucketing, the SRM chi-square check,
and why peeking inflates false positives. Python 3.12 stdlib only."""
import hashlib, math, random
from statistics import NormalDist

Z = NormalDist()

def sample_size(p, rel_mde, alpha=0.05, power=0.80):
    """Users per variant to detect a relative lift `rel_mde` on conversion rate p."""
    delta = p * rel_mde
    z = Z.inv_cdf(1 - alpha / 2) + Z.inv_cdf(power)
    return math.ceil(2 * z * z * p * (1 - p) / delta ** 2)

print("users per variant (alpha 0.05, power 80%):")
for p, mde in [(0.05, 0.02), (0.05, 0.05), (0.05, 0.10), (0.30, 0.02)]:
    print(f"  baseline {p:4.0%}, detect {mde:4.0%} relative lift -> {sample_size(p, mde):>10,}")

def bucket(user_id, salt, buckets=1000):              # stable, per-experiment salt
    h = hashlib.sha256(f"{salt}:{user_id}".encode()).digest()
    return int.from_bytes(h[:8], "big") % buckets

users = [f"u{i}" for i in range(200_000)]
a_treat = {u for u in users if bucket(u, "exp-carousel") < 500}
b_treat = {u for u in users if bucket(u, "exp-search") < 500}
both = len(a_treat & b_treat) / len(users)
print(f"\nstable: same user, same bucket every call -> {bucket('u42', 'exp-carousel') == bucket('u42', 'exp-carousel')}")
print(f"independent salts: P(treated in A and B) = {both:.3f} (expect 0.250)")

def srm_p_value(n_a, n_b, expected_a=0.5):
    total = n_a + n_b
    ea, eb = total * expected_a, total * (1 - expected_a)
    chi2 = (n_a - ea) ** 2 / ea + (n_b - eb) ** 2 / eb
    return chi2, math.erfc(math.sqrt(chi2 / 2))         # chi-square, 1 degree of freedom
chi2, p = srm_p_value(821_588, 815_482)                  # numbers from the Microsoft SRM paper
print(f"\nSRM check 821,588 vs 815,482: chi2 = {chi2:.1f}, p = {p:.2e} (about 1 in {1 / p:,.0f})")

# Peeking: an A/A test (no real difference) checked every day for 20 days,
# stopping at the first p < 0.05, versus one test at the planned end.
rng = random.Random(11)
def aa_trial(days=20, per_day=2_000, p=0.05):
    ca = cb = na = nb = 0
    peek_hit = False
    for _ in range(days):
        na += per_day; nb += per_day
        ca += sum(rng.random() < p for _ in range(per_day))
        cb += sum(rng.random() < p for _ in range(per_day))
        pa, pb = ca / na, cb / nb
        pool = (ca + cb) / (na + nb)
        se = math.sqrt(pool * (1 - pool) * (1 / na + 1 / nb))
        pval = 2 * (1 - Z.cdf(abs(pa - pb) / se))
        if pval < 0.05: peek_hit = True
    return peek_hit, pval < 0.05
runs = [aa_trial() for _ in range(400)]
print(f"\nA/A false positives: peeking daily {sum(r[0] for r in runs) / len(runs):.0%}, "
      f"single test at the end {sum(r[1] for r in runs) / len(runs):.0%} (nominal 5%)")

Run it: save as ab_lab.py and run python ab_lab.py.

What to look for.

  1. Sample size per variant: about 745,600 to detect a 2% relative lift on a 5% baseline; 119,300 for a 5% lift; 29,800 for 10%; and 91,600 for a 2% lift on a 30% baseline. Halving the effect quadruples the sample.
  2. Bucketing is stable (the same user, salt and bucket every call), and with different salts the share of users treated in both experiments is 0.250 — the product of the two 50% shares, which is what independence means.
  3. The SRM check on 821,588 versus 815,482 gives χ² ≈ 22.8 and p ≈ 1.8 × 10−6, about 1 in 549,000 — matching the paper's "less than 1 in 500k".
  4. Peeking: about 24% of A/A tests checked daily for 20 days show p < 0.05 at some point, versus about 5% when tested once at the end.

Try next: add CUPED to the A/A simulation — give each user a pre-period conversion correlated with their in-experiment conversion — and watch the standard error fall; then make treatment drop 1% of its users at random versus 1% of its least engaged users and see which one biases the result.

Work it out

A team tests a new checkout flow. Baseline conversion is 8%. They expect a 3% relative lift (8% → 8.24%). Traffic is 200,000 checkout visitors/day, split 50/50. They check results daily and on day 4 see treatment at 8.4% vs control 8.1%, p = 0.043, and want to ship.

(a) How long should this test run? (b) Is the day-4 result trustworthy? (c) They also notice 412,000 exposures in control and 408,000 in treatment. What does that mean?

Answer

(a) Required sample size. For a two-proportion test at 80% power and 5% significance, the sample per arm is roughly 16 × p(1−p) / Δ², where Δ is the absolute difference. Here p = 0.08 and Δ = 0.0024.

16 × 0.08 × 0.92 ÷ 0.0024² = 1.178 ÷ 0.00000576 ≈ 204,000 per arm, which at 100,000 visitors per arm per day is only about 2 days. That looks encouraging, but 80% power means a 20% chance of missing a real effect; targeting 90% power raises the multiplier from about 15.7 to about 21, roughly 273,000 per arm, about 3 days. And running a full week is the usual minimum anyway, to cover weekday and weekend behaviour. A test that spans only part of a weekly cycle measures a different population, not just a smaller one.

(b) The day-4 result. p = 0.043 is below 0.05, so it looks significant. It isn't trustworthy, for three reasons:

  • They peeked. Checking daily gives several chances to cross the threshold. Under no real effect, the probability of at least one daily check showing p < 0.05 is well above 5% — roughly 15–20% for a week of daily looks, and the lab's 20 daily looks gave 24%. A p-value is only valid at a pre-specified stopping point; stopping because it became significant is what invalidates it.
  • p = 0.043 is weak evidence even at face value, and the observed lift (8.4 vs 8.1, 3.7% relative) is larger than the expected 3%. Early significant results are systematically overstated, because the ones that cross the line early are those where noise happened to add to the effect. Expect the true effect to be smaller.
  • The weekly cycle. Four days isn't a full week; checkout behaviour differs between weekdays and weekends.

The right answer: run to the pre-registered horizon — at least a full week — and read the result then. If they genuinely need continuous monitoring, the platform should use sequential methods (always-valid confidence intervals), which stay correct under peeking at the cost of slightly more data.

(c) 412,000 vs 408,000 — sample ratio mismatch, the finding that matters most. With a 50/50 split and 820,000 total, the expected split is 410,000 each with a standard deviation of √(820,000 × 0.25) ≈ 453. The observed imbalance is 2,000 from expectation, about 4.4 standard deviations — roughly 1 in 100,000 by chance (χ² = 2 × 2,000² ÷ 410,000 ≈ 19.5).

Something is systematically removing users from the treatment arm, and the likely explanations all bias the result in the same direction:

  • The new checkout crashes or errors for some users, who never log an exposure. Those users would mostly have failed to convert, so removing them inflates treatment's conversion rate.
  • The new flow is slower to load, so users on poor connections abandon before the exposure fires — again removing likely non-converters from treatment.
  • A bot filter or redirect behaves differently between arms — the MSN carousel story.

The conclusion: the result is invalid and must not be shipped, and the bias plausibly explains the entire observed 0.3-point lift. Stop the experiment, find the missing 2,000 users (check SRM by browser, platform and day), fix the cause and restart. A platform that showed "significant, +3.7%, ship it" next to an unflagged SRM would cause a bad decision — which is why automatic SRM detection with a hard block is the most valuable feature in the whole system.

Estimate it

An LLM product wants to test a new system prompt. The primary metric is "thumbs-up rate per conversation", currently 12%, with 400,000 conversations a day from 150,000 users. They hope for a 3% relative improvement. How long must the test run, and what would you change?

Answer

δ = 0.12 × 0.03 = 0.0036; variance 0.12 × 0.88 = 0.1056; n ≈ 15.7 × 0.1056 ÷ 0.0036² ≈ 128,000 per variant at 80% power. But the unit should be the user (randomise by user so nobody flips prompts), and conversations from one user are correlated, so the effective sample is closer to users than to conversations. With 150,000 users a day split 50/50 and users returning, unique users per variant accumulate to 128,000 in perhaps a week or two; analyse the per-user thumbs-up rate with the delta method. Changes: only about a fraction of conversations get any rating, so consider a denser metric (task completion, follow-up rephrasing rate) or combine; apply CUPED with each user's pre-period rating rate; run at least two full weeks for novelty effects; and add cost and latency guardrails.
