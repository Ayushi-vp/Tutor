At 03:12 an on-call engineer's phone buzzes: "checkout error rate above 2% for 5 minutes". They open a dashboard and see, on one screen, error rate per service, latency percentiles, database connection pool usage and deployment markers for the last hour — and spot that errors began the minute a new version of the payment client rolled out. That whole experience depends on a system that collects numbers from tens of thousands of processes every few seconds, keeps them for weeks, answers questions over them in under a second, and decides by itself when something is wrong enough to wake a person.

Building that system looks easy at first. Every service exposes counters; something collects them; something draws graphs. Then the problems arrive. Storage explodes because a developer added a label containing user IDs, creating millions of new series overnight. Dashboards time out because a 30-day query reads billions of raw points. The fleet-wide p99 latency on the dashboard is wrong because someone averaged per-host p99s. An outage goes unnoticed for forty minutes because the monitoring system shared a network with the thing that failed, and its alerts silently stopped.

At Facebook in 2015, the monitoring system generated over 2 billion unique time series with about 12 million data points added per second — over a trillion points a day. Stored naively at 16 bytes per point, the most recent day alone would need 16 TB of memory. The engineering answer, the Gorilla time-series database, is this lesson's case study and the source of the compression trick every modern metrics store uses.

This lesson designs a metrics and monitoring system in interview order: requirements, estimates done by hand, the data model and API, the write path and storage, then deep dives into cardinality, compression, querying (counters, rates, histograms), retention and downsampling, and alerting. It ends with how Gorilla worked in production and a lab that compresses real-looking time series bit by bit.

The mental model: a ledger of readings

Picture a building manager who walks past every meter in a large building every 15 seconds and writes down the reading: "14:00:00, floor 3 east, electricity, 1,204.5 kWh". Each meter is identified not by a serial number but by a description — floor, wing, kind of meter. Each distinct description is one column in the ledger; each walk adds one row. Later, the manager answers questions by reading columns: "how fast did floor 3's electricity use grow over the last hour?", "which floors are using more than usual?".

That is a metrics system. A time series is one column: a stream of samples, each a timestamp and a number. The column's description is a metric name plus labels — key-value pairs like http_requests_total{service="checkout", status="500", region="eu"}. The walk every 15 seconds is the scrape interval. And the questions are queries that aggregate across columns and over time.

Two features of this ledger make it unlike a normal database. First, it is almost all writes: every series gets a new sample every interval whether or not anyone looks. Second, the shape of the data is extremely regular — timestamps arrive at near-fixed intervals, and most values barely change between readings — which is what makes extreme compression possible. Where the analogy breaks: the manager's ledger has a fixed set of meters, but in software the set of series changes constantly (every new pod, every new label value creates a new column), and the number of columns, not the number of rows, is what ends up breaking things.

What kinds of numbers

A counter only goes up — requests served, bytes sent, errors — and resets to zero when a process restarts. Nobody cares about its raw value; you query its rate of increase. A gauge goes up and down — memory in use, queue length, temperature. A histogram counts observations into buckets — how many requests took under 50 ms, under 100 ms, under 250 ms — so you can estimate percentiles (the 99th percentile, or p99, is the value 99% of observations are below). These three cover nearly everything.

Where metrics sit among logs and traces

Monitoring data comes in three shapes, often called the three pillars of observability. Metrics are numbers aggregated over time — cheap to store and fast to query, perfect for dashboards and alerts, but they can't tell you about one particular request. Logs are individual events with arbitrary fields — rich, high-cardinality, and expensive to keep and search. Traces follow one request across services, showing where its time went — detailed, usually sampled. A good monitoring design uses each for what it's good at: metrics say that something is wrong and roughly where, traces and logs say why. Most metrics-system disasters come from forcing log-shaped data (one value per user or request) into the metrics store.

The rest of the lesson explains each box, with one rule running through all of them: the number of distinct series is the budget. Everything else — compression, sharding, downsampling — is about making each series cheap; controlling cardinality is about not having too many.

1 · Requirements

Functional. Collect numeric metrics (counters, gauges, histograms) from hosts, containers, applications and managed services. Store them with labels. Query with aggregation across labels and functions over time (rate, sum by service, percentile from histograms). Dashboards. Alert rules with notification routing, grouping and silencing. Multiple teams or tenants.

Non-functional. Ingest millions of samples per second. Fresh data — a sample is queryable within seconds of collection, because alerting depends on it. Dashboard queries over the last hour in well under a second; over 30 days in a few seconds. Retain raw data for weeks and rolled-up data for a year or more. Highly available, especially the alerting path; and crucially, more available than the systems it monitors, and independent of them. Cost proportional to the value of the data.

Out of scope, said aloud: logs and traces (related, but different data shapes: events and request trees rather than numbers over time), and business analytics over raw events.

Clarifying questions. "How many hosts and containers, and how many series does a typical service expose?" "Scrape interval?" "Retention at full resolution, and for rollups?" "Is this multi-tenant — must one team be unable to hurt another?" The last question decides whether per-tenant limits are a feature or an afterthought.

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Scenario: 20,000 pods and hosts, each exposing on average 2,000 series (runtime, HTTP, database client, business metrics, histogram buckets add up fast), scraped every 15 seconds, raw retention 15 days, rollups for 13 months.

Active series. 20,000 × 2,000 = 40 million series. This is the number to state first.

Ingest rate. 40M series ÷ 15 s = 2.67 million samples/s.

Raw storage. Uncompressed, a sample is a 64-bit timestamp plus a 64-bit float: 16 bytes, so 2.67M × 16 = 42.7 MB/s, 3.7 TB/day. Compressed Gorilla-style at about 1.5 bytes/sample (Prometheus documents 1–2): 2.67M × 1.5 = 4 MB/s, 346 GB/day, and 15 days = 5.2 TB per stored copy. Compression is a 10× saving on disk and, more importantly, on memory.

Memory for recent data. Ingesters keep about two hours in memory, compressed: 2.67M samples/s × 7,200 s × 1.5 bytes = 28.8 GB per copy for the samples themselves. But each active series also costs an index entry, its label strings and an open chunk — a few kilobytes per series in practice. At 3 KB × 40M = 120 GB per copy, and with replication factor 3 in the write path, 360 GB across the ingester fleet: about 12 ingesters with 32 GB of usable memory each, plus headroom. Note which term dominated: the per-series overhead, not the samples. That is why cardinality, not sample rate, is the cost driver.

Rollups. For 13 months keep 5-minute rollups: per series, 288 intervals a day × (min, max, sum, count) = 1,152 values a day; at ~2 bytes each compressed, 2.3 KB per series per day, × 40M series = 92 GB/day, ~36 TB for 13 months — but series churn (pods replaced daily create new series) means the total number of series over a year is several times the active count, so real long-term storage is larger. Object storage makes this affordable.

Queries. A dashboard panel showing p99 latency for one service across 500 pods over 24 hours at 1-minute steps reads 500 pods × (say) 12 histogram buckets × 5,760 raw samples (24 h of 15 s) = 34.6 million samples. Decoding at hundreds of millions of samples per second per core, that is a fraction of a second — fine once. But a dashboard with 20 panels, refreshed every 30 s by 200 engineers during an incident, is 20 × 200 ÷ 30 = 133 such queries per second. Recording rules and caching are not optional.

       / sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="checkout"}[5m])) > 0.02

Note the shape of the query: rate(…[5m]) turns a counter into per-second increase over a 5-minute window, and sum by (service) aggregates away every other label. Nearly every useful query is "rate, then sum by a few labels", which is what the storage must make fast.

4 · Data model

A series is identified by its full label set (the metric name is just the label __name__). Internally, each series gets a numeric ID, and an inverted index maps each label pair to the list of series IDs that have it: service="checkout" → [17, 42, 91, …], status="500" → [42, 77, …]. A query selecting {service="checkout", status="500"} intersects the two lists. Samples for each series are stored in compressed chunks of about two hours each. On disk, a block holds all series' chunks for a time range plus the index for that range, and is immutable once written; Prometheus keeps the current two hours in memory (the head block), protected by a write-ahead log in 128 MB segments, and compacts older two-hour blocks into larger ones.

The inverted index is why high cardinality hurts twice: more series means more memory for the series themselves and longer posting lists to intersect for every query that touches them.

5 · High-level architecture

Collection. In a pull design, scrapers discover targets from the orchestrator (Kubernetes) and fetch each target's /metrics endpoint every interval. In a push design, an agent on each host (Datadog's agent, an OpenTelemetry collector) gathers local metrics and sends batches. Either way, samples arrive at the ingest tier in batches.

Write path. Stateless distributors authenticate the tenant, validate labels, enforce limits (series per tenant, samples per second, labels per series), compute a hash of each series' label set, and send it to the ingesters that own that hash range — three of them, for replication — using a consistent-hash ring (in2). An ingester appends samples to its WAL, adds them to compressed in-memory chunks, and every two hours cuts a block and uploads it to object storage. A compactor later merges blocks from the three replicas (deduplicating), merges small blocks into larger ones, applies retention and builds downsampled rollups. This is the architecture of horizontally scaled Prometheus-compatible stores such as Cortex, Thanos and Grafana Mimir.

Read path. A query frontend splits long-range queries into day-sized pieces, serves completed pieces from a results cache, and sends the rest to queriers, which fetch recent data from ingesters and older data from blocks in object storage (through a store gateway that caches block indexes), then merge.

Rules and alerts. A rule evaluator runs recording rules (precompute expensive aggregates into new series) and alerting rules every evaluation interval. Firing alerts go to an alert manager, which deduplicates (several evaluator replicas fire the same alert), groups related alerts into one notification, applies silences and inhibitions, and routes to pagers, chat or tickets.

6 · Deep dive: cardinality, the real cost driver

Every distinct combination of label values is a separate series. The number of possible combinations is the product of each label's distinct values. The lab computes it for a typical HTTP metric: 40 services × 25 endpoints × 6 status codes × 30 pods × 3 regions = 540,000 possible series for one metric (in practice fewer, since each pod belongs to one service). Add a user_id label with a million values and the ceiling becomes 540 billion. Even if only a small fraction of combinations ever occur, the series count jumps by orders of magnitude.

The Prometheus documentation gives the rules plainly: do not use labels for "user IDs, email addresses, or other unbounded sets of values"; try to keep a metric's cardinality below 10, and investigate alternatives for anything that could exceed 100. Its own example: 10,000 nodes exposing a per-filesystem metric gives about 100,000 series, which is fine; adding a per-user quota label with 10,000 users would reach "a double digit number of millions", which "is too much".

Cardinality also grows silently through churn. Every Kubernetes deployment replaces pods, and each new pod name is a new value of the pod label, so old series stop and new ones start. Active series stay flat, but the total number of series in a day's blocks and in the index grows with every deploy. A service deploying 20 times a day with 50 pods creates 1,000 pod values a day for each of its metrics.

The controls, in order of importance: limits at ingestion (per tenant and per metric, rejecting excess series with a clear error), alerts on series growth (a metric whose series count doubled in ten minutes is a bad deploy), relabelling rules that drop or rewrite labels at collection time, and a cultural rule: per-entity detail belongs in logs and traces, which are designed for high-cardinality fields.

7 · Deep dive: Gorilla compression

A raw sample is a 64-bit timestamp and a 64-bit IEEE-754 float: 16 bytes. Gorilla's insight is that consecutive samples in one series are extremely similar, and it compresses timestamps and values separately within two-hour blocks.

Timestamps: delta of deltas. With a 60-second interval, timestamps go 1000, 1060, 1120, 1179, 1240. The deltas are 60, 60, 59, 61; the deltas of the deltas are 0, −1, +2. The paper's encoding: a delta-of-delta of 0 is stored as a single 0 bit; a value in [−63, 64] as 10 plus 7 bits; [−255, 256] as 110 plus 9 bits; [−2047, 2048] as 1110 plus 12 bits; anything else as 1111 plus 32 bits. Facebook found that "about 96% of all time stamps" compressed to a single bit.

Values: XOR with the previous value. Two similar floats share their sign, exponent and leading mantissa bits, so their bitwise XOR is mostly zeros, with a short run of "meaningful" bits in the middle. The encoding: if the XOR is zero (same value), store a single 0. Otherwise store 1, then either 0 and just the meaningful bits if they fit inside the previous value's window of leading and trailing zeros, or 1, 5 bits for the number of leading zeros, 6 bits for the length of the meaningful bits, and the bits themselves. In production, roughly 51% of values compressed to a single bit (unchanged), about 30% used the reuse-window case at 26.6 bits on average, and 19% the full case at 36.9 bits. Overall: 1.37 bytes per point, a 12× reduction, with two-hour blocks chosen because longer blocks gave diminishing returns.

The lab implements both encodings. Results depend sharply on the data: an idle gauge that flips between 0 and 1 with slightly jittery timestamps averages 1.5 bytes per point; a counter 2.7; a CPU percentage rounded to one decimal place 7.7 (decimal fractions like 41.3 have messy binary mantissas, so many low bits change); and random floats 8.2. Integers and slowly changing values compress best — a reason to record counts and integer milliseconds where you can.

8 · Deep dive: counters, rates and histograms

Counters and rate. A counter's raw value is meaningless (it depends on when the process started). rate(x[5m]) takes the samples in the last five minutes, computes the increase from first to last, and divides by the time span. If the counter drops (a process restart reset it to zero), the function treats the drop as a reset and adds the pre-reset value, so restarts don't produce negative rates. Because rate needs at least two samples in the window, the window must be several scrape intervals long — 5 minutes for 15-second scrapes is a common choice.

Percentiles need histograms. Suppose server A served 9,000 requests with p99 = 100 ms and server B served 1,000 with p99 = 900 ms. The average of the p99s is 500 ms, which describes nothing: the combined p99 is determined by the slowest 1% of all 10,000 requests, i.e. the slowest 100, and since B alone has at least 10 requests above 900 ms and A has 90 above 100 ms, the true answer depends on the full distributions. A histogram stores counts per bucket (le="0.1" means "less than or equal to 0.1 s"), counters that can be summed across servers. The fleet percentile is computed from the summed buckets by finding the bucket containing the 99th-percentile rank and interpolating inside it. The accuracy is limited by bucket boundaries, so choose them around your SLO thresholds (if the target is 300 ms, have a bucket edge at 0.3). Client-side summaries compute exact quantiles per process but cannot be aggregated across processes at all.

Newer formats (native or exponential histograms in Prometheus and OpenTelemetry) use automatically scaled buckets with a fixed relative error, getting better accuracy without hand-picked edges — at the cost of more complex storage.

9 · Deep dive: retention, downsampling and the query path

Raw data at 15-second resolution is valuable for days; nobody needs it for a graph of last year. A compactor turns old blocks into rollups: for each series and each 5-minute (and later 1-hour) interval, store min, max, sum and count. From those you can derive the average (sum ÷ count), the extremes, and for counters the increase. A one-year query at 1-hour resolution reads 8,760 rollup points per series instead of 2.1 million raw samples — 240 times less. Keep max as well as average: averaging erases the spikes you might be looking for.

On the query side, three techniques carry the load. Recording rules precompute expensive aggregates on a schedule — "error ratio per service" every minute — so dashboards and alerts read a handful of series instead of aggregating thousands at query time. Time-splitting and result caching: the query frontend splits a 7-day query into seven 1-day queries run in parallel, and caches the six completed days, so a refresh recomputes only today. Pushing work to the data: queriers evaluate aggregations next to the storage of each shard and send back partial results rather than raw samples.

10 · Deep dive: alerting that wakes the right person

An alerting rule is a query evaluated every 15–60 seconds. If its result is non-empty for the rule's for duration (say 5 minutes), the alert fires; the duration filters out brief blips. Good rules alert on symptoms — what users feel: error rate, latency against the SLO, the rate at which the error budget is being burned — rather than causes like high CPU, which may or may not matter. Google's SRE book frames this around the four golden signals: latency, traffic, errors and saturation.

The alert manager turns alerts into notifications humans can act on. Deduplication: run two or three evaluators for availability and collapse their identical alerts. Grouping: 200 pods failing at once become one notification, "checkout: 200 instances erroring", not 200 pages. Inhibition: if the whole region is down, suppress the per-service alerts it causes. Silences: mute known issues during maintenance. Routing: page for urgent, chat or ticket for slow burns, by team ownership labels.

Finally, alerting must keep working when everything else is failing. Run the alerting path on separate infrastructure with minimal dependencies, monitor it from outside, and wire a dead man's switch: an alert that always fires and is sent to an external service, which pages if it stops arriving.

11 · Failure modes and trade-offs

Cardinality explosion: ingester memory climbs, out-of-memory crashes, and the WAL replay recreates the same series on restart, so ingesters crash-loop — ingestion stops for everyone. Per-tenant limits at the distributor are the defence. Ingester loss: with replication factor 3, queries still find two copies; the lost ingester's unflushed head is recovered from its WAL on restart, or from the replicas. Object storage slow or unavailable: recent queries still work from ingesters; long-range ones fail; ingesters must buffer blocks until upload succeeds. Query of death: one huge ad-hoc query (all series, 90 days, no aggregation) exhausts querier memory; limit samples per query and series per query, and isolate tenants. Scrape failures: a target that can't be scraped produces gaps; alert on up == 0, and make alert rules robust to missing data (an error ratio with no data should not read as 0%).

The trade-offs to state in the close: resolution versus retention and cost; cardinality (detail) versus stability; pull's liveness signal versus push's flexibility; freshness of alerts versus noise (the for duration); and independence of the monitoring stack versus the convenience of sharing infrastructure with what it monitors.

Why Gorilla was an in-memory write-through cache, and what that bought

Facebook's existing system, ODS, stored data in HBase. Dashboards and automated analysis overwhelmingly queried recent data — the paper reports that at least 85% of all queries to ODS were for data collected in the past 26 hours — and HBase reads were too slow for interactive exploration. Gorilla kept the most recent 26 hours of all series in memory, compressed, in front of HBase; new data was written to both. Twenty-six hours, not 24, so a day-over-day comparison of the full last day always fits. The design requirements were 2 billion unique series identified by string keys, 700 million data points per minute, and 26 hours retained, with tolerance for single-node failures and network cuts. Two regions each ran a full copy and did not coordinate synchronously; a query retried in the other region if one was unhealthy. The paper reports between 73× and 350× lower query latency than the HBase path depending on query size, which enabled new tools — correlation search across millions of series and denser dashboards — that had been impossible before. The lesson for a design interview: tiering by age (memory for hot, disk or object storage for cold) follows the access pattern, and the access pattern of monitoring is overwhelmingly "now".

Sharding by series, and the hot-tenant problem

Hashing the full label set spreads series evenly, but query patterns don't align with that hash: "sum by service for checkout" touches series scattered across every ingester, so every query fans out to every shard. Some systems shard by a subset of labels (tenant, or tenant and metric name) so that one tenant's series stay together and a query touches fewer shards; the risk is a hot shard when one tenant is huge. Mimir's "shuffle sharding" gives each tenant a small, pseudo-random subset of ingesters, so tenants rarely share all their shards — one tenant's cardinality explosion overloads only its subset and few other tenants share that exact subset. This is the hot-key problem from in2 and in3 in a different costume.

Exemplars bridge metrics and traces

A latency histogram tells you p99 rose; it can't tell you which request was slow, because request IDs can't be labels. An exemplar attaches a sample trace ID to a histogram bucket observation — "here's one request that landed in the 1–2.5 s bucket at 14:03:12, trace ID abc123" — stored alongside the series but not as a label, so it adds no cardinality. A dashboard can then link from a spike directly to a representative trace. It is the clean answer to the developer who wanted customer_id as a label.

Out-of-order and late samples

Gorilla's encoding assumes samples arrive in time order per series. Pushed metrics from agents that buffer during network outages, or from many sources writing the same series, can arrive late. Options: reject samples older than the head's newest timestamp (simple, loses data), accept them into a separate out-of-order buffer that is merged at compaction (Prometheus added such a window), or require that each series has exactly one writer. Interviewers like this edge case because it tests whether you understood why the compression works.

Pull at scale: discovery, federation and sharded scrapers

A single Prometheus server scrapes perhaps a few million series before memory and CPU run out. Pull scales by splitting the work: each scraper is assigned a subset of targets (by hashing target addresses, or by Kubernetes namespace) and forwards samples to the central store with remote write, so no single scraper sees everything. Service discovery keeps target lists current as pods come and go — the scraper asks the orchestrator's API, rather than reading a static list — which is also how pull gets its liveness signal: a target that is in the discovered list but fails to answer is down, and the synthetic series up records 0 for it. Federation, where a global server scrapes pre-aggregated series from regional servers, is the older pattern for multi-region views; it works for a few aggregate series, but shipping raw series through it recreates the single-server bottleneck at the top.

Monitoring LLM and GPU fleets

AI serving brings metrics with unusual shapes. Latency splits into time to first token and time per output token, each a histogram per model and version. Throughput is tokens per second, input and output separately. Saturation is GPU memory, KV-cache occupancy and batch size rather than CPU. These are all fine as long as labels stay bounded — model, version, region, replica, GPU type — and per-request details (prompt length, user, conversation) go to traces with exemplars. Quality metrics such as evaluation scores, refusal rates or thumbs-down rates can also be time series, low-cardinality by model version, which makes "the new model version is worse" an alert like any other.

The real case: Gorilla, Facebook's in-memory time-series database (VLDB 2015)

Pelkonen and colleagues describe the problem in numbers. As of spring 2015, Facebook's monitoring systems generated "more than 2 billion unique time series of counters, with about 12 million data points added per second", over a trillion points per day; at 16 bytes per point, the paper notes, the resulting 16 TB of RAM "would be too resource intensive for practical deployment". The fix was the streaming compression in this lesson, reaching "an average of 1.37 bytes per point, a 12x reduction in size".

Design choices worth quoting in an interview: data is sharded by series key, each host owns its shards with no coordination between hosts ("share-nothing"); a two-hour block per series is the compression unit; recent data lives in memory while all data is also written to the long-term HBase store; and the system tolerates losing a small amount of recent data during failures in exchange for availability, since an aggregate dashboard rarely depends on a single missing point. When Gorilla launched, the last 26 hours of all series fit into 1.3 TB of RAM on 20 machines; eighteen months later, after data growth, the clusters had doubled twice to 80 machines each — growth handled "simply" by adding hosts and changing the shard mapping.

Two operational lessons from the paper stand out. Prioritise recent data: when a host restarted, it was better to accept new writes immediately than to block until old data was reloaded. And faster queries changed behaviour: once interactive, query volume grew and new tools appeared, so the capacity of the read path must anticipate success.

The paper's fault-tolerance section is equally instructive. Gorilla ran in two regions, each a complete copy, with writes streamed to both and no synchronous coordination between them; reads went to the closer region and failed over to the other if it was unhealthy or returned partial data. Within a region, a shard manager assigned shards to hosts; when a host failed, its shards moved, and clients buffered writes (about a minute's worth) while that happened — data beyond the buffer was dropped, a loss the designers accepted because monitoring is dominated by aggregate analysis rather than individual points, and the long-term HBase store kept everything. Recent data was explicitly prioritised: a restarted host began accepting new writes before older data was reloaded. Each of these choices trades a small, bounded loss of old or individual points for availability of the fresh aggregate view that on-call engineers need — exactly the right trade for monitoring and the wrong one for a billing ledger.

What goes wrong, and what to watch

Cardinality explosions after a deploy — watch active series per tenant and per metric, and alert on growth rate. Ingester memory pressure and WAL replay time — a restart that takes 20 minutes to replay is 20 minutes of reduced redundancy. Query latency and rejected queries per tenant — a single heavy dashboard can starve others. Scrape duration and failures — targets that take longer to scrape than the interval produce gaps. Rule evaluation lag — if evaluating all rules takes longer than the interval, alerts are late. Notification delivery — the alert manager's success rate to pagers, and the dead man's switch. And the meta-rule: the monitoring of the monitoring system lives somewhere else.

Goal: implement Gorilla's two encodings — delta-of-delta timestamps and XOR'd float values — and measure bytes per point on four kinds of series; then see how label combinations multiply into series. Python 3.12, standard library only, under a second.

"""Gorilla-style time-series compression (delta-of-delta timestamps, XOR'd
float values) and a cardinality calculator. Python 3.12 stdlib only."""
import math, random, struct

class Bits:
    def __init__(self): self.n, self.v = 0, 0          # bit count, big integer
    def put(self, value, width):
        self.v = (self.v << width) | (value & ((1 << width) - 1)); self.n += width

def f2i(x): return struct.unpack(">Q", struct.pack(">d", x))[0]

def compress(points):                                  # points: [(unix_ts, float)]
    b = Bits()
    t0, v0 = points[0]
    b.put(t0, 64); b.put(f2i(v0), 64)                  # first point stored raw
    prev_t, prev_delta, prev_v = t0, None, f2i(v0)
    prev_lead, prev_trail = None, None
    for t, v in points[1:]:
        delta = t - prev_t
        dod = delta if prev_delta is None else delta - prev_delta
        if dod == 0:                b.put(0b0, 1)
        elif -63 <= dod <= 64:      b.put(0b10, 2);   b.put(dod, 7)
        elif -255 <= dod <= 256:    b.put(0b110, 3);  b.put(dod, 9)
        elif -2047 <= dod <= 2048:  b.put(0b1110, 4); b.put(dod, 12)
        else:                       b.put(0b1111, 4); b.put(dod, 32)
        prev_t, prev_delta = t, delta
        x = f2i(v) ^ prev_v
        if x == 0:
            b.put(0, 1)                                # same value: one bit
        else:
            lead = min(64 - x.bit_length(), 31)
            trail = (x & -x).bit_length() - 1
            if prev_lead is not None and lead >= prev_lead and trail >= prev_trail:
                b.put(0b10, 2)                         # reuse previous window
                b.put(x >> prev_trail, 64 - prev_lead - prev_trail)
            else:
                b.put(0b11, 2); b.put(lead, 5)
                sig = 64 - lead - trail; b.put(sig - 1, 6); b.put(x >> trail, sig)
                prev_lead, prev_trail = lead, trail
        prev_v = f2i(v)
    return b.n

rng = random.Random(0)
start = 1_760_000_000
def series(kind, n=720):                               # 2 hours at 10 s intervals
    out, ctr = [], 0
    for i in range(n):
        ts = start + 10 * i + (rng.choice([0, 0, 0, 0, 1, -1]) if i else 0)
        if kind == "idle gauge":   v = 0.0 if rng.random() < 0.6 else 1.0
        elif kind == "counter":    ctr += rng.randrange(0, 40); v = float(ctr)
        elif kind == "cpu %":      v = round(40 + 10 * math.sin(i / 50) + rng.gauss(0, 2), 1)
        else:                      v = rng.random() * 1000      # noisy float
        out.append((ts, v))
    return out
for kind in ("idle gauge", "counter", "cpu %", "random float"):
    bits = compress(series(kind))
    print(f"{kind:12}: {bits / 8 / 720:5.2f} bytes/point (raw: 16)")

# Cardinality: every distinct label combination is its own time series.
labels = {"service": 40, "endpoint": 25, "status_code": 6, "pod": 30, "region": 3}
total = math.prod(labels.values())
print(f"\nhttp_requests_total with {labels} -> up to {total:,} series")
print(f"add user_id (1,000,000 users)          -> up to {total * 1_000_000:,} series")

Run it: save as tsdb_lab.py and run python tsdb_lab.py. (It computes compressed size only; writing the matching decoder is a good exercise.)

What to look for.

  1. All four series start at 16 bytes per point raw. The idle gauge compresses to about 1.5 bytes: most values repeat (1 bit) and most timestamps are perfectly regular (1 bit); the jittered ones cost 9 bits.
  2. The counter compresses to about 2.7 bytes: integer-valued floats change only a few mantissa bits per step.
  3. The CPU percentage rounded to one decimal place costs about 7.7 bytes, and random floats 8.2: decimal fractions have long, changing binary mantissas, so XOR leaves many meaningful bits. Gorilla's 1.37 average reflects that real monitoring data is dominated by repeats and integers.
  4. One HTTP metric with five ordinary labels can reach 540,000 series; adding a user ID label lifts the ceiling to 540 billion. Bytes per point barely matter next to that.

Try next: remove the timestamp jitter and watch the idle gauge approach Gorilla's numbers; store CPU as an integer number of basis points (4,130 instead of 41.3) and compare.

Work it out

A service exposes http_requests_total{service, host, route, method, status}. There are 40 services, 200 hosts each, 30 routes, 5 methods and 8 status codes. A developer adds a customer_id label to help debug one customer's issue. The platform has 100,000 tenants.

(a) Cardinality before and after? (b) What happens operationally? (c) What should have prevented it, and how should the developer get what they wanted?

Answer

(a) Before. If every host served every route, method and status, the ceiling would be 40 × 200 × 30 × 5 × 8 = 9,600,000. But a host belongs to one service, so the real ceiling is 8,000 hosts × 30 × 5 × 8 = 9.6 million only if every combination occurs — and most routes accept one or two methods and return two or three status codes. Realistically this is on the order of 100,000–300,000 active series. Comfortable.

After. Adding customer_id multiplies by the number of customers actually appearing in each combination. Even if only 10% of customers hit any given route, that is 200,000 × 10,000 = 2 billion series. At a few kilobytes of memory per active series, that is terabytes of RAM.

(b) What happens. Not a graceful degradation — a cascade:

  • The ingesters' in-memory index grows on every scrape as new customer IDs appear. Memory climbs over minutes.
  • They run out of memory and restart. On restart they replay the write-ahead log, re-creating the same series, and run out again — a crash loop that cannot self-recover, because the data causing the problem is durably in the WAL.
  • Ingestion stops for every team, not just the one that deployed. Dashboards go blank.
  • Alerting evaluates against missing data. Depending on rule semantics, alerts either all fire or — worse — all resolve, so the system reports that everything is fine.

The critical detail: this happens during a deploy, and the monitoring you would use to diagnose it is the thing that is broken. The defence has to be preventive.

(c) What should have prevented it:

  • A per-metric and per-tenant series limit enforced at ingestion. Cap http_requests_total at, say, 500,000 series and reject the excess with a loud alert. One team's metric degrades; the platform survives. This is the single most important control.
  • Alert on series-growth rate, not just absolute count — a metric whose cardinality doubles in ten minutes is a deploy problem, and catching it at 2× rather than 10,000× is the difference between a warning and an outage.
  • A label allow-list for high-traffic metrics, so adding a label is a reviewed change rather than a one-line commit.
  • Isolate ingestion by tenant or team (shuffle sharding) so blast radius is bounded.

How the developer should get what they wanted. They had a legitimate need: debugging one customer. Metrics are the wrong tool, because metrics are aggregates and per-entity identifiers don't belong in them. The right answers are traces with customer_id as a span attribute (per request, sampled; attributes don't create series), linked from the histogram by exemplars; logs with structured fields, queried ad hoc; or a deliberately scoped metric covering only the handful of customers under investigation, with a documented expiry.

The principle to close on: a label's cardinality must be bounded and small, and must not grow with your user base. Host, route, status and version are fine. User ID, request ID, email address, full URL with query string and error message are not — and that list belongs in a lint rule, not a wiki page.

Estimate it

Your team wants per-second resolution for 2 million series of GPU and serving metrics, kept for 30 days. Using 1.5 bytes per sample, what does that cost in storage versus 15-second resolution, and what would you propose?

Answer

Per-second: 2M samples/s × 1.5 B = 3 MB/s = 259 GB/day; × 30 = 7.8 TB per copy. At 15 s: 15 times less, about 520 GB. Memory for the in-memory head also grows: two hours at 1 s is 7,200 samples per series instead of 480. Propose per-second resolution only for the few series where sub-15-second behaviour matters (batch step time on training jobs, token throughput during load tests), kept for 2 days, then downsampled to 15 s or 1 min with min/max/sum/count so spikes survive in the max; everything else at 15 s. Check first whether the question is really about events (individual slow requests), which belong in traces.
