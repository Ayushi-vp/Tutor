Every product eventually accumulates bytes that aren't rows. A photo app stores billions of images in several sizes. A video service stores every upload and every transcoded rendition. A machine-learning team stores training datasets measured in petabytes and model checkpoints of hundreds of gigabytes each, written every hour. Logs, backups and database snapshots pile up behind every service. None of this fits in a database — it is too big, too binary and never queried by its contents — and a single file server runs out of disks, bandwidth and luck quickly.

The first instinct is a big network file system. It fails at scale for reasons that are worth understanding. Directory trees with millions of entries become bottlenecks; in-place updates and appends require locking and consistency machinery across machines; and a file system wants to be one coherent namespace, which is hard to spread over thousands of servers. The second instinct, "keep three copies of everything", works but triples the hardware bill — and at hundreds of petabytes, a factor of three is the difference between one data centre and three.

Object storage — Amazon S3, Google Cloud Storage, Azure Blob Storage, and internal systems like Facebook's Haystack and f4 — takes a deliberately narrower deal: objects are written whole, never modified, addressed by a flat key, and accessed over HTTP. That narrowness lets the system spread data over enormous fleets of cheap disks, protect it with erasure codes that cost a fraction of triple replication, and still promise that a stored object survives with probabilities quoted in "nines".

This lesson designs an object store in interview order: requirements, estimates, API and data model, architecture, then deep dives into metadata, placement, replication versus erasure coding with the arithmetic done by hand, durability and repair, consistency, and tiering hot and warm data. It closes with Facebook's f4 paper, which documents exactly how much erasure coding saved on a real photo and video store, and a lab that computes the trade-offs and rebuilds a lost block from parity.

The mental model: a coat check and a warehouse

Picture a huge coat check. You hand over a bag; the attendant gives you a ticket with a number. Later you hand back the ticket and get the bag. You can't open the bag on the shelf and swap one item — to change it you take it back and check in a new one. You don't care where in the building it's stored. That is an object store: PUT a blob under a key, GET it back by key, DELETE it, and nothing else touches the inside of an object.

Behind the counter there are two very different operations. The ticket ledger records, for every ticket, what was checked, when, how big, and exactly which shelves it's on. It is small per entry but consulted for every single request, and it must never be wrong. The warehouse holds the bags themselves: enormous, cheap, rarely visited for most items. A large bag might be split across several shelves, and for safety the warehouse keeps either spare copies or something cleverer — enough "recovery information" on other shelves to reconstruct any bag even if a few shelves burn down.

That split is the core of the architecture: a metadata service (the ledger) and storage nodes (the warehouse). Where the analogy breaks: a real object store has billions of tickets and thousands of shelves spread across buildings and cities, shelves fail every day, and "recovery information" turns out to be a precise mathematical construction — erasure coding — that can protect data at a fraction of the cost of copies.

Three ways to protect bytes

Replication keeps N full copies on different machines. Three copies survive any two losses, and reading needs only one copy. It costs 3× the space. Parity stores, alongside k data blocks, one block that is the XOR of all of them; lose any one block and XOR the rest to get it back. It costs only (k + 1)/k, but tolerates one loss. Erasure coding generalises parity: Reed-Solomon RS(n, k) computes k parity fragments from n data fragments such that any n of the n + k fragments rebuild the data. RS(10,4) stores 14 fragments for every 10 of data — 1.4× overhead — and survives any 4 losses.

One more distinction before the deep dives: an object store's promise is quoted as durability — the probability a stored object survives a year — separately from availability, the probability you can read it right now. A store can be briefly unavailable (a metadata partition is failing over) without losing anything, and the design budgets for the two differently: durability is protected by redundancy and repair, availability by replicated front ends and metadata.

The whole interview turns on that figure: which data deserves which protection, and what the cheaper option costs elsewhere. A reader who stops here should know that object storage is a small immutable API over a metadata index and a huge, failure-tolerant pool of fragments.

1 · Requirements

Functional. Buckets with names, regions and policies. PUT an object (bytes plus user metadata) under a key; GET it, including byte ranges; HEAD for metadata; DELETE; LIST keys by prefix with pagination. Multipart upload for large objects. Optional versioning, lifecycle rules (move to a colder class after 30 days, delete after a year), and pre-signed URLs that grant temporary access.

Non-functional. Durability first — a stored object must essentially never be lost; S3 states a design target of 99.999999999% (eleven nines) durability over a year for its standard classes. Availability around 99.9–99.99%. Scale to hundreds of petabytes and hundreds of billions of objects. High aggregate throughput, with single-object reads limited mostly by the network. Cost per gigabyte as low as possible, because storage is a recurring bill. Strong read-after-write consistency for PUT and DELETE.

Clarifying questions. "What's the object size distribution?" (Small objects dominate counts; large ones dominate bytes — and they need different treatment.) "Read pattern by age?" (If reads fall off with age, tiering pays.) "Single region or cross-region durability?" "Is it an internal blob store or a multi-tenant public service?" (Public means per-tenant isolation, billing and abuse protection.)

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Scenario: an internal object store for an AI company: 100 PB of logical data, 100 billion objects (so an average of 1 MB, but a skewed mix: billions of small files, plus huge dataset shards and checkpoints), 100,000 GETs/s and 20,000 PUTs/s at peak.

Raw capacity. 3× replication: 300 PB raw. RS(10,4): 100 × 1.4 = 140 PB raw. Difference: 160 PB of disks. With 20 TB drives, that's 15,000 disks versus 7,000 — 8,000 drives, plus their servers, racks, power and cooling. This is why erasure coding exists.

Disk failures. 7,000 disks at a 2% annualised failure rate (AFR) = 140 failures a year, about one every 2.6 days. Failure is routine operations, not an emergency.

Repair traffic. A failed 20 TB disk under RS(10,4) holds fragments of many stripes; rebuilding each lost fragment reads 10 surviving fragments, so the rebuild reads 10 × 20 TB = 200 TB (and writes 20 TB). Spread over 140 failures a year: 140 × 200 TB ÷ 365 days ≈ 77 TB a day, about 0.9 GB/s continuously. Spread across thousands of servers that is modest, but it must be rate-limited so it doesn't starve client reads. Under replication the same rebuild reads only 20 TB.

Metadata. 100 billion objects × ~500 bytes (key averaging 100 bytes, size, checksum, version, timestamps, ACL (access-control list) reference, list of fragment locations) = 50 TB, replicated 3 times = 150 TB in a partitioned, strongly consistent store. If each metadata partition handles ~10,000 operations a second, peak load of 120,000 requests/s plus LISTs needs a few dozen partitions at minimum — in practice thousands, split by key range and load.

Bandwidth. 100,000 GETs/s × 1 MB = 100 GB/s of reads (800 Gbit/s) across the fleet; 20,000 PUTs/s × 1 MB × 1.4 = 28 GB/s of writes to disks including parity. At, say, 200 storage servers holding 35 disks each, that is 500 MB/s of reads per server — so the network, not the disks, and the distribution of hot objects decide per-server load.

POST   /{bucket}/{key}?uploadId=...                -> commit list of (part, ETag)

Multipart upload makes large objects practical. In S3, parts are 5 MiB to 5 GiB, there can be up to 10,000 of them, and the maximum object size is 48.8 TiB (exactly 10,000 × 5 GiB). Parts upload in parallel and failed parts are retried individually; the object becomes visible atomically when the commit succeeds. Ranged GETs are the read-side counterpart: a client fetches a large object as many parallel ranges.

4 · Data model

Metadata record, keyed by (bucket, key, version): size, checksum (and ETag), content type, user metadata, creation time, storage class, encryption key reference, and the object's layout — which stripe or volume IDs hold it, at which offsets. Range-partitioned by (bucket, key), so a LIST for a prefix is a range scan within one or a few partitions.

Data layout. Objects aren't stored as individual files. Large objects are cut into chunks (GFS used 64 MB; f4 uses 1 GB blocks) and each chunk is replicated or erasure-coded. Small objects are packed into large append-only volumes — Facebook's Haystack packs many photos into volumes of about 100 GB — so a 4 KB object costs one index entry and one seek, not a file and a separate stripe. Volumes are written while "unlocked", then sealed ("locked") and from then on read-only, which makes them easy to erasure-code, replicate and move.

Placement map. For each volume or stripe: the nodes and disks holding each fragment. The placement service maintains it and enforces failure-domain rules.

5 · High-level architecture

Front-end (API) servers are stateless: they authenticate, authorise, rate-limit per tenant and per prefix, and orchestrate each request. The metadata service is a partitioned, replicated key-value store with strong consistency (each partition is a small consensus group — in2's design with a leader per partition, like DynamoDB). The placement service picks storage nodes for new data. Storage nodes hold fragments on local disks, verify checksums on read and expose simple "write fragment / read fragment range" operations. Background services — repair, scrubbing, garbage collection, tiering and re-encoding — run continuously.

A PUT of a 64 MB object: the front end asks placement for a stripe of 14 nodes on 14 distinct racks; splits the object into 10 data fragments of 6.4 MB; computes 4 parity fragments; writes all 14 in parallel along with checksums; waits for enough acknowledgements to meet its durability rule (for example, all 14, or at least 12 with the rest repaired soon); then commits the metadata record — the moment the object becomes visible. If the client sent a checksum, it is verified before commit. Small objects instead append to an open, replicated volume and get an index entry.

A GET: the front end reads the metadata record, then reads only the data fragments covering the requested range — for a 1 MB range inside fragment D3, one read from one node. If that node is down, it performs a degraded read: fetch the same range from any 10 surviving fragments and decode. Data never passes through the metadata service; GFS made the same choice, with clients asking the master for chunk locations and then reading directly from chunk servers.

A worked trace with numbers. A client in the same region uploads a 64 MB object over a 10 Gbit/s path: 64 MB × 8 ÷ 10 Gbit/s ≈ 51 ms of transfer at full speed. The front end encodes 4 parity fragments (Reed-Solomon runs at several GB/s per core, so roughly 10–20 ms for 64 MB) and writes 14 fragments of 6.4 MB to 14 nodes in parallel; each node writes 6.4 MB in tens of milliseconds, and the slowest of the 14 sets the pace. The metadata commit is a consensus write of a few hundred bytes: a few milliseconds. Total: on the order of 100–200 ms, dominated by the transfer and the slowest fragment write. That is why per-object PUT latency matters less than aggregate throughput, and why clients upload big objects as many parallel parts.

6 · Deep dive: replication versus erasure coding, by hand

Take 60 PB of data and the two schemes.

RS(10,4) is better on space and on losses tolerated. What it gives up is everything that involves reconstructing: repair reads 10× the data, degraded reads touch 10 disks (so tail latency suffers during failures), and small writes are expensive. Choosing parameters is a balance. More data fragments lower overhead — RS(17,3), Backblaze's 17-data, 3-parity layout across 20 storage pods, costs only 20/17 ≈ 1.18× — but tolerate fewer losses and make each repair read 17 fragments. More parity fragments raise durability and cost space.

Local Reconstruction Codes attack the repair cost. Microsoft's Azure Storage paper describes LRC(12,2,2): 12 data fragments in two groups of 6, one local parity per group, and 2 global parities — 16 fragments, 1.33× overhead. A single lost data fragment, by far the most common failure, is rebuilt from the 5 other fragments in its group plus the local parity — 6 reads instead of 12 — "half the number required" by the equivalent Reed-Solomon code, while the global parities still protect against multiple failures.

How Reed-Solomon works, briefly. Treat each byte position across the 10 data fragments as 10 numbers. Reed-Solomon fits a polynomial through them and evaluates it at 4 more points to produce the parity bytes, using arithmetic in a finite field of 256 elements so every value stays a byte. Any 10 known points determine the polynomial, so any 10 fragments recover the rest. In practice it is implemented as a matrix multiply with a carefully chosen matrix, which modern CPUs do at gigabytes per second per core with vector instructions. You don't need to derive it in an interview; you need to say "any n of n + k" and know the costs above. The lab shows the k = 1 case — plain XOR parity — rebuilding a lost block.

7 · Deep dive: durability nines, repair and correlated failures

Durability is a race between failures and repair. Data is lost only if more fragments of the same stripe fail than the code tolerates, before repair restores them. With independent failures, the probability of losing a given stripe in one repair window is the chance that more than k of its n + k fragments fail inside that window.

Work it for 3× replication with a 2% AFR and a 6-hour repair window: the chance one disk fails in 6 hours is about 0.02 × 6 ÷ 8,766 ≈ 1.4 × 10−5; all three copies failing in the same window is (1.4 × 10−5)3 ≈ 2.6 × 10−15. For RS(10,4) with a slower 24-hour repair (rebuilds read more), p ≈ 5.5 × 10−5, and losing 5 of 14 is about C(14,5) × p5 = 2,002 × 5.2 × 10−22 ≈ 1.0 × 10−18. The lab computes these and RS(6,3) and RS(17,3) too. These numbers say two things. First, tolerating more losses beats a faster repair by orders of magnitude. Second, they are so small that independent disk failure is not what actually loses data.

What does lose data is correlation: a rack power failure takes out every fragment on that rack at once; a bad batch of drives fails together; a firmware bug corrupts writes on every disk of one model; a software bug in the repair system deletes the wrong fragments; an operator decommissions the wrong hosts. The defences are structural. Place fragments across independent failure domains — f4 cells use 14 racks of 15 hosts, so the 14 fragments of a stripe can each sit on a different rack and a rack failure costs at most one fragment per stripe. Mix hardware batches and models within stripes. Make repair fast and prioritised: stripes missing 3 fragments are repaired before stripes missing 1. Rate-limit destructive operations — deletes and decommissions — and keep a recycle bin. And scrub: read every fragment periodically and verify checksums, because a silently corrupted fragment is a loss you haven't counted yet.

8 · Deep dive: metadata, listing and consistency

GFS (2003) showed the simplest design: one master holds all metadata in memory — the namespace, file-to-chunk mapping and chunk locations — logs every mutation to an operation log replicated on other machines, and checkpoints it. Clients ask the master where a chunk is and then talk to chunk servers directly, so the master is never in the data path. One master is simple and strongly consistent, but its memory and request rate cap the number of files, which is exactly the limit later systems removed by partitioning metadata.

A modern object store partitions metadata by key range. LIST with a prefix is a range scan in one partition or a few adjacent ones; a partition that gets too big or too busy splits. This is why Amazon documents throughput "per partitioned prefix": at least 3,500 PUT/COPY/POST/DELETE and 5,500 GET/HEAD requests per second per prefix, with no limit on the number of prefixes, so a workload needing 50,000 reads/s should spread keys over at least 10 prefixes. A common anti-pattern is keys that start with a timestamp (2026-10-02T10:15:…/…), which sends every new write to the same partition; hashing or reversing part of the key spreads them.

Consistency. The clean way to get strong read-after-write consistency is to make the metadata commit the single moment an object becomes visible: write all fragments durably first, then commit the metadata record with a new version in a strongly consistent store; GETs always resolve the current version through it. Overwrites write new fragments and switch the pointer; the old fragments become garbage. Amazon S3 originally offered only eventual consistency for overwrites and listings, and since December 2020 provides strong read-after-write consistency for PUT and DELETE in all regions — which required making its metadata caches consistent, not changing how bytes are stored. What typically stays eventually consistent: cross-region replication, CDN caches in front of the store, and some secondary listings or inventories.

9 · Deep dive: hot, warm and cold tiers

Access frequency falls steeply with age. Facebook measured it across nine kinds of user-facing content: for all nine, content less than a day old received more than 100 times the request rate of one-year-old content; for eight, the rate dropped by an order of magnitude in less than a week. That makes tiering the most powerful cost lever. New, hot data goes to a tier optimised for throughput and low latency — replicated, possibly on faster disks or SSDs, with caches and CDNs in front. As data cools, a background process re-encodes it into an erasure-coded warm tier, and eventually into a cold or archival tier (dense disks or tape) with retrieval measured in minutes or hours. Lifecycle rules let users express this ("move to infrequent access after 30 days, archive after 180, delete after 3 years"), and automatic tiering moves objects by observed access.

Small objects need special handling in every tier. Erasure-coding a 4 KB object into 14 fragments of about 300 bytes would make every read a scatter of tiny I/Os. Packing small objects into large volumes and coding the volume avoids it: one read of 4 KB from one fragment in the normal case.

10 · Failure modes and trade-offs

Disk failure (every couple of days): repair reconstructs its fragments onto other disks; reads of affected stripes are degraded meanwhile. Host or rack failure: many fragments at once, but at most one per stripe if placement is right; repair prioritises the most-damaged stripes. Zone or region failure: needs data spread across zones or a cross-region layer — f4 pairs volumes in two regions and stores their XOR in a third, so any one region can be lost. Metadata partition unavailable: objects in that key range can't be read or written even though the bytes are safe — metadata availability is object availability. Hot object: one viral file at 50,000 GETs/s overloads the few nodes holding its fragments; a cache or CDN in front, or temporary extra replicas, absorbs it. Bugs: a faulty garbage collector or repair process can destroy data faster than any disk; stage rollouts, rate-limit deletions, and keep deleted data recoverable for a window.

The trade-offs to state in the close: erasure coding's space savings against repair traffic and degraded reads; immutability's simplicity against no in-place updates; strong metadata consistency against a heavier metadata tier; and tiering's savings against slower access to old data.

Encryption keys as the delete mechanism

Deleting a 4 KB photo from the middle of a sealed, erasure-coded 100 GB volume is expensive: you'd have to rewrite the volume and recompute parity. f4 avoids that entirely. Every object is encrypted with its own key, stored in a separate database; deleting the key makes the bytes unreadable — "logically deletes it" — and the space is reclaimed whenever the volume is eventually rewritten. This crypto-shredding approach also makes deletion verifiable for privacy laws: once the key is gone, every copy, replica and backup of the ciphertext is useless. The trade-off is that the key store becomes a critical, highly available dependency on every read.

Repair is a scheduling problem

At 140 disk failures a year, repair never stops. The scheduler should prioritise by risk: a stripe with three lost fragments (one more and it's at the edge) before a thousand stripes with one. It should spread reads over many source disks and writes over many destinations so no single node becomes a bottleneck, and throttle against client traffic. It must also distinguish a disk that is gone from one that is temporarily unreachable — a host rebooting for 10 minutes shouldn't trigger 20 TB of reconstruction. Systems wait a grace period before declaring fragments lost, accepting slightly higher risk during the wait in exchange for avoiding vast unnecessary repair traffic.

Write paths for erasure-coded data

Encoding needs a full stripe, so small writes are awkward. Common patterns: write new data replicated (fast, simple), then re-encode sealed volumes in the background (Haystack to f4, or "replicate then transition" storage classes); or buffer incoming objects into stripes in memory on the front end and write the stripe when full. Either way, durability of the in-flight data must match the promise made when the PUT is acknowledged — acknowledging a PUT held only in one server's memory is not acceptable.

Integrity end to end

Corruption can happen in client memory, on the network, in a server's RAM, in a disk's firmware or on the platter. Checksums must cover every hop: the client sends a checksum with the upload, the front end verifies it before committing, each fragment is stored with its own checksum, every read verifies, and scrubbing re-verifies cold data. Amazon has published work on lightweight formal methods used to validate ShardStore, the storage-node software in S3 — a sign that at this scale, testing alone isn't considered enough for the code that touches the bytes.

Choosing n and k

Three forces set the parameters. Overhead is (n + k)/n, so larger n is cheaper. Durability grows with k and falls as n + k grows (more pieces means more chances for several failures in the same stripe). Repair and degraded-read cost grows with n, because rebuilding reads n fragments. The failure-domain count caps n + k: you need at least n + k independent racks, or k must be large enough to cover fragments that share a domain. Typical production points sit between RS(6,3) at 1.5× and RS(17,3) at about 1.18×, with RS(10,4) a common middle. Larger stripes also mean a minimum object or volume size before encoding is efficient, which again pushes small objects into packed volumes.

Versioning, retention and protection from your own mistakes

Most data loss in object stores is caused not by disks but by people and programs: a script deletes the wrong prefix, a lifecycle rule expires live data, ransomware overwrites objects with encrypted junk. Versioning keeps prior versions when objects are overwritten or deleted (a delete adds a "delete marker" instead of removing data), so mistakes can be undone. Retention locks ("write once, read many") prevent deletion or overwrite until a date, even by administrators, which is how regulated data and backups are protected. Both cost storage — every version is billed — so they pair with lifecycle rules that expire old versions after a window. Mentioning these in an interview shows you know where real data loss comes from.

Serving ML workloads

Training jobs read datasets as large sequential shards at very high aggregate throughput — hundreds of GPUs each pulling hundreds of megabytes per second — and write checkpoints of hundreds of gigabytes in bursts. Object stores handle this well if access is shaped for them: large objects (hundreds of MB to a few GB per shard) rather than millions of tiny files, many parallel ranged GETs per client, keys spread across prefixes, and a local or regional cache tier between the store and the GPUs. Checkpoint writes use parallel multipart uploads; the newest checkpoints stay in a hot tier for fast restart, older ones move to cheaper storage.

The real case: f4, Facebook's warm BLOB storage (OSDI 2014)

Facebook stores photos, videos and other "BLOBs" (binary large objects) that are "created once, read many times, never modified". Its original store, Haystack, packs BLOBs into volumes of about 100 GB and keeps each volume as three physical replicas — two in a primary data centre on different racks, one in another data centre — with RAID-6 (a disk-array layout inside each host that survives two disk failures) adding a further 1.2× overhead. The paper does the multiplication: an effective replication factor of 3 × 1.2 = 3.6. That is the right design for hot content, optimised for input/output operations per second, but most content isn't hot.

The paper's measurements make the case for a warm tier: for all nine content types studied, content less than a day old received more than 100 times the request rate of one-year-old content, and an age of about a month was a safe dividing line between hot and warm for all but two types (profile photos stayed in Haystack). Warm content was large and growing — in the oldest interval studied, more than 80% of objects.

f4 stores sealed volumes in cells — at the time, 14 racks of 15 hosts with thirty 4 TB drives per host — and encodes each volume's data file with Reed-Solomon(10,4) in 1 GB blocks, laid out so the 14 blocks of a stripe sit on different racks. The cell tolerates disk, host and rack failures at 1.4× overhead. Small index files stay triple-replicated because coding them isn't worth it. Keeping a full copy of a cell in a second data centre gives 2 × 1.4 = 2.8. The final design pairs each volume with a "buddy" volume in a different region and stores their XOR in a third region: each of the two volumes costs 1.4× in its own cell, and the XOR volume, also coded at 1.4×, is shared by the pair, so the factor is (1.4 × 2 + 1.4) ÷ 2 = 2.1 — while still surviving the loss of any one region. Reads during failures go through "backoff nodes" that reconstruct missing data from companion and parity blocks; reads after a region loss fetch the XOR block and the buddy block and XOR them together.

At publication f4 stored "over 65PB of logical data" and saved "over 53PB of storage". Deletes use the encryption-key approach above. The design embodies this lesson's trade-offs: replicate hot data where request rates demand it; erasure-code warm data where bytes dominate; place fragments across racks to defeat correlated failure; and add cross-region protection with the cheapest code that meets the goal — XOR, because tolerating one region loss only requires one parity.

What goes wrong, and what to watch

Repair backlog — the number of stripes missing one, two, three fragments, and the time to drain each class; a growing tail at "three missing" is the most important durability alarm. Scrub coverage — the age of the oldest unverified fragment. Degraded-read rate and latency — rising during failures and rebuilds. Hot prefixes and hot objects — throttling (HTTP 503 Slow Down in S3's case) from per-prefix limits. Metadata partition load and splits. Garbage backlog — space held by deleted objects not yet compacted. Capacity runway per tier, because buying and racking disks takes months. And for every destructive background process, a rate and an audit trail.

Goal: compare 3× replication with Reed-Solomon schemes on storage overhead, data read to rebuild, and a rough probability of losing a stripe in one repair window; then rebuild a lost block from XOR parity. Python 3.12, standard library only, instant.

"""Replication vs erasure coding: storage overhead, repair traffic and a
rough durability estimate, plus a working XOR parity rebuild.
Python 3.12 stdlib only."""
import math, os

def p_loss(n, tolerate, afr, repair_hours):
    """P(more than `tolerate` of the n pieces fail within one repair window),
    assuming independent failures; ignores correlated failures (racks, bad batches)."""
    p = 1 - (1 - afr) ** (repair_hours / 8766)          # one piece fails in the window
    return sum(math.comb(n, i) * p**i * (1 - p)**(n - i) for i in range(tolerate + 1, n + 1))

AFR = 0.02                                               # 2% annual failure rate per disk
schemes = [  # name, pieces, failures tolerated, overhead, hours to repair one piece
    ("3x replication",  3, 2, 3.0,  6),
    ("RS(6,3)",         9, 3, 1.5, 12),
    ("RS(10,4)",       14, 4, 1.4, 24),
    ("RS(17,3)",       20, 3, 20/17, 24),
]
print(f"{'scheme':15} {'overhead':>8} {'read to rebuild 1 GB':>21} {'P(loss) per window':>19}")
for name, n, tol, ovh, hours in schemes:
    data_pieces = n - tol if name != "3x replication" else 1
    print(f"{name:15} {ovh:7.2f}x {data_pieces:18d} GB {p_loss(n, tol, AFR, hours):19.2e}")

# Storing 10 PB of logical data:
for name, n, tol, ovh, _ in schemes:
    print(f"10 PB logical with {name:14} -> {10 * ovh:5.1f} PB raw")

# A working (k=1) parity code: XOR of the data blocks rebuilds any ONE lost block.
blocks = [os.urandom(1024) for _ in range(4)]            # 4 data blocks of 1 KB
parity = bytes(a ^ b ^ c ^ d for a, b, c, d in zip(*blocks))
lost = 2
survivors = [b for i, b in enumerate(blocks) if i != lost] + [parity]
rebuilt = bytes(x ^ y ^ z ^ w for x, y, z, w in zip(*survivors))
print("\nXOR parity rebuilt lost block correctly:", rebuilt == blocks[lost],
      "| had to read", len(survivors), "blocks to rebuild 1")

What to look for.

  1. Overhead: 3.0× for replication, 1.5× for RS(6,3), 1.4× for RS(10,4), 1.18× for RS(17,3). For 10 PB of data that is 30, 15, 14 and 11.8 PB raw.
  2. Rebuild reads: 1 GB for replication versus 6, 10 and 17 GB for the codes — the hidden cost of erasure coding.
  3. Loss probability per repair window (independent failures, 2% AFR): about 2.6 × 10−15 for replication with a 6-hour repair, 1.0 × 10−18 for RS(10,4) even with a 24-hour repair, and 4.5 × 10−14 for RS(17,3), which tolerates only 3 losses across 20 pieces. Tolerating more losses matters more than repairing faster.
  4. The XOR parity rebuilds the lost block exactly — after reading all 4 surviving blocks. That's k = 1 erasure coding, and the same reason f4 can use XOR across regions.

(a) Raw capacity and disk count for each? (b) Failure rate and repair traffic for each? (c) Which do you choose, and for what?

Answer

(a) Capacity. 3× replication: 60 PB × 3 = 180 PB raw = 9,000 disks. Erasure coding 6+3: overhead is (6+3)/6 = 1.5×, so 60 PB × 1.5 = 90 PB raw = 4,500 disks. Erasure coding saves 4,500 disks — half the fleet, and with it half the racks, power and cooling. At any realistic cost this is the dominant line item.

(b) Failures and repair traffic.

Replication: 9,000 disks × 2% = 180 failures/year ≈ one every 2 days. Repairing a 20 TB disk means copying 20 TB from surviving replicas — read 20 TB, write 20 TB. Spread over many source servers, this is modest.

Erasure coding: 4,500 disks × 2% = 90 failures/year ≈ one every 4 days — half as many, because there are half as many disks. But each repair is far more expensive: reconstructing each lost fragment reads 6 fragments for every 1 rebuilt, so recovering a 20 TB disk reads 120 TB across the network and decodes all of it.

Per event that is 6× the traffic at half the frequency, so average repair bandwidth is about 3× higher. The bigger problem is the peak: 120 TB at 10 Gbps per server, even pulling from 20 sources in parallel, is 120 TB ÷ (20 × 1.25 GB/s) = 4,800 s ≈ 80 minutes of sustained full-rate network on 20 servers. During that window those servers are degraded for normal traffic, and the durability maths assumed a repair window — if repair is slow, the probability of further failures during it rises, and the nines you quoted are wrong.

(c) The choice — and it is not one or the other.

  • Erasure-code the cold, large majority. Most bytes in any object store are old, rarely read and large. They suit erasure coding: the storage saving is enormous, the read amplification rarely materialises because nobody reads them, and repair can be throttled because latency doesn't matter.
  • Replicate the hot and the small. A frequently read object should be readable from one disk even during failures, not reconstructed from six — and small objects fragment badly: a 4 KB object split nine ways is nine ~450-byte I/Os. Pack small objects into volumes first.
  • Transition by age or access. Write new objects replicated and re-encode them once they've been cold for, say, 30 days. That's what storage classes are, and it's what Facebook did moving content from Haystack to f4.

The number that decides it: if 90% of bytes are cold, erasure-coding just those saves 0.9 × 60 PB × (3 − 1.5) = 81 PB of raw capacity — almost the whole benefit — while leaving the 10% hot working set on cheap-to-read replication. The right answer to a storage trade-off is almost always a tiering policy rather than a single scheme, and saying which fraction of the benefit each tier delivers is what makes it a design rather than a preference.

Estimate it

A training cluster of 512 GPUs reads a 400 TB dataset once per epoch, and an epoch takes 10 hours. Each GPU host (8 GPUs) has a 100 Gbit/s network card. Data is stored as 1 GB shards. What aggregate read throughput does the store need, how many GETs per second, and is anything likely to throttle?

Answer

400 TB ÷ 36,000 s ≈ 11.1 GB/s aggregate. 512 GPUs ÷ 8 = 64 hosts, so about 174 MB/s per host — under 2% of a 100 Gbit/s (12.5 GB/s) card, so the network is fine. 400 TB ÷ 1 GB = 400,000 shards per epoch; ÷ 36,000 s ≈ 11 shard reads per second if each is one GET — trivial. Even fetching each shard as 64 ranged GETs of 16 MB for parallelism, that's ~710 GETs/s, well under one prefix's 5,500/s. Nothing throttles at the store; the likely bottleneck is per-host decode and preprocessing. The design would change if the dataset were 400 million 1 MB files: 11,100 GETs/s, which must be spread over several prefixes — another argument for packing small samples into large shards.
