Imagine you run three copies of a payments reconciliation service, and exactly one of them must be the "primary" that writes the daily ledger; the others wait as hot standbys. How do they decide which one is primary? The obvious answer — "the one with the lowest IP address" — breaks the first time the network splits: each side sees only itself and its neighbours, and two primaries write two ledgers. "Whoever grabs a row lock in the database" makes the database a single point of failure and doesn't help when a primary's process freezes while holding the lock. The same question appears everywhere in a distributed system: which replica of a database is the leader, which scheduler owns a partition, what the current feature-flag configuration is, which servers are alive and where they live.

Every one of those answers needs agreement: all participants must see the same answer, even when machines crash, messages are delayed and networks split. Getting agreement right is one of the hardest problems in computing — it took decades of research to produce algorithms (Paxos, ZAB, Raft) that are provably correct and still practical, and implementations are notoriously easy to get subtly wrong. So instead of every team building agreement into every service, large systems build it once, as a small, extremely reliable service, and everyone else asks it.

That is a coordination service. Google built Chubby for this; Yahoo built ZooKeeper; CoreOS built etcd, which now stores the entire state of every Kubernetes cluster. Its author at Google noted that its most popular use turned out to be as a name service, and that a single instance could see 90,000 clients talking directly to its master.

This lesson designs a coordination service in interview order: requirements, estimates, API, data model and architecture, then deep dives into the consensus log (Raft's elections, terms, commit rule and safety), reads and their consistency, sessions and leases, watches, recipes for locks and leader election, and fencing. It ends with Chubby's production lessons and a lab that simulates Raft elections and shows why the timeouts are randomized.

The mental model: a committee that keeps minutes

Picture a committee of five people responsible for a register of important facts: who is the current manager of each team, which meeting rooms are booked, what the office opening hours are. The rules are strict. One member is the chair. Anyone who wants to change the register asks the chair; the chair writes the proposed change as the next numbered line in the minutes and reads it to the others; once at least three of the five (a majority) have written it into their own copy, the change is official and the chair announces it. If the chair stops showing up, after a while some member calls for a new chair; whoever gets three votes first takes over, and a new "term" of the committee begins.

Why three of five? Because any two groups of three from five people must share at least one person. So whatever the previous chair got approved, at least one member of any future majority knows about it — and the rules for electing a new chair make sure that member's knowledge isn't lost. That overlap is the whole trick of majority quorums, and it's why the committee survives two absent members but not three.

The register itself is small and the changes are rare compared with ordinary business; what matters is that everyone agrees exactly. Members also keep a "who's here" list: anyone who wants to be on it must check in every few seconds, and if they stop checking in, their name is struck off automatically — which other people can watch for. Where the analogy breaks: real machines don't just "stop showing up"; they pause, come back and believe they are still chair. The design spends much of its care on making a returning ex-chair harmless.

What clients actually do with it

Four uses cover almost everything. Leader election: many replicas of a service, one must be primary; they race to create a key, and the winner is the leader until its session dies. Locks: mutual exclusion around a shared resource, with a queue of waiters. Configuration: a small set of values (feature flags, shard maps, which database is primary) that many processes read and watch for changes. Membership and discovery: each live server registers an ephemeral key with its address; clients list the keys to find servers and watch them to notice arrivals and departures. All four need the same two properties — every client sees the same answer, and the answer updates promptly when a participant dies — which is exactly what a consensus log plus sessions provides.

The pieces

A replicated log: every change is an entry in an ordered log, copied to all servers. A consensus protocol decides the order and when an entry is safe (committed): Raft in etcd, ZAB (ZooKeeper Atomic Broadcast) in ZooKeeper, Paxos in Chubby. A state machine on each server applies committed entries, in order, to an in-memory data store: a tree of small nodes (ZooKeeper, Chubby) or a flat key-value map (etcd). Sessions or leases track live clients. Watches notify clients of changes. On top of these, clients build recipes: locks, leader election, membership, configuration.

1 · Requirements

Functional. A hierarchical namespace (or flat key space) of small data items with create, read, update with compare-and-swap (only if the version is still X), delete, and list children. Ephemeral items tied to client sessions. Sequential items whose names get an increasing counter. Watches that notify clients of changes. Transactions over a few keys ("if A's version is 5 and B doesn't exist, write both"). Building blocks for locks, leader election, membership and configuration.

Non-functional. Correctness above all: writes are linearizable — once a write succeeds, every later read that asks for the latest state sees it — and no committed write is ever lost. High availability: survive the loss of any minority of servers, and fail over in seconds. Low write latency within a region (milliseconds). Support tens of thousands of clients, most of them mostly idle (holding a session, watching a few keys). Small data: kilobytes per item, megabytes to a few gigabytes in total.

Out of scope, said aloud: being a general database. High write throughput, large values and analytical queries are explicitly not goals — and saying so is part of the answer.

Clarifying questions. "Within one region or global?" (Consensus across regions costs a cross-region round trip per write.) "How many clients, and how many watches per key?" "Do readers need the latest value, or is a slightly stale view fine?"

2 · Back-of-envelope estimates

Scenario: the coordination service for a company's production region: 50,000 client processes, 200,000 keys averaging 1 KB, a 5-node cluster across three availability zones.

Data size. 200,000 × 1 KB = 200 MB — fits in memory on every replica with room to spare, which is the design point: every server holds a full copy, in RAM, so reads are cheap. (Chubby's paper notes that its data "fits in RAM, so most operations are cheap".)

Session keep-alive load. 50,000 clients each renewing a session every 10 s = 5,000 keep-alives/s. Chubby found exactly this: "KeepAlives are by far the dominant type of request", and failing to process them in time "is the typical failure mode of an overloaded server". Spread across 5 servers if sessions can attach to any server (ZooKeeper's model), that's 1,000/s each; if all go to one master (Chubby's model), 5,000/s on one machine, which is why Chubby could stretch lease times from 12 s to about 60 s under heavy load.

Writes. Locks taken and released, leader elections, configuration changes, membership changes as pods start and stop: suppose 500 writes/s. Each write is one round trip from the leader to the two fastest followers needed for a majority of 5, plus a synchronous disk write (fsync) on each. Within one region across zones: round trip ~1 ms, fsync on SSD ~0.5–2 ms, so write latency is a few milliseconds and 500/s is far within capacity. If a replica sat in another region 70 ms away, it wouldn't be in the fastest majority, but losing a local replica would make every write wait 70 ms.

Watch fan-out. A global configuration key watched by all 50,000 clients: one change produces 50,000 notifications and up to 50,000 follow-up reads. At even 20,000 notifications/s per server spread over 5 servers, that's half a second of saturated work — plan for it with caching proxies and jittered re-reads.

Failover. Election timeout 1 s (a common etcd setting, against heartbeats every 100 ms): leader crash → followers notice after ~1 s → election in one or two round trips → new leader within ~1–2 s. Client sessions must survive that pause, so session timeouts are set longer than election time (e.g. 10 s).

Everything is versioned: each node or key carries a version (or etcd's global revision, a counter that increases with every change in the cluster). That makes compare-and-swap possible — write only if nobody changed it since I read it — which is how recipes avoid races without locks inside the service.

4 · Data model

ZooKeeper and Chubby expose a tree, like a file system: /services/payments/primary, /locks/ledger/req-0000000042. Each node holds a small blob of data plus metadata: data version, children version, creation and modification transaction IDs, and, for ephemeral nodes, the owning session. etcd uses a flat key space ordered by key (so a prefix like /services/payments/ acts like a directory) stored in a multi-version store: every revision of every key is kept until compaction, so a watcher can resume from revision r and miss nothing. Underneath, every server has the same three things: the replicated log on disk, periodic snapshots of the in-memory state (so the log can be truncated), and the in-memory state itself.

5 · High-level architecture

Five server processes, one per machine, spread over three zones (2-2-1) so a zone loss leaves 3 of 5. One is the leader; the rest are followers. Clients connect to any server. Writes are forwarded to the leader, which appends them to its log, replicates to followers, and once a majority have them durably, commits, applies them to its state and replies; followers apply committed entries in the same order. Reads are served from in-memory state — by the leader with a freshness check for linearizable reads, or by the local server for cheaper, possibly stale reads. Sessions are tracked through keep-alives; when one expires, the leader proposes deleting its ephemeral nodes, through the log like any other write. Watches are registered on the server the client is connected to and fire when that server applies a matching change. For read scaling, extra non-voting replicas (ZooKeeper observers, etcd learners) receive the log and serve reads without slowing writes.

6 · Deep dive: Raft leader election

Raft divides time into terms, numbered 1, 2, 3, …; each term begins with an election and has at most one leader. Terms act as a logical clock: every message carries the sender's term; a server that sees a higher term updates its own and, if it was leader or candidate, steps down; a message with a lower term is rejected. This is what makes a returning ex-leader harmless inside the cluster — its term is stale.

Servers start as followers. The leader sends periodic heartbeats (empty AppendEntries messages). A follower that hears nothing for its election timeout assumes the leader is dead, becomes a candidate, increments the term, votes for itself, and sends RequestVote to everyone. Each server grants at most one vote per term, first come first served — with one restriction: it refuses candidates whose log is less up to date than its own (compared by the term of the last entry, then by length). A candidate that collects votes from a majority of the full cluster becomes leader and immediately sends heartbeats to stop other elections. If it hears from a leader with a term at least as high, it steps down.

Why randomize the timeout? If every follower used exactly 150 ms, they would all become candidates at nearly the same moment, each voting for itself, and no one would get a majority — a split vote — then all time out again together. The Raft paper measured this: with no randomness, elections "consistently took longer than 10 seconds" because of repeated split votes; adding just 5 ms of randomness gave a median downtime of 287 ms; with a 150–300 ms range they recommend, elections are rarely split. The lab reproduces the same shape: fixed timeouts gave a median of about 12.8 seconds, 150–155 ms gave about 313 ms, and 150–300 ms gave a median of 182 ms with a worst case of 529 ms over 1,000 trials.

Choosing the timeout. The paper states the timing requirement as broadcastTime ≪ electionTimeout ≪ MTBF: the time to send a message to every server and get replies (0.5–20 ms, depending on storage, since replies require an fsync) must be much smaller than the election timeout, which must be much smaller than the MTBF (mean time between failures) of a server — months. Too short and normal hiccups trigger needless elections; too long and failover is slow. The paper's suggestion of 10–500 ms is for a LAN; production etcd clusters typically use a 1-second election timeout with 100 ms heartbeats to tolerate network and disk jitter.

7 · Deep dive: log replication, commit and safety

The leader appends each client command to its log as an entry tagged with the current term and an index. It sends AppendEntries to each follower containing new entries plus the index and term of the entry just before them. A follower accepts only if it has a matching entry at that previous position — the log matching check. If not, it rejects, and the leader steps back one entry at a time (or faster, with hints) until they find the point where their logs agree; the follower then deletes anything after that point and takes the leader's entries. This is how a follower that missed entries or holds entries from a deposed leader is brought into exact agreement.

An entry is committed once the leader knows it is stored on a majority. The leader then applies it and replies to the client; followers learn the new commit index in the next AppendEntries and apply it too. In the architecture figure, entry 42 is on the leader, F1 and F2 — 3 of 5 — so it's committed; entry 43 is only on the leader and F1, so it isn't, and if the leader crashed now, 43 might be lost, which is fine because no client was told it succeeded.

Why committed entries survive leader changes. A committed entry is on a majority. Any new leader needs votes from a majority. The two majorities share at least one server, and that server refuses to vote for a candidate whose log is less up to date than its own — so the winner must already hold the committed entry. This voting restriction plus majority overlap gives Raft's leader completeness property: every leader holds every entry committed in earlier terms.

One subtle rule completes it: a leader only counts replicas toward committing entries from its own current term. The Raft paper's Figure 8 shows why: an entry from an older term can be on a majority and still be overwritten if a server that lacked it wins the next election (possible because that server has a newer last term). Once the leader commits an entry from its own term, every earlier entry before it is committed too, by the log matching property. In practice, a new leader immediately appends a no-op entry in its term to commit everything before it.

Snapshots and membership changes. The log can't grow forever: each server periodically snapshots its state and discards the log up to that point; a follower too far behind receives the snapshot instead of old entries. Adding or removing servers must avoid a moment where two different majorities could exist (old configuration and new); Raft does this with joint consensus (decisions need majorities of both configurations during the change) or by changing one server at a time, which can never create two disjoint majorities.

8 · Deep dive: reads and their consistency

Writes go through the log and are linearizable by construction. Reads are where systems differ. A leader can't simply answer from memory: it might have been deposed by a partition it doesn't know about, while a new leader on the majority side has committed newer writes. Three options:

  • Read through the log: append the read as a log entry and answer when it commits. Correct, but as expensive as a write.
  • ReadIndex: the leader notes its current commit index, sends a heartbeat round and waits for a majority to acknowledge (proving it's still leader), waits until its state machine has applied up to that index, then answers from memory. One round trip, no disk write. Followers can also serve linearizable reads by asking the leader for the read index.
  • Lease reads: the leader assumes that after a successful heartbeat round, no other leader can be elected for slightly less than an election timeout, and answers from memory within that window. No extra round trip, but correctness now depends on bounded clock drift — a time assumption Raft otherwise avoids. Chubby's master lease is this idea.

Cheaper still are stale reads from whichever server the client is connected to. ZooKeeper does exactly this by default: reads are served locally and may lag, but each client sees its own writes in order and never goes backwards in time (if it reconnects to another server, that server must be at least as up to date as what the client has seen). A client that needs the latest value calls sync first, which makes its server catch up with the leader. etcd defaults to linearizable reads and offers serializable reads that any member answers from local state. The design answer: make the default safe, and let callers who can tolerate staleness opt in for speed and scale.

9 · Deep dive: sessions, leases and watches

A client opens a session (ZooKeeper, Chubby) or creates a lease (etcd) with a timeout, and sends keep-alives. As long as they arrive, the session lives; if they stop for longer than the timeout, the service expires it and deletes everything attached to it — ephemeral nodes, held locks. This turns "is that process alive?" into data in the store, which other clients can read and watch. The service, not each client, decides on expiry, so all observers agree on when a client is considered gone.

Chubby's protocol shows the details that matter. A KeepAlive is held at the master until the client's lease is close to expiring, then answered with a new lease timeout (12 seconds by default), and the reply also carries events and cache invalidations, so one long-lived call does all the work. The client keeps a slightly conservative local copy of the lease. If it expires locally (perhaps the master is failing over), the client enters "jeopardy": it disables its cache and waits a grace period (45 seconds by default) for a new master before declaring the session lost — so a quick failover is invisible to applications.

Watches let clients react without polling. ZooKeeper's classic watches are one-time triggers: you set a watch with a read, you get one notification when the data changes, and you must read again (setting a new watch) to see the new value — so changes between the notification and the re-read aren't individually delivered, only the latest state. Persistent and recursive watches were added later. etcd watches are streams from a revision: "send me every change to keys under /config/ since revision 9,312", which can't miss intermediate events while the history is retained. Both have the same scaling problem — fan-out to many watchers — handled by spreading clients across servers, proxies that watch once on behalf of many clients, and jitter on re-reads.

10 · Deep dive: recipes — locks, leader election, fencing

The naive lock: "create /lock as ephemeral; if it exists, watch it and retry when it's deleted". When the holder releases, every waiter's watch fires and every waiter rushes to create the node — a thundering herd for one winner. The ZooKeeper recipe uses ephemeral sequential nodes instead: each client creates /lock/req- and gets a unique increasing suffix (req-0000000041, …42, …43). The lowest number holds the lock. Every other client reads the children without a watch and sets a watch only on the node immediately below its own. When 41 is deleted (released, or its session expired), only 42 is notified; it checks that it is now lowest and takes the lock. Waiters form a queue, each watching one predecessor: fair, and one notification per handover. Leader election is the same recipe: the lowest node is the leader; the rest are standbys watching their predecessor.

Fencing. The service can only guarantee that one session holds the lock in its own state. The holder can be paused (garbage collection, a stalled VM) past its session timeout, lose the lock without knowing, and keep acting while the next holder also acts. Chubby addressed this two ways. A sequencer — an opaque string naming the lock, its mode and its generation number — is passed by the holder with each request to the resource, which checks it against Chubby or against the most recent sequencer it has seen and rejects stale ones. For resources that can't be changed to check sequencers, a lock-delay: if a holder fails or loses its session without releasing, the lock can't be re-acquired for a period (up to one minute) so the old holder's in-flight requests drain first. In etcd the natural fencing token is the lock key's creation revision, which increases with every acquisition. The drill traces exactly this failure with a 14-second pause.

11 · Failure modes and trade-offs

Leader crash: writes pause for detection plus election (often 1–2 s in production); reads served locally may continue. Minority partition: the old leader, cut off with a minority, can't commit anything and should step down once it fails to hear from a majority; clients connected to the minority get errors or stale reads. Loss of quorum (3 of 5 down): no writes at all; recovery may require manually forcing a new cluster from a surviving replica and can lose committed writes — the worst operation you'll perform on this system. Slow disk on the leader: every write waits for the leader's fsync, so a slow disk slows the whole cluster; etcd's documentation stresses how sensitive it is to disk write latency. Overload from clients: keep-alive storms after a failover (every client reconnects at once), watch fan-out, or a buggy client issuing a flood of reads; Chubby's outages included overload, and its answers were caching, proxies and longer leases. Large values or too many keys: snapshots and replication slow down; enforce request and storage limits.

The trade-offs to state in the close: strong consistency and simplicity of reasoning in exchange for write throughput and cross-region latency; small clusters for fast writes in exchange for limited fault tolerance; short timeouts for fast failover in exchange for false elections; and the fact that a lock service can only make resources safe if they participate (fencing).

ZAB versus Raft

ZooKeeper's protocol predates Raft and differs in emphasis. ZAB is an atomic broadcast: the leader assigns each state change a transaction ID (zxid) made of an epoch (like a term) and a counter, and followers deliver changes in zxid order. On leader election, ZAB has an explicit synchronization phase in which the new leader makes sure a quorum of followers have exactly its history — including any proposals from the previous epoch that it must commit — before accepting new writes. Raft achieves the same outcome implicitly through the vote restriction and log matching. Both use a single leader, majority quorums and epochs/terms to reject stale leaders; both give linearizable writes; both rely on followers being brought into exact agreement with the leader's log. In an interview, it's enough to say they are the same family and why: leader-based, majority-committed, epoch-fenced logs.

Why the service is small on purpose

Every byte written goes through consensus — a majority round trip and an fsync on each replica — and every byte stored lives in RAM on every replica and in every snapshot. So the system's throughput is roughly that of one machine's disk, not the cluster's, and adding servers doesn't increase write capacity (it decreases it). That is why etcd caps requests at 1.5 MiB and storage at 2 GiB by default with 8 GiB as a suggested maximum, and ZooKeeper nodes default to just under 1 MB. The right pattern is to store pointers and small control state in the coordination service — "the primary for shard 17 is at 10.0.3.4, generation 42" — and keep bulk data in purpose-built stores. Chubby's authors list abuses they had to fight: clients using it as a high-volume storage system or a publish/subscribe bus.

Multi-region coordination

A five-node cluster spread across three regions (2-2-1) survives a region outage, but every write must reach a majority, which always includes at least one remote region: tens to over a hundred milliseconds per write. Elections are slower too. Most designs avoid this: a cluster per region for local coordination (leader election for regional services, local configuration), and only rare, global decisions — which region is primary for a tenant — go through a cross-region cluster or a manual process. Reads in remote regions can be served by learners/observers with bounded staleness.

Testing and the gap between paper and implementation

Consensus algorithms come with proofs; implementations come with bugs. Chubby's paper is candid that in a few dozen cell-years it lost data six times, due to database software errors (four) and operator error (two), with the operator errors ironically happening during upgrades meant to avoid the software errors; none involved hardware failure. Production consensus systems are tested with deterministic simulation, fault injection that partitions networks and pauses processes, and Jepsen-style histories checked against linearizability. If an interviewer asks how you'd gain confidence in your design, "a formal model of the protocol and fault-injection tests of the implementation" is the expected answer.

Read scaling with learners and observers

Because voters determine write latency and fault tolerance, you don't add voters to serve more reads. Instead, add non-voting replicas — ZooKeeper calls them observers, etcd calls them learners — that receive the committed log and apply it, but never vote and never count toward a write's majority. They can serve stale-tolerant reads and hold client sessions and watches close to the clients (for example, one in each zone or a remote region), absorbing the keep-alive and watch fan-out load that Chubby handled with proxies. A learner is also how a new member joins safely: it first catches up as a learner and is promoted to voter only once its log is current, so adding it never makes the cluster wait on a replica that is hours behind.

Coordination for AI infrastructure

Training and serving fleets lean on the same primitives. Distributed training jobs need a rendezvous — every worker must agree on the membership and ranks of the job before starting, and must agree again after a worker fails (elastic training frameworks use an etcd-backed or similar rendezvous for this). Model-serving routers elect a leader per model to plan placement and rollouts; feature-flag and prompt-version configuration is pushed through watches. The usual mistakes apply: putting large artifacts (weights, prompts with embedded data) into the store instead of pointers, and letting thousands of GPU workers each hold watches on hot keys without proxies.

The real case: Google's Chubby (OSDI 2006)

Mike Burrows' paper describes the lock service Google built for "loosely-coupled distributed systems". A Chubby cell is typically five replicas, placed to reduce correlated failure (for example in different racks), running a Paxos-based consensus protocol to elect a master. The master holds a master lease — "a few seconds" during which the other replicas promise not to elect a different master — and serves all reads and writes; writes are acknowledged once they reach a majority. A failed master is replaced when its lease expires; "a new master will typically be elected in a few seconds. For example, two recent elections took 6s and 4s, but we see values as high as 30s." Clients find the master through DNS. A typical deployment used one cell per data centre of several thousand machines.

The design deliberately exposes coarse-grained locks — held for hours or days, such as electing the master of GFS or Bigtable — rather than fine-grained ones held for milliseconds, because coarse locks put little load on the service and tolerate brief unavailability. Locks are advisory. Sequencers and lock-delay (up to one minute) handle the stale-holder problem described above.

The production numbers are the paper's most quoted section. "We have seen 90,000 clients communicating directly with a Chubby master." A snapshot of one cell showed 22,000 direct clients plus 32,000 through proxies, 18 days since the last failover, a 14-second failover duration, and an RPC mix dominated by KeepAlives (93%). Most stored files were small: 90% under 1 KB. Usage surprised the designers: "we found that its most popular use was as a name server", because Chubby's explicit cache invalidation gave consistent naming where DNS time-to-live caching produced either stale answers or heavy load.

Availability: on a sample of cells covering about 700 cell-days, the authors "recorded 61 outages over a period of a few weeks". Most were 15 s or less and 52 were under 30 s, short enough that most applications weren't significantly affected; the remaining nine were caused by network maintenance (4), suspected network connectivity problems (2), software errors (2) and overload (1). Data loss: "In a few dozen cell-years of operation, we have lost data on six occasions, due to database software errors (4) and operator error (2); none involved hardware error."

The scaling techniques map directly onto the estimates in this lesson: more cells so clients use a nearby one; raising the lease time from 12 s up to about 60 s under heavy load to cut KeepAlive traffic; aggressive client-side caching with invalidation; and proxies that handle KeepAlives and reads for many clients. The meta-lesson the paper states is that the main challenge wasn't the consensus algorithm but managing how thousands of clients — and their developers' assumptions — used the service.

What goes wrong, and what to watch

Leader changes — frequent elections mean timeouts are too tight for the environment (slow disks, network jitter, CPU starvation). Disk fsync latency on every member — the leading indicator for etcd instability. Proposal commit and apply lag — followers falling behind. Database size against quota — etcd stops accepting writes when the quota is hit. Number of watchers and keep-alive rate — reconnect storms after failovers. Snapshot duration — long snapshots stall the leader. And on the client side: session expirations and lock-loss events, each of which should be rare and investigated.

Goal: simulate Raft leader election after a leader crash in a 5-node cluster, with message delays of 1–5 ms, and compare fixed and randomized election timeouts over 1,000 trials each; then print the quorum table. Python 3.12, standard library only, about 3 seconds.

"""Raft leader election after a leader crash: fixed vs randomized election
timeouts, measured over many trials; plus quorum arithmetic.
Python 3.12 stdlib only (a small discrete-event simulation)."""
import heapq, random, statistics

def elect(n, timeout_lo, timeout_hi, rng, delay=(1.0, 5.0)):
    """Leader just crashed at t=0. Return (ms until a new leader, elections started)."""
    term = [1] * n; voted = [None] * n; votes = [0] * n; role = ["follower"] * n
    events, seq = [], 0                       # (time, seq, kind, node, data)
    def push(t, kind, node, data=None):
        nonlocal seq; seq += 1; heapq.heappush(events, (t, seq, kind, node, data))
    deadline = [0.0] * n
    def reset_timer(i, now):
        deadline[i] = now + rng.uniform(timeout_lo, timeout_hi)
        push(deadline[i], "timeout", i)
    for i in range(1, n):                     # node 0 was the leader and is dead
        # followers last heard a heartbeat at slightly different times
        deadline[i] = rng.uniform(0, 2) + rng.uniform(timeout_lo, timeout_hi)
        push(deadline[i], "timeout", i)
    elections = 0
    while events:
        now, _, kind, i, data = heapq.heappop(events)
        if kind == "timeout" and now == deadline[i] and role[i] != "leader":
            term[i] += 1; role[i] = "candidate"; voted[i] = i; votes[i] = 1; elections += 1
            for j in range(1, n):
                if j != i: push(now + rng.uniform(*delay), "request_vote", j, (i, term[i]))
            reset_timer(i, now)
        elif kind == "request_vote":
            cand, t = data
            if t > term[i]: term[i], voted[i], role[i] = t, None, "follower"
            grant = t == term[i] and voted[i] in (None, cand)
            if grant: voted[i] = cand; reset_timer(i, now)
            push(now + rng.uniform(*delay), "vote", cand, (t, grant))
        elif kind == "vote":
            t, grant = data
            if role[i] == "candidate" and t == term[i] and grant:
                votes[i] += 1
                if votes[i] > n // 2: return now, elections   # majority of ALL n
    return float("inf"), elections

What to look for.

  1. Fixed 150 ms timeouts: a median of about 12.8 seconds and every trial needing more than one election. Followers time out together, vote for themselves and split the vote, round after round — the paper's "longer than 10 seconds" result.
  2. 5 ms of randomness (150–155 ms): the median drops to about 313 ms, close to the paper's 287 ms, though most trials still needed a second election.
  3. 150–300 ms (the paper's recommendation): a median of about 182 ms, worst case about 529 ms, and only around 6% of trials needing more than one election. One follower usually wins before the others wake.
  4. 12–24 ms: a median of about 23 ms — fast, but the window is now comparable to message delays, so many trials split once. The paper warns that going this low breaks the timing requirement in real systems with heartbeats and disk writes.
  5. The quorum table: 4 nodes tolerate only 1 failure, the same as 3; 5 tolerate 2; 7 tolerate 3.

Try next: make one node's messages 50 ms slower (a remote replica) and see how often it wins; then implement the vote restriction (refuse candidates with shorter logs) and give one follower a stale log.

Work it out

A 5-node etcd cluster runs with a 1-second election timeout. A service uses it for leader election with a 10-second lease, renewed every 3 seconds. The elected leader writes to a shared database.

(a) How many node failures can the cluster survive, and what is the impact of each? (b) The leader's process pauses for 14 seconds in garbage collection. Walk through what happens. (c) How do you make this safe?

Answer

(a) Fault tolerance. A majority of 5 is 3, so the cluster survives 2 failures.

  • One follower lost: no impact. Writes still reach a majority of the remaining 4.
  • Two followers lost: still working, but at the edge — 3 nodes remain and all 3 must acknowledge every write. Any further failure, including a routine restart, loses quorum. Latency also rises, because every write now waits for the slowest of three rather than the second-fastest follower of four.
  • The etcd leader lost: no writes for roughly one election timeout plus the election — 1–2 seconds. Clients see write errors and must retry.
  • Three lost: quorum is gone. Writes fail, and recovery requires manual intervention that may lose committed data. Operational implication: a rolling restart across 5 nodes must wait for each node to rejoin and catch up before touching the next, or you can create this state during routine maintenance.

(b) The 14-second GC pause. Trace it precisely:

  1. t=0 — the service leader pauses. It last renewed within the previous 3 seconds, so its lease has between 7 and 10 seconds left.
  2. t≈7–10 — no keep-alive arrives. The lease expires and etcd deletes the leader-election key.
  3. t≈10 — watchers are notified, a second instance acquires the key and believes it is the leader. It begins writing to the shared database. Correct behaviour — from etcd's view, the old leader is gone.
  4. t=14 — the original process resumes. From its perspective no time has passed. It doesn't know it was paused, still thinks its lease is valid, and continues writing.
  5. t=14 onwards — two leaders are writing concurrently. Both behave correctly according to their own state. etcd hasn't malfunctioned. No error is logged anywhere.

The essential insight: a lease proves a node was reachable recently, not that it is dead now. A paused, partitioned or swap-thrashing process is indistinguishable from a crashed one, and it can come back.

(c) Making it safe — three layers, in order of strength:

  • Fencing tokens (the real fix). Each acquisition comes with an increasing number — the election key's etcd revision works directly. The leader includes it with every database write, and the database rejects any write with a token lower than the highest it has seen. The returning old leader carries 41, the database has seen 42, and its writes are refused. Note where enforcement lives: in the resource, not in etcd. A lock service can't protect a resource that doesn't check.
  • Check-before-act with a short horizon. Before each batch of side effects, re-verify leadership and abort if lost. This narrows the window but doesn't close it — the process could pause between the check and the write. A useful mitigation, not a guarantee, and being clear about that matters.
  • Idempotent writes. If every write is keyed so that applying it twice is harmless, two leaders are inefficient rather than incorrect. Often the most practical answer when you can't modify the resource to support fencing. (Chubby's lock-delay is the other option for unmodifiable resources: delay re-acquisition after a holder fails so in-flight requests drain.)

What not to do: lengthen the lease. It reduces the probability of a spurious expiry but increases failover time proportionally, trading availability for a risk you haven't eliminated — the pause could simply be longer. Tuning timeouts feels like a fix and isn't one.

The generalisation: every distributed lock is unsafe unless the protected resource participates. It's the same conclusion as the job scheduler's worker lease and the ticket-booking hold, and recognising it as one recurring principle rather than three separate problems is what the interview is looking for.

Estimate it

A Kubernetes-style control plane stores 300,000 objects averaging 4 KB in etcd. 20,000 node agents each hold a watch on their own objects and send a lease keep-alive every 10 s; controllers write 800 updates per second. Is a 3-node etcd cluster with SSDs adequate, and what would you watch?

Answer

Data: 300,000 × 4 KB = 1.2 GB — under the 2 GiB default quota, but close enough that you'd raise the quota (to the 8 GiB suggested maximum) and compact old revisions regularly, since history multiplies size. Keep-alives: 20,000 ÷ 10 s = 2,000/s, cheap. Writes: 800/s × 4 KB = 3.2 MB/s through consensus, each needing a leader-plus-one-follower fsync — fine on SSDs with fsync around 1 ms, but disk latency is now the whole story, so watch fsync and commit latency percentiles. Watches: 20,000 is manageable if each agent watches only its own small prefix; a global watch by every agent would be a fan-out problem. Three nodes tolerate one failure; five would tolerate two at slightly higher write latency — choose five if a zone outage plus a node failure must be survivable. Monitor leader changes, database size versus quota, and apply lag.
