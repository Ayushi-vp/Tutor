A retail API serves 3,000 requests per second at lunchtime and 200 at four in the morning. Running enough pods and nodes for lunch all day wastes most of the bill; running enough for the night collapses at noon. Autoscaling promises the obvious fix — add capacity when load rises, remove it when load falls — and Kubernetes offers it at every level: more pods, bigger pods, more nodes. Yet autoscaling is one of the most common contributors to incidents and to surprisingly high bills.

The reasons are mechanical. A team sets a CPU target, and their I/O-bound service never scales because its CPU stays at 30% while requests queue up behind a slow database. Another team's service scales up fine in the morning but takes ten minutes to scale down after a spike, and they cannot tell whether that is a bug. A batch platform wants consumers to scale to zero overnight and discovers the built-in autoscaler cannot do it. A cluster-wide autoscaler happily adds nodes for pods whose requests are three times their usage. A cost-saving node consolidator keeps restarting a fourteen-hour training job. And a flash sale peaks in forty seconds while new capacity takes four minutes to arrive.

Every one of these is predictable from the control loops involved: what signal each one reads, how often, what formula it applies, what it deliberately does slowly, and what it needs from the layers below it. sc15 introduced the three layers and why scaling takes minutes. This lesson opens each loop. It works the Horizontal Pod Autoscaler's formula on a real metric series, including the tolerance band, how it treats pods that are still starting, and the five-minute scale-down window. It follows metrics from the kubelet to the autoscaler, explains how the Vertical Pod Autoscaler learns requests and applies them, compares the two node autoscalers — Cluster Autoscaler's node groups and Karpenter's per-pod provisioning and consolidation — and shows how KEDA scales on queues and to zero. It ends with the timeline of a real scale-up and what to do about the minutes it takes.

Three thermostats in one building

Picture a building with three independent thermostats. One decides how many heaters are on in each room (the Horizontal Pod Autoscaler: how many pods). One decides how powerful each heater is (the Vertical Pod Autoscaler: how much CPU and memory each pod requests). And one decides how many rooms the building has (the node autoscaler: how many machines). Each thermostat reads its own gauge and acts on its own schedule, and none of them talks to the others. They interact only through the building: when the first thermostat switches on more heaters than the rooms can hold, the heaters wait in the corridor (pending pods), and the third thermostat, which watches the corridor, builds new rooms.

Two design choices run through all of them, and explain most of their behaviour:

  • Act on requests, not on hope. The HPA's CPU utilisation is usage divided by request; node autoscalers decide based on whether pods' requests fit. Every loop trusts requests (k8s10), so wrong requests mislead all of them.
  • Fast up, slow down. Adding capacity late hurts users; removing it early and adding it back moments later hurts both users and stability. So every loop scales up readily and scales down cautiously, with windows, thresholds and delays that look like bugs until you know why they are there.

What each loop reads, and how often

It helps to keep a one-line card for each loop. The HPA reads a metric averaged over the workload's pods every 15 seconds and writes replicas. KEDA reads an outside signal such as queue length every 30 seconds, switches a workload between zero and one replica itself, and hands the rest to an HPA. The VPA reads days of usage history and writes requests, either into new pods or into running ones. The Cluster Autoscaler reads the list of unschedulable pods every 10 seconds and writes the desired size of cloud node groups; Karpenter reads the same list and creates individual machines, and it also reads node utilisation continuously to remove or replace nodes. When an autoscaling question comes up — "why didn't it scale?" — the first step is always to ask which loop should have acted, look at the signal it reads, and check its status and events, which every one of them publishes.

Where the analogy breaks: a thermostat responds to temperature within seconds and a heater warms up in a minute. Here, the slowest step — building a new room, then moving a heater in and warming it up — takes minutes, so the autoscalers are good at following a daily curve and poor at catching a sudden spike. The building needs some spare heaters already running.

1. The HPA's loop and formula

A HorizontalPodAutoscaler (API version autoscaling/v2) targets a workload's scale subresource (a Deployment, StatefulSet or anything implementing it) and lists one or more metrics with targets:

apiVersion: autoscaling/v2
kind: HorizontalPodAutoscaler
metadata: { name: api }
spec:
  scaleTargetRef: { apiVersion: apps/v1, kind: Deployment, name: api }
  minReplicas: 2
  maxReplicas: 20
  metrics:
  - type: Resource
    resource: { name: cpu, target: { type: Utilization, averageUtilization: 60 } }

The HPA controller runs inside the kube-controller-manager. Every 15 seconds (--horizontal-pod-autoscaler-sync-period) it reads the metric for the target's pods and computes:

desiredReplicas = ceil( currentReplicas × currentMetricValue / desiredMetricValue )

With a CPU utilisation target, the current value is the average over pods of usage ÷ request. Pods using 450m against a 500m request are at 90%; with a 60% target the ratio is 1.5, so 4 pods become ceil(6) = 6. Four more rules shape the result:

  1. Tolerance. If the ratio is within 0.1 of 1.0 (between 0.9 and 1.1 by default), nothing happens. This prevents constant small adjustments from noisy metrics.
  2. Multiple metrics. With several metrics, the HPA computes a desired count for each and takes the largest, so any one of them can drive a scale-up.
  3. Starting and missing pods. Pods being deleted are ignored and failed pods discarded. For CPU, pods that are not yet ready — or became ready only recently, within the initial-readiness delay (30 s) or CPU initialisation period (5 min) — and pods with no metrics are set aside. The ratio is computed from the rest, then recomputed conservatively: assume missing pods use 100% of target when the decision is a scale-down, and missing and not-ready pods use 0% when it is a scale-up. The aim is to damp decisions that are based on incomplete data, and to stop start-up CPU spikes (JIT compilation, cache warming) from triggering yet more scale-up.
  4. Behaviour. The raw recommendation passes through scaling policies and stabilization windows (section 3), and is bounded by minReplicas and maxReplicas.

The result is written to the Deployment's replicas, and the Deployment and ReplicaSet controllers do the rest (k8s7). The HPA records its reasoning in its status conditions (AbleToScale, ScalingActive, ScalingLimited) and events (SuccessfulRescale … reason: cpu resource utilization (percentage of request) above target), visible with kubectl describe hpa.

The HPA reads metrics through three aggregated APIs served by add-ons, not by Kubernetes itself:

metrics-server scrapes each kubelet's resource-metrics endpoint (which reads cgroup usage through the runtime) every 15 seconds by default and keeps only the latest values in memory. That pipeline is why the HPA's view of CPU lags reality by up to a few tens of seconds. For custom metrics, the application exports a counter such as http_requests_total to Prometheus, which scrapes it every 15-60 seconds; the adapter turns a PromQL query (rate(http_requests_total[2m]) per pod) into the custom metrics API. Each hop adds delay and averaging, which smooths noise but slows reaction. Choosing the metric is the most important decision: CPU for CPU-bound work, request rate or in-flight requests for I/O-bound services, queue depth or consumer lag for workers, and for GPU inference a serving-engine signal such as queued requests (k8s14).

3. Behaviour: why scale-down is slow on purpose

The behavior field controls the pace, separately for each direction. Without it, the defaults are:

  • Scale up: stabilization window 0 s; policies "add up to 100% of current replicas per 15 s" and "add up to 4 pods per 15 s", whichever allows more. So 2 pods can become 6 (or 4 → 8) in one step.
  • Scale down: stabilization window 300 s; policy "remove up to 100% per 15 s". The window means the HPA looks at every recommendation it made in the last five minutes and uses the highest. Capacity is only released once the need for it has been absent for five minutes.

The asymmetry is deliberate: removing pods during a brief lull and re-adding them a minute later costs a cold start each time and can turn a dip into an outage. You can tune it per HPA — a longer scale-down window for spiky services, a slower scale-up policy for services whose dependencies cannot take sudden load, or selectPolicy: Disabled to stop scale-down entirely during an event.

5. The Vertical Pod Autoscaler

The HPA changes how many pods there are. The Vertical Pod Autoscaler (a separate project in the kubernetes/autoscaler repository, installed as three components) changes how big they are, by recommending and applying requests:

  • The recommender watches usage from the metrics API, keeps decaying histograms of each container's CPU and memory usage (memory based on peaks, including OOM events), and computes a target request (a high percentile plus a safety margin) with lower and upper bounds. Recommendations appear in the VPA object's status.
  • The admission controller is a mutating webhook (k8s6) that sets requests on new pods to the current recommendation.
  • The updater applies recommendations to running pods when they drift far enough, according to the updateMode: Off (recommend only), Initial (only at pod creation), Recreate (evict pods so they are recreated with new requests, respecting PodDisruptionBudgets), or InPlaceOrRecreate (use in-place resize, k8s10, falling back to eviction). The older Auto mode is deprecated in favour of the explicit ones.

VPA is best at right-sizing over days — finding that the service copied from a template with 2 CPUs and 4 GiB really needs 400m and 900 MiB — rather than reacting to a spike. Many teams run it in Off mode across the cluster and feed its recommendations into code review or a cost dashboard. The rule from the myths applies: never let HPA and VPA act on the same resource metric for the same workload.

6. Node autoscaling with Cluster Autoscaler

More replicas only help if they can be scheduled. When they cannot, they sit Pending with a FailedScheduling event, and the node autoscaler acts. The Cluster Autoscaler (CA) works with node groups — cloud groups of identical instances (an AWS Auto Scaling group, a GKE node pool) with a minimum and maximum size.

Scale-up. Every 10 seconds, CA lists unschedulable pods. For each node group it builds a template node (the instance type's allocatable resources, labels and taints) and runs the scheduler's own filtering logic in simulation: "if I added one node of this group, would these pods fit?" Several groups may qualify; an expander picks one — least-waste (least idle CPU and memory after placing the pods), priority (a configured preference order, for example spot before on-demand), random and others. CA then increases the group's desired size; the cloud launches instances, which boot, join and become Ready, and the scheduler places the pods.

Scale-down. A node becomes a candidate when the sum of its pods' requests is below 50% of its allocatable CPU and memory (--scale-down-utilization-threshold=0.5) for 10 minutes (--scale-down-unneeded-time), and every pod on it can be moved: managed by a controller, allowed by its PodDisruptionBudget, not using local storage, not annotated cluster-autoscaler.kubernetes.io/safe-to-evict: "false". CA simulates rescheduling them elsewhere, then cordons and drains the node and deletes the instance. Note "requests", not usage again: a node full of pods with inflated requests never looks underused.

The node-group model is simple and predictable, and it is also rigid: each group is one instance shape, so a cluster with varied workloads needs many groups, and a 30-CPU pod needs a group whose instances are big enough even if that is wasteful for everything else.

7. Node autoscaling with Karpenter

Karpenter (originally from AWS, now a Kubernetes SIG Autoscaling project with providers for AWS and Azure) drops node groups. A NodePool describes what nodes may be created — requirements on instance families, sizes, architectures, zones and capacity type (spot or on-demand), plus limits on total CPU and memory — and a provider-specific node class (for AWS, an EC2NodeClass) describes images, subnets and security groups.

Provisioning. Karpenter watches unschedulable pods directly, groups them by compatible constraints, and solves a bin-packing problem: which instance types, among all those the NodePool allows, can hold these pods most cheaply? Ten pending pods each requesting 1 CPU and 2 GiB might get one 16-vCPU, 32 GiB instance rather than ten small ones, or a mix that respects the pods' zone spread rules. It calls the cloud's fleet API directly, often with several instance types as options so spot capacity can be found, which usually makes nodes appear faster than a node group scale-up.

Disruption and consolidation. Karpenter also continuously removes or replaces nodes. consolidationPolicy: WhenEmptyOrUnderutilized (the default, with consolidateAfter: 0s) makes it look for nodes whose pods could run on other existing nodes (delete), or on a cheaper replacement (replace), and act on them. WhenEmpty only removes nodes with no workload pods. Nodes are also replaced when they drift from their NodePool spec (a new image) or reach an expiry age. All of this evicts pods, so it is bounded by NodePool disruption budgets (for example "at most 10% of nodes at a time", or none during a schedule), by PodDisruptionBudgets, and by the karpenter.sh/do-not-disrupt annotation on pods that must finish, such as long training runs. Spot interruption notices are handled by draining the node before the cloud reclaims it.

8. KEDA: events, queues and zero

Some workloads should scale on something outside the cluster: messages waiting in a queue, lag on a Kafka topic, rows in a table, a schedule. And some should scale to zero when there is nothing to do. KEDA (Kubernetes Event-Driven Autoscaling, a CNCF graduated project) provides both. A ScaledObject names a workload and one or more triggers from KEDA's catalogue of scalers:

apiVersion: keda.sh/v1alpha1
kind: ScaledObject
metadata: { name: thumbnailer }
spec:
  scaleTargetRef: { name: thumbnailer }
  minReplicaCount: 0          # scale to zero
  maxReplicaCount: 50
  pollingInterval: 30         # default 30 s
  cooldownPeriod: 300         # default 300 s before returning to zero
  triggers:
  - type: aws-sqs-queue
    metadata: { queueURL: https://sqs.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/123456789012/thumbs,
                queueLength: "20", activationQueueLength: "1", awsRegion: us-east-1 }

KEDA splits the job. Its operator polls the trigger every pollingInterval and handles the 0↔1 transitions itself: when the queue exceeds the activation threshold (here one message) it scales the Deployment from 0 to 1; when the trigger has been inactive for cooldownPeriod, it scales back to 0. For 1↔N it creates and manages an ordinary HPA whose metric is an external metric served by KEDA's metrics server, with a per-replica target — here 20 messages per replica. With 340 messages queued, the HPA computes ceil(340 / 20) = 17 replicas (the ratio form of the same formula). As workers drain the queue, the HPA's scale-down window brings replicas down, and when the queue has been empty for five minutes, KEDA takes it to zero.

For work that should run as discrete batches, a ScaledJob creates Jobs (k8s7) in proportion to pending events instead of scaling long-running consumers, which suits long tasks that should not be interrupted by a scale-down. Scale-to-zero always buys cost with latency: the first message after a quiet period waits for a pod to be scheduled, pulled and started — seconds for a small image on a warm node, minutes for a GPU model on a new one.

9. The end-to-end timeline of a scale-up

Put the layers together for a surge on a cluster with no spare nodes:

Roughly: 15-60 s for the metric to reflect the surge; up to 15 s for the HPA; seconds for the scheduler and node autoscaler to react; 60-120 s for an instance to boot, run its bootstrap, register and become Ready (network plugin up); seconds to minutes to pull the image; then the application's start and warm-up before readiness passes. Two to five minutes is normal. If the surge peaks in 30 seconds, all of it arrives too late.

The practical answers are layered. Headroom: target 50-70% utilisation, not 90%, so existing pods absorb a burst. Overprovisioning: run a Deployment of low-priority placeholder pods (a pause image with real requests and a negative-priority PriorityClass); real pods preempt them instantly (k8s10), and the evicted placeholders become pending, which makes the node autoscaler add a node in the background. Scheduled scaling for known events (raise minReplicas before the sale, with a CronJob or KEDA's cron trigger). Faster start: small images (k8s3), pre-pulled images on node images, lazy-loading snapshotters, fast application start. And load shedding for what nobody predicted (sc12).

10. Choosing and testing an autoscaling setup

Choosing comes down to three questions. What signal tracks the load this workload's users feel? CPU for CPU-bound request handling; request rate or concurrency for I/O-bound services; queue depth or lag for consumers; serving-engine queues for model inference. That picks the HPA metric or the KEDA trigger. How fast must capacity follow, and how fast can it arrive? If new pods take ten seconds and the traffic curve is daily, reactive scaling is enough; if pods take minutes or traffic jumps in seconds, add headroom, overprovisioning or scheduled scaling. What should the nodes look like? A few uniform workloads fit node groups and the Cluster Autoscaler; varied workloads, spot capacity and cost pressure favour Karpenter, with disruption budgets set before it is enabled. VPA sits beside all of these in recommendation mode, keeping requests honest.

Testing matters because autoscaling only acts under load, which is exactly when you cannot afford to discover it misbehaves. A useful drill: run a load test that ramps traffic from baseline to twice the expected peak over ten minutes, holds, then drops sharply. Record replica count, pending pods, node count and the latency percentiles against time. You should see replicas follow the ramp within a minute or two, pending pods appear briefly as nodes are added, latency stay within its objective during the ramp (if it does not, headroom is too low or start-up too slow), and capacity hold for the scale-down window after the drop before shrinking without errors. Repeat after any change to the metric, the requests, the images or the node configuration.

Why the HPA is a proportional controller, and what that implies

The HPA's formula makes the replica count proportional to the ratio of observed to target metric: a pure proportional controller, with a dead band (the tolerance) and asymmetric rate limits. It assumes the metric per pod falls in proportion as pods are added — true for CPU on a load-balanced stateless service, false when the bottleneck is elsewhere. If a database is saturated, adding API pods raises neither throughput nor lowers per-pod latency; a latency-based HPA will keep scaling up against maxReplicas and make the database worse. Good HPA metrics are ones the pods themselves control; when they are not, cap maxReplicas at what downstream systems can take and alert when the HPA sits at its maximum.

Metric staleness and the feedback delay

A control loop with a delay between action and observed effect overshoots. Here the delay is long: new pods take a minute or more to start contributing, and CPU metrics are averages over scrape windows. The HPA compensates by ignoring not-yet-ready pods' CPU and by the scale-down window, but scale-up can still overshoot when a burst is short: the HPA sees 150% utilisation, doubles, and by the time the new pods are ready the burst is over. That overshoot is usually acceptable (capacity is released five minutes later); undershoot during sustained growth is not. Scaling on a leading indicator such as request rate or queue depth rather than a lagging one such as CPU reduces both.

Bin packing, fragmentation and why node autoscalers disagree with intuition

A cluster at 40% average utilisation can still have pending pods if the free capacity is scattered in pieces too small for them — 1.5 CPUs free on each of ten nodes cannot hold one 2-CPU pod. Node autoscalers fix that by adding a node, raising cost while utilisation falls further. Consolidation (Karpenter) or descheduling (k8s10) reverses fragmentation by moving pods so whole nodes empty out, at the price of disruption. Requests, again, sit underneath: pods with 2-CPU requests and 300m usage fragment a cluster that would otherwise pack easily, which is why right-sizing with VPA recommendations often saves more than any autoscaler setting.

Spot capacity and interruption

Spot or preemptible instances cost a fraction of on-demand but can be reclaimed with a short notice (two minutes on AWS). Node autoscalers make them practical: Karpenter and CA's priority expander prefer spot pools, diversify across many instance types so a shortage in one does not block scaling, and fall back to on-demand. Interruption handling turns the cloud's notice into a cordon and drain so pods move gracefully. Workloads still need to tolerate it: stateless services with several replicas spread across nodes, PodDisruptionBudgets, and checkpointing for batch and training jobs. Keeping a baseline of on-demand capacity for the minimum replicas, and running bursts on spot, is the usual compromise.

Predictive and scheduled scaling

Reactive loops can only follow; for traffic with strong daily or weekly patterns, the lag can be removed by acting on a forecast. The simplest form is scheduled scaling: raise minReplicas (or a KEDA cron trigger's desired count) half an hour before the morning ramp and lower it in the evening, letting the HPA handle deviations. More elaborate systems fit a forecast to past traffic and set the floor from it. Both keep the reactive HPA as the safety net and simply move the starting point, so a forecast error costs some money rather than an outage.

Autoscaling stateful and GPU workloads

Scaling a StatefulSet's replicas is mechanically identical, but adding a database replica or a Kafka broker means data movement that takes far longer than pod start-up, so these are scaled by operators or humans on capacity plans rather than by an HPA reacting to minutes of load. GPU inference sits in between: replicas are stateless, but each holds a model that takes tens of seconds to minutes to load, and nodes are scarce and slow to provision. The patterns that work are leading metrics from the serving engine (queue length, KV-cache use), conservative scale-down, warm pools of nodes or placeholder pods, and caching model weights on nodes so a new replica on a warm node starts in seconds (k8s14).

Cost as a feedback signal

Autoscalers optimise what they can see — metrics and pending pods — not money. Closing the loop on cost means feeding it back: per-namespace cost from requests multiplied by node prices (tools such as OpenCost do this), alerts when a workload's requested-to-used ratio drifts upward, and reviews of HPA minimums, which quietly set the floor of a service's spend. A service whose HPA never drops below its minimum is not autoscaled at all; it is a fixed fleet with extra steps.

The case: the HPA algorithm and Karpenter's consolidation, as documented

The Kubernetes documentation's "algorithm details" section is the authoritative account of the behaviours that most often confuse teams in production, and reading it explains a long list of tickets. It states the formula and the 0.1 default tolerance; that pods with a deletion timestamp are ignored and failed pods discarded; that for CPU, pods not yet ready — judged with a 30-second initial-readiness delay and a five-minute CPU initialisation period — and pods with missing metrics are set aside; and that the average is then recomputed "more conservatively, assuming those pods were consuming 100% of the desired value in case of a scale down, and 0% in case of a scale up". It also documents that with multiple metrics the largest recommendation wins, and the default behaviour: no stabilization for scale-up with doubling-or-four-pods policies, and a 300-second window for scale-down. Teams who see "the HPA didn't scale even though CPU was high" usually find not-ready pods dampening the decision or the tolerance band; teams who see "it took five minutes to scale down" have met the window.

Karpenter's disruption documentation plays the same role for node churn. It states that if not set, NodePools use consolidationPolicy: WhenEmptyOrUnderutilized with consolidateAfter: 0s — the most aggressive cost setting — and explains the trade-off: WhenEmpty for the most conservative behaviour, underutilised consolidation for savings at the cost of moving pods. Teams adopting Karpenter for its fast provisioning are often surprised by regular pod evictions on day two; the documented remedies are disruption budgets on the NodePool, PodDisruptionBudgets on workloads, a longer consolidateAfter, and karpenter.sh/do-not-disrupt on pods that must not move.

Failure modes and symptoms

  • HPA shows <unknown> targets: metrics-server or the adapter is down, or pods have no requests for the resource. Events say FailedGetResourceMetric.
  • Never scales despite load: wrong metric (CPU on an I/O-bound service), requests too high, or pinned at maxReplicas (condition ScalingLimited).
  • Flapping: a too-short scale-down window, or HPA and VPA fighting.
  • Pods pending, no new nodes: node group at its maximum, no instance type satisfies the pods' constraints, cloud capacity or quota exhausted, or a taint and toleration mismatch the autoscaler's template does not satisfy; CA and Karpenter both log why.
  • Nodes never scale down: pods without controllers, local storage, restrictive PDBs (maxUnavailable: 0), safe-to-evict: false, or system pods without PDBs on every node.
  • Regular unexplained evictions: consolidation, spot interruptions or node expiry.
  • Autoscaling made it worse: scaling out a service whose bottleneck is downstream overloads the dependency; cap replicas and shed load.

Knobs, in order of impact

The metric and its target; accurate requests; minReplicas (your floor of headroom); the scale-down stabilization window; scale-up policies for dependencies that cannot take sudden load; node autoscaler limits and instance diversity; consolidation policy and disruption budgets; and the size of the overprovisioning buffer.

What to watch

HPA current versus desired replicas and time at max; the HPA's metric versus target; pending pod count and age; time from pod creation to Ready on new nodes (the scale-up latency you actually have); node count and cost by capacity type; consolidation and interruption events; and the ratio of requested to used CPU and memory across the cluster, which bounds how much any autoscaler can save.

A note on limits

Every autoscaler should have a ceiling that reflects what the rest of the system can absorb: maxReplicas sized to downstream capacity and connection limits, NodePool or node-group limits sized to budget and cloud quotas. Hitting a ceiling should page someone, because it means demand exceeded what was planned for, and the answer is a decision, not an automatic scale-up.

    import math
def hpa(demand_m, request_m=500, target=60, start=4, lo=2, hi=20, tol=0.1, window=5):
    pods, recs = start, []
    for t, d in enumerate(demand_m):
        util = d * 100 // (pods * request_m)      # integer percent, as the HPA computes it
        ratio = util / target
        rec = pods if abs(1 - ratio) <= tol else math.ceil(pods * ratio)
        rec = max(lo, min(hi, rec, max(pods * 2, pods + 4)))   # scale-up policy
        recs.append(rec)
        new = rec if rec >= pods else max(recs[-window:])       # scale-down window

Design: autoscaling a queue-driven document pipeline

A document-processing service pulls jobs from an SQS queue. Each job takes 20-40 seconds of CPU on one core; each worker pod (request 1 CPU, 1 GiB) processes one job at a time. Traffic: near zero overnight, 2,000 jobs per hour on weekdays, and month-end bursts of 30,000 jobs within an hour. The business wants jobs finished within 15 minutes of arrival and the lowest cost overnight. Today the Deployment has a CPU-based HPA (target 70%, min 3, max 30) and a fixed node group of 10 four-core nodes. Diagnose the current setup and design a better one.

Answer

Current problems: CPU utilisation saturates at ~100% for any busy worker whether one job or a thousand are queued, so the HPA cannot tell a backlog from steady load and scales to at most a ratio of 100/70 per step; minimum 3 wastes capacity overnight; and 10 nodes cap the cluster at about 36 workers (after DaemonSets), far below month-end needs.

Sizing: at 30 s average, a worker handles about 120 jobs per hour. Weekday load of 2,000 per hour needs ~17 workers. A 30,000-job burst finished within roughly 15 minutes of arrival means draining ~30,000 jobs in about an hour plus the 15-minute target: around 250-300 workers at peak (30,000 ÷ 120 ≈ 250 worker-hours).

Design: a KEDA ScaledObject on the SQS queue with a per-replica target of about 10 queued messages (so the backlog per worker stays under ~5 minutes), minReplicaCount: 0, activation at 1 message, maxReplicaCount: 300, a scale-up policy allowing large jumps, and a scale-down window of several minutes. Karpenter (or CA with a large node group and the least-waste expander) for nodes, preferring spot with on-demand fallback and many instance types; workers must handle SIGTERM by finishing or returning the current job to the queue (visibility timeout) so spot interruptions and scale-down lose nothing. Overnight, the first job waits for a cold start — acceptable against a 15-minute target. For month-end, add a scheduled pre-scale (KEDA cron trigger) shortly before the known burst, so nodes are ready when it arrives. Monitor queue age (the SLO metric), not just length.
