It is 2 a.m. and the pager says checkout is failing. kubectl get pods shows a column of words — CrashLoopBackOff, 0/1 Running, ImagePullBackOff, Pending, Terminating — and a restart count of 47. The engineer on call tries kubectl logs and gets two lines of start-up banner. They try kubectl exec … bash and get "executable file not found", because the image is distroless. They restart the Deployment, which makes everything worse for ten minutes. The cause, when they find it an hour later, was printed in plain text all along — in the previous container's logs, in the Last State section of kubectl describe, in an event that said exactly which Secret key was missing.

Debugging Kubernetes is less about clever tricks than about knowing where each kind of failure leaves its evidence. Every component in k8s6 writes something down: the scheduler records why a pod could not be placed; the kubelet records image pulls, probe failures, kills and back-offs as events and as the container's last state; the kernel's OOM killer leaves a reason and an exit code; controllers record what they created and why they stalled. A small number of commands reads all of it, and a small number of patterns covers most incidents.

Some failures are made worse by Kubernetes' own machinery when it is configured carelessly. The best-known example is the liveness probe that checks a database: one slow query, and the kubelet restarts every replica of every service at once. Zalando's engineers, who run hundreds of clusters, wrote this up in 2019 under the title "Liveness Probes are Dangerous" and started a public collection of Kubernetes failure stories so that others could learn from such incidents without repeating them.

This lesson gives a systematic method — follow the pod's life from scheduling to serving and ask, at each stage, "did this step happen, and if not, what does the evidence say?" — and then works through each common state with real kubectl describe output: Pending, ContainerCreating, ImagePullBackOff, CreateContainerConfigError, CrashLoopBackOff (with exit codes decoded), OOMKilled, probe failures, Running but not Ready, Ready but failing, and stuck Terminating. It ends with the toolkit for images without shells and for node-level problems, and with what to put in place so the next 2 a.m. is shorter.

A parcel's tracking page

When a parcel goes missing you do not search every warehouse. You open the tracking page and find the last scan: "accepted", "sorted", "out for delivery", "delivery attempted — no access". The last successful step tells you where to look, and the failed step usually comes with a reason.

A pod has the same kind of tracking history, and the stages always happen in the same order:

The tracking page itself is three commands. kubectl get pods gives the one-word summary per pod: STATUS, READY, RESTARTS. kubectl describe pod gives the full record: each container's current state and Last State (the previous run's exit code and reason), the pod's conditions, and the most recent events for that pod. kubectl logs --previous gives what the crashed instance printed before it died. Most incidents are solved by reading these three in order, before touching anything.

Who wrote each clue

Every line of evidence was written by one component, and knowing which tells you how far the pod got. A FailedScheduling event comes from the scheduler: the pod never reached a node. FailedMount, Pulling, BackOff, Unhealthy and Killing come from the kubelet on the pod's node: the pod is bound and the node is trying. OOMKilled in Last State was recorded by the runtime after the kernel killed the process. SuccessfulCreate or FailedCreate on a ReplicaSet comes from its controller: if pods do not even exist, look there, because an admission webhook or a quota may have refused them. The From column in the events table is therefore as informative as the message, and a missing kind of event — no scheduler event at all, no kubelet events — points straight at the component that is not doing its job.

The second habit is to stop before "fixing". Restarting pods, deleting them or scaling up destroys evidence — the previous container's logs, the last state, the pod that showed the problem — and in some failure modes (liveness cascades, overloaded dependencies) makes the outage worse. Read first, then act; if users are hurting, roll back the last change (k8s7), which is usually safer than any improvised fix.

Where the analogy breaks: a tracking page keeps its history forever, but Kubernetes events are kept for only about an hour by default, logs of a container are only kept for the current and previous instance, and a deleted pod takes its status with it. Evidence evaporates, so capture it early — and ship events and logs somewhere durable before you need them.

2. Exit codes: decode before you read logs

When a container stops, its process's exit status is recorded. Shells and runtimes use the convention that a process killed by signal N reports 128 + N. That makes the last state's exit code the fastest clue:

Exit codeMeaningTypical cause in Kubernetes
0SuccessA process that should keep running exited (wrong command, daemon that forks to background)
1, 2 …The application's own errorConfiguration missing, dependency unreachable at start, unhandled exception
126Command cannot be executedNot executable, wrong permissions
127Command not foundTypo in command, binary missing in image, shell-form command in a shell-less image
128 + 9 = 137SIGKILLOOM kill (reason OOMKilled), or killed after grace period (liveness failure, deletion, eviction)
128 + 11 = 139SIGSEGVNative crash: segfault in a C extension, incompatible library or architecture
128 + 15 = 143SIGTERMStopped gracefully on request (deletion, liveness restart with a handler)

The reason next to the exit code narrows it further: OOMKilled (the kernel, k8s10), Error (non-zero exit), Completed (exit 0), ContainerCannotRun or StartError (the runtime could not start the process at all — exec format errors from wrong-architecture images, k8s1, land here). Applications can also write a short explanation to /dev/termination-log, which appears as the termination message.

3. CrashLoopBackOff, worked

When a container exits and the pod's restart policy says to restart it, the kubelet does — after a delay that starts at 10 seconds and doubles each time (20, 40, 80, 160 s) up to a cap of 5 minutes, and resets once the container has run successfully for 10 minutes. While waiting, the container's state is Waiting with reason CrashLoopBackOff. (Kubernetes 1.33 added alpha options to shorten this — a 1-second initial delay and 60-second cap, or a per-node maximum — but the defaults above apply unless your cluster enables them.)

Here is a real-shaped kubectl describe pod for a crash-looping API (trimmed):

Name:         api-6b7d9c5f4-q8xkz
Namespace:    shop
Status:       Running
Containers:
  api:
    Image:          ghcr.io/myorg/api@sha256:3f9c…
    State:          Waiting
      Reason:       CrashLoopBackOff
    Last State:     Terminated
      Reason:       Error
      Exit Code:    1
      Started:      Sat, 04 Oct 2026 02:11:40 +0000
      Finished:     Sat, 04 Oct 2026 02:11:42 +0000
    Ready:          False
    Restart Count:  6
    Environment Variables from:
      api-config    ConfigMap  Optional: false
Conditions:
  Ready             False
Events:
  Type     Reason     Age                  From     Message
  ----     ------     ----                 ----     -------
  Normal   Pulled     7m (x7 over 13m)     kubelet  Container image "ghcr.io/myorg/api@sha256:3f9c…" already present on machine
  Normal   Created    7m (x7 over 13m)     kubelet  Created container: api
  Normal   Started    7m (x7 over 13m)     kubelet  Started container api
  Warning  BackOff    2m (x26 over 12m)    kubelet  Back-off restarting failed container api in pod api-6b7d9c5f4-q8xkz

Read it top to bottom. The image is present and the container is created and started each time, so stages 1-3 are fine. Last State says the previous run lasted two seconds and exited with code 1 and reason Error: the application itself gave up, not the kernel or the kubelet. Restart count 6 over 13 minutes matches the back-off schedule. Nothing about probes. So the answer is in the application's output:

$ kubectl logs api-6b7d9c5f4-q8xkz --previous
Traceback (most recent call last):
  File "/app/api/settings.py", line 14, in <module>
    DATABASE_URL = os.environ["DATABASE_URL"]
KeyError: 'DATABASE_URL'

The new release renamed the variable in the ConfigMap to DB_URL. Fix the ConfigMap or the code and roll forward, or kubectl rollout undo while you do. Without --previous, kubectl logs would have shown the current instance, which in the waiting state has no output at all.

Three other common shapes of the same state:

  • Exit code 0, reason Completed, still CrashLoopBackOff: the process finished successfully but the restart policy is Always. The command runs a one-shot script, or a daemon forks to the background and the foreground process exits. Make the main process stay in the foreground, or use a Job (k8s7).
  • Exit code 127 or 126: the command does not exist or cannot run in this image — a typo in command, a shell-form command in a distroless image (k8s3), a script without execute permission.
  • Exit code 139: a native crash. Common after a base-image change that brought an incompatible library, or an image built for another architecture under emulation.

4. OOMKilled

    Last State:     Terminated
      Reason:       OOMKilled
      Exit Code:    137
      Started:      Sat, 04 Oct 2026 01:52:03 +0000
      Finished:     Sat, 04 Oct 2026 02:06:47 +0000
    Restart Count:  3
    Limits:
      memory:  2Gi
    Requests:
      cpu:     500m
      memory:  2Gi

The container ran for about fifteen minutes and was killed by the kernel for exceeding its 2 GiB limit (k8s10). The application logs usually end mid-sentence: there was no chance to log anything. Questions to answer:

  1. Growth or spike? Graph container_memory_working_set_bytes for the pod: a steady climb over the fifteen minutes is a leak or an unbounded cache; a sudden jump is one large request, a batch job or a data load.
  2. What kind of memory? From a debug container or the node, read the cgroup's memory.stat: large anon is heap; large shmem is /dev/shm or memory-backed emptyDirs (data-loader workers); large file is page cache that could not be reclaimed fast enough.
  3. Does the runtime know its limit? A JVM, Node.js or a worker pool sized from the node's memory rather than the container's will overshoot (k8s3).
  4. Is the limit simply too low for the real workload? If usage is legitimate, raise the limit — and the request with it.

Distinguish this from eviction, where the pod (not just the container) is terminated by the kubelet under node pressure: the pod's status shows reason: Evicted and a message such as "The node was low on resource: memory", and its controller creates a replacement elsewhere.

5. Image pull failures

Events:
  Warning  Failed     12s (x3 over 52s)  kubelet  Failed to pull image "ghcr.io/myorg/api:2.7.3": failed to resolve reference "ghcr.io/myorg/api:2.7.3": ghcr.io/myorg/api:2.7.3: not found
  Warning  Failed     12s (x3 over 52s)  kubelet  Error: ErrImagePull
  Normal   BackOff    1s (x4 over 51s)   kubelet  Back-off pulling image "ghcr.io/myorg/api:2.7.3"
  Warning  Failed     1s (x4 over 51s)   kubelet  Error: ImagePullBackOff

ErrImagePull is a failed attempt; ImagePullBackOff is the wait before the next one (with the same doubling back-off). The registry's message decides the fix:

  • not found / manifest unknown: wrong repository or tag, or CI never pushed it. Check with docker buildx imagetools inspect or crane manifest from outside the cluster.
  • unauthorized / 403 / pull access denied: private registry without credentials. Add imagePullSecrets (a kubernetes.io/dockerconfigjson Secret) to the pod or its service account, or configure node-level credentials.
  • toomanyrequests: registry rate limit (Docker Hub, k8s5). Authenticate or use a mirror.
  • i/o timeout, no such host: the node cannot reach the registry: DNS, egress firewall, proxy, private endpoint.
  • no matching manifest for linux/arm64: the image has no variant for the node's architecture (k8s1).

6. Pending, ContainerCreating and configuration errors

Pending with FailedScheduling. The scheduler explains itself:

  Warning  FailedScheduling  30s  default-scheduler  0/6 nodes are available: 2 node(s) had untolerated taint {nvidia.com/gpu: present}, 4 Insufficient memory. preemption: 0/6 nodes are available: 6 No preemption victims found for incoming pod.

Six nodes: two are GPU nodes the pod does not tolerate, four lack enough unrequested memory for its request, and nothing lower-priority could be preempted. Options: reduce the request if it is inflated, add capacity (or check why the autoscaler did not, k8s11), or add a toleration if the pod belongs on GPU nodes. Other common reasons in the same message: didn't match Pod's node affinity/selector, didn't match pod topology spread constraints, had volume node affinity conflict, pod has unbound immediate PersistentVolumeClaims (k8s9). No event at all means no scheduler has looked at it (k8s6's drill).

ContainerCreating for minutes. The pod is bound but the kubelet cannot finish preparing it. Events name the step: FailedMount or FailedAttachVolume (a volume, often a Multi-Attach error or a missing Secret volume, k8s9), FailedCreatePodSandBox with a CNI error (no IPs left, the CNI DaemonSet not running on that node, k8s8).

CreateContainerConfigError. The kubelet could not assemble the container's configuration, so it never started it:

  Warning  Failed  5s (x4 over 40s)  kubelet  Error: couldn't find key DATABASE_URL in Secret shop/db

or container has runAsNonRoot and image has non-numeric user (app), cannot verify user is non-root (k8s3). The message is exact; fix the reference.

7. Probes: when health checks cause the failure

Probes (sc15, k8s7) are run by the kubelet with defaults of periodSeconds: 10, timeoutSeconds: 1, successThreshold: 1, failureThreshold: 3. A liveness failure looks like this:

  Warning  Unhealthy  40s (x3 over 60s)  kubelet  Liveness probe failed: Get "http://10.244.1.17:8080/healthz": context deadline exceeded (Client.Timeout exceeded while awaiting headers)
  Normal   Killing    40s                kubelet  Container api failed liveness probe, will be restarted

The probe timed out three times in a row (one second each), so the kubelet killed the container: SIGTERM, then SIGKILL after the grace period, giving exit code 143 or 137 with reason Error — easily mistaken for an OOM. Ask why /healthz took more than a second. If the app was busy (CPU saturated, throttled by a CPU limit, k8s10, or in a long garbage-collection pause), the liveness probe has just restarted a slow but working container, losing its in-flight requests and its warm cache, and the remaining pods now carry more load and may fail their probes too. If /healthz checks the database, a database slowdown fails every pod's liveness at once.

The rules that prevent this: liveness checks only that the process can make progress (a trivial local endpoint, no dependencies); thresholds and timeouts generous enough to survive GC pauses and load spikes (for example timeoutSeconds: 5, failureThreshold: 6); readiness — which removes the pod from traffic instead of killing it — is where dependency and overload checks belong; a startupProbe protects slow starters, so the liveness probe does not kill a JVM that needs 60 seconds to warm up; and many services are better off with no liveness probe at all.

Running but not Ready is the readiness probe failing:

  Warning  Unhealthy  3s (x40 over 6m)  kubelet  Readiness probe failed: HTTP probe failed with statuscode: 503

The pod is out of the Service's endpoints and receives no traffic. Check the probe's port and path against what the app really serves (a port renamed in the new release, a health path that moved), whether the app is still warming up, and whether readiness depends on a failing downstream — in which case every pod is unready and the Service has no endpoints at all.

8. Ready, but the service still fails

If pods are Running, Ready and not restarting, but clients see errors, move down the path traffic takes (k8s8):

  1. Endpoints. kubectl get endpointslices -l kubernetes.io/service-name=api. Empty, or missing the new pods, means the Service's selector does not match the pods' labels, or targetPort names a port the pods do not have.
  2. From inside. Start a debug pod (kubectl run -it --rm dbg --image=nicolaka/netshoot -- bash) and test in layers: curl <pod-ip>:8080 (the app), curl api.shop:80 (Service and DNS), nslookup api.shop (DNS alone). The first layer that fails is the culprit.
  3. Policy. If direct pod access fails only from some namespaces, a NetworkPolicy is likely; check policies selecting the pods and the caller's egress.
  4. Entry point. For external traffic, the Gateway's and HTTPRoute's status conditions say whether the route is accepted and its backends resolved; the load balancer's health checks say whether nodes or pods are considered healthy.
  5. The application. Logs, metrics and traces — the cluster may be fine and the bug in the code; kubectl port-forward pod/api-… 8080 lets you hit one pod directly from your laptop.

9. Debugging without a shell: kubectl debug

Distroless and minimal images (k8s3) have no shell or tools. Ephemeral containers solve this by adding a temporary container to a running pod:

kubectl debug -it pod/api-6b7d9c5f4-q8xkz --image=busybox:1.37 --target=api -- sh
/ # ps aux                       # sees the api process (shared process namespace via --target)
/ # ls /proc/1/root/app          # the api container's file system
/ # wget -qO- localhost:8080/healthz   # same network namespace as the pod

The ephemeral container shares the pod's network namespace, so localhost is the app; with --target it also joins the target container's process namespace, so it can see the app's processes and, through /proc/<pid>/root, its files and mounted Secrets (which is why creating ephemeral containers requires its own RBAC permission). Ephemeral containers cannot be removed and have no resource limits or probes; they disappear with the pod. Two variants: kubectl debug pod/X --copy-to=X-debug --set-image=api=busybox:1.37 or --share-processes creates a modified copy of the pod (useful when the original crashes too fast to attach to, since you can change its command to sleep); and kubectl debug node/worker-3 -it --image=ubuntu runs a privileged-enough pod in the node's host namespaces with the node's root file system mounted at /host, from which chroot /host, crictl ps, journalctl -u kubelet and the cgroup files are reachable. --profile (general, baseline, restricted, netadmin, sysadmin) chooses the security settings of the debug container.

10. Stuck Terminating

A pod that stays Terminating long after its grace period usually has one of two causes. Its node is gone or unreachable, so no kubelet will ever confirm the containers stopped (k8s6); the pod object waits, which for StatefulSets is a deliberate safety guarantee (k8s7). Or it has finalizers — keys in metadata.finalizers that block deletion until a controller removes them after cleanup — and the controller responsible is not running. The same applies to namespaces stuck terminating, usually because of custom resources whose operator was deleted first. kubectl delete --force --grace-period=0 removes the pod object without waiting for confirmation; if the node is actually alive, the containers may keep running while a replacement starts, which for anything with a volume or a unique identity is how split-brain happens. Find the cause first; force deletion is the last step.

11. kubectl techniques that save time

A few commands turn the method into seconds rather than minutes. kubectl get pods -A --field-selector=status.phase=Pending lists every pending pod in the cluster; kubectl get pods -o wide --sort-by=.status.containerStatuses[0].restartCount puts the restart-heavy ones last. kubectl events --for pod/api-6b7d9c5f4-q8xkz --types=Warning shows only that pod's warnings, and kubectl get events -A --field-selector type=Warning --sort-by=.lastTimestamp gives a cluster-wide incident view. JSONPath extracts exactly the field you need, for example kubectl get pod P -o jsonpath='{range .status.containerStatuses[*]}{.name}{" "}{.lastState.terminated.reason}{" "}{.lastState.terminated.exitCode}{"\n"}{end}' to list each container's last termination reason and code. kubectl logs -l app=api --prefix --since=10m reads recent logs from every pod of a service at once. kubectl rollout history and kubectl rollout status --timeout=120s tie symptoms to the release that caused them. kubectl wait --for=condition=Ready pod -l app=api --timeout=60s makes scripts and runbooks wait correctly instead of sleeping. kubectl auth can-i create pods/ephemeralcontainers -n shop tells you whether you are allowed to debug before you try. And adding -v=6 (or -v=8 for bodies) to any command shows the API calls it makes, which settles "is it kubectl, RBAC or the API server?" in one line.

These are worth putting into a team runbook with the outputs annotated, because at 2 a.m. nobody remembers JSONPath syntax, and a runbook that turns "read Last State for every container" into one pasted command is the difference between a ten-minute and an hour-long diagnosis.

Events are lossy; plan for it

Events are ordinary API objects with a time-to-live (one hour by default, set on the API server), and repeated events are aggregated into counts ("x26 over 12m"). Under load, components rate-limit event creation, and big clusters often store events in a separate etcd to protect the main one. The consequences: events for an incident that happened overnight are gone by morning, and events for a burst may be incomplete. Production platforms export events to their logging system with an event exporter, so incident timelines can be reconstructed, and alert on warning events by reason (BackOff, FailedScheduling, FailedMount, Unhealthy) rather than waiting for someone to run describe.

Logs live on the node

Container output goes to files on the node, managed by the kubelet and runtime (under /var/log/pods/), rotated at a size limit (10 MiB per file by default) and keeping a few files. kubectl logs streams them through the API server and kubelet. Only the current and previous container instances are reachable; earlier crashes, deleted pods and pods on dead nodes take their logs with them. That is why cluster-level log collection (a DaemonSet shipping files to a central store) is part of any real platform, and why applications should log to stdout and stderr rather than to files inside the container, which no one collects and which fill the writable layer (k8s2).

Signals, grace periods and what the app sees

Every way a container is stopped by Kubernetes — deletion, rollout, scale-down, eviction, preemption, liveness failure, node drain — goes through the same sequence: preStop hook, then SIGTERM to PID 1, then SIGKILL after terminationGracePeriodSeconds (k8s1, k8s7). The exit code tells you whether the app exited on SIGTERM (143) or had to be killed (137). Seeing 137 with reason Error on every rollout means the app ignores SIGTERM, which also means in-flight requests are cut off on every deploy; fix PID 1 before chasing anything else. The termination reason, events from the component that initiated the stop (kubelet, scheduler for preemption, eviction API, Karpenter or the cluster autoscaler) and the timing pattern together identify who pulled the trigger.

Debugging the node and the runtime

Some failures are below the pod: a kubelet that cannot talk to the API server (expired certificates), a container runtime that hangs, a full disk, exhausted PIDs or inodes, a kernel bug. Node conditions (MemoryPressure, DiskPressure, PIDPressure, Ready) summarise the kubelet's view; the Node Problem Detector adds conditions and events for kernel deadlocks, file-system corruption and runtime problems. From a node debug pod: journalctl -u kubelet -u containerd, crictl ps -a and crictl logs for containers the API no longer shows, df -h and df -i, and dmesg for OOM-killer reports and hardware errors. When a node is sick in ways that are hard to fix, cordon, drain and replace it; nodes are cattle.

Admission and quota: failures before a pod exists

Sometimes there is no failing pod to describe, because the pod was never created. A Deployment shows fewer ready replicas than desired, and kubectl get pods shows nothing new. The ReplicaSet's events explain it: FailedCreate … exceeded quota (a ResourceQuota on the namespace), violates PodSecurity "restricted" (Pod Security Admission rejected the template), or admission webhook … denied the request (a policy engine such as Kyverno). Because these rejections happen in the API server's write path (k8s6), they appear on the controller that tried to create the pod, not on any pod — a common blind spot when people only ever describe pods.

Making the next incident shorter

The most effective debugging work happens before the incident. Structured logs with request IDs; metrics for every service's request rate, errors and latency; traces across services; events and logs exported off-cluster; dashboards for restarts, OOM kills, throttling and pending pods; alerts on symptoms users feel rather than on every warning event; runbooks for the states in this lesson; and a habit of rolling back first and debugging second when users are affected. And the design rules that prevent whole classes of incident: safe probes, PID 1 that handles SIGTERM, accurate requests, memory limits equal to requests, and images that identify their source.

Reproducing a failure safely

When a failure cannot be understood from the evidence, reproduce it outside production rather than experimenting on the live service. kubectl get pod P -o yaml gives the exact spec; strip the status and node-specific fields and apply it in a staging namespace with the same image digest, ConfigMaps and Secrets (or safe copies), and the same resource limits. A crash that reproduces there can be investigated freely: change the command to sleep and step through start-up with a debug container, lower the memory limit to trigger the OOM on purpose, or point the readiness probe at the endpoint you suspect. A crash that does not reproduce narrows the cause to something about the production environment — data, traffic, a dependency, the node — which is itself a strong clue.

The case: "Liveness Probes are Dangerous" and the Kubernetes Failure Stories

Henning Jacobs, then at Zalando, which operated a large fleet of Kubernetes clusters, published "Liveness Probes are Dangerous" in September 2019, adapted from Zalando's internal developer documentation. The post's opening point is that a wrong liveness probe "can worsen high-load situations (cascading failure + potential long container/app start)" and that "a Liveness Probe in combination with an external DB health check dependency is the worst situation: a single DB hiccup will restart all your containers!" It walks through the defaults (period 10 s, timeout 1 s, failure threshold 3), recommends always defining a readiness probe for HTTP services, warns against liveness probes that check dependencies or share an overloaded thread pool with request handling, and against probes on a separate management port that stays healthy while the main port is broken. Its conclusion is not "never use liveness probes" but "use them only with a clear use case and an understanding of the consequences".

Jacobs and colleagues also started Kubernetes Failure Stories (k8s.af), a curated list of public incident write-ups from many companies: DNS and ndots latency, CPU limits and throttling, OOM kills, etcd and API-server overload, admission webhooks blocking clusters, autoscaling surprises, cloud integration failures. Reading through it is the fastest way to learn which failure modes recur in practice; nearly every entry maps onto a mechanism in this track, and most were diagnosed with exactly the commands in this lesson. The meta-lesson the collection makes is that Kubernetes incidents are rarely exotic: they come from defaults left unexamined (probe timeouts, DNS settings, limits) interacting with load.

A triage runbook

  1. Scope: one pod, one Deployment, one node, one namespace or everything? kubectl get pods -A | grep -v Running, node status, recent changes (deploys, config, cluster upgrades).
  2. If a deploy just happened and users are affected: kubectl rollout undo (or revert in Git), then investigate.
  3. Describe one failing pod; identify the first stage that failed (primer figure).
  4. Read Last State and logs --previous; decode the exit code.
  5. For Pending or ContainerCreating, read events; for Ready-but-failing, walk endpoints → pod → Service → DNS → policy → entry point.
  6. Capture evidence (describe output, logs, events) before restarting or deleting anything.
  7. After the fix, write down what the evidence said and what alert would have caught it sooner.

Common traps during response

Restarting everything (kubectl rollout restart) as a first move hides the evidence and, in a liveness cascade or an overloaded dependency, restarts the cascade. Scaling up a service whose bottleneck is a database adds load to the database. Force-deleting stuck pods risks two writers on one volume. Editing live objects in a GitOps-managed cluster gets reverted within minutes (k8s12). The safe defaults are: capture evidence, roll back the last change, then debug.

Signals worth alerting on

Containers restarting more than N times in M minutes; pods in CrashLoopBackOff, ImagePullBackOff or CreateContainerConfigError for more than a few minutes; OOM kills (kube_pod_container_status_last_terminated_reason{reason="OOMKilled"}); pods Pending longer than the expected scale-up time; Deployments whose available replicas are below desired for minutes; readiness failures across many pods of one service at once (a dependency problem); nodes NotReady or under pressure.

    import re
SIG = {137: "SIGKILL (OOM kill or killed after grace period)", 143: "SIGTERM (graceful stop)",
       139: "SIGSEGV (native crash)", 127: "command not found", 126: "command not executable",
       0: "exited successfully but restartPolicy wants it running"}
def triage(text):
    state = re.search(r"State:\s+(\w+)\s+Reason:\s+(\S+)", text)
    last = re.search(r"Last State:\s+Terminated\s+Reason:\s+(\S+)\s+Exit Code:\s+(\d+)", text)
    events = re.findall(r"(Warning|Normal)\s+(\w+)\s+.*?\s{2,}(\S+)\s{2,}(.*)", text)
    out = []
    if state: out.append(f"now: {state.group(1)} / {state.group(2)}")
    if last:
        reason, code = last.group(1), int(last.group(2))
        meaning = SIG.get(code, "application error" if code < 128 else f"killed by signal {code-128}")
        out.append(f"last run: {reason}, exit {code} = {meaning}")
        out.append("next: kubectl logs --previous" if reason == "Error" and code < 128 else
                   "next: check memory limit and memory.stat" if reason == "OOMKilled" else
                   "next: look for liveness/eviction/preemption events")
    for typ, reason, src, msg in events:
        if typ == "Warning": out.append(f"event {reason} from {src}: {msg.strip()}")
    return "\n".join(out)

Events:
  Warning  BackOff    2m (x26 over 12m)  kubelet  Back-off restarting failed container api"""
print(triage(sample))

Diagnose: every pod restarts during the evening peak

Every evening at about 19:00, all 30 pods of a Java API restart within two minutes, latency spikes and errors last ten minutes. Describe on a restarted pod shows Last State: Terminated, Reason: Error, Exit Code: 137, Restart Count: 1, and no OOMKilled. Container spec: limits: {cpu: "1", memory: 3Gi}, requests: {cpu: 500m, memory: 3Gi}; liveness probe httpGet /health on port 8080, default timing; /health runs SELECT 1 against Postgres and pings Redis. The database team reports a nightly report starting at 18:58 that makes queries slower for a few minutes. Explain the chain of events and fix it.

Answer

Chain: at 18:58 the report slows Postgres. /health runs a query, so its latency rises; the evening peak also pushes the 1-CPU limited JVM into CFS throttling (k8s10), slowing the health handler further. With the default timeoutSeconds: 1 and failureThreshold: 3, three consecutive slow responses within 30 seconds fail liveness on every pod at nearly the same time. The kubelet kills each container: SIGTERM, then SIGKILL after the grace period (exit 137, reason Error — not an OOM). All 30 JVMs restart together, with cold JIT and connection pools, under peak traffic, and their start-up queries hit the already slow database. Ten minutes of errors follow — a cascading failure caused by the health check, exactly the scenario Zalando's post describes.

Fix: make liveness check only the JVM's ability to respond (a trivial endpoint with no dependencies, served off the main request thread pool), with timeoutSeconds: 5 and failureThreshold: 6, or remove it; move the database and Redis checks into readiness so slow dependencies take pods out of rotation instead of killing them (and consider not failing readiness on every dependency blip, or every pod becomes unready together); add a startup probe for JVM warm-up; remove or raise the CPU limit to stop throttling; make the app exit cleanly on SIGTERM so restarts that do happen are graceful. Ask the database team to move the report to a replica. Verify by watching restarts and probe failure events during the next evening peak.
