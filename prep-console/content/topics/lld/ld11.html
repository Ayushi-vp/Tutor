An interviewer asks for tic-tac-toe. Twenty minutes later the candidate has a working program: a 3 by 3 list, a loop alternating X and O, and a function that checks eight lines (three rows, three columns, two diagonals). Then the follow-up arrives, as it always does: "Now let's make it Connect-4." The board is 7 by 6. A move names a column, not a cell, and the piece falls to the bottom. Four in a row wins, along lines that can start anywhere. Almost nothing in the first program survives. The candidate starts rewriting with ten minutes left.

The turn-based game question is not about tic-tac-toe. It asks whether you can see, before you are told, which parts of a problem will change and which will stay the same, and design the boundary between them. Every turn-based grid game shares the same skeleton: players take turns, a move is validated, applied, and checked for a win or a draw, and moves can be undone. What differs is the board's size, how a move becomes a filled cell, and how many in a row wins. Draw that line and Connect-4, Gomoku and tic-tac-toe become three small rule objects plugged into one engine.

The question also has a classic efficiency insight. After a move, only lines through the cell just played can contain a new win, so the engine checks four directions through that cell rather than scanning the board. On tic-tac-toe it makes no visible difference; on a 15 by 15 Gomoku board it is the difference between checking 572 possible lines and walking a few cells in four directions, and once an AI starts searching thousands of positions per move, it decides how far ahead the AI can see.

Finally, the question tests whether your code can be tested. A game that reads moves from the keyboard and prints the board cannot be driven by a test. An engine whose players are injected objects can be driven by scripted moves, random players and a perfect AI, and the AI itself becomes a test: two perfect players at tic-tac-toe must draw.

Candidates who do well here usually say one sentence early: "I expect you will ask for another game, so I will keep the rules separate from the engine." It costs nothing, sets up every follow-up, and shows the interviewer the design is deliberate rather than accidental.

This lesson follows the round: clarify the requirements, separate what varies from what does not, draw the class diagram, write the naive version and watch it fail the follow-up, then build the engine in stages (a parameterised board, rules that resolve moves, localised win detection, the engine with validation and undo, injectable players and a negamax AI), trace a game, test it, and cover edge cases, extensions such as online play and faster AI, complexity, and what it means that Connect-4 is a solved game.

Engine versus game

An engine is the machinery that runs any game of a family; a game is the specific rules. The useful exercise, before writing code, is a two-column table.

Same in every game (engine)Varies by game (rules)
Players take turns in orderBoard dimensions
Reject moves out of turn or after the game endsWhich moves are legal
Apply a move, record it, undo itWhich cell a move fills (free placement or gravity)
Detect a win through the new piece; detect a full boardHow many in a row wins

The left column becomes one Engine class; the right column becomes a small Rules interface. A tic-tac-toe rules object says "3 by 3, place on any empty cell, 3 in a row"; Gomoku says "15 by 15, any empty cell, 5"; Connect-4 says "7 columns by 6 rows, a move is a column and the piece falls, 4". The table is worth drawing on the whiteboard, because it is the design, and it shows the interviewer you anticipated the follow-up.

m,n,k games: the family

Mathematicians already named this family. An m,n,k game is played on an m by n board; players alternately place a piece on an empty cell; the first to get k in a row (horizontally, vertically or diagonally) wins. Tic-tac-toe is (3,3,3) and, with perfect play, a draw. Gomoku on a 15 by 15 board is (15,15,5) and a first-player win, shown by Victor Allis' computer search. Connect-4 is (7,6,4) with one extra rule, gravity: pieces fall to the lowest empty row of the chosen column. It is a first-player win too. So the engine is really an m,n,k engine with a pluggable placement rule.

The localised win check

A win is k pieces of one player in a line. After a move, the only lines that might newly contain such a run are lines through the cell just filled; every other line is exactly as it was after the previous move, when the engine already checked. So the engine looks in four directions through the new cell (left-right, up-down, and the two diagonals), and for each direction counts matching pieces outward on both sides until it hits a different piece, an empty cell or the edge. If the count reaches k, the player has won.

Why testability decides this round

Interviewers often end this round by asking how you would test the engine, and the answer is mostly decided by earlier choices. If the program reads moves with input() and prints the board, a test must fake the keyboard and parse text. If the engine exposes play and undo and players are objects passed in, a test is a list of moves and an assertion. Designing for testability is not an extra step at the end; it is choosing interfaces that let code be driven and observed without a human.

The analogy, and where it breaks

Think of a referee. The referee does not know the rules of every sport; a rulebook tells them what counts as a legal move and a score, and they enforce turn order, record what happened and announce the result. The engine is the referee and the rules object is the rulebook. The analogy breaks at the board: a referee watches a physical field, while the engine owns the board's state and is the only thing allowed to change it, which is what makes its record of moves complete enough to undo.

1 · Clarify the requirements

Ask which games must be supported now and which are likely next; board sizes; whether a move is a cell or something else (a column, a direction); the win condition; how many players; whether undo, replay or saving games is needed; whether players can be computer opponents and how strong; whether there are time limits per move; and whether the game runs locally or online. Each answer moves something into or out of the rules interface.

A typical agreed scope: two players; tic-tac-toe now, Connect-4 and Gomoku as the expected follow-ups; win, draw and illegal-move detection; undo; pluggable players including a computer opponent; no networking. Invariants to state: only the current player may move; every applied move fills exactly one empty cell; the game ends at the first k in a row or a full board; after the game ends no move is accepted; undo restores exactly the previous position and turn.

2 · Entities and responsibilities

Board stores the grid, its dimensions and the count of filled cells, and knows bounds; it knows nothing about winning. Rules is an interface: new_board(), legal_moves(board), resolve(board, move) returning the cell the move fills or raising if illegal, and the win length k. Two implementations: MNK (free placement; tic-tac-toe and Gomoku are instances) and Gravity (Connect-4). Engine owns the board, the player list, the turn counter, the history and the status, and implements play and undo. The free function wins_through does localised win detection. Players implement choose(engine).

What stays out of the interface

It is as important to decide what not to abstract. Pieces need no class hierarchy: a piece is the player's symbol. Moves need no class either: a cell tuple or a column number is enough, and the rules know how to interpret them. Rendering is not the engine's job: a separate function can draw the board for a terminal or a web page. And a separate "WinChecker" interface is unnecessary for m,n,k games, because every game in the family wins the same way, k in a row; it would become worthwhile only for a game with different winning rules, at which point it can be extracted. Naming what you deliberately left out, and the trigger that would make you add it, shows the interviewer judgement rather than pattern enthusiasm.

3 · Stage one: the naive version and the follow-up

board = [[" "] * 3 for _ in range(3)]
LINES = [[(0,0),(0,1),(0,2)], [(1,0),(1,1),(1,2)], [(2,0),(2,1),(2,2)],
         [(0,0),(1,0),(2,0)], [(0,1),(1,1),(2,1)], [(0,2),(1,2),(2,2)],
         [(0,0),(1,1),(2,2)], [(0,2),(1,1),(2,0)]]

def play(player, r, c):
    board[r][c] = player
    return any(all(board[i][j] == player for i, j in line) for line in LINES)

It plays tic-tac-toe, and every line is wrong for Connect-4. The board size is fixed. The eight win lines are enumerated by hand; a 7 by 6 board has 69 possible lines of four and a 15 by 15 board 572 lines of five, which nobody should list. The move is (r, c), but a Connect-4 player picks a column. Nothing checks whose turn it is, whether the cell is empty, or whether the game is already over. There is no undo, and the board is a global, so two games cannot exist at once and no test can start from a clean board.

4 · Stage two: a parameterised board

Board(rows, cols) creates the grid, keeps a count of filled cells so full() is O(1), and provides in_bounds, get, put and clear. put asserts the cell is empty, a cheap guard that catches any rule that resolves a move to an occupied cell. The board deliberately has no notion of players, turns or winning; it could serve chess or Go unchanged.

Choosing the board's representation

A list of lists is the clearest representation and the one to write first: grid[r][c] reads like the problem. Alternatives have their place. A dictionary from cell to piece suits huge sparse boards (an infinite Gomoku variant), since empty cells cost nothing. A flat list indexed by r * cols + c is slightly faster and makes neighbours simple arithmetic. Bitboards, discussed in the deep section, are fastest for search. Because the engine only talks to the board through a few methods, any of these can replace the list of lists later without touching rules, players or tests, which is the practical payoff of keeping the board behind a small interface.

5 · Stage three: rules that resolve moves

MNK(m, n, k) treats a move as a (row, col) pair. legal_moves lists empty cells; resolve checks the cell is on the board and empty and returns it, or raises IllegalMove. Tic-tac-toe is MNK(3, 3, 3) and Gomoku MNK(15, 15, 5): no new code. Gravity(rows=6, cols=7, k=4) treats a move as a column number. legal_moves lists columns whose top cell is empty; resolve checks the column is valid and not full, then returns the lowest empty row in it. Because both rules return the cell a move fills, the engine never needs to know which kind of game it is running. That single return value is the most important design decision in the lesson.

6 · Stage four: localised win detection

wins_through(board, cell, k) reads the piece at the cell and, for each of the four directions (0, 1), (1, 0), (1, 1) and (1, −1), counts consecutive matching pieces outward in both the positive and negative direction, starting from 1 for the cell itself and stopping at the edge, an empty cell or another player's piece, or once the run reaches k. If any direction reaches k, it is a win. The worst case reads 4 × 2 × (k − 1) cells: 16 for tic-tac-toe, 24 for Connect-4, 32 for Gomoku. A full scan of a 15 by 15 board, by contrast, examines 572 lines of five, 2,860 cell reads. The solution keeps the slow full scan, wins_anywhere, as a test oracle: in 200 random games across three game types, the localised check and the full scan always agree.

7 · Stage five: the engine

Engine.play(player, move) runs a fixed sequence. If the game is over, raise GameOver. If player is not the current player, raise NotYourTurn. Ask the rules to resolve the move to a cell, which raises IllegalMove if it is not legal. Put the piece, record (player, move, cell) in the history, then check for a win through the cell; if none, check for a full board (a draw); if neither, advance the turn. The current player is players[turn % len(players)], so more than two players need no change. undo() pops the last record, clears its cell, and restores the turn and status: if the undone move had ended the game, the game is in progress again and the turn stays with the player who made it; otherwise the turn steps back.

8 · Stage six: undo as recorded commands

The history is an instance of the Command pattern: each move is recorded as an object holding everything needed to reverse it, here the player, the move as given, and the resolved cell. Undo is O(1) whatever the board size, unlike restoring a full snapshot of the board. The same list gives replay (start a new engine and play the recorded moves), a game record to save or send over a network, and, as the next stage shows, the cheap play and undo that game-tree search needs. Storing the resolved cell, not just the move, matters for Connect-4: to undo "column 3" the engine must know which row the piece landed in.

9 · Stage seven: players and a perfect AI

A player is anything with choose(engine) that returns a move. RandomPlayer picks uniformly from the legal moves with a seeded generator, which is what the property test uses. NegamaxPlayer plays perfectly in small games. Negamax is minimax for two-player zero-sum games: the value of a position for the player to move is the maximum over legal moves of the negated value of the resulting position for the opponent. It searches by calling play, recursing and calling undo, so it uses exactly the engine's rules and win detection. A won position scores −(1000 + remaining depth) for the player to move (the opponent just won), so the AI prefers quicker wins and slower losses; a draw scores 0.

Alpha-beta pruning makes this practical. The search carries a window: alpha, the score the player to move is already guaranteed elsewhere, and beta, the score the opponent is already guaranteed. As soon as one move proves the position at least as good as beta, the opponent would never let the game reach it, so the remaining moves are skipped. Two perfect players at tic-tac-toe draw, and the solution's test confirms it while counting about 42,500 positions searched across the whole game, far fewer than the 255,168 possible games. For Connect-4 a full search is out of reach, so the player takes a depth limit; ordering moves centre-first improves pruning. The Connect-4 test sets up a bottom-row threat and checks that a depth-3 search blocks it as the defender and takes it as the attacker.

10 · A game, traced

The Connect-4 test's diagonal: R and Y alternate dropping pieces in columns 0, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 2, 3, 3, 6, 3, R first. Resolving with gravity (row 5 is the bottom), R's six pieces land at (5,0), (4,1), (4,2), (3,2), (3,3) and finally (2,3); Y's at (5,1), (5,2), (5,3), (4,3) and (5,6). Follow the last move. R's piece in column 3 lands on row 2 because rows 5, 4 and 3 of that column are taken. wins_through checks the cell (2,3). Horizontally, both neighbours in row 2 are empty: a run of 1. Vertically, the cell below is R and the one below that is Y: a run of 2. On the falling diagonal, both neighbours are empty: 1. On the rising diagonal, down-left from (2,3) are (3,2), (4,1) and (5,0), all R: 1 + 3 = 4, so R wins and the walk stops. The engine sets the status to WIN and records R as the winner; any further move raises GameOver. The whole decision read about a dozen cells.

11 · Testing the engine

Four groups of tests cover it. Scripted outcomes: a tic-tac-toe diagonal win, a full-board draw, a Connect-4 stack landing on rows 5, 4, 3, 2, a rising-diagonal win, a Gomoku five in a row. Errors: out-of-turn, occupied cell, off-board, full column and post-game moves each raise their own exception. Undo: undoing a winning Gomoku move restores the empty cell, the in-progress status and the same player to move, and a different move then wins too. Properties and oracles: 200 random games where the localised detector must agree with the full-board scan, and two perfect players at tic-tac-toe, who must draw. The perfect-play test is a strong check of the whole engine, because any bug in legality, win detection or undo changes the result of a search that visits tens of thousands of positions.

12 · Talking through the design

A good walkthrough: "I separated what varies from what doesn't. The engine owns turns, validation, applying moves, win and draw detection and undo; the rules supply the board, the legal moves, the win length, and how a move resolves to a cell, which is how one engine runs both free placement and Connect-4's gravity. Win detection only looks through the last cell, so it is O(k) on any board size. History stores the resolved cell, so undo is constant time and replay is free. Players are injected, which makes the engine testable with scripts and random players, and a negamax player with alpha-beta both adds an AI and acts as a deep test: perfect tic-tac-toe must draw."

13 · The full solution

The file below contains the board, two rules families, localised and full-scan win detection, the engine with undo, random and negamax players, and six tests. Python 3.12, standard library only; it runs in about a second, most of it the perfect tic-tac-toe search.

"""A turn-based game engine: one engine, pluggable rules (tic-tac-toe, Gomoku, Connect-4 as
m,n,k games with or without gravity), localised win detection, undo by move history,
injectable players including a negamax AI. Python 3.12, standard library only.
Run: python games.py"""
from __future__ import annotations

import random
from dataclasses import dataclass
from enum import Enum
from typing import Protocol

Cell = tuple[int, int]                                  # (row, col); row 0 is the top
DIRECTIONS = ((0, 1), (1, 0), (1, 1), (1, -1))          # row, column, two diagonals


class Board:
    def __init__(self, rows: int, cols: int) -> None:
        self.rows, self.cols = rows, cols
        self.grid: list[list[str | None]] = [[None] * cols for _ in range(rows)]
        self.filled = 0

    def in_bounds(self, r: int, c: int) -> bool:
        return 0 <= r < self.rows and 0 <= c < self.cols

    def get(self, cell: Cell) -> str | None:
        return self.grid[cell[0]][cell[1]]

    def put(self, cell: Cell, piece: str) -> None:
        assert self.grid[cell[0]][cell[1]] is None
        self.grid[cell[0]][cell[1]] = piece
        self.filled += 1

    def clear(self, cell: Cell) -> None:
        self.grid[cell[0]][cell[1]] = None
        self.filled -= 1

    def full(self) -> bool:
        return self.filled == self.rows * self.cols


def wins_through(board: Board, cell: Cell, k: int) -> bool:
    """Localised check: only lines through the cell just played can have changed. O(k)."""
    piece = board.get(cell)
    for dr, dc in DIRECTIONS:
        run = 1
        for sign in (1, -1):
            r, c = cell[0] + sign * dr, cell[1] + sign * dc
            while board.in_bounds(r, c) and board.grid[r][c] == piece and run < k:
                run += 1
                r, c = r + sign * dr, c + sign * dc
        if run >= k:
            return True
    return False


def wins_anywhere(board: Board, k: int) -> str | None:
    """Naive full scan, kept as a test oracle. O(rows * cols * k)."""
    for r in range(board.rows):
        for c in range(board.cols):
            p = board.grid[r][c]
            if p is None:
                continue
            for dr, dc in DIRECTIONS:
                if all(board.in_bounds(r + i * dr, c + i * dc) and board.grid[r + i * dr][c + i * dc] == p
                       for i in range(k)):
                    return p
    return None


# ------------------------------------------------------------------ rules (the part that varies)
class Rules(Protocol):
    name: str
    k: int

    def new_board(self) -> Board: ...
    def legal_moves(self, board: Board) -> list: ...
    def resolve(self, board: Board, move) -> Cell: ...   # which cell does this move fill?


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class MNK:
    """Place anywhere empty: tic-tac-toe is MNK(3,3,3), Gomoku MNK(15,15,5). Move = (row, col)."""
    m: int
    n: int
    k: int
    name: str = "mnk"

    def new_board(self) -> Board:
        return Board(self.m, self.n)

    def legal_moves(self, board: Board) -> list[Cell]:
        return [(r, c) for r in range(board.rows) for c in range(board.cols) if board.grid[r][c] is None]

    def resolve(self, board: Board, move: Cell) -> Cell:
        r, c = move
        if not board.in_bounds(r, c) or board.grid[r][c] is not None:
            raise IllegalMove(f"{move} is not an empty cell")
        return move


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Gravity:
    """Connect-4 style: a move names a column and the piece falls to the lowest empty row."""
    rows: int = 6
    cols: int = 7
    k: int = 4
    name: str = "connect4"

    def new_board(self) -> Board:
        return Board(self.rows, self.cols)

    def legal_moves(self, board: Board) -> list[int]:
        return [c for c in range(board.cols) if board.grid[0][c] is None]

    def resolve(self, board: Board, col: int) -> Cell:
        if not 0 <= col < board.cols or board.grid[0][col] is not None:
            raise IllegalMove(f"column {col} is full or off the board")
        r = max(r for r in range(board.rows) if board.grid[r][col] is None)
        return (r, col)


# ------------------------------------------------------------------ engine (the part that does not)
class IllegalMove(Exception): ...
class NotYourTurn(Exception): ...
class GameOver(Exception): ...


class Status(Enum):
    IN_PROGRESS = "in progress"
    WIN = "win"
    DRAW = "draw"


class Engine:
    def __init__(self, rules: Rules, players: list[str]) -> None:
        if len(players) < 2:
            raise ValueError("need at least two players")
        self.rules, self.players = rules, players
        self.board = rules.new_board()
        self.turn = 0
        self.history: list[tuple[str, object, Cell]] = []
        self.status, self.winner = Status.IN_PROGRESS, None

    @property
    def current(self) -> str:
        return self.players[self.turn % len(self.players)]

    def play(self, player: str, move) -> Status:
        if self.status is not Status.IN_PROGRESS:
            raise GameOver(self.status.value)
        if player != self.current:
            raise NotYourTurn(f"it is {self.current}'s turn")
        cell = self.rules.resolve(self.board, move)       # rules decide legality and the cell
        self.board.put(cell, player)
        self.history.append((player, move, cell))
        if wins_through(self.board, cell, self.rules.k):
            self.status, self.winner = Status.WIN, player
        elif self.board.full():
            self.status = Status.DRAW
        else:
            self.turn += 1
        return self.status

    def undo(self) -> None:
        if not self.history:
            raise IllegalMove("nothing to undo")
        player, _, cell = self.history.pop()
        self.board.clear(cell)
        if self.status is Status.IN_PROGRESS:
            self.turn -= 1
        self.status, self.winner = Status.IN_PROGRESS, None


# ------------------------------------------------------------------ players (injectable)
class RandomPlayer:
    def __init__(self, seed: int) -> None:
        self.rng = random.Random(seed)

    def choose(self, e: Engine):
        return self.rng.choice(e.rules.legal_moves(e.board))


class NegamaxPlayer:
    """Perfect play for small games: negamax with alpha-beta, using play/undo on the engine."""

    def __init__(self, depth: int = 99) -> None:
        self.depth, self.nodes = depth, 0

    def _search(self, e: Engine, depth: int, alpha: int, beta: int) -> int:
        self.nodes += 1
        if e.status is Status.WIN:
            return -(1000 + depth)                        # the player who just moved won
        if e.status is Status.DRAW or depth == 0:
            return 0
        best = -10_000
        for mv in self._ordered(e):
            e.play(e.current, mv)
            score = -self._search(e, depth - 1, -beta, -alpha)
            e.undo()
            best = max(best, score)
            alpha = max(alpha, score)
            if alpha >= beta:
                break                                     # opponent will never allow this line
        return best

    def _ordered(self, e: Engine) -> list:
        moves = e.rules.legal_moves(e.board)
        if isinstance(e.rules, Gravity):                  # centre columns first: better pruning
            moves.sort(key=lambda c: abs(c - e.board.cols // 2))
        return moves

    def choose(self, e: Engine):
        best_mv, best = None, -10_001
        for mv in self._ordered(e):
            e.play(e.current, mv)
            score = -self._search(e, self.depth - 1, -10_000, 10_000)
            e.undo()
            if score > best:
                best_mv, best = mv, score
        return best_mv


def run(rules: Rules, agents: dict[str, object], max_moves: int = 1_000) -> Engine:
    e = Engine(rules, list(agents))
    while e.status is Status.IN_PROGRESS and len(e.history) < max_moves:
        e.play(e.current, agents[e.current].choose(e))
    return e


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- tests
def test_tictactoe_win_draw_and_illegal() -> None:
    e = Engine(MNK(3, 3, 3), ["X", "O"])
    for p, mv in [("X", (0, 0)), ("O", (1, 0)), ("X", (1, 1)), ("O", (2, 0))]:
        e.play(p, mv)
    for bad, exc in [(("O", (2, 2)), NotYourTurn), (("X", (1, 1)), IllegalMove), (("X", (3, 0)), IllegalMove)]:
        try:
            e.play(*bad)
        except exc:
            pass
        else:
            raise AssertionError(f"{bad} accepted")
    assert e.play("X", (2, 2)) is Status.WIN and e.winner == "X"     # diagonal
    try:
        e.play("O", (0, 1))
    except GameOver:
        pass
    d = Engine(MNK(3, 3, 3), ["X", "O"])
    for mv in [(0, 0), (0, 1), (0, 2), (1, 1), (1, 0), (1, 2), (2, 1), (2, 0), (2, 2)]:
        d.play(d.current, mv)
    assert d.status is Status.DRAW


def test_connect4_gravity_and_wins() -> None:
    e = Engine(Gravity(), ["R", "Y"])
    for col in (3, 3, 3, 3):
        e.play(e.current, col)
    assert [cell for *_, cell in e.history] == [(5, 3), (4, 3), (3, 3), (2, 3)]   # stacks up
    e2 = Engine(Gravity(), ["R", "Y"])
    for col in (0, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 2, 3, 3, 6, 3):                # R builds a rising diagonal
        e2.play(e2.current, col)
    assert e2.status is Status.WIN and e2.winner == "R"
    e3 = Engine(Gravity(rows=2, cols=2, k=4), ["R", "Y"])
    for col in (0, 0):
        e3.play(e3.current, col)
    try:
        e3.play(e3.current, 0)
    except IllegalMove:
        pass


def test_gomoku_five_in_a_row_and_undo() -> None:
    e = Engine(MNK(15, 15, 5), ["B", "W"])
    for i in range(4):
        e.play("B", (7, 3 + i))
        e.play("W", (0, i))
    e.play("B", (7, 7))
    assert e.status is Status.WIN
    e.undo()
    assert e.status is Status.IN_PROGRESS and e.current == "B" and e.board.get((7, 7)) is None
    e.play("B", (7, 2))
    assert e.winner == "B"


def test_localised_check_matches_full_scan() -> None:
    for seed in range(200):
        rules = [MNK(3, 3, 3), MNK(6, 6, 4), Gravity()][seed % 3]
        e = run(rules, {"A": RandomPlayer(seed), "B": RandomPlayer(seed + 1)})
        full = wins_anywhere(e.board, rules.k)
        assert (e.winner == full) or (e.status is Status.DRAW and full is None), (seed, e.winner, full)

14 · Edge cases

A win on the last empty cell: the win check runs before the full-board check, so it is a win, not a draw. k larger than both dimensions: no one can win; every game is a draw, which the rules could reject at construction. Undo with no history: raises. Undo after a win: restores play with the same player to move. Moves of the wrong type (a column given to tic-tac-toe): the rules' resolve rejects them. Three or more players: the turn rotation handles them; the negamax player does not, because it assumes two players. Concurrent requests in an online version: serialise moves per game, since play is a check-then-act sequence.

15 · Extensions the interviewer will ask for

Online multiplayer: the engine runs on the server as the authority; clients send moves and receive the resulting state. Persistence and replay: save the move history; rebuild a game by replaying it. Timers: a clock per player, with a loss on timeout, as a wrapper around play. Different win conditions (exactly five, not six, in Gomoku's stricter variants): a win-rule hook in the rules interface. Faster AI: bitboards, transposition tables, iterative deepening, an evaluation function for depth-limited search. Hex boards or other topologies: the board's neighbour directions become part of the rules.

16 · Complexity

play is O(k) for win detection plus O(1) bookkeeping (Gravity's resolve is O(rows) as written, O(1) with a per-column height counter). undo is O(1). legal_moves is O(cells) for MNK and O(columns) for Gravity. Memory is O(cells + moves). A full negamax search is exponential in the number of remaining moves; alpha-beta with good ordering can roughly halve the effective depth exponent in the best case, which is why it is the standard method. For perspective: tic-tac-toe has 255,168 possible games, the solution searched about 42,500 positions to play one perfectly, and Connect-4 has over four trillion positions, so the same code that is instant on one is hopeless on the other without the optimisations in the deep section.

Solved games and what "perfect play" means

A game is solved when its outcome under perfect play from the start is known. Tic-tac-toe, the (3,3,3) game, is a draw, which the solution's search confirms in a fraction of a second. Connect-4 is a first-player win: James Dow Allen and Victor Allis solved it independently in October 1988, Allis with a knowledge-based approach built on a set of strategic rules, and later work confirmed it by brute-force search with negamax, alpha-beta pruning, move ordering and transposition tables. The classic 7 by 6 board has 4,531,985,219,092 positions with 0 to 42 pieces, which is why plain search without those optimisations is hopeless. Gomoku on a 15 by 15 board is also a first-player win, shown by Allis' computer search. For an interview the point is calibration: perfect search is fine for tic-tac-toe, needs serious engineering for Connect-4, and is out of reach for most larger games, where engines use depth limits and evaluation functions.

Why the first player cannot lose in m,n,k games

A neat argument shows that in any m,n,k game the second player can never have a winning strategy. Suppose they did. The first player makes an arbitrary first move and then pretends to be the second player, following that winning strategy. An extra piece on the board can never hurt in these games, and if the strategy ever calls for the cell already taken, the first player just plays anywhere else. So the first player would win, contradicting the assumption. This strategy-stealing argument proves the outcome is a first-player win or a draw, without saying which or how. It is a lovely example of reasoning about a design (here, a game's rules) at a level above any algorithm.

Bitboards: representation as performance

Serious Connect-4 solvers store the board as two integers, one per player, with one bit per cell (the 7 by 6 board fits in 49 bits with a sentinel row per column). Placing a piece is setting a bit; checking for four in a row in one direction is a shift and an AND: m = b & (b >> d); won = m & (m >> 2*d) for the direction's bit offset d. Win detection for the whole board becomes a handful of machine instructions, faster even than the localised walk. The lesson for design is that representation and algorithm are linked: the same engine interface could sit on a bitboard implementation of Board, chosen when performance demands it, without the rules or players changing.

Transposition tables and iterative deepening

Different move orders often reach the same position: X at a then O at b then X at c equals X at c then O at b then X at a. A transposition table caches the score of each position already searched, keyed by a hash of the board, so repeated positions cost a lookup. Iterative deepening searches to depth 1, then 2, then 3, and so on until time runs out, always keeping the best move from the last completed depth; the shallow searches are cheap and their results order moves for the deeper ones, improving pruning. Both are standard in game engines and worth naming when asked how to make the AI stronger.

Evaluation functions for depth-limited search

When the search cannot reach the end of the game, it stops at a depth limit and must score the position it reached without knowing the result. An evaluation function estimates who is better: in Connect-4, count each player's open lines (windows of four cells containing only their pieces and empties), weighting three-in-a-window much more than two, and add a small bonus for pieces in the centre columns, which take part in more lines. The search's strength is then a product of depth and evaluation quality: a deeper search with a crude evaluation and a shallower one with a good evaluation can play equally well. Difficulty levels in consumer games are usually implemented by reducing depth or adding randomness to move choice, not by changing the engine.

Immutable states versus play and undo

An alternative design makes the game state immutable: apply(state, move) returns a new state. That simplifies reasoning (no undo bugs, states can be shared between threads and cached), and in functional languages it is the norm. The cost is copying: each move allocates a new board, which for search means millions of allocations. Mutable play and undo, as in the solution, is faster and is what performance-critical engines use, at the price of carefully keeping play and undo symmetric. The property test and the perfect-play test exist largely to catch asymmetries.

How game engines fail in production

Online game servers fail mostly at the boundary between the engine and the network. Two moves for the same game arrive at once (a double click, or a client retry) and both pass the turn check before either updates the turn: the same check-then-act race as in the parking lot, fixed by processing each game's moves one at a time, for example with a per-game lock or a per-game queue. A client's view drifts from the server's after a dropped message; the fix is for the server to be the single authority and send full state or sequence-numbered moves so clients can detect gaps. And clients cheat by sending moves the user interface would never allow, which is why every move must be validated by the server's engine, never trusted from the client.

Inside the engine, the classic bugs are asymmetric undo (a status or turn not restored), a win missed at a board edge (bounds checks), and a draw reported when the last move also won (checks in the wrong order). Each has a test in this lesson's suite.

AI opponents in practice

A perfect opponent is no fun for most players, and a deterministic one can be memorised: once a player finds a winning line against it, they can repeat it forever. Production AI opponents therefore add controlled randomness (choosing among moves within a small margin of the best score), cap search depth or time per difficulty level, and run the search off the request thread so a slow search cannot block other games. Time limits must be enforced by the search itself, checking a clock between iterations of iterative deepening, because a depth that is instant on an empty board can explode in a complex middle game.

What to measure

For an online game: move latency from client to acknowledgement, rejected moves by reason (a spike in "not your turn" usually means a client bug or a retry storm), games abandoned mid-play, and for AI opponents the time per move and search depth reached. For AI quality, play it against itself and against reference engines and track the win rate across releases.

Operations

Store every game as its move history, which is compact and lets support staff replay disputed games exactly. Version the rules: a change to a rule (a new opening restriction in Gomoku, for example) must not alter how old recorded games replay, so each game stores the rules version it was played under. And keep the engine deterministic, with no hidden randomness or clock reads, so a replay always produces the same result.

The real case: Connect-4 as a solved m,n,k game

The standard references describe Connect-4 as an m,n,k game, (7,6,4), with restricted piece placement, which is exactly the structure of this lesson's design: an m,n,k engine with a gravity placement rule. They record that the game is solved, with the first player able to always win by playing the right moves, first by James Dow Allen on 1 October 1988 and independently by Victor Allis on 16 October 1988, and that it has since been solved by brute force, beginning with John Tromp's work, using minimax or negamax with alpha-beta pruning, move ordering and transposition tables. The number of positions, 4,531,985,219,092 for boards with 0 to 42 pieces, explains why those optimisations are not optional. For the m,n,k family, the references give tic-tac-toe as a draw and (15,15,5) as a first-player win by Allis' computer search. Taken together they show the arc of this question in miniature: one engine shape, three games, and an AI whose feasibility depends on the size of the game tree and on exactly the engineering choices (cheap play and undo, good ordering, caching) discussed here.

Goal: run the engine, then break, measure and extend it. Python 3.12, standard library only.

  1. Save the full solution as games.py and run it. Six tests print ok, including perfect tic-tac-toe (a draw) and the localised-versus-full-scan property test over 200 random games.
  2. Break it. In wins_through, remove the negative direction (loop over (1,) instead of (1, -1)). Run the property test: it fails on a game where the winning piece was placed in the middle of its line.
  3. Break it again. Make undo forget to restore the status. The Gomoku test fails, and the negamax search returns nonsense, because search depends on symmetric play and undo.
  4. Measure it. Count positions searched by perfect tic-tac-toe with and without alpha-beta (remove the break), and with Connect-4 at depths 4, 6 and 8 with and without centre-first ordering.
  5. Extend it. Add a per-column height array to Gravity so resolve is O(1), and a transposition table (a dict from a board key to a score) in NegamaxPlayer.
  6. Extend it again. Add a game record: save the history as JSON and write replay(rules, record) that rebuilds the final position. Check that replaying every random game from the property test reproduces its winner.

Keep the seeds fixed so runs are comparable. For each change, predict the effect on node counts or test results first, then run it. The perfect-play test is your safety net: if two perfect tic-tac-toe players stop drawing, something in play, undo or win detection is wrong, even if every small test still passes.

Work it out

You have a working tic-tac-toe. The interviewer says: "Now support Connect-4, and also Gomoku on a 15 by 15 board where you need five in a row."

(a) What breaks in a typical tic-tac-toe implementation? (b) Which abstractions fix it? (c) What does win detection cost per move under a full scan and under the localised check, for each game?

Answer

(a) What breaks. Four things. The board is hard-coded 3 by 3, with literal 3s in bounds checks. The win lines are enumerated: eight for tic-tac-toe, but 69 lines of four on a 7 by 6 board and 572 lines of five on a 15 by 15 board. The move is a cell, but a Connect-4 move is a column with gravity choosing the row. And legality differs: "is the cell empty?" versus "is the column full?".

(b) The abstractions. Three, and no more. A board with dimensions as parameters. A rules interface whose resolve(board, move) returns the cell a move fills (or raises), plus the legal moves and the win length k; tic-tac-toe and Gomoku are one free-placement class with different parameters, Connect-4 a gravity class. And direction-vector win detection through the last cell with a configurable k. The engine then handles turns, validation, history and outcomes for all three without change. Factories, a piece class hierarchy or a separate engine per game would be over-engineering.

(c) Cost per move. A full scan checks every possible line of k:

GameLines of kFull-scan cell readsLocalised worst case 4 × 2 × (k − 1)
Tic-tac-toe (3,3,3)82416
Connect-4 (7,6,4)6927624
Gomoku (15,15,5)5722,86032

For Gomoku: horizontal lines 15 × 11 = 165, vertical 165, and each diagonal direction 11 × 11 = 121, total 572; times 5 cells is 2,860 reads, against at most 32 for the localised walk. For a human-speed game both are instant. It matters once an AI searches: a search visiting a million positions does 2.86 billion reads with the full scan and at most 32 million with the localised check, which is the difference between seeing a few moves ahead and many. The principle generalises beyond games: after a change, recompute only what the change can affect.
