A product manager in London sets up a weekly sync with an engineer in New York for 9:00 New York time. For six months it works. Then one week in March the New York engineer joins an empty call: the meeting has moved an hour in their calendar. The United States changed its clocks for daylight saving two weeks before the United Kingdom did, and the scheduler had stored the recurrence as "every 7 days at 13:00 UTC" instead of "every Tuesday at 9:00 New York time". In the same quarter a room-booking bug let two teams book the same room for the same hour, because the system checked the room, then checked the attendees, and another booking slipped in between.

Calendars look like simple CRUD, create, read, update, delete, over a list of events. They are not, for two reasons that interviewers like to probe. First, time is genuinely hard: intervals need a precise definition of overlap, instants must be compared in one reference (UTC) while rules are defined in local time, and local time jumps twice a year by different amounts on different dates in different places. Second, booking is a concurrency problem across several resources: a meeting needs every attendee and a room to be free at once, and two requests can race for any of them.

The round also contains a neat algorithm. "Find a 30-minute slot this week when Ana, Ben and Cy are all free and within their working hours" sounds like it needs trying every possible start time. It does not: merge everyone's busy time into one sorted list and sweep it for gaps, a technique that works for any number of people and any slot granularity.

As a machine-coding round, the scheduler rewards a candidate who says, early, "I'll use half-open intervals in UTC, and expand recurrence in the event's own time zone", and then backs it with code and tests. It also connects to the concurrency cases that end this family: booking several calendars atomically needs several locks, and several locks need an ordering rule to avoid deadlock.

The payoff for getting it right is large and invisible: when a calendar works, nobody thinks about it. When it fails, thousands of people miss meetings at once, which is why calendar teams invest heavily in time-zone tests and in matching the standard exactly.

This lesson follows the round: clarify the requirements, model intervals and calendars, draw the classes, write a naive version and list its failures, then build the design in stages (half-open intervals and the overlap test, sorted busy lists with binary search, all-or-nothing booking with ordered locks, the free-slot sweep with working hours, recurrence in local time), trace and test it (including RFC 5545's own example across a daylight-saving change), and cover edge cases, extensions, complexity, and the iCalendar standard every calendar product implements.

Intervals, precisely

A meeting occupies an interval of time. The convention that avoids almost every off-by-one error is the half-open interval [start, end): it includes its start and excludes its end. A meeting from 9:00 to 10:00 covers every instant from 9:00 up to, but not including, 10:00. Two half-open intervals overlap exactly when each starts before the other ends: a.start < b.end and b.start < a.end. A 9:00-10:00 meeting and a 10:00-11:00 meeting do not overlap, which matches what people expect of back-to-back meetings, and iCalendar defines an event's end the same way (DTEND is non-inclusive).

Instants versus rules

Calendars deal with two kinds of time. An instant is a point on the global timeline: "the meeting starts at 13:00 UTC on 6 October". Instants are stored and compared in UTC, Coordinated Universal Time, which never jumps, so subtraction and ordering are always correct. A rule is defined in someone's local time: "every Tuesday at 9:00 New York time", "I work 9:00 to 17:00 London time". Local time belongs to a time zone, a set of rules giving its offset from UTC on each date, including when daylight saving time (DST) starts and ends. The correct pattern is to interpret rules in their own zone, day by day, and convert the resulting instants to UTC. Python represents this with timezone-aware datetimes; the time zone rules normally come from the IANA time zone database through zoneinfo.

Calendars as sorted lists of busy time

For conflict checking, a person's or room's calendar is just a list of busy intervals, kept sorted by start time and with no two overlapping (two events cannot both occupy a single person; tentative double-bookings are a separate feature). Sorted order makes the key queries fast with binary search. And free time is never stored: it is derived on demand from busy time, working hours and the window being searched, the same "store facts, derive views" principle as the expense ledger.

Why calendars are harder than they look

Several properties combine to make calendars tricky. Time is not uniform: local days are sometimes 23 or 25 hours long. Time zone rules are political and change. Recurrence is a small language of its own, with frequency, interval, weekday selections, counts, end dates and exceptions. Several people and rooms must be free simultaneously, and requests race. And everything is visible to users, who notice immediately when a meeting moves by an hour. A good design isolates each difficulty: the interval type owns overlap semantics, the time zone owns offsets, the expander owns recurrence, the calendar owns conflicts, and the scheduler owns atomicity across calendars.

The analogy, and where it breaks

Think of a shared wall planner with a column per person and per room, time running down the page. Booking a meeting means finding a row where every relevant column is empty and writing the meeting into all of them at once. Finding a slot means laying a ruler across the columns and sliding it down until every column is clear for long enough. The analogy breaks in two places: a wall planner has one time zone printed on it, while each person's working hours and recurring meetings live in their own zone; and with a physical planner only one person writes at a time, while a scheduling service handles many bookings concurrently and must stop two of them writing into the same empty cell.

1 · Clarify the requirements

Ask what is being scheduled (meetings with attendees, rooms with capacity, both), whether attendees can be double-booked or tentative, whether users are in different time zones, whether working hours constrain suggestions, what recurrence patterns are needed (daily, weekly on certain days, monthly, with exceptions), whether invitations need accept or decline, how many users and rooms there are, and whether many bookings happen concurrently.

A typical agreed scope: people and rooms each have a calendar; a meeting has a title, attendees, an optional room and a time interval; no double-booking of any person or room; users have time zones and working hours; find common free slots of a given length in a window; weekly recurrence with count or until, by-day and excluded dates; concurrent booking requests. Invariants: no calendar ever contains two overlapping intervals; a booking either appears in every involved calendar or in none; every stored instant is timezone-aware and compared in UTC.

2 · Entities and responsibilities

Interval is a frozen, ordered value: aware start and end with start before end, and an overlaps method. Calendar owns one person's or room's busy list (tuples of start, end and event id, sorted) and a lock, with conflicts(interval) and add. Scheduler owns all calendars (rooms are calendars named room:Orion), books meetings across several calendars, and finds free slots. expand_weekly turns a recurrence rule into intervals. A time zone object supplies offset and daylight-saving rules; in production that is zoneinfo.ZoneInfo("America/New_York").

3 · Stage one: a naive version

meetings = []                                      # (person, start, end), local naive datetimes

def is_free(person, start, end):
    return all(not (s <= start <= e or s <= end <= e)
               for p, s, e in meetings if p == person)

def book(people, start, end):
    for p in people:
        if not is_free(p, start, end):
            return False
    for p in people:
        meetings.append((p, start, end))
    return True

Count the problems. The overlap test uses closed intervals, so back-to-back meetings conflict, and it misses the case where the new meeting completely contains an existing one (start before s and end after e). It scans every meeting of every person for every check. The datetimes are naive local times, so people in different zones cannot be compared, and a meeting that crosses a daylight-saving change has the wrong length. The check and the write are separate steps without a lock, so two concurrent bookings can both pass the check. There are no rooms, no working hours, no way to find a slot, and recurrence would have to be written as hundreds of separate rows.

4 · Stage two: intervals and the overlap test

Interval is a frozen dataclass with order=True, so intervals sort by start then end. Its constructor rejects naive datetimes and non-positive lengths, failing fast on the two mistakes that cause most calendar bugs. overlaps is the half-open test. The test checks three intervals: 9:00-10:00 and 10:00-11:00 do not overlap; 9:30-10:30 overlaps both. Because the overlap test is symmetric and handles containment, there is no special case for a new meeting that swallows an existing one.

5 · Stage three: conflict detection with binary search

A calendar's busy list is sorted by start, and the invariant is that no two entries overlap. To check a new interval, bisect_left finds the first entry whose start is at or after the new start. Only two kinds of entries can conflict: the one just before that position, if it ends after the new start (it began earlier and runs into us), and the entries from that position onward that begin before the new end. Because entries do not overlap each other, the scan stops at the first entry starting at or after the new end. The cost is O(log n) for the search plus O(k) for k conflicts, compared with O(n) for scanning everything. Insertion keeps the list sorted with insort, which is O(n) for the list shift in the worst case but very fast in practice; a balanced tree or interval tree makes it O(log n) when calendars are large.

6 · Stage four: all-or-nothing booking with ordered locks

Scheduler.book collects every calendar involved, the attendees and the room, sorts their names, and acquires their locks in that order. While holding all of them it computes conflicts in every calendar. If any calendar has a conflict, it raises Conflict listing them and writes nothing. Otherwise it generates one event id and inserts the interval into every calendar, then releases the locks in reverse order. Holding all the locks across the check and the write turns a check-then-act race into one atomic step across several resources, so a booking appears everywhere or nowhere.

The sorting is not decoration. Suppose booking X needs Ana and Ben and booking Y needs Ben and Ana. Without a rule, X might lock Ana and Y lock Ben; then X waits for Ben and Y waits for Ana, forever. This is a deadlock, a cycle of threads each waiting for a lock another holds. If every booking acquires locks in sorted name order, both X and Y try Ana first; one gets it, the other waits, and the first proceeds to Ben and finishes. A cycle cannot form because every thread climbs the same ladder in the same direction. The solution's concurrency test starts twelve threads at once, each booking a different pair from four people plus the same room for the same hour, in rotating orders; it asserts that no thread hangs and that exactly one booking won. Deadlock and lock ordering are the subject of the last case in this family; here they appear in their most practical form.

7 · Stage five: finding common free slots

free_slots(attendees, window, duration, hours) answers "when can these people meet for this long, within this window, inside everyone's working hours?" It builds, for each attendee, the list of their busy intervals overlapping the window, and adds, for each attendee, the time outside their working hours as busy. Working hours are local, so for every day in the window the code builds that day's start and end of work in the person's own time zone and converts both to UTC; days outside work hours become two busy blocks, midnight to start of work and end of work to midnight. Treating "not working" as "busy" means one uniform algorithm handles both constraints.

All these sorted lists are merged with heapq.merge, which merges k sorted iterables lazily in O(n log k). A single pass coalesces overlapping or touching busy intervals into maximal blocks. A second pass, the sweep, walks the blocks keeping a cursor at the end of the last busy block; every gap between the cursor and the next block's start that is at least the requested duration is a free slot, clipped to the window.

8 · Stage six: recurrence in local time

expand_weekly(dtstart, length, count, until, byday, exdates) implements the weekly subset of an iCalendar recurrence rule. It refuses to expand a rule with neither a count nor an until date, because an infinite series must be expanded only for a bounded window. Starting from the Monday of the week containing dtstart, it walks week by week; for each selected weekday it builds the occurrence as a local datetime with dtstart's wall-clock time and time zone, skips days before dtstart, stops at until, counts the occurrence (excluded dates still count toward COUNT, matching the standard's definition of the recurrence set before exclusions), and, unless the date is excluded, converts it to UTC and emits it. Because each occurrence is built in local time and converted individually, the UTC offset is computed for that day, which is what keeps 9:00 at 9:00 across daylight saving.

The first test replays RFC 5545's example verbatim: DTSTART 2 September 1997 9:00 in New York, FREQ=WEEKLY;COUNT=10. The standard lists the occurrences as 2, 9, 16, 23 and 30 September and 7, 14 and 21 October at 9:00 EDT, then 28 October and 4 November at 9:00 EST. The test checks exactly those ten dates, that every one is at 9:00 local, and that the UTC hour moves from 13:00 for the first eight to 14:00 for the last two. Because this machine's Python has no time zone database, the solution includes a small hand-written tzinfo for New York under the 1987-2006 United States rule (first Sunday in April to last Sunday in October), which is enough for the 1997 example; production code would use zoneinfo, which reads the IANA database.

Scaling to many rooms

"Find me any free room for ten people at 14:00 tomorrow" changes the shape of the query: instead of a few calendars and many candidate times, it is one time and many calendars. Filter rooms by static attributes first (capacity at least ten, the right building, a screen) using an index, which usually cuts thousands of rooms to dozens. Then check each remaining room's busy list with one binary search, O(log n) per room. For "the earliest time any suitable room is free", run the sweep per candidate room and take the minimum, or maintain per-room free lists for the coming days, refreshed on each booking. Popular rooms create contention on their locks; that is acceptable, because it reflects real demand, but the lock should be held only for the check and insert, never for the search.

9 · A trace: one booking, two races

TimeBooking X (Ana, Ben, room Orion)Booking Y (Ben, Cy, room Orion)
1sorted locks: Ana, Ben, room:Orion; acquires Anasorted locks: Ben, Cy, room:Orion; acquires Ben
2waits for Benacquires Cy
3waitsacquires room:Orion; checks Ben, Cy, Orion: all free; inserts into all three
4waitsreleases room:Orion, Cy, Ben
5acquires Ben, then room:Orion; checks: Orion now busy at 10:00done: booked
6raises Conflict, writes nothing, releases all

Both bookings wanted the room for the same hour; exactly one got it, and the loser's attendees were not left with a phantom meeting. Notice that the two threads never held locks in conflicting orders: X wanted Ana before Ben and Y wanted Ben before Cy, and both wanted room:Orion last, because "room:Orion" sorts after every person's name here. Any consistent global order works; sorting by a stable id is the simplest to explain.

10 · Testing a scheduler

Time code needs tests that pin semantics precisely. The overlap test checks back-to-back and partial overlaps. The recurrence tests check against an external authority, RFC 5545's published example, rather than against expectations the author computed, which catches errors in both the code and the author's understanding; the second recurrence test exercises UNTIL, BYDAY and EXDATE together. The free-slot test uses three people in three time zones with meetings and working hours, and asserts the exact slots. The concurrency test uses a barrier so twelve threads start together, overlapping attendee pairs in different orders, and a join timeout to turn a deadlock into a failed assertion rather than a hung test run. A small performance test shows a 30-day query over 1,500 meetings completes in a few milliseconds.

11 · Talking through the design

"Half-open intervals in UTC with aware datetimes, so back-to-back is fine and everything compares correctly. Each calendar is a sorted, non-overlapping busy list; conflicts are a binary search plus neighbours. Booking locks every involved calendar in sorted order, checks all, writes all: atomic, and sorted order prevents deadlock. Free slots: treat outside-working-hours as busy, merge everyone's busy lists, sweep for gaps of the needed length. Recurrence is stored as a rule and expanded in the organiser's time zone, so 9:00 stays 9:00 across daylight saving; I tested it against RFC 5545's example. Free time is never stored; it's derived."

12 · The full solution

The file below contains the time zone shim, intervals, calendars, the scheduler, weekly recurrence and six tests. Python 3.12, standard library only.

"""Meeting scheduler: half-open UTC intervals, per-calendar sorted busy lists with bisect,
conflict-checked booking of people and a room under ordered locks, a common-free-slot finder
(merge busy times, intersect working hours), and weekly recurrence expanded in local time
across a daylight-saving change (checked against RFC 5545's own example).
Python 3.12, standard library only (DST rule for US Eastern 1987-2006 written by hand,
because this Windows Python has no tz database). Run: python scheduler.py"""
from __future__ import annotations

import bisect
import heapq
import itertools
import threading
from dataclasses import dataclass
from datetime import date, datetime, time, timedelta, timezone, tzinfo

UTC = timezone.utc


# ------------------------------------------------------------------ a hand-written time zone
class USEastern1987(tzinfo):
    """America/New_York under the 1987-2006 rule: DST from 02:00 on the first Sunday in April
    to 02:00 on the last Sunday in October. Enough to replay RFC 5545's 1997 examples."""

    def _dst_bounds(self, year: int) -> tuple[datetime, datetime]:
        apr1 = datetime(year, 4, 1)
        start = apr1 + timedelta(days=(6 - apr1.weekday()) % 7, hours=2)
        nov1 = datetime(year, 11, 1)
        end = nov1 - timedelta(days=(nov1.weekday() + 1) % 7 or 7) + timedelta(hours=2)
        return start, end

    def utcoffset(self, dt: datetime) -> timedelta:
        return timedelta(hours=-5) + self.dst(dt)

    def dst(self, dt: datetime) -> timedelta:
        start, end = self._dst_bounds(dt.year)
        return timedelta(hours=1) if start <= dt.replace(tzinfo=None) < end else timedelta(0)

    def tzname(self, dt: datetime) -> str:
        return "EDT" if self.dst(dt) else "EST"


# ------------------------------------------------------------------ intervals and calendars
@dataclass(frozen=True, order=True)
class Interval:
    start: datetime          # inclusive, timezone-aware
    end: datetime            # exclusive

    def __post_init__(self) -> None:
        if self.start.tzinfo is None or self.end.tzinfo is None:
            raise ValueError("use timezone-aware datetimes")
        if not self.start < self.end:
            raise ValueError("an interval must have positive length")

    def overlaps(self, other: "Interval") -> bool:
        return self.start < other.end and other.start < self.end   # back-to-back is fine


class Conflict(Exception): ...


class Calendar:
    """Busy intervals sorted by start; no two overlap (the invariant). Insert is O(log n + n)."""

    def __init__(self, owner: str) -> None:
        self.owner = owner
        self.busy: list[tuple[datetime, datetime, str]] = []
        self.lock = threading.Lock()

    def conflicts(self, iv: Interval) -> list[str]:
        i = bisect.bisect_left(self.busy, (iv.start,))
        out = []
        if i > 0 and self.busy[i - 1][1] > iv.start:              # previous may run into us
            out.append(self.busy[i - 1][2])
        while i < len(self.busy) and self.busy[i][0] < iv.end:     # these start inside us
            out.append(self.busy[i][2])
            i += 1
        return out

    def add(self, iv: Interval, event_id: str) -> None:
        if c := self.conflicts(iv):
            raise Conflict(f"{self.owner} busy with {c}")
        bisect.insort(self.busy, (iv.start, iv.end, event_id))

    def remove(self, event_id: str) -> None:
        self.busy = [b for b in self.busy if b[2] != event_id]


class Scheduler:
    def __init__(self) -> None:
        self.cals: dict[str, Calendar] = {}
        self._ids = itertools.count(1)

    def cal(self, name: str) -> Calendar:
        return self.cals.setdefault(name, Calendar(name))

    def book(self, title: str, attendees: list[str], room: str | None, iv: Interval) -> str:
        """All-or-nothing: lock every calendar involved in a fixed (sorted) order, check all,
        then write all. Sorted lock order means two bookings can never deadlock."""
        names = sorted(set(attendees) | ({f"room:{room}"} if room else set()))
        cals = [self.cal(n) for n in names]
        for c in cals:
            c.lock.acquire()
        try:
            problems = {c.owner: c.conflicts(iv) for c in cals}
            problems = {k: v for k, v in problems.items() if v}
            if problems:
                raise Conflict(f"{title}: {problems}")
            event_id = f"E{next(self._ids)}"
            for c in cals:
                bisect.insort(c.busy, (iv.start, iv.end, event_id))
            return event_id
        finally:
            for c in reversed(cals):
                c.lock.release()

    def free_slots(self, attendees: list[str], window: Interval, duration: timedelta,
                   hours: dict[str, tuple[time, time, tzinfo]], step: timedelta | None = None
                   ) -> list[Interval]:
        """Gaps of at least `duration` when everyone is free and inside everyone's working hours."""
        blocked = [[(s, e) for s, e, _ in self.cal(a).busy if e > window.start and s < window.end]
                   for a in attendees]
        for a in attendees:                                       # outside working hours = busy
            start_t, end_t, tz = hours[a]
            day = window.start.astimezone(tz).date() - timedelta(days=1)
            last = window.end.astimezone(tz).date() + timedelta(days=1)
            off = []
            while day <= last:
                ws = datetime.combine(day, start_t, tz).astimezone(UTC)
                we = datetime.combine(day, end_t, tz).astimezone(UTC)
                off.append((datetime.combine(day, time(0), tz).astimezone(UTC), ws))
                off.append((we, datetime.combine(day + timedelta(days=1), time(0), tz).astimezone(UTC)))
                day += timedelta(days=1)
            blocked.append(off)
        merged: list[tuple[datetime, datetime]] = []
        for s, e in heapq.merge(*[sorted(b) for b in blocked]):  # k-way merge of sorted lists
            if merged and s <= merged[-1][1]:
                merged[-1] = (merged[-1][0], max(merged[-1][1], e))
            else:
                merged.append((s, e))
        slots, cursor = [], window.start
        for s, e in merged + [(window.end, window.end)]:
            if s > cursor and min(s, window.end) - cursor >= duration:
                slots.append(Interval(cursor, min(s, window.end)))
            cursor = max(cursor, e)
            if cursor >= window.end:
                break
        return slots


# ------------------------------------------------------------------ recurrence
WEEKDAYS = {"MO": 0, "TU": 1, "WE": 2, "TH": 3, "FR": 4, "SA": 5, "SU": 6}


def expand_weekly(dtstart: datetime, length: timedelta, count: int | None = None,
                  until: datetime | None = None, byday: list[str] | None = None,
                  exdates: set[date] | None = None) -> list[Interval]:
    """FREQ=WEEKLY in the event's own time zone: the wall-clock time stays fixed and the UTC
    offset follows daylight saving. DTSTART is always the first occurrence (RFC 5545)."""
    if count is None and until is None:
        raise ValueError("refusing to expand an infinite rule; pass count or until")
    tz = dtstart.tzinfo
    days = sorted(WEEKDAYS[d] for d in (byday or [list(WEEKDAYS)[dtstart.weekday()]]))
    week0 = dtstart.date() - timedelta(days=dtstart.weekday())
    out, n = [], 0
    for w in itertools.count():
        for d in days:
            day = week0 + timedelta(weeks=w, days=d)
            local = datetime.combine(day, dtstart.timetz().replace(tzinfo=None), tz)
            if local < dtstart:
                continue
            if until is not None and local > until:
                return out
            n += 1
            if not (exdates and day in exdates):
                out.append(Interval(local.astimezone(UTC), (local + length).astimezone(UTC)))
            if count is not None and n >= count:
                return out

NY = USEastern1987()


def t(h: int, m: int = 0, day: int = 6) -> datetime:

def test_rfc5545_weekly_count_10_across_dst() -> None:
    occ = expand_weekly(datetime(1997, 9, 2, 9, 0, tzinfo=NY), timedelta(hours=1), count=10)
    local = [o.start.astimezone(NY) for o in occ]
    assert [(d.month, d.day) for d in local] == [(9, 2), (9, 9), (9, 16), (9, 23), (9, 30),
                                                 (10, 7), (10, 14), (10, 21), (10, 28), (11, 4)]
    assert all(d.hour == 9 for d in local)                         # 9:00 wall clock every week
    assert [o.start.hour for o in occ] == [13] * 8 + [14] * 2       # but 13:00Z EDT, 14:00Z EST
    assert [d.tzname() for d in local][-3:] == ["EDT", "EST", "EST"]

def test_overlap_semantics_and_conflicts() -> None:
    a, b, c = Interval(t(9), t(10)), Interval(t(10), t(11)), Interval(t(9, 30), t(10, 30))
    assert not a.overlaps(b) and a.overlaps(c) and c.overlaps(b)
    cal = Calendar("ana")
    cal.add(a, "standup"); cal.add(b, "review")                   # back to back: allowed
    assert cal.conflicts(c) == ["standup", "review"]
    try:
        cal.add(c, "clash")
    except Conflict:
        pass
    else:
        raise AssertionError("overlap accepted")


def test_free_slots_three_people_and_hours() -> None:
    s = Scheduler()
    s.book("ana 1:1", ["ana"], None, Interval(t(14), t(15)))
    s.book("ben block", ["ben"], None, Interval(t(15, 30), t(17)))
    s.book("cy lunch", ["cy"], None, Interval(t(17), t(18)))
    hours = {"ana": (time(9), time(17), timezone(timedelta(hours=-4))),   # 13:00-21:00Z

             "cy": (time(15), time(20), timezone(timedelta(hours=1)))}    # 14:00-19:00Z
    slots = s.free_slots(["ana", "ben", "cy"], Interval(t(0), t(23, 59)), timedelta(minutes=30), hours)
    assert [(x.start.strftime("%H:%M"), x.end.strftime("%H:%M")) for x in slots] == \
        [("15:00", "15:30"), ("18:00", "19:00")]


def test_booking_race_one_winner_and_no_deadlock() -> None:
    s = Scheduler()
    iv = Interval(t(10), t(11))
    wins, start = [], threading.Barrier(12)

    def try_book(i: int) -> None:
        start.wait()
        team = ["ana", "ben", "cy", "dee"]
        attendees = [team[i % 4], team[(i + 1) % 4]]               # overlapping, different orders
        try:
            wins.append(s.book(f"m{i}", attendees, "Orion", iv))
        except Conflict:
            pass
    ts = [threading.Thread(target=try_book, args=(i,)) for i in range(12)]
    for th in ts: th.start()
    for th in ts: th.join(timeout=10)
    assert all(not th.is_alive() for th in ts), "deadlock"
    assert len(wins) == 1 and len(s.cal("room:Orion").busy) == 1

    import time as _time
    s = Scheduler()
    for off, p in ((0, "ana"), (0.25, "ben"), (0.5, "cy")):
        for k in range(500):                                       # 500 half-hour meetings each
            st = t(0) + timedelta(hours=k * 2 + off)                # together busy 1 h in every 2
            try:
                s.book(f"{p}{k}", [p], None, Interval(st, st + timedelta(minutes=30)))
            except Conflict:
                pass
    hours = {p: (time(0), time(23, 59), UTC) for p in ("ana", "ben", "cy")}
    t0 = _time.perf_counter()
    slots = s.free_slots(["ana", "ben", "cy"], Interval(t(0), t(0) + timedelta(days=30)),
                         timedelta(minutes=45), hours)
    ms = (_time.perf_counter() - t0) * 1000
    print(f"    30-day query over 1,500 meetings: {len(slots)} slots in {ms:.1f} ms")

13 · Edge cases

Nonexistent and repeated local times: when clocks spring forward, 2:30 does not exist; when they fall back, 1:30 happens twice. Python's fold attribute distinguishes the two 1:30s; a scheduler should refuse or adjust meetings in the gap and pick a rule for the overlap. Half-hour and 45-minute offsets (India UTC+5:30, Nepal UTC+5:45): never assume whole hours. All-day events: dates, not times, and they follow the viewer's local day. Meetings crossing midnight: the working-hours expansion covers the day before and after the window for this reason. UNTIL in UTC versus local time: RFC 5545 requires UNTIL in UTC when DTSTART has a zone; compare in UTC. Empty attendee list or a duration longer than the window: return no slots. Clock changes in rules: governments change DST rules with little notice, so recurring events must be re-expanded with updated time zone data.

14 · Extensions the interviewer will ask for

Invitations with accept, decline and tentative states, tentative counting as "busy-tentative" in free/busy. Room search by capacity, equipment and building, then free/busy per candidate room. Monthly and yearly rules ("second Tuesday", "last weekday") from the full RRULE grammar. Series edits: "this occurrence", "this and following" (split the series), "all". Interval trees for very large calendars. Suggestions ranked by preferences (avoid lunch, prefer mornings, fewest people at the edge of their day). Distributed booking: calendars in a database, booking as one transaction with row locks taken in a fixed order, or conditional inserts guarded by exclusion constraints (PostgreSQL supports constraints that forbid overlapping ranges).

15 · Complexity

Conflict check: O(log n + k) per calendar. Insertion: O(n) list shift, O(log n) with a tree. Booking: O(p log n) for p calendars, plus lock acquisition. Free slots: O(B log k) to merge B busy intervals from k attendees plus O(D × k) for working-hours blocks over D days, then a linear sweep. Weekly expansion: O(occurrences). Memory: O(total busy intervals); free time and expanded recurrences are computed, not stored. For an office with a few hundred people and rooms, every query in this design runs in milliseconds; the costs only start to matter at the scale of a company-wide calendar service, where the interval trees, indexes and caches in the extensions take over.

Why half-open intervals win

Closed intervals [start, end] make adjacent meetings overlap at their shared instant; open intervals (start, end) lose the start instant itself; half-open [start, end) partitions time cleanly: consecutive intervals tile the timeline with no gaps and no overlaps, durations are simply end minus start, and splitting an interval at t gives [start, t) and [t, end) without double-counting t. Dijkstra argued for this convention for integer ranges for the same reasons, and it is why Python's ranges and slices are half-open. iCalendar's non-inclusive DTEND applies the same idea to events.

Time zones are data that changes

A time zone is not an offset; it is a history and a forecast of offsets, maintained in the IANA time zone database and updated several times a year when governments change their rules, sometimes with only weeks of notice. This has two consequences for a calendar. Store future meetings that are defined in local time as local time plus zone (and the rule), not only as a UTC instant, because if the zone's rules change before the meeting, the correct UTC instant changes too. And keep the time zone database up to date in every service that expands rules, or the same series will expand differently on different machines. Python's zoneinfo uses the operating system's database or the tzdata package; this lesson's machine has neither for Python, which is why the solution carries a hand-written zone for its historical test.

Exceptions and modified instances

Real series are messy: one Tuesday's meeting is cancelled, another moves to Wednesday, a third gets a different room. iCalendar models these as exceptions on the series rather than as new rules. A cancelled occurrence is an EXDATE. A moved or changed occurrence is a separate event that carries a RECURRENCE-ID naming the original occurrence it replaces, so the expander emits the override instead of the generated instance. A scheduler that expands series must therefore apply three layers in order: generate from the rule, remove exclusions, substitute overrides. Getting the identity right matters: overrides are keyed by the original occurrence's start, so if the series' start time is later edited, every override must be re-keyed or it silently detaches.

Interval trees and segment structures

A sorted list works when intervals in one calendar do not overlap, because then sorting by start also sorts by end. For collections where intervals overlap freely (all meetings in a building, all tentative holds), an interval tree answers "which intervals overlap this one?" in O(log n + k): it is a balanced search tree on start times in which each node also stores the maximum end time in its subtree, so whole subtrees that end before the query starts are skipped. Databases provide the same capability through range types and indexes; PostgreSQL's range types with GiST indexes and exclusion constraints can enforce "no two bookings of a room overlap" inside the database itself.

Lock ordering as a general technique

The booking path needs several locks at once, and the rule that makes it safe, acquire in a fixed global order, is the standard defence against deadlock in any code that locks multiple resources: bank transfers between two accounts, moving an item between two inventories, merging two documents. It works because a deadlock requires a cycle in the "waits for" graph, and if every thread acquires locks in increasing order, every edge in that graph points from a lower lock to a higher one, so no cycle can exist. The alternatives are a single global lock (simple, no parallelism), try-lock with back-off (avoids deadlock but can livelock), or optimistic concurrency with version checks. In a database, the same principle appears as locking rows in a consistent order, typically by primary key.

Good slots, not just valid ones

The sweep returns every valid slot; users want a few good ones. Ranking is where product judgement enters, and a clean design keeps it separate from finding: generate candidate start times within each free gap (on the half hour, say), score each candidate, and return the top few. Useful scoring terms include how early the slot is, how close it is to the edge of anyone's working day (a meeting at 8:30 for one person and 18:30 for another is worse than one comfortably inside both days), whether it splits someone's long focus block into two short fragments, whether it overlaps lunch, and how many people would need to travel between rooms. Each term is a small function, the total is a weighted sum, and the weights are tuned from what users accept. Because the finder never changes, ranking can evolve without risking correctness.

Free/busy and privacy

Scheduling across people requires knowing when they are busy, not what they are doing. iCalendar separates the two with the VFREEBUSY component and free/busy types (FREE, BUSY, BUSY-TENTATIVE, BUSY-UNAVAILABLE), and calendar servers expose free/busy to colleagues who cannot see event details. The scheduler in this lesson only ever needs busy intervals, so it can run against a free/busy feed, which is how cross-organisation scheduling tools work without access to private calendars.

How calendar systems fail

The most common production bugs are about time. Daylight-saving transitions move recurring meetings when rules are expanded in UTC rather than local time, or when a service's time zone data is out of date. Meetings at nonexistent local times (2:30 on a spring-forward night) crash naive code or silently shift. Off-by-one errors at UNTIL boundaries drop or add the last occurrence. Time zone rule changes by governments (several each year) shift future meetings unexpectedly if instants were stored without the original zone. Each class of bug is cheap to prevent with aware datetimes, local-time rules with zones, and tests against the iCalendar specification's examples.

The second class is concurrency: double-booked rooms from unlocked check-then-act sequences, deadlocks when bookings lock calendars in inconsistent orders, and phantom partial bookings when a multi-calendar write fails halfway without a transaction. All-or-nothing booking with ordered locks, or a database transaction with consistent row-lock order, prevents them.

Reminders and notifications

Calendars also push: reminders fifteen minutes before a meeting, invitation emails, updates when a series changes. Reminders are timers on expanded occurrences, so they inherit every time-zone subtlety: a reminder for a 9:00 meeting must fire at 8:45 in the meeting's zone, including the week the clocks change. They are best implemented by a scheduler that expands the next occurrence of each series, computes its reminder instant in UTC, and stores that instant in a queue ordered by time (the timing-wheel or heap structures from the key-value store case). When a series is edited, its pending reminders are recomputed. Duplicate or missing reminders around clock changes are a classic, visible bug.

What to measure

Booking conflicts and their rate (users fighting for popular rooms), booking latency including lock wait time, free-slot query latency at the 99th percentile, the number of occurrences expanded per request (an unbounded rule expanded by mistake shows up here first), and reconciliation checks such as rooms booked by events that no longer exist.

Operations

Keep time zone data current everywhere rules are expanded, and plan for the day it changes: re-expand affected series and notify attendees whose meetings moved. Cap recurrence expansion windows. Archive past events so active calendars stay small. And provide an audit trail for bookings, because "who took my room?" is a frequent support question.

The real case: RFC 5545, iCalendar

Every major calendar product exchanges events in the iCalendar format defined by RFC 5545, and the standard settles most of the semantic questions in this lesson. DTSTART specifies the first instance of a recurrence and always counts as the first occurrence; DTEND is the non-inclusive end of an event, the half-open convention. A recurrence rule (RRULE) has a frequency and may be bounded by COUNT, the number of occurrences, or UNTIL, the last allowed instant, given in UTC when DTSTART has a time zone; without either, the rule repeats forever. EXDATE removes individual occurrences. Times with a TZID parameter are interpreted in that zone's rules. The standard's examples make the daylight-saving behaviour concrete: a weekly event starting 2 September 1997 at 9:00 in America/New_York with COUNT=10 occurs at 9:00 EDT through 21 October and at 9:00 EST on 28 October and 4 November. For scheduling across people, the VFREEBUSY component publishes busy time without event details, with free/busy types such as BUSY and BUSY-TENTATIVE. The solution's recurrence test reproduces that example exactly, which is the strongest kind of test for time code: agreement with the specification's own worked output.

Goal: run the scheduler, then break, measure and extend it. Python 3.12, standard library only.

  1. Save the full solution as scheduler.py and run it. Six tests print ok, including RFC 5545's weekly example across the 1997 daylight-saving change and a twelve-thread booking race.
  2. Break it. In expand_weekly, compute occurrences by adding seven days to the first occurrence in UTC instead of rebuilding each in local time. The RFC test fails: the last two meetings land at 8:00 local.
  3. Break it again. In book, remove sorted so locks are taken in the attendee order given. Run the race test several times; with unlucky timing threads deadlock and the join timeout reports it. Add time.sleep(0.01) after each lock acquisition to widen the window and it deadlocks on every run, as it did when we tried it.
  4. Measure it. Grow each person's calendar to 10,000 meetings and time free_slots for a 30-day window, then replace the busy list with a structure that inserts in O(log n) and compare booking time.
  5. Extend it. Add tentative bookings that count as busy for free-slot search only if a flag says so, and a cancel(event_id) that removes an event from every calendar atomically.
  6. Extend it again. Add monthly recurrence by day of month, and decide what happens on 31 February (RFC 5545 skips invalid dates).

Whatever you change, keep the RFC example as a regression test; time code that passes the specification's own examples is far less likely to surprise users.

Work it out

Ana (New York, works 9:00-17:00 local), Ben (London, 9:00-17:00 local) and Chitra (Bangalore, UTC+5:30, 10:00-18:00 local) need a 30-minute meeting on a Wednesday in late March, when New York has already moved to daylight time (UTC−4) and London has not (UTC+0). Ana is busy 9:00-10:00 New York time.

(a) In UTC, what are each person's working hours that day? (b) What common slots exist? (c) Two weeks later London moves to UTC+1. What changes, and what does that say about recurring meetings?

Answer

(a) Working hours in UTC. Ana: 9:00-17:00 at UTC−4 is 13:00-21:00 UTC. Ben: 9:00-17:00 at UTC+0 is 09:00-17:00 UTC. Chitra: 10:00-18:00 at UTC+5:30 is 04:30-12:30 UTC.

(b) Common slots. Intersect the three: Ana starts at 13:00 UTC, Chitra ends at 12:30 UTC. There is no common time within everyone's working hours. Ana's 9:00-10:00 New York meeting (13:00-14:00 UTC) does not even matter. The scheduler should say so clearly and offer the best compromises: for example 12:30-13:00 UTC (8:30 for Ana, 12:30 for Ben, 18:00-18:30 for Chitra, each just outside one person's hours), ranked by how far outside hours each person is. Merging busy and outside-hours intervals and sweeping finds no gap, which is the correct and useful answer.

(c) Two weeks later. London moves to UTC+1, so Ben's hours become 08:00-16:00 UTC; Ana and Chitra are unchanged (India has no daylight saving). There is still no common time, but any compromise slot found earlier shifts for Ben by an hour. If a weekly meeting had been stored as a fixed UTC time, it would now sit an hour earlier in Ben's day; if stored as a rule in the organiser's time zone (say New York), it stays fixed for Ana and moves for Ben and Chitra in local terms. This is the opening story of the lesson: recurring meetings must be stored as local-time rules with an explicit zone, the organiser's, and expanded per occurrence, because each participant's offset changes on different dates or not at all.
