The interviewer has just watched you build an LRU cache (ld1) and says: "Good. Now the same interface, but evict the key that has been used least often. Ties go to the least recently used. Both operations in O(1)." Most candidates reach for a heap keyed by frequency, then realise every get changes a frequency and costs O(log n) to fix the heap — and that the heap does not break ties by recency without extra fields. Others keep a counter per key and scan for the minimum on eviction, which is O(n). The constant-time answer exists, it is elegant, and it rests on one observation: frequencies only ever go up by exactly one.

LFU also matters beyond the puzzle. Caches in front of databases, content delivery networks and model-serving systems choose between recency and frequency, and the choice changes hit rates by tens of points depending on the workload. A batch job that reads every product once will flush an LRU cache of the 50 products that serve most of the traffic; an LFU cache shrugs it off. But when popularity shifts — a new season's catalogue, a breaking news story — LFU clings to yesterday's favourites. Knowing which workload favours which policy, and why production systems use approximate, ageing frequency counts, is what turns the data-structure exercise into a design conversation.

This lesson builds the O(1) LFU in interview order: clarify the semantics, define the entities, write the naive versions (scan, heap) and their costs, then the hash map plus per-frequency doubly linked lists plus a running minimum, with the two rules that keep the minimum correct without a scan. A trace by hand follows every pointer. The full solution is a runnable Python 3.12 file with six tests: the trace, edge cases, a side-by-side comparison with a brute-force reference on 25,000 random operations, a timing test showing constant cost per operation, a threaded stress test, and a workload comparison with LRU — LFU 70% versus LRU 49% on a scan-heavy trace, LRU 99% versus LFU 83% when popularity shifts.

The analogy: a library's shelf of popular books

A small library has one display shelf with room for a fixed number of books, and wants it to hold the books borrowed most often. The librarian keeps the shelf in sections: "borrowed once", "borrowed twice", "borrowed three times", and so on, and within each section books stand in the order they were last borrowed, oldest on the left. When a book is borrowed, it moves from its section to the right-hand end of the next section up. When the shelf is full and a new book arrives, the librarian removes the left-most book of the lowest section — the least borrowed, and among those, the one borrowed longest ago. The librarian also remembers which section is currently the lowest non-empty one, so finding the victim needs no searching. A new book always starts in "borrowed once", which therefore becomes the lowest section.

The sections are the frequency lists; left-to-right order within a section is recency; moving a book up one section is the O(1) touch; remembering the lowest section is min_freq; the catalogue card that tells you where any book stands is the hash map from key to node.

The analogy breaks in a useful way: a real librarian would notice that a book popular five years ago is no longer borrowed and take it off the shelf. Pure LFU does not — its counts never fall — which is exactly the weakness production caches fix with ageing.

Where frequency-based eviction shows up

Frequency matters wherever a small set of items carries most of the traffic for a long time. Content delivery networks see it in static assets — a site's logo, its main script bundle — requested on every page view for months. Database buffer pools see it in index root pages, touched by every query. Model-serving systems see it in embeddings or features for the most active users and the most popular items. In each case, a recency-only policy can be fooled by a burst of one-off reads (a crawler, a nightly export, a full-table scan) into evicting the items that matter most. That is the scenario to describe when an interviewer asks why anyone would bother with LFU — and the shifting-popularity scenario is the one to describe when they ask why it is rarely used in its exact form.

1 · Clarify the requirements

Ask: when two keys have the same frequency, which goes — the least recently used (usual), or either? Does put on an existing key count as a use? Does a get miss count as anything? What happens at capacity 0? Is there a delete? Must it be thread-safe? Should frequency ever decay? Agreed: ties broken by least recent use; updating counts as a use; misses do nothing; capacity 0 stores nothing; no delete (an extension); a single lock for thread safety; no decay (an extension). Target: get and put in O(1) time, O(capacity) space.

2 · Entities and the interface

The node stores its own frequency, so after a hash lookup we know exactly which list it lives in. Lists use sentinels, as in ld1, so append and remove have no empty-list special cases. Empty lists are deleted from freqs, which keeps an invariant the tests rely on: every list in the map is non-empty, and min_freq equals the smallest key of freqs.

3 · Stage one: the scan, and the heap

Counter and scan. Store key → (value, freq, last_tick); on eviction, scan all keys for the smallest (freq, last_tick). Get and put are O(1), eviction O(n). This is the solution's ReferenceLFU: too slow at scale, but obviously correct, which makes it the ideal test oracle.

Heap. A min-heap ordered by (freq, last_tick) gives the victim in O(1) and removes it in O(log n). But every get changes a key's frequency and tick; updating an element inside a heap needs its position (an index kept in sync on every swap) and a sift, O(log n). With lazy deletion — push a new entry, ignore stale ones when they surface — the heap grows with every access. O(log n) per operation, extra memory, more code. Better than the scan, but not the target.

4 · Stage two: the O(1) structure

The key observation. An access raises a key's frequency from f to exactly f + 1. So if keys are grouped by frequency, an access moves a key to the neighbouring group — no search for where it belongs. If each group is a doubly linked list in recency order (oldest at the head, newest at the tail), moving a key is: unlink it from list f (O(1), because the node has prev and next pointers) and append it at the tail of list f + 1 (O(1)). Recency order within each list breaks ties: the head of a list is the least recently used key among those with that frequency.

Finding the victim. The victim is the head of the list with the smallest frequency. Keep that frequency in min_freq. Two rules maintain it, and no others are needed:

  • Insert: a new key has frequency 1, the smallest possible, so min_freq = 1.
  • Touch: if a node leaves list f and that list becomes empty and f was min_freq, then min_freq = f + 1 — the node has just been appended there, so that list is non-empty and nothing smaller exists.

What about eviction? Removing the victim may empty the minimum list, leaving min_freq pointing at nothing. It does not matter: eviction only happens inside put of a new key, which immediately inserts that key at frequency 1 and resets min_freq = 1. The stale value is never read. This is the argument interviewers want to hear stated explicitly.

get(key): hash lookup; on a miss return the default; on a hit touch the node and return its value. put(key, value): if capacity is 0, do nothing; if the key exists, replace the value and touch (an update is a use); otherwise, if full, pop the head of freqs[min_freq], delete its list if now empty, and remove the victim from nodes; then create a node with frequency 1, append it to list 1 and set min_freq = 1. Every step is a constant number of dictionary operations and pointer updates.

6 · Stage four: testing against a reference

Hand traces cover the rules you thought of; a reference covers the ones you did not. ReferenceLFU stores (value, frequency, last_tick) and evicts by scanning for the minimum (frequency, tick) — five lines, nothing clever, nothing to get wrong. The test runs the fast cache and the reference side by side on 5,000 random operations for each capacity from 1 to 10 over a small key space (so evictions and ties are frequent), asserting every get returns the same value and the final key sets match. Every 250 steps it also runs check_invariants: sizes agree, no list is empty, min_freq is the smallest frequency present, and every node's stored frequency matches the list it sits in. Any divergence pinpoints the step where the fast version went wrong.

The timing test makes the complexity claim measurable: 100,000 mixed operations with a cache of 100 entries and with a cache of 100,000 entries. An O(n) eviction would make the second run hundreds of times slower per operation; the solution requires it to be within 4× (it is close to 1×, the difference being memory effects of a larger dictionary).

7 · Stage five: LFU versus LRU on real-looking workloads

The last test replays two synthetic traces through both policies, filling the cache on each miss. Scan-heavy: 70% of 30,000 requests go to a stable hot set of 50 keys, 30% are one-off keys never seen again, and the cache holds 60. LRU lets each one-off key push a hot key towards eviction, so the hot set never fully fits: 48.6% hits. LFU's one-off keys enter at frequency 1 and evict each other, never the hot keys with large counts: 69.7% — close to the 70% ceiling. Shifting: six phases of 5,000 requests, each with a different set of 40 keys accessed with a skewed (Pareto) distribution, cache of 40. LRU forgets the previous phase within a few hundred requests: 99.2%. LFU keeps the previous phase's favourites because of their large counts, and new keys must climb from frequency 1 while they evict each other: 82.6%. Neither policy is better in general; the workload decides.

8 · The full solution

Six tests in about two seconds; the workload comparison prints both hit rates. Python 3.12, standard library only.

"""LFU cache with O(1) get and put: a hash map to nodes, one doubly linked list per frequency
(least recent first, so ties break by recency), and a running minimum frequency. Checked against a
brute-force reference on random operations, timed for constant cost per operation, and compared
with LRU on a scan-heavy workload and on a shifting workload.
Python 3.12, standard library only.  Run: python lfu.py"""
from __future__ import annotations

import random
import threading
import time
from collections import OrderedDict
from typing import Generic, Hashable, TypeVar

K = TypeVar("K", bound=Hashable)
V = TypeVar("V")


class _Node:
    __slots__ = ("key", "value", "freq", "prev", "next")

    def __init__(self, key=None, value=None) -> None:
        self.key, self.value, self.freq = key, value, 1
        self.prev: _Node | None = None
        self.next: _Node | None = None


class _DList:
    """Doubly linked list with sentinels: append at the tail (most recent), pop from the head."""
    __slots__ = ("head", "tail", "size")

    def __init__(self) -> None:
        self.head, self.tail, self.size = _Node(), _Node(), 0
        self.head.next, self.tail.prev = self.tail, self.head

    def append(self, n: _Node) -> None:
        n.prev, n.next = self.tail.prev, self.tail
        self.tail.prev.next = n
        self.tail.prev = n
        self.size += 1

    def remove(self, n: _Node) -> None:
        n.prev.next, n.next.prev = n.next, n.prev
        n.prev = n.next = None
        self.size -= 1

    def pop_oldest(self) -> _Node:
        n = self.head.next
        self.remove(n)
        return n


class LFUCache(Generic[K, V]):
    def __init__(self, capacity: int) -> None:
        if capacity < 0:
            raise ValueError("capacity must be >= 0")
        self.capacity = capacity
        self.nodes: dict[K, _Node] = {}
        self.freqs: dict[int, _DList] = {}
        self.min_freq = 0
        self.evictions = 0
        self._lock = threading.Lock()

    def _touch(self, n: _Node) -> None:
        """Move n from its frequency list to the next one; keep min_freq correct."""
        lst = self.freqs[n.freq]
        lst.remove(n)
        if lst.size == 0:
            del self.freqs[n.freq]
            if self.min_freq == n.freq:
                self.min_freq += 1           # n moves to freq + 1, so that is the new minimum
        n.freq += 1
        self.freqs.setdefault(n.freq, _DList()).append(n)

    def get(self, key: K, default: V | None = None) -> V | None:
        with self._lock:
            n = self.nodes.get(key)
            if n is None:
                return default
            self._touch(n)
            return n.value

    def put(self, key: K, value: V) -> None:
        with self._lock:
            if self.capacity == 0:
                return
            n = self.nodes.get(key)
            if n is not None:                # update counts as a use
                n.value = value
                self._touch(n)
                return
            if len(self.nodes) >= self.capacity:
                victim = self.freqs[self.min_freq].pop_oldest()   # least frequent, then least recent
                if self.freqs[self.min_freq].size == 0:
                    del self.freqs[self.min_freq]
                del self.nodes[victim.key]
                self.evictions += 1
            n = _Node(key, value)
            self.nodes[key] = n
            self.freqs.setdefault(1, _DList()).append(n)
            self.min_freq = 1                # a new key always has the lowest frequency

    def frequency(self, key: K) -> int:
        n = self.nodes.get(key)
        return 0 if n is None else n.freq

    def __len__(self) -> int:
        return len(self.nodes)

    def check_invariants(self) -> None:
        assert len(self.nodes) <= self.capacity
        assert sum(l.size for l in self.freqs.values()) == len(self.nodes)
        assert all(l.size > 0 for l in self.freqs.values())
        if self.nodes:
            assert self.min_freq == min(self.freqs)
        for f, lst in self.freqs.items():
            n = lst.head.next
            while n is not lst.tail:
                assert n.freq == f and self.nodes[n.key] is n
                n = n.next


class LRUCache:
    """ld1's ten-line LRU, for comparison."""

    def __init__(self, capacity: int) -> None:
        self.capacity, self.d = capacity, OrderedDict()

    def get(self, key, default=None):
        if key not in self.d:
            return default
        self.d.move_to_end(key)
        return self.d[key]

    def put(self, key, value) -> None:
        if key in self.d:
            self.d.move_to_end(key)
        self.d[key] = value
        if len(self.d) > self.capacity:
            self.d.popitem(last=False)


class ReferenceLFU:
    """Brute force: O(n) eviction scan over (frequency, last-use tick). Obviously correct."""

    def __init__(self, capacity: int) -> None:
        self.capacity, self.d, self.tick = capacity, {}, 0

    def get(self, key, default=None):
        self.tick += 1
        if key not in self.d:
            return default
        v, f, _ = self.d[key]
        self.d[key] = (v, f + 1, self.tick)
        return v

    def put(self, key, value) -> None:
        self.tick += 1
        if self.capacity == 0:
            return
        if key in self.d:
            _, f, _ = self.d[key]
            self.d[key] = (value, f + 1, self.tick)
            return
        if len(self.d) >= self.capacity:
            victim = min(self.d, key=lambda k: (self.d[k][1], self.d[k][2]))
            del self.d[victim]
        self.d[key] = (value, 1, self.tick)


def hit_rate(cache, trace: list[int]) -> float:
    hits = 0
    for k in trace:
        if cache.get(k) is not None:
            hits += 1
        else:
            cache.put(k, k)
    return hits / len(trace)


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- tests
def test_worked_trace() -> None:
    c: LFUCache[int, str] = LFUCache(2)
    c.put(1, "a"); c.put(2, "b")                 # freq: 1→1, 2→1
    assert c.get(1) == "a"                       # 1→2
    c.put(3, "c")                                # evict 2 (freq 1, the only one at min)
    assert c.get(2) is None and c.get(3) == "c"  # 3→2
    c.put(4, "d")                                # 1 and 3 both freq 2: evict 1, the least recent
    assert c.get(1) is None and c.get(3) == "c" and c.get(4) == "d"
    assert (c.frequency(3), c.frequency(4)) == (3, 2) and c.evictions == 2
    c.check_invariants()


def test_update_counts_as_use_and_capacity_zero() -> None:
    c = LFUCache(2)
    c.put("x", 1); c.put("y", 1); c.put("x", 2)  # x: freq 2 after update
    c.put("z", 1)                                # evicts y
    assert c.get("x") == 2 and c.get("y") is None
    z = LFUCache(0)
    z.put("a", 1)
    assert z.get("a") is None and len(z) == 0


def test_matches_reference_on_random_operations() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(3)
    for cap in (1, 2, 3, 5, 10):
        fast, ref = LFUCache(cap), ReferenceLFU(cap)
        for step in range(5000):
            k = rng.randint(0, 15)
            if rng.random() < 0.5:
                assert fast.get(k) == ref.get(k), (cap, step)
            else:
                v = rng.random()
                fast.put(k, v); ref.put(k, v)
            if step % 250 == 0:
                fast.check_invariants()
        assert set(fast.nodes) == set(ref.d)


def test_constant_time_per_operation() -> None:
    def per_op(n_ops: int, cap: int) -> float:
        c, rng = LFUCache(cap), random.Random(0)
        keys = [rng.randint(0, cap * 2) for _ in range(n_ops)]
        t0 = time.perf_counter()
        for i, k in enumerate(keys):
            if i & 1:
                c.get(k)
            else:
                c.put(k, i)
        return (time.perf_counter() - t0) / n_ops
    small, large = per_op(100_000, 100), per_op(100_000, 100_000)
    assert large < small * 4, (small, large)     # 1000x larger cache, not ~1000x slower


def test_lfu_resists_scans_lru_adapts_to_shifts() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(11)
    hot = list(range(50))                                     # a stable hot set
    scan_trace = []
    for i in range(30_000):
        scan_trace.append(rng.choice(hot) if rng.random() < 0.7 else 10_000 + i)   # 30% one-off keys
    lfu_scan, lru_scan = hit_rate(LFUCache(60), scan_trace), hit_rate(LRUCache(60), scan_trace)
    shift_trace = []
    for phase in range(6):                                    # popularity moves every 5,000 requests
        base = phase * 100
        shift_trace += [base + int(rng.paretovariate(1.2)) % 40 for _ in range(5_000)]
    lfu_shift, lru_shift = hit_rate(LFUCache(40), shift_trace), hit_rate(LRUCache(40), shift_trace)
    print(f"   scan-heavy:   LFU {lfu_scan:.3f}  LRU {lru_scan:.3f}")
    print(f"   shifting:     LFU {lfu_shift:.3f}  LRU {lru_shift:.3f}")
    assert lfu_scan > lru_scan + 0.05                         # one-off keys cannot evict the hot set
    assert lru_shift > lfu_shift                              # old favourites linger in LFU


def test_thread_safety_stress() -> None:
    c, errors = LFUCache(64), []

    def worker(seed: int) -> None:
        rng = random.Random(seed)
        try:
            for _ in range(20_000):
                k = rng.randint(0, 200)
                if rng.random() < 0.5:
                    c.get(k)
                else:
                    c.put(k, k)
        except Exception as e:          # pragma: no cover
            errors.append(e)
    ts = [threading.Thread(target=worker, args=(s,)) for s in range(8)]
    for t in ts: t.start()
    for t in ts: t.join()
    assert not errors
    c.check_invariants()

9 · Edge cases

Capacity 0: put returns immediately; without that check, eviction would pop from a non-existent list. Capacity 1: every new key evicts the old one regardless of frequency, because the old key is the only candidate. Updating an existing key: must touch, not insert — inserting would create a second node and break the size invariant. None as a value: get returning None is then ambiguous; the solution's get takes a default, or use a sentinel object. Unhashable keys: rejected by the dictionary, as in any hash-based cache. Integer overflow: not an issue in Python; in fixed-width languages a frequency counter on a very hot key can overflow, one more reason production systems use saturating logarithmic counters.

10 · Extensions the interviewer will ask for

delete(key): unlink and remove; if that empties the minimum list, min_freq is stale and the next eviction needs the next non-empty frequency — keep frequencies in a linked list of frequency nodes, as the original paper does, so the next one is a pointer away. Ageing: periodically halve all counts (O(n), amortised over a window), or use LFU with dynamic aging (priority = frequency + L, with L raised to the evicted key's priority), or switch to approximate decaying counters. TTL: store an expiry per node and treat expired keys as misses, evicting them lazily. Size-aware eviction: with values of different sizes, evict by frequency per byte. Concurrency: a single lock, or shard by key hash into independent caches. Admission: TinyLFU's idea — admit a new key only if its estimated frequency beats the victim's — prevents one-off keys from displacing anything at all.

11 · Complexity

get: one hash lookup, unlink, possibly delete an empty list and increment min_freq, possibly create a list, append — O(1). put of an existing key: the same. put of a new key: possibly pop the head of the minimum list and delete it and its node, then create a node and append — O(1). Space: O(capacity) nodes plus at most O(capacity) frequency lists (each non-empty list holds at least one node), so O(capacity) overall; each node carries a key, value, frequency and two pointers, about twice the per-entry overhead of an LRU node's bookkeeping because of the extra frequency dictionary.

The paper's layout: a list of frequencies

Shah, Mitra and Matani's paper (dated 16 August 2010, posted to arXiv in 2021) organises the same idea differently. Instead of a dictionary from frequency to list plus a min_freq integer, it keeps a doubly linked list of frequency nodes in increasing order, each frequency node pointing to a list of the items that have that frequency; a hash table maps keys to item nodes, and each item points back to its frequency node. An access moves an item from its frequency node to the next one — creating it if the next node's frequency is not exactly f + 1, deleting the old one if it empties. The minimum is simply the head of the frequency list. The two designs have the same complexity; the paper's version also supports deletion in O(1) without any stale minimum, at the cost of more pointer bookkeeping.

Why frequency alone is a poor predictor over time

LFU estimates future access probability by past access count, which assumes popularity is stationary. Real workloads drift: items have lifecycles, and a key's count from last week says little about next minute. The shifting trace shows the cost — LFU's hit rate falls to 83% while LRU stays near 99% — and the mechanism: new keys enter at frequency 1 and compete only with each other, because every old key has a higher count. Ageing fixes this by making counts forget: halving all counts every W accesses gives each access an exponentially decaying weight, so frequency becomes "recent frequency". Redis decays its counters by a configured amount of time (lfu-decay-time, default one minute) when a key is sampled; TinyLFU halves its sketch's counters after a sample period.

Approximate LFU: counting in a few bits

Storing an exact count per key costs 4-8 bytes and a pointer-heavy structure to keep keys ordered. Redis instead keeps an 8-bit logarithmic counter per key — a Morris counter, which increments with probability that falls as the counter grows, so 255 can represent around a million accesses with the default log factor of 10 — and does not maintain any ordering at all: at eviction time it samples a few keys and evicts the one with the lowest counter, with a pool of good candidates carried between evictions. Its documentation's table shows the trade-off the log factor controls: with factor 10, 100 hits read as about 10, 1,000 as 18, and 100,000 as 142, so the counter distinguishes low counts well and saturates around a million. Sampling makes eviction approximate but O(1) without any lists, and the counter fits in bits the key object already has.

W-TinyLFU: frequency for admission, recency for eviction

The TinyLFU paper (Einziger, Friedman and Manes) separates two decisions that LFU merges: admission (should this new item enter the cache at all?) and eviction (which item leaves?). A compact frequency sketch — a count-min sketch with small counters, periodically halved — estimates how often each key has been requested recently, including keys not in the cache. When a new key would evict a victim, it is admitted only if its estimated frequency exceeds the victim's. Caffeine's W-TinyLFU adds a small LRU "window" in front, so brand-new keys get a short chance to build up frequency. The result keeps LFU's scan resistance and LRU's adaptability, which is why it became a default in high-performance caches.

Frequency ties and the role of recency

In a cache dominated by one-off keys, most entries sit at frequency 1, so the frequency-1 list behaves exactly like an LRU list: the oldest one-off key is evicted first. LFU is therefore "LRU for the cold keys, protection for the warm ones". That observation explains both its scan resistance (cold keys only compete among themselves) and why tie-breaking by recency rather than arbitrarily matters so much in practice: most evictions happen inside the largest tie.

Interview framing: complexity versus usefulness

The O(1) LFU is a beautiful data-structure answer, and the interviewer will usually push next on whether you would use it. A strong close: exact LFU is right when popularity is stable and the cache is small enough for its pointer overhead; for general caches use LRU or an approximate, ageing frequency policy; and measure on replayed traffic, because a ten-point hit-rate difference in either direction is common and depends entirely on the workload.

How LFU behaves in production

Cache pollution after a change in popularity. Symptom: hit rate drifts down for days after a catalogue refresh or a traffic shift. Cause: old keys with large counts occupy slots no one needs. Detection: age of the least recently accessed key among high-frequency keys; hit rate on recently created keys. Fix: ageing or decay, or switch policy.

New content never sticks. Symptom: freshly published items are cache misses every time until they suddenly become hot. Cause: new keys at frequency 1 evict each other. Fix: an admission window (W-TinyLFU), or seed new items' counts.

Lock contention. Symptom: CPU time in the cache lock rises with traffic. Cause: every hit mutates the structure. Fix: sharding, or batching access records and applying them asynchronously, or approximate sampled eviction that needs no ordering on hits.

Memory overhead. Symptom: the cache holds fewer entries than expected for its memory budget. Cause: per-entry pointers and frequency bookkeeping, significant when values are small. Fix: approximate counters in spare bits, as Redis does.

Choosing the policy without measuring. Symptom: a policy change that looked obviously right (switching to LFU to resist scans) lowers the hit rate. Cause: the workload's popularity shifts faster than anyone assumed. Fix: replay a day of real access logs through both policies at the production capacity before switching — a cache simulator is a few dozen lines, as this lesson's hit_rate function shows — and keep the replay as a regression test when traffic patterns change.

Thundering herds on eviction of hot keys. Symptom: latency spikes on the backing store when a very hot key is evicted and hundreds of requests miss at once. Cause: under LRU a scan can evict even the hottest key; under LFU it is rare but possible after ageing. Fix: request coalescing (one fetch per key in flight) regardless of policy.

What to measure

Hit rate overall and for recently inserted keys; eviction rate; the distribution of frequencies among evicted keys (evicting high-frequency keys means the cache is too small; evicting only frequency-1 keys means one-off traffic is being correctly absorbed); time since last access of resident keys; lock wait time; and memory per entry.

Knobs

Capacity; ageing interval or decay time; for logarithmic counters, the log factor (resolution at low counts versus range at high counts); sample size for sampled eviction (Redis's maxmemory-samples, default 5); admission window size for W-TinyLFU; and the choice of policy itself, which should be revisited when the workload changes.

The real references

Redis documents both of its approximations. Its LRU samples a few keys (maxmemory-samples, default 5) and, since 3.0, keeps a pool of good candidates. Its LFU mode, available since Redis 4.0 as allkeys-lfu and volatile-lfu, approximates frequency with a Morris counter using a few bits per object, combined with a decay period so that the counter is reduced over time and the algorithm adapts to shifts in the access pattern; by default the counter saturates around one million requests (lfu-log-factor 10) and decays every minute (lfu-decay-time 1). The O(1) exact algorithm is from Shah, Mitra and Matani; TinyLFU's sketch-based admission is from Einziger, Friedman and Manes.

Trace it

Capacity 3. Operations: put(a), put(b), put(c), get(a), get(a), get(b), put(d), get(c), get(d), put(e), get(b). Which keys are evicted, and what is in the cache at the end with their frequencies?

Answer

After three puts: a, b, c at frequency 1, min 1. get(a) twice: a = 3. get(b): b = 2. List 1 = [c], list 2 = [b], list 3 = [a]. put(d): evict head of list 1 = c; d enters at 1, min 1. get(c): miss. get(d): d = 2; list 1 empties and was the minimum, so min = 2; list 2 = [b, d]. put(e): evict head of list 2 = b (tied with d at 2, b less recent); e enters at 1. get(b): miss. Final: a = 3, d = 2, e = 1. Evicted: c, then b.

Decide it

You run a cache in front of a recommendation model's feature store. 80% of lookups are for about 5,000 active users' feature vectors, stable over weeks; a nightly batch job reads all 20 million users' vectors once; the cache holds 50,000 entries. LRU, LFU, or something else?

Answer

LRU would be flushed every night by the batch job: 20 million one-off reads push the 5,000 hot users out, and the morning starts cold. Exact LFU would protect them — batch keys enter at frequency 1 and evict only each other — but would slowly accumulate stale users who were active months ago. Best: an LFU-style policy with ageing (or W-TinyLFU), or simply route the batch job around the cache (it gains nothing from caching single reads). If the batch must go through the cache, an admission policy that rejects keys with no prior frequency is the cleanest fix.
