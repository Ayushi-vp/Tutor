A desktop trading application has a dozen components that care about prices: the chart, the order ticket, the risk panel, an alerting module, a log. At first the price feed calls each of them directly. Every new feature means editing the feed. Then someone adds an event bus — components subscribe to prices.*, the feed publishes — and it works until the market opens. The risk panel's handler takes 200 milliseconds per update, and because handlers run inside the feed's thread, prices on the chart freeze. A plug-in's handler throws an exception on a malformed symbol, and every subscriber after it in the list stops receiving prices. Closing a window unsubscribes its chart while the feed is mid-publish, and the closed chart's handler is called once more on freed state and crashes the app.

The in-process event bus is a standard machine-coding round because it compresses several classic problems into one small class: pattern matching (topics with wildcards), concurrency (multiple publishers, asynchronous subscribers), flow control (what to do when a subscriber falls behind), fault isolation (one bad listener must not break the others), and the subtle lifecycle question every observer system faces — what exactly does "unsubscribed" guarantee when other threads are publishing at the same moment?

This lesson builds the bus in stages: requirements and entities; a naive list of callbacks and its failures; topic matching with AMQP-style wildcards, first by recursion and then with a trie; publishing from a snapshot without holding the lock; synchronous and asynchronous subscribers with per-subscriber ordering; bounded queues with block, drop-newest and drop-oldest policies; error isolation with dead letters; and an unsubscribe that, once it returns, guarantees the handler will never be called again — even with three threads publishing. The full solution is a runnable Python 3.12 file with nine tests, including the RabbitMQ tutorial's routing examples, a trie checked against a reference matcher on 500 random topics, ordering across four publishers, and a churn test with eight publishers and four threads subscribing and unsubscribing continuously.

The analogy: an office's internal mail room

Departments in a large office send memos labelled with a subject line like orders.europe.created. Staff register interests with the mail room: "send me anything about orders.europe.*" or "everything under orders.#". The mail room keeps a directory of interests organised by subject words, so it can find everyone interested in a memo without reading the whole list. Some staff want memos handed to them immediately, while the sender waits at the desk (synchronous); others have an in-tray that a clerk fills and they work through at their own pace (asynchronous). In-trays have limited room: when one is full, the mail room either makes the sender wait, throws away the new memo, or throws away the oldest memo in the tray, depending on what that recipient asked for. If a recipient refuses a memo angrily, the mail room notes it in a log and carries on delivering to everyone else. And when someone cancels their interest, the mail room promises: once the cancellation is confirmed, nothing more will reach them — not even a memo that was already on its way down the corridor.

The directory organised by subject words is the trie; "*" and "#" are the wildcards; handing over while the sender waits is synchronous delivery; the in-tray is a bounded queue with a worker; the three full-tray rules are back-pressure policies; the log of refusals is the dead-letter list; the cancellation promise is safe unsubscribe.

The analogy breaks in one important place. In a real office the person delivering a memo cannot be in two places at once. In a program, a publisher in one thread can be calling a handler at the very moment another thread cancels it. Making the cancellation promise true is where the locking design earns its keep.

Inside one process, not between services

This round is about the bus inside a program — a desktop application, a game engine, a single backend service whose modules should not import each other. Everything lives in one memory space, so delivery is a function call or a queue put, events are ordinary objects, and nothing survives a restart. Keeping that scope clear early in the interview avoids a detour into brokers, persistence and network failures, which belong to a different question (sc10). The in-process version still has all the hard parts that make concurrency rounds interesting: shared mutable routing state, callbacks into unknown code, and threads that start and stop while events are in flight.

What the round expects

subscribe(pattern, handler, …) returning a handle, unsubscribe(handle), publish(topic, event), and close(); wildcard matching with a clear definition; synchronous and asynchronous modes; a stated ordering guarantee; a back-pressure policy for bounded queues; error isolation; thread safety, including unsubscribe during publish; and tests that exercise concurrency rather than only single-threaded calls.

1 · Clarify the requirements

Ask: what do topics look like, and which wildcard syntax? Must publish wait for handlers, or return immediately? What ordering is required — per subscriber, per publisher, global? What should happen when a subscriber cannot keep up? What happens when a handler throws? Can handlers subscribe, unsubscribe or publish from inside a callback? What exactly does unsubscribe guarantee? How is the bus shut down? Agreed: dot-separated topics with AMQP (Advanced Message Queuing Protocol, the standard RabbitMQ implements) semantics ('*' one word, '#' zero or more); per-subscription choice of synchronous or asynchronous delivery; FIFO per subscriber per publisher; bounded async queues with a per-subscription overflow policy; handler errors isolated and recorded; handlers may unsubscribe themselves; after unsubscribe returns, the handler is never called again; close(drain) delivers or drops what is queued.

The handle returned by subscribe is the subscription itself, so unsubscribing needs no lookup by handler (handlers are often lambdas with no identity worth comparing). publish returns how many subscribers accepted the event, which makes dropped events visible to callers and tests.

3 · Stage one: a list of callbacks

class NaiveBus:
    def __init__(self): self.subs, self.lock = [], threading.Lock()
    def subscribe(self, topic, h):
        with self.lock: self.subs.append((topic, h))
    def publish(self, topic, e):
        with self.lock:
            for t, h in self.subs:
                if t == topic: h(topic, e)       # handler runs under the lock

Every failure from the opening story is here. Handlers run under the bus lock: a slow handler blocks every publisher and every subscribe call, and a handler that subscribes or publishes deadlocks immediately (the lock is not re-entrant). An exception in one handler propagates out of publish and skips the rest. There is no wildcard matching and no way to deliver asynchronously. And if subscribe did not take the lock, appending to the list during iteration in another thread would be a data race. The fixes below address each.

4 · Stage two: topics and wildcards

The rules. Following RabbitMQ's topic exchange, a topic is a list of words separated by dots; in a subscription pattern, * stands for exactly one word and # for zero or more words. Using the RabbitMQ tutorial's examples, which the first test replays exactly: *.orange.* matches quick.orange.rabbit and lazy.orange.elephant but not orange or quick.orange.new.rabbit; *.*.rabbit matches quick.orange.rabbit; lazy.# matches lazy.brown.fox, lazy.orange.new.rabbit and plain lazy (zero words). lazy.pink.rabbit matches two of one queue's patterns but is delivered to it once. Validation rejects empty words (a..b), wildcards inside words (a.b*), and wildcards in published topics.

A reference matcher. The rules are easiest to state as recursion over word lists: an empty pattern matches only an empty topic; a pattern starting with # matches if the rest of the pattern matches the topic with any number (0 to all) of leading words removed; otherwise the first pattern word must be * or equal to the first topic word, and the rest must match the rest. This takes ten lines, is obviously correct, and becomes the test oracle.

The trie. Checking every subscription's pattern on every publish costs O(subscriptions × topic length). A trie shares work between patterns: each node's children are keyed by word, with * and # as ordinary keys, and subscriptions sit at the node where their pattern ends. To match topic words w0…wn−1, walk from the root with an index i: if the node has a # child, recurse into it for every j from i to n (the # consumes wi…wj−1, possibly nothing); if i = n, collect the node's subscriptions; otherwise recurse into the child for wi and the * child with i + 1. Matching orders.eu.created against the patterns orders.*.created, orders.# and #.created: from the root, the orders branch leads to a * node (consuming "eu") and then a created node where the first subscription sits; its # child, entered with i = 1, absorbs "eu.created"; and the root's # child, entered with j = 2, leaves "created" to match its created child. Results are collected in a dictionary by subscription id, so a subscription reachable two ways is delivered once. remove prunes nodes left with no subscriptions and no children, which the stress test checks (after heavy churn, the trie is empty). The second test compares the trie with the reference matcher on 300 random patterns and 500 random topics.

5 · Stage three: publish from a snapshot

publish validates the topic, takes the bus lock just long enough to run trie.match and copy the result (sorted by subscription id, so synchronous handlers run in subscription order), releases the lock, and only then delivers. Holding the bus lock only for the routing lookup means slow handlers never block other publishers or subscribe calls, and handlers can call subscribe, unsubscribe or publish without deadlocking on the bus lock. The price is that the snapshot can be stale: a subscription removed a microsecond after the snapshot is still in the list. Stage six turns that staleness into a non-issue.

6 · Stage four: synchronous and asynchronous delivery

Synchronous subscribers are called in the publisher's thread, one after another. Each call happens inside a try: an exception is recorded in dead_letters as (topic, event, subscription id, "ZeroDivisionError: division by zero") and delivery continues with the next subscriber. The test subscribes three handlers to x, the middle one dividing by zero: the first and third receive both events in order, and two dead letters are recorded.

Asynchronous subscribers each get a queue.Queue(maxsize) and a dedicated daemon worker thread that loops: take the next item, call the handler (with the same error isolation), repeat until a stop sentinel arrives. One queue and one worker per subscriber is what gives FIFO order: events from one publisher enter the queue in publish order and leave it in the same order. The test runs four publisher threads each publishing 1,000 numbered events to seq.pN and checks that, for each publisher, the subscriber received 0…999 in order — while the four streams interleave arbitrarily, as expected.

7 · Stage five: back-pressure

A bounded queue fills when the subscriber is slower than the publishers. Three policies, chosen per subscription:

PolicyWhen fullLoses events?Publisher affected?Use for
BLOCKpublisher waits up to a timeout, then BackPressureNo (error instead)Yes, slowed or failedCommands that must be handled
DROP_NEWESTincoming event discardedYes, newestNoWork where the backlog matters more
DROP_OLDESToldest queued event discarded, new one enqueuedYes, oldestNoPrices, telemetry, UI state

The test blocks three consumers behind a gate and publishes ten events to each kind. Drop-newest and drop-oldest each record at least six drops (the queue holds three, one may be in the worker's hands); after the gate opens, the drop-oldest subscriber's last three events are 7, 8, 9 — the newest survived. The blocking subscriber, with a queue of two, accepts event 0 (taken by the worker), then 1 and 2 (queued); publishing 3 waits 50 ms and raises BackPressure: subscriber … is 2 events behind. Each subscription counts its drops, so monitoring can see which consumers are falling behind.

8 · Stage six: unsubscribe while publishing

The race. Thread P takes a snapshot containing subscription S and is about to call S's handler. Thread U calls unsubscribe(S), removes it from the trie, returns — and the component that owned S frees its resources. Then P calls the handler on freed state. A guarantee is needed: once unsubscribe returns, the handler is not running and will never be called again.

The mechanism. Each subscription has an active flag and a call_lock. Every delivery — synchronous in the publisher's thread, or asynchronous in the worker — takes the call lock, checks active, and only then calls the handler, releasing the lock after it returns. unsubscribe first removes the subscription from the table and trie under the bus lock (no new snapshots will include it), then takes the call lock — which waits for any in-flight call to finish — and sets active = False. Any stale snapshot that still holds S now finds it inactive and skips it. For an asynchronous subscriber, unsubscribe also discards its queued events and stops its worker. The test runs three publisher threads hammering one subscriber, unsubscribes it, then sets a flag; the handler records any call that sees the flag. Run for both delivery modes, it records none.

Unsubscribing yourself. A handler that unsubscribes its own subscription — "handle the first event only" — already holds the call lock. With a plain lock that would deadlock; the call lock is a re-entrant RLock, so the same thread acquires it again, sets the flag, and the current call simply finishes. For an asynchronous subscriber, the worker cannot join itself, so _drain_and_stop skips the join when called from the worker thread; the worker exits after the current call. The test checks both modes deliver exactly one event.

The remaining hazard. If handler A, running in one thread, unsubscribes B while B's handler, running in another, unsubscribes A, each waits for the other's call lock: a deadlock (os6's circular wait). It is rare and worth stating; the remedy is to avoid unsubscribing other subscriptions from inside handlers, or to defer it until the handler returns.

9 · Stage seven: shutdown and the stress test

close(drain=True) marks the bus closed (new publishes and subscribes fail), then for every asynchronous subscriber enqueues a stop sentinel behind its pending events and joins its worker, so everything already accepted is delivered; close(drain=False) discards pending events first. The churn test runs eight publishers sending 2,000 events each to random topics while four threads repeatedly subscribe a random pattern in a random mode with drop-newest queues, sleep a fraction of a millisecond, and unsubscribe. It requires no exceptions anywhere and, at the end, an empty subscription table and an empty trie — proving remove prunes correctly under concurrency.

10 · The full solution

Nine tests in about a second, stable across repeated runs. Python 3.12, standard library only.

"""An in-process pub-sub event bus: dot-separated topics with AMQP-style wildcards ('*' = exactly one
word, '#' = zero or more) matched through a trie, synchronous and asynchronous subscribers, FIFO
order per subscriber, bounded queues with back-pressure policies, error isolation with a dead-letter
record, and unsubscribe that is safe while publishing (once it returns, the handler is never called
again). Python 3.12, standard library only.  Run: python event_bus.py"""
from __future__ import annotations

import itertools
import queue
import random
import threading
import time
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from enum import Enum
from typing import Any, Callable

Handler = Callable[[str, Any], None]


class Overflow(Enum):
    BLOCK = "block"                 # publisher waits up to a timeout, then BackPressure
    DROP_NEWEST = "drop_newest"     # discard the event being published
    DROP_OLDEST = "drop_oldest"     # discard the oldest queued event to make room


class BackPressure(Exception):
    pass


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- topic matching
def _valid(pattern: str, allow_wildcards: bool) -> list[str]:
    words = pattern.split(".")
    if not pattern or any(w == "" for w in words):
        raise ValueError(f"bad topic {pattern!r}: empty word")
    if not allow_wildcards and any(w in ("*", "#") for w in words):
        raise ValueError(f"wildcards are not allowed in published topics: {pattern!r}")
    if any(("*" in w or "#" in w) and w not in ("*", "#") for w in words):
        raise ValueError(f"a wildcard must be a whole word: {pattern!r}")
    return words


class _TrieNode:
    __slots__ = ("children", "subs")

    def __init__(self) -> None:
        self.children: dict[str, _TrieNode] = {}
        self.subs: dict[int, "Subscription"] = {}


class TopicTrie:
    def __init__(self) -> None:
        self.root = _TrieNode()

    def add(self, words: list[str], sub: "Subscription") -> None:
        node = self.root
        for w in words:
            node = node.children.setdefault(w, _TrieNode())
        node.subs[sub.id] = sub

    def remove(self, words: list[str], sub_id: int) -> None:
        path, node = [], self.root
        for w in words:
            path.append((node, w))
            node = node.children[w]
        node.subs.pop(sub_id, None)
        for parent, w in reversed(path):                    # prune empty branches
            child = parent.children[w]
            if child.subs or child.children:
                break
            del parent.children[w]

    def match(self, words: list[str]) -> dict[int, "Subscription"]:
        found: dict[int, Subscription] = {}

        def walk(node: _TrieNode, i: int) -> None:
            hash_child = node.children.get("#")
            if hash_child is not None:                       # '#' consumes words i..j for every j >= i
                for j in range(i, len(words) + 1):
                    walk(hash_child, j)
            if i == len(words):
                found.update(node.subs)
                return
            for key in (words[i], "*"):
                child = node.children.get(key)
                if child is not None:
                    walk(child, i + 1)
        walk(self.root, 0)
        return found                                         # a subscription matched twice is delivered once


def reference_match(pattern: list[str], topic: list[str]) -> bool:
    """Reference matcher for tests: the rules written as plain recursion, obviously correct."""
    if not pattern:
        return not topic
    if pattern[0] == "#":
        return any(reference_match(pattern[1:], topic[i:]) for i in range(len(topic) + 1))
    if not topic:
        return False
    return pattern[0] in ("*", topic[0]) and reference_match(pattern[1:], topic[1:])


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- subscriptions
@dataclass(eq=False)
class Subscription:
    id: int
    pattern: str
    handler: Handler
    asynchronous: bool
    overflow: Overflow
    maxsize: int
    block_timeout: float
    active: bool = True
    call_lock: threading.RLock = field(default_factory=threading.RLock)   # held while the handler runs
    q: queue.Queue | None = None
    worker: threading.Thread | None = None
    delivered: int = 0
    dropped: int = 0


_STOP = object()


class EventBus:
    def __init__(self) -> None:
        self._lock = threading.Lock()              # protects the trie and the subscription table
        self._trie = TopicTrie()
        self._subs: dict[int, Subscription] = {}
        self._ids = itertools.count(1)
        self.dead_letters: list[tuple[str, Any, int, str]] = []   # (topic, event, sub id, error)
        self._dl_lock = threading.Lock()
        self._closed = False

    # ---- subscribe / unsubscribe
    def subscribe(self, pattern: str, handler: Handler, *, asynchronous: bool = False,
                  maxsize: int = 1000, overflow: Overflow = Overflow.BLOCK,
                  block_timeout: float = 1.0) -> Subscription:
        words = _valid(pattern, allow_wildcards=True)
        sub = Subscription(next(self._ids), pattern, handler, asynchronous, overflow, maxsize, block_timeout)
        if asynchronous:
            sub.q = queue.Queue(maxsize=maxsize)
            sub.worker = threading.Thread(target=self._run_worker, args=(sub,), daemon=True,
                                          name=f"bus-sub-{sub.id}")
            sub.worker.start()
        with self._lock:
            if self._closed:
                raise RuntimeError("bus is closed")
            self._trie.add(words, sub)
            self._subs[sub.id] = sub
        return sub

    def unsubscribe(self, sub: Subscription) -> None:
        """After this returns, the handler is not running and will never be called again
        (unless called from inside the handler itself, where the current call simply finishes)."""
        with self._lock:
            if self._subs.pop(sub.id, None) is None:
                return
            self._trie.remove(sub.pattern.split("."), sub.id)
        with sub.call_lock:                         # waits for an in-flight call in another thread
            sub.active = False
        if sub.asynchronous:
            self._drain_and_stop(sub)

    # ---- publish
    def publish(self, topic: str, event: Any) -> int:
        """Deliver to every matching subscription. Returns how many accepted the event."""
        words = _valid(topic, allow_wildcards=False)
        with self._lock:
            if self._closed:
                raise RuntimeError("bus is closed")
            targets = sorted(self._trie.match(words).values(), key=lambda s: s.id)   # snapshot
        accepted = 0
        for sub in targets:                         # the lock is NOT held while handlers run
            if sub.asynchronous:
                accepted += self._enqueue(sub, topic, event)
            else:
                accepted += self._call(sub, topic, event)
        return accepted

    def _enqueue(self, sub: Subscription, topic: str, event: Any) -> int:
        if not sub.active:
            return 0
        item = (topic, event)
        if sub.overflow is Overflow.BLOCK:
            try:
                sub.q.put(item, timeout=sub.block_timeout)
                return 1
            except queue.Full:
                raise BackPressure(f"subscriber {sub.id} ({sub.pattern}) is {sub.maxsize} events behind")
        try:
            sub.q.put_nowait(item)
            return 1
        except queue.Full:
            if sub.overflow is Overflow.DROP_NEWEST:
                sub.dropped += 1
                return 0
            while True:                             # DROP_OLDEST: make room, retry
                try:
                    sub.q.get_nowait()
                    sub.dropped += 1
                except queue.Empty:
                    pass
                try:
                    sub.q.put_nowait(item)
                    return 1
                except queue.Full:
                    continue

    def _call(self, sub: Subscription, topic: str, event: Any) -> int:
        with sub.call_lock:
            if not sub.active:                       # unsubscribed after the snapshot was taken
                return 0
            try:
                sub.handler(topic, event)
                sub.delivered += 1
            except Exception as e:                   # isolate: one bad handler cannot hurt others
                with self._dl_lock:
                    self.dead_letters.append((topic, event, sub.id, f"{type(e).__name__}: {e}"))
            return 1

    def _run_worker(self, sub: Subscription) -> None:
        while True:
            item = sub.q.get()
            if item is _STOP:
                return
            self._call(sub, *item)

    def _drain_and_stop(self, sub: Subscription, deliver: bool = False) -> None:
        if not deliver:                              # discard what is queued
            while True:
                try:
                    sub.q.get_nowait()
                except queue.Empty:
                    break
        sub.q.put(_STOP)
        if threading.current_thread() is not sub.worker:
            sub.worker.join()

    def close(self, drain: bool = True) -> None:
        """Stop accepting events; deliver (or drop) what async subscribers still have queued."""
        with self._lock:
            self._closed = True
            subs = list(self._subs.values())
        for sub in subs:
            if sub.asynchronous:
                self._drain_and_stop(sub, deliver=drain)


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- tests
def collector():
    got, lock = [], threading.Lock()

    def handler(topic, event):
        with lock:
            got.append((topic, event))
    return got, handler


def test_wildcards_follow_amqp_rules() -> None:
    bus, results = EventBus(), {}
    for p in ["*.orange.*", "*.*.rabbit", "lazy.#", "#", "orders.#.created", "a.b"]:
        got, h = collector()
        bus.subscribe(p, h)
        results[p] = got
    for t in ["quick.orange.rabbit", "lazy.orange.elephant", "quick.orange.fox", "lazy.brown.fox",
              "lazy.pink.rabbit", "quick.brown.fox", "orange", "quick.orange.new.rabbit",
              "lazy.orange.new.rabbit", "lazy", "orders.created", "orders.eu.vip.created", "a.b"]:
        bus.publish(t, None)
    topics = {p: [t for t, _ in g] for p, g in results.items()}
    assert topics["*.orange.*"] == ["quick.orange.rabbit", "lazy.orange.elephant", "quick.orange.fox"]
    assert topics["*.*.rabbit"] == ["quick.orange.rabbit", "lazy.pink.rabbit"]
    assert topics["lazy.#"] == ["lazy.orange.elephant", "lazy.brown.fox", "lazy.pink.rabbit",
                                "lazy.orange.new.rabbit", "lazy"]          # '#' matches zero words too
    assert len(topics["#"]) == 13
    assert topics["orders.#.created"] == ["orders.created", "orders.eu.vip.created"]
    assert topics["a.b"] == ["a.b"]


def test_trie_agrees_with_reference_matcher() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(5)
    vocab = ["a", "b", "c"]
    patterns = {".".join(rng.choice(vocab + ["*", "#"]) for _ in range(rng.randint(1, 4))) for _ in range(300)}
    trie, subs = TopicTrie(), {}
    for i, p in enumerate(sorted(patterns), 1):
        s = Subscription(i, p, lambda t, e: None, False, Overflow.BLOCK, 1, 1.0)
        trie.add(p.split("."), s)
        subs[i] = p
    for _ in range(500):
        topic = ".".join(rng.choice(vocab) for _ in range(rng.randint(1, 5)))
        expected = {i for i, p in subs.items() if reference_match(p.split("."), topic.split("."))}
        assert set(trie.match(topic.split("."))) == expected, topic


def test_sync_order_and_error_isolation() -> None:
    bus, order = EventBus(), []
    bus.subscribe("x", lambda t, e: order.append(("first", e)))
    bus.subscribe("x", lambda t, e: 1 / 0)
    bus.subscribe("x", lambda t, e: order.append(("third", e)))
    assert bus.publish("x", 1) == 3 and bus.publish("x", 2) == 3
    assert order == [("first", 1), ("third", 1), ("first", 2), ("third", 2)]
    assert [d[3] for d in bus.dead_letters] == ["ZeroDivisionError: division by zero"] * 2


def test_async_preserves_per_subscriber_order_per_publisher() -> None:
    bus = EventBus()
    got, h = collector()
    bus.subscribe("seq.#", h, asynchronous=True, maxsize=10_000)

    def publisher(p: int) -> None:
        for i in range(1000):
            bus.publish(f"seq.p{p}", i)
    ts = [threading.Thread(target=publisher, args=(p,)) for p in range(4)]
    for t in ts: t.start()
    for t in ts: t.join()
    bus.close(drain=True)
    assert len(got) == 4000
    for p in range(4):
        assert [e for t, e in got if t == f"seq.p{p}"] == list(range(1000))   # FIFO per publisher


def test_back_pressure_policies() -> None:
    gate = threading.Event()

    def slow(t, e):
        gate.wait()                                  # consumer stuck until the gate opens
    bus = EventBus()
    newest = bus.subscribe("n", slow, asynchronous=True, maxsize=3, overflow=Overflow.DROP_NEWEST)
    oldest_got: list[int] = []
    oldest = bus.subscribe("o", lambda t, e: (gate.wait(), oldest_got.append(e)), asynchronous=True,
                           maxsize=3, overflow=Overflow.DROP_OLDEST)
    blocking = bus.subscribe("b", slow, asynchronous=True, maxsize=2, overflow=Overflow.BLOCK, block_timeout=0.05)
    for i in range(10):
        bus.publish("n", i)
        bus.publish("o", i)
    assert newest.dropped >= 6 and oldest.dropped >= 6
    bus.publish("b", 0); time.sleep(0.05); bus.publish("b", 1); bus.publish("b", 2)   # worker holds 0; queue 1,2
    try:
        bus.publish("b", 3)
    except BackPressure as e:
        assert "2 events behind" in str(e)
    else:
        raise AssertionError("no back-pressure")
    gate.set()
    bus.close(drain=True)
    assert oldest_got[-3:] == [7, 8, 9]              # the newest events survived


def test_unsubscribe_inside_own_handler() -> None:
    bus, calls = EventBus(), []
    sub = None

    def once(t, e):
        calls.append(e)
        bus.unsubscribe(sub)                          # re-entrant: allowed, no deadlock
    sub = bus.subscribe("x", once)
    bus.publish("x", 1); bus.publish("x", 2)
    assert calls == [1]
    asub = None

    def aonce(t, e):
        calls.append(("a", e))
        bus.unsubscribe(asub)
    asub = bus.subscribe("y", aonce, asynchronous=True)
    bus.publish("y", 1); bus.publish("y", 2)
    asub.worker.join(timeout=2)
    assert calls == [1, ("a", 1)] and not asub.worker.is_alive()


def test_no_call_after_unsubscribe_returns() -> None:
    for asynchronous in (False, True):
        bus, done, violations = EventBus(), threading.Event(), []
        sub = None

        def h(t, e):
            if done.is_set():
                violations.append(e)
            time.sleep(0.0005)
        sub = bus.subscribe("t", h, asynchronous=asynchronous, maxsize=100_000)
        stop = threading.Event()

        def publisher() -> None:
            i = 0
            while not stop.is_set():
                bus.publish("t", i)
                i += 1
        ps = [threading.Thread(target=publisher) for _ in range(3)]
        for p in ps: p.start()
        time.sleep(0.05)
        bus.unsubscribe(sub)
        done.set()                                     # anything after this is a violation
        time.sleep(0.05)
        stop.set()
        for p in ps: p.join()
        assert violations == [], (asynchronous, violations[:3])


def test_stress_churn() -> None:
    bus, errors, stop = EventBus(), [], threading.Event()
    totals = [0]
    lock = threading.Lock()

    def h(t, e):
        with lock:
            totals[0] += 1

    def churn(seed: int) -> None:
        rng = random.Random(seed)
        try:
            while not stop.is_set():
                s = bus.subscribe(rng.choice(["s.*", "s.#", "#", "s.a"]), h,
                                  asynchronous=rng.random() < 0.5, overflow=Overflow.DROP_NEWEST, maxsize=50)
                time.sleep(rng.random() / 1000)
                bus.unsubscribe(s)
        except Exception as e:      # pragma: no cover
            errors.append(e)

    def pub(seed: int) -> None:
        rng = random.Random(seed)
        try:
            for i in range(2000):
                bus.publish(rng.choice(["s.a", "s.b", "t"]), i)
        except Exception as e:      # pragma: no cover
            errors.append(e)
    cs = [threading.Thread(target=churn, args=(s,)) for s in range(4)]
    ps = [threading.Thread(target=pub, args=(s,)) for s in range(8)]
    for t in cs + ps: t.start()
    for t in ps: t.join()
    stop.set()
    for t in cs: t.join()
    assert not errors and bus._subs == {} and bus._trie.root.children == {}   # everything pruned


def test_rejects_bad_topics() -> None:

11 · Edge cases

The same handler subscribed twice gets two subscriptions and two deliveries; the handle, not the function, identifies a subscription. Overlapping patterns on one subscription cannot happen (one pattern per subscription), but one subscriber with two subscriptions matching the same topic receives the event twice — by design. Unsubscribe twice: the second call finds nothing and returns. Publish after close: raises. A handler that blocks forever: a synchronous one blocks its publisher; an asynchronous one blocks only its own queue, which then applies its overflow policy — and unsubscribe on it would wait forever for the call lock, so long-running handlers need their own timeouts. Events mutated by handlers: all subscribers receive the same object; pass immutable events (frozen dataclasses) or copies.

12 · Extensions the interviewer will ask for

Priorities: order synchronous subscribers by a priority field rather than subscription order. Filters: a predicate on the event in addition to the topic pattern. Re-entrant publish: queue events published from inside synchronous handlers and deliver them after the outer publish, giving breadth-first order and preventing unbounded recursion. Retry and dead-letter replay: retry failed deliveries a few times with backoff before recording a dead letter, and offer a way to replay dead letters after a fix. A shared worker pool instead of a thread per async subscriber, keeping per-subscriber order by never running two events of one subscriber at once (a per-subscriber "busy" flag and pending queue). asyncio: the same design with asyncio.Queue and tasks; cancellation replaces the stop sentinel. Match caching: memoise trie.match results per topic, invalidated on subscribe and unsubscribe, when the set of topics is small and publishes are frequent.

13 · Complexity

Subscribe and unsubscribe: O(p) trie work for a pattern of p words, plus thread start or join for asynchronous subscriptions. Publish: matching costs O(t) per path for a topic of t words, multiplied by the branching from '*' children and the up-to-t+1 splits at each '#' node — in practice small, and independent of the total number of subscriptions — plus O(k log k) to sort k matches, plus delivery. Synchronous delivery cost is the sum of handler times; asynchronous delivery is O(1) per subscriber (a queue put). Memory: O(total pattern words) for the trie, O(maxsize) per asynchronous queue, one thread per asynchronous subscriber.

The two locks, and why there are two

One lock protects the routing table (the trie and the subscription map); another, per subscription, protects the act of calling its handler. They solve different problems with different contention profiles. The bus lock is held for microseconds — a trie walk or an insert — and serialises routing changes so the trie is never seen half-modified. The call lock is held for as long as a handler runs, but only contends with unsubscribe and with other deliveries to the same subscription, so a slow handler slows only itself. Combining them into one lock would bring back the naive bus's problems; dropping the call lock would break the unsubscribe guarantee. This "global lock for structure, per-object lock for use" split appears in connection pools, caches and schedulers too.

Copy-on-write as an alternative to snapshots

Instead of copying matches under a lock on every publish, a bus can keep its routing table immutable: subscribe and unsubscribe build a new table (a copy with the change) and swap a single reference to it; publish reads the current reference without any lock. Reads become lock-free and cheap; writes cost a copy of the table, which is fine when subscriptions change rarely and publishes are frequent — the common case for an event bus. In Python, swapping one attribute is atomic, so readers always see either the old or the new table. Many observer implementations in UI frameworks work exactly this way.

Ordering guarantees are a product decision

"In order" has several meanings. Per subscriber, per publisher (this bus): each listener sees each producer's events in sequence. Per key: all events about order 4471 arrive in sequence, even from different producers — which needs routing by key to a single queue, the way Kafka partitions work. Global total order: every subscriber sees all events in the same order — which needs a single sequencer and costs throughput (cd15 built one for an exchange). Stronger orders cost more concurrency. Ask which one the consumers actually need; most need per-key order at most.

Back-pressure propagates, or it does not

Blocking publishers propagates back-pressure upstream: if the slow consumer matters, the whole pipeline slows to its pace, which is correct for work that must be done and dangerous when the publisher is something that cannot slow down (a UI thread, a network reader that must keep up with a socket). Dropping policies stop propagation and lose data instead. A middle ground is to give critical and non-critical subscribers different policies — the risk engine blocks, the chart drops oldest — so one slow, unimportant listener can never stall the feed. Recording drops per subscriber turns invisible data loss into a metric.

Lifetimes: who keeps whom alive

A subscription holds a reference to its handler, and a handler that is a bound method or a closure holds a reference to its owner. So a component that subscribes and never unsubscribes is kept alive by the bus forever — a memory leak that also keeps receiving events. Three remedies are common. Tie the subscription to the component's lifecycle explicitly (unsubscribe in dispose, or return the subscription from a context manager). Hold handlers through weak references, so a garbage-collected owner silently drops out — convenient, but surprising when a lambda subscriber disappears immediately because nothing else referenced it. Or scope subscriptions to a parent object that unsubscribes all its children at once. Whichever you choose, the bus should expose a count of live subscriptions so leaks are visible.

In-process bus versus a message broker

An in-process bus is fast (a function call or a queue put), simple and needs no infrastructure, but its events vanish if the process dies, it cannot cross processes, and its back-pressure is limited to one machine's memory. A broker — RabbitMQ, Kafka, a cloud pub/sub service — adds durable storage, delivery across processes and machines, acknowledgements and redelivery, consumer groups and retention, at the cost of latency, operations and at-least-once semantics that make handlers idempotent. Many systems use both: an in-process bus inside each service for decoupling modules, and a broker between services. The concepts transfer directly; RabbitMQ's topic exchange is where the wildcard rules in this lesson come from.

How event buses fail in production

Frozen UI or stalled feed. Symptom: everything stops while one listener works. Cause: a slow synchronous handler on a thread that must not block, or a BLOCK policy on a slow asynchronous subscriber. Detection: handler duration histograms per subscription; time publishers spend blocked. Fix: make slow subscribers asynchronous with an appropriate overflow policy.

Silent loss. Symptom: a listener misses events intermittently. Cause: drop policies under load, with nobody watching the drop counters. Detection: export dropped per subscription and alert on it. Fix: larger queues, faster handlers, or a policy that matches the event's importance.

Crashes after closing a component. Symptom: rare errors in handlers referencing closed resources. Cause: unsubscribe that does not wait for in-flight calls, so a handler runs after its owner shut down. Fix: the call-lock guarantee.

Memory growth. Symptom: process memory rises steadily. Cause: unbounded queues behind a slow subscriber, or subscriptions never unsubscribed (components that subscribe on creation and forget to unsubscribe on disposal, keeping themselves alive through the handler closure). Detection: queue depths and subscription counts over time. Fix: bounded queues, and tying subscription lifetime to component lifetime (context managers, weak references).

Event storms. Symptom: CPU spikes as handlers publish events that trigger other handlers. Cause: cycles in event chains. Detection: events published per second by topic; recursion depth. Fix: break cycles, deduplicate, or queue nested publishes.

Thread explosion. Symptom: thousands of threads in a process that should have dozens. Cause: one worker thread per asynchronous subscriber, with subscribers created per request or per object. Fix: a shared worker pool that preserves per-subscriber order (only one event per subscriber in flight at a time), or limit asynchronous subscriptions to long-lived components.

What to measure

Events published per topic; deliveries, drops and dead letters per subscription; handler latency percentiles per subscription; queue depth per asynchronous subscriber; time publishers spend blocked by back-pressure; and the number of live subscriptions and worker threads.

Tracing helps too: attach a correlation id to each published event and log it on delivery, so a single user action can be followed through every handler it triggered — the in-process equivalent of distributed tracing, and the fastest way to answer "who reacted to this, and in what order?".

Knobs

Delivery mode per subscription; queue size and overflow policy per subscription; block timeout; whether nested publishes are delivered immediately or queued; whether failed deliveries are retried before becoming dead letters; and, at scale, a shared worker pool size instead of a thread per subscriber.

The real references

The wildcard semantics come from RabbitMQ's topic exchange, whose tutorial defines routing keys as dot-delimited words (up to 255 bytes), with * substituting for exactly one word and # for zero or more, and walks through the examples the first test replays — including that a message matching two of a queue's bindings is delivered to it once, and that lazy.# matches a four-word key. Python's standard library supplies the rest: queue.Queue provides the bounded FIFO with blocking puts and timeouts used for asynchronous subscribers, and threading.RLock the re-entrant call lock that lets a handler unsubscribe itself.

Design it

A trading terminal publishes price ticks at up to 5,000 per second on topics like prices.NASDAQ.AAPL. Subscribers: a chart (needs the latest price, redraws at 30 frames per second), a risk engine (must see every tick for its positions, takes 0.1 ms per tick), an audit log (must not lose anything, writes in batches), and third-party plug-ins of unknown quality. Choose patterns, delivery modes and overflow policies.

Answer

Chart: prices.*.* filtered to visible symbols, asynchronous with a tiny queue and DROP_OLDEST (it only needs the latest value; stale ticks are worthless) — or better, conflate to latest-per-symbol. Risk engine: subscribe only to its symbols (prices.NASDAQ.AAPL etc.), asynchronous with a large queue and BLOCK: 5,000 ticks at 0.1 ms is 0.5 s of work per second, so it keeps up, and if it ever cannot, slowing the feed is safer than missing ticks. Audit log: prices.#, asynchronous, large queue, BLOCK with an alert on queue depth, batch writes in the handler. Plug-ins: asynchronous only (never synchronous on the feed thread), modest queues with DROP_NEWEST, error isolation, and per-plug-in drop and dead-letter metrics so a bad plug-in is visible without hurting anyone else.

Diagnose it

After adding a "close tab" feature, a desktop app occasionally crashes with an error inside a chart handler that references a disposed canvas. The chart unsubscribes in its dispose() method before freeing the canvas. Prices are published from a background thread. What is happening and how do you fix it?

Answer

The publisher thread took a snapshot including the chart's subscription, then dispose() unsubscribed and freed the canvas, then the publisher called the handler from its stale snapshot — or the handler was mid-call when the canvas was freed. The bus's unsubscribe does not guarantee "no more calls". Fix in the bus: an active flag checked under a per-subscription call lock before each call, and unsubscribe that takes the call lock (waiting for an in-flight call) before returning. Then dispose() can safely free the canvas after unsubscribe returns. Alternatively, the handler can marshal to the UI thread and check a disposed flag there, but the bus-level guarantee fixes every subscriber at once.
