A team builds a retrieval-augmented chatbot over their company wiki. They split 200,000 pages into chunks, turn each chunk into an embedding (a list of 768 numbers produced by a model so that chunks with similar meaning have nearby lists), and need, for every user question, the ten chunks closest to the question's embedding. The first version compares the question with every chunk. It works in a notebook. Then the corpus grows to five million chunks, every question takes half a second just to search, product wants per-team permissions so people only see their own documents, the legal team asks for deleted documents to disappear immediately, and someone wants to try a faster library without rewriting the service.

Every one of those demands lands on one component: the vector index. It answers a single question, "which stored vectors are closest to this query vector?", but it must answer it quickly at scale, respect filters, handle deletes, and allow its internals to be replaced. That is why it has become the standard machine-coding round in AI-engineer interviews: it is small enough to build in 45 minutes, and it tests exactly the judgement the job needs.

The judgement is mostly about interfaces and measurement, not algorithms. Nobody expects you to write a production graph index from memory. The interviewer expects a small, honest interface; an exact implementation that is correct and fast enough for modest sizes; an approximate implementation behind the same interface; and, crucially, a way to measure how often the approximate one is wrong. Approximate indexes fail silently: they return ten plausible results with no error even when half of them should be different. A candidate who builds the measurement first shows they understand that.

The round also has traps that separate people who have run these systems from people who have read about them. Deleting from an approximate index is hard, so real systems mark entries deleted and clean up later, with consequences for quality. Filtering by metadata breaks approximate search in a specific way that a selective filter, such as a small team's permissions, exposes. And memory, not speed, is usually what forces the design.

This lesson follows the round: the requirements to clarify, the entities and the interface, then three stages of building. First an exact flat index that is the baseline for everything; then an inverted-file (IVF) index, the approximate method you can actually write in the round; then how HNSW, the graph index inside most vector databases, works and plugs in behind the same interface. Along the way it covers deletes, filters, edge cases, the extensions an interviewer will ask for, complexity and memory arithmetic, and the full tested solution, which measures recall at several settings.

What "closest" means

An embedding is a vector: a list of d numbers, where d is the dimension, such as 384, 768 or 1,536. Two embeddings are similar when they point in nearly the same direction. The usual measure is cosine similarity, the cosine of the angle between the vectors: 1 when they point the same way, 0 when perpendicular, -1 when opposite. Cosine ignores length, which suits embeddings, because many models produce vectors whose length carries little meaning.

Cosine similarity is the dot product divided by the two lengths. If you divide every vector by its length when you store it, making it a unit vector, and do the same to the query, the lengths are all 1 and cosine similarity becomes a plain dot product. Then scoring the query against every stored vector is a single matrix-vector multiplication: stack the stored unit vectors as rows of a matrix and multiply by the query. NumPy does that with optimised machine code, so it is far faster than a Python loop.

Exact search first, approximate when the numbers demand it

Exact search (also called flat or brute-force search) scores everything and keeps the best k. Its cost is n × d multiply-adds for n vectors. For 100,000 vectors of dimension 768 that is about 77 million operations, a few milliseconds on a laptop. For 10 million vectors it is 7.7 billion, hundreds of milliseconds, too slow for a chat request that has other work to do. Approximate nearest neighbour (ANN) search avoids scoring everything. It accepts missing a few of the true nearest vectors in exchange for searching a small fraction of the data.

How much it misses is measured by recall@k: run the same query through exact search and through the approximate index, and count what fraction of the exact top k the approximate one also returned. A recall@10 of 0.95 means that on average 9.5 of the true 10 are found. You can only compute recall if you have exact results to compare against, which is why the exact index is never thrown away: it becomes the measuring instrument.

The analogy: a library with sections

Finding the books most similar to one you are holding, by comparing it with every book in a national library, is exact search. A library organises books into sections. If you first find the two or three sections most like your book and then compare only with books in those sections, you will find almost all of the best matches in a fraction of the time. Occasionally a great match sits in a neighbouring section you did not visit, and you miss it. That is the inverted-file index, IVF, exactly: the sections are clusters found by k-means, and the number of sections you visit is the parameter nprobe.

Where the analogy breaks

Library sections are chosen by people and fixed. IVF cells are learned from the data by k-means, so they fit the data you trained on, and they slowly stop fitting if new data looks different. And a 768-dimensional space is nothing like a floor plan: almost every point is near many borders, which is why visiting a single cell finds less than half of the true neighbours in this lesson's test, and why nprobe matters so much.

What the round expects you to build

An abstract VectorIndex with add, delete, search and a length. A FlatIndex that is exact. One approximate backend, here IVF, behind the same interface. A recall_at_k function that compares them. Deletes, metadata filters and a clear account of how HNSW would plug in. Reaching that with clean code and honest measurements is a strong performance even if the approximate backend is simple.

1 · Clarify the requirements

Ask about scale first, because it decides whether you need an approximate index at all: how many vectors now and in a year, what dimension, how many queries per second, and what latency budget per search. Then the semantics: which similarity (cosine is the default for text embeddings), what k, whether ids are caller-supplied, whether adding an existing id replaces it (upsert), whether deletes must take effect immediately, and what metadata filters are needed (tenant, language, date range, permissions). Finally the operational questions: is it single-process and in-memory, must it survive restarts, can writes and reads happen concurrently?

A typical agreed scope: in-memory, single process, cosine similarity, caller-supplied hashable ids with upsert, immediate deletes, equality filters on metadata fields, a pluggable backend, and a way to report recall. Persistence and concurrency are discussed as extensions. State the non-functional targets out loud too: search should be well under the latency budget at the stated size, and recall@10 should be above an agreed target such as 0.95 for the approximate backend.

2 · Entities and responsibilities

Four pieces. The store holds the data every backend needs: the matrix of unit vectors (one row per vector), maps between caller ids and row numbers, the metadata per row, a boolean "alive" flag per row for tombstones, and an inverted index from (field, value) to the set of rows that have it, used for filtering and for estimating selectivity. A backend (flat or IVF) decides which rows to score for a query. A hit is the result record: id and score. The recall harness compares any backend with the flat one. Keeping storage separate from the search strategy means every backend gets ids, metadata, deletes and filters for free, and the code that differs between backends is only "which rows are candidates".

3 · The interface

The interface is deliberately narrow. Backend-specific knobs, such as nprobe for IVF or ef for HNSW, are optional keyword arguments on the concrete class, not part of the abstract method, so callers that do not care never see them and swapping a backend never breaks a call site. search returns a list of frozen Hit records sorted best first, with the score included, because downstream code (a reranker, a threshold "only answer if the best score is above 0.3") needs it.

4 · Stage one: the flat index

The naive flat index appends each vector to a Python list and loops over the list computing cosine similarity in Python. It is correct and painfully slow: a Python loop over 100,000 vectors with 768 multiplications each takes seconds. The first improvement is to store vectors as rows of a NumPy float32 matrix and score them all with mat @ q, which runs in optimised native code.

The second improvement fixes adding. np.vstack on every insert copies the whole matrix, which makes building n vectors cost O(n²). The store instead preallocates rows and doubles the matrix when it fills, like Python's own list does, so each add is O(d) amortised: the occasional copy is paid for by the many cheap adds between copies.

The third concerns top-k. Sorting all n scores is O(n log n). NumPy's argpartition(-scores, k-1) rearranges indices so the k best come first, in O(n), without ordering the rest; sorting those k costs O(k log k). The helper _top_k does exactly that. With n = 20,000 and k = 10 the difference is small, but at millions of vectors it matters, and the interviewer will ask why you chose it.

Normalisation happens once, in the store's add. A zero vector has no direction, so the store rejects it rather than dividing by zero and storing NaN (not-a-number) values that would silently poison every future score. The query is normalised in search. With both unit length, mat[rows] @ q is the vector of cosine similarities.

5 · Stage two: an IVF backend

The inverted-file index, as described in the FAISS documentation, splits the space into cells. Training runs k-means on the stored vectors to find nlist centroids. k-means alternates two steps: assign each vector to its nearest centroid, then move each centroid to the mean of its assigned vectors. Because our vectors are unit length and we measure similarity by dot product, the solution uses "spherical" k-means: after averaging, each centroid is renormalised to unit length. Fifteen iterations from a random start are plenty for an interview and for this test.

After training, each vector's row number goes into the list of its nearest centroid. These lists are the "inverted file": for each cell, which vectors live there. At query time, compute the query's similarity to all nlist centroids (cheap: 128 centroids instead of 20,000 vectors), take the nprobe best cells, gather the rows in their lists, drop tombstoned rows, and score only those exactly. Vectors added after training are assigned to their nearest existing cell. Vectors added before training sit in a pending list that is always searched, so the index is correct even before it is trained.

That table is the conversation to have in the interview: there is no single right setting, only a curve, and the requirement (recall target, latency budget) picks the point on it. Because nprobe is a search argument, different callers can pick different points: a background job can use 32, an autocomplete box 4.

6 · Measuring recall

recall_at_k(approx, exact, queries, k) runs each query through both indexes and averages the overlap of the id sets divided by the exact set's size. Build it before you build the approximate backend, and say so: it turns every later change into a measurable experiment. Use queries that resemble real ones, drawn from the same distribution as the data; random vectors in an empty region of space give meaningless numbers. In production, sample a small fraction of live queries, run them against an exact index in the background, and chart the result, because an approximate index degrades without raising any error.

7 · Deletes and upserts

Deleting from the flat index could physically remove the row, but deleting from IVF would mean finding and rewriting the row's list, and deleting from a graph index means repairing every link that pointed to the node. The common approach for all backends is a tombstone: set the row's alive flag to false, remove its id from the map and its rows from the metadata postings, and leave the vector where it is. Search skips dead rows. An upsert, adding an id that already exists, is a delete followed by an add, so the old vector can never be returned again.

Tombstones accumulate. They waste memory and, in approximate indexes, waste search effort: the IVF lists still contain dead rows that must be filtered, so the useful candidates per probe fall. The solution compacts when the dead fraction passes a threshold (20 percent by default): it builds a fresh store from live rows only and retrains. In the test, deleting 250 of 1,000 vectors triggers a compaction, after which the store holds fewer rows and no deleted id is ever returned. In production compaction runs in the background and the new index is swapped in atomically; doing it inside a user's delete call, as the interview version does for simplicity, would make one unlucky delete very slow.

8 · Metadata filters and selectivity

Filters are where vector search gets interesting. The store keeps postings: for each (field, value) pair, the set of rows that have it. Intersecting the sets for a filter's conditions (smallest first, because intersection cost follows the smaller set) gives the exact matching rows and, as a by-product, the filter's selectivity, the fraction of the corpus that matches.

Two strategies exist. Post-filtering runs the approximate search and discards results that do not match. It works when most rows match, but when the filter matches 0.2 percent of the corpus, the approximate candidates contain almost no matching rows, and the search returns far fewer than k results. The test reproduces this: forced to post-filter, the IVF index returned fewer than 10 results for all 30 queries. Brute-forcing the matching subset scores only matching rows, exactly. When the subset is small that is both fast and perfect: 20 rows out of 10,000 is a trivial multiply. The solution switches on selectivity: if the matching set is no larger than 2 percent of live rows (or than k), brute-force it; otherwise search approximately and post-filter. With the switch, the selective tenant's recall is exactly 1.0. The counterintuitive conclusion is worth saying aloud: the user with the most restrictive filter should get the fastest and most accurate search, not the worst.

9 · Stage three: how HNSW works and plugs in

HNSW, hierarchical navigable small world, is the index inside most vector databases. It is a graph: each vector is a node linked to some of its near neighbours. Searching a graph greedily means starting somewhere, looking at the current node's neighbours, moving to whichever is closest to the query, and repeating until no neighbour is closer. On one big graph that can take many steps and get stuck in a local optimum. HNSW adds layers. Every node is in the bottom layer; a random, exponentially shrinking fraction is also in each layer above, so the top layer has very few nodes with long-range links. Search starts at the top, greedily walks to the node nearest the query, drops down a layer, continues from there, and so on, like using motorways then main roads then streets. In the bottom layer it keeps a candidate list of size ef rather than a single current node, which makes it far less likely to get stuck.

Three parameters matter, and the hnswlib documentation describes them. M is the number of links created per node (default 16 in hnswlib; 12 to 48 suits most uses) and drives memory, roughly M × 8 to 10 bytes per element for links. ef_construction (default 200) is the candidate-list size while building, trading build time for graph quality. Both are fixed at build time. ef, set with set_ef, is the search-time candidate list and must be at least k; it can change between queries. Distinguishing build-time from query-time parameters is the point interviewers probe.

Plugging hnswlib in means an adapter that implements VectorIndex. hnswlib uses integer labels, so the adapter keeps the shared store for ids, metadata and the selective-filter fallback, calls add_items with the row number as the label, mark_deleted on delete, and knn_query (which accepts a label filter function) on search. Then the same recall_at_k harness tunes M, ef_construction and ef against the flat index. Callers change nothing.

10 · The full solution

The file below implements the store, the flat and IVF backends, the selectivity switch, compaction and the recall harness, with five tests. It needs Python 3.12 and NumPy (no other packages) and runs in about three seconds. The IVF test prints the recall curve and per-query timings; the filter test prints how often naive post-filtering came up short.

"""In-memory vector index with a pluggable ANN backend.
FlatIndex (exact, the recall baseline) and IVFIndex (approximate, k-means buckets),
behind one interface, with deletes, metadata filters and a recall harness.
Python 3.12 + NumPy. Run: python vecindex.py"""
from __future__ import annotations

import time
from abc import ABC, abstractmethod
from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import Any, Hashable

import numpy as np

Meta = dict[str, Any]


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Hit:
    id: Hashable
    score: float            # cosine similarity, higher is closer


class VectorIndex(ABC):
    """What callers see. Every backend implements exactly this."""

    @abstractmethod
    def add(self, id: Hashable, vec: np.ndarray, meta: Meta | None = None) -> None: ...

    @abstractmethod
    def delete(self, id: Hashable) -> bool: ...

    @abstractmethod
    def search(self, q: np.ndarray, k: int = 10, where: Meta | None = None) -> list[Hit]: ...

    @abstractmethod
    def __len__(self) -> int: ...


def _unit(v: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    v = np.asarray(v, dtype=np.float32)
    n = np.linalg.norm(v, axis=-1, keepdims=True)
    if np.any(n == 0):
        raise ValueError("zero vector has no direction")
    return v / n                                       # cosine similarity becomes a dot product


def _top_k(scores: np.ndarray, k: int) -> np.ndarray:
    """Indices of the k largest scores, best first: O(n) partition + O(k log k) sort."""
    k = min(k, scores.size)
    if k == 0:
        return np.empty(0, dtype=np.int64)
    part = np.argpartition(-scores, k - 1)[:k]
    return part[np.argsort(-scores[part], kind="stable")]


class _Store:
    """Rows of unit vectors with id <-> row maps, metadata, tombstones and an inverted
    metadata index for selectivity estimates. Shared by every backend."""

    def __init__(self, dim: int) -> None:
        self.dim = dim
        self.mat = np.zeros((16, dim), dtype=np.float32)   # grows by doubling: amortised O(d) add
        self.n = 0
        self.alive = np.zeros(16, dtype=bool)
        self.row_of: dict[Hashable, int] = {}
        self.id_of: list[Hashable] = []
        self.meta: list[Meta] = []
        self.postings: dict[tuple[str, Any], set[int]] = {}

    def add(self, id: Hashable, vec: np.ndarray, meta: Meta | None) -> int:
        v = _unit(vec)
        if v.shape != (self.dim,):
            raise ValueError(f"expected dim {self.dim}, got {v.shape}")
        if id in self.row_of:                              # upsert = delete + add
            self.delete(id)
        if self.n == len(self.mat):
            self.mat = np.vstack([self.mat, np.zeros_like(self.mat)])
            self.alive = np.concatenate([self.alive, np.zeros_like(self.alive)])
        r = self.n
        self.mat[r], self.alive[r] = v, True
        self.row_of[id] = r
        self.id_of.append(id)
        self.meta.append(dict(meta or {}))
        for kv in self.meta[r].items():
            self.postings.setdefault(kv, set()).add(r)
        self.n += 1
        return r

    def delete(self, id: Hashable) -> int | None:
        r = self.row_of.pop(id, None)
        if r is None:
            return None
        self.alive[r] = False                              # tombstone: row stays, never returned
        for kv in self.meta[r].items():
            self.postings[kv].discard(r)
        return r

    def matching_rows(self, where: Meta) -> set[int]:
        sets = [self.postings.get(kv, set()) for kv in where.items()]
        return set.intersection(*sorted(sets, key=len)) if sets else set()

    @property
    def live(self) -> int:
        return len(self.row_of)


class FlatIndex(VectorIndex):
    """Exact search: score every live vector. O(n*d) per query. Also the measuring instrument."""

    def __init__(self, dim: int) -> None:
        self.s = _Store(dim)

    def add(self, id, vec, meta=None) -> None:
        self.s.add(id, vec, meta)

    def delete(self, id) -> bool:
        return self.s.delete(id) is not None

    def __len__(self) -> int:
        return self.s.live

    def search(self, q, k=10, where=None) -> list[Hit]:
        q = _unit(q)
        if where:
            rows = np.fromiter(self.s.matching_rows(where), dtype=np.int64)
        else:
            rows = np.flatnonzero(self.s.alive[: self.s.n])
        if rows.size == 0:
            return []
        scores = self.s.mat[rows] @ q
        best = _top_k(scores, k)
        return [Hit(self.s.id_of[rows[i]], float(scores[i])) for i in best]


class IVFIndex(VectorIndex):
    """Inverted-file index: k-means splits space into `nlist` cells; a query scores only the
    vectors in its `nprobe` nearest cells. Recall/latency knob: nprobe (per query)."""

    def __init__(self, dim: int, nlist: int = 64, nprobe: int = 8, seed: int = 0,
                 selective_below: float = 0.02, compact_above: float = 0.2) -> None:
        self.s = _Store(dim)
        self.nlist, self.nprobe = nlist, nprobe
        self.rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
        self.centroids: np.ndarray | None = None
        self.lists: list[list[int]] = []
        self.pending: list[int] = []                       # rows added before training
        self.selective_below, self.compact_above = selective_below, compact_above

    # ---- build ----
    def train(self, iters: int = 15) -> None:
        rows = np.flatnonzero(self.s.alive[: self.s.n])
        x = self.s.mat[rows]
        c = x[self.rng.choice(len(x), size=min(self.nlist, len(x)), replace=False)].copy()
        for _ in range(iters):                             # spherical k-means on unit vectors
            assign = np.argmax(x @ c.T, axis=1)
            for j in range(len(c)):
                members = x[assign == j]
                if len(members):
                    m = members.sum(axis=0)
                    c[j] = m / np.linalg.norm(m)
        self.centroids = c
        self.lists = [[] for _ in range(len(c))]
        for r, j in zip(rows, np.argmax(x @ c.T, axis=1)):
            self.lists[j].append(int(r))
        self.pending.clear()

    def add(self, id, vec, meta=None) -> None:
        r = self.s.add(id, vec, meta)
        if self.centroids is None:
            self.pending.append(r)                          # searched exactly until trained
        else:
            self.lists[int(np.argmax(self.centroids @ self.s.mat[r]))].append(r)

    def delete(self, id) -> bool:
        if self.s.delete(id) is None:
            return False
        dead = self.s.n - self.s.live
        if self.centroids is not None and dead / self.s.n > self.compact_above:
            self.compact()
        return True

    def compact(self) -> None:
        """Rebuild without tombstones (the job a real system runs in the background)."""
        old = self.s
        self.s = _Store(old.dim)
        for id, r in sorted(old.row_of.items(), key=lambda kv: kv[1]):
            self.s.add(id, old.mat[r], old.meta[r])
        self.train()

    def __len__(self) -> int:
        return self.s.live

    # ---- query ----
    def _candidates(self, q: np.ndarray, nprobe: int) -> np.ndarray:
        cells = _top_k(self.centroids @ q, nprobe)
        rows = [r for j in cells for r in self.lists[j]] + self.pending
        rows = np.asarray(rows, dtype=np.int64)
        return rows[self.s.alive[rows]] if rows.size else rows

    def search(self, q, k=10, where=None, nprobe: int | None = None) -> list[Hit]:
        q = _unit(q)
        if self.centroids is None:                          # untrained: exact over everything
            rows = np.flatnonzero(self.s.alive[: self.s.n])
        elif where:
            match = self.s.matching_rows(where)
            if len(match) <= max(k, self.selective_below * self.s.live):
                rows = np.fromiter(match, dtype=np.int64)   # selective: brute-force the subset
            else:                                           # broad: ANN, then post-filter
                rows = self._candidates(q, nprobe or self.nprobe)
                keep = np.fromiter((r in match for r in rows), dtype=bool, count=rows.size)
                rows = rows[keep]
        else:
            rows = self._candidates(q, nprobe or self.nprobe)
        if rows.size == 0:
            return []
        scores = self.s.mat[rows] @ q
        return [Hit(self.s.id_of[rows[i]], float(scores[i])) for i in _top_k(scores, k)]


def recall_at_k(approx: VectorIndex, exact: VectorIndex, queries: np.ndarray, k: int = 10,
                **kw) -> float:
    """Mean fraction of the true top-k (from the exact index) the approximate one returned."""
    got = 0
    for q in queries:
        truth = {h.id for h in exact.search(q, k, kw.get("where"))}
        found = {h.id for h in approx.search(q, k, **kw)}
        got += len(truth & found) / max(1, len(truth))
    return got / len(queries)


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- tests
def clustered(n: int, dim: int, centres: int, rng: np.random.Generator) -> np.ndarray:
    """Synthetic embeddings with topic structure, like real text embeddings."""
    c = rng.normal(size=(centres, dim))
    return c[rng.integers(centres, size=n)] + 0.35 * rng.normal(size=(n, dim))


def test_exact_on_axes() -> None:
    idx = FlatIndex(3)
    for i, v in enumerate(np.eye(3)):
        idx.add(f"e{i}", v * (i + 1))                       # lengths differ; cosine ignores them
    hits = idx.search(np.array([1.0, 0.1, 0.0]), k=2)
    assert [h.id for h in hits] == ["e0", "e1"] and hits[0].score > hits[1].score


def test_delete_upsert_and_filter() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
    idx = FlatIndex(8)
    for i in range(100):
        idx.add(i, rng.normal(size=8), {"lang": "en" if i % 4 else "de"})
    q = rng.normal(size=8)
    top = idx.search(q, 5)[0].id
    idx.delete(top)
    assert top not in {h.id for h in idx.search(q, 100)} and len(idx) == 99
    de = idx.search(q, 10, where={"lang": "de"})
    assert len(de) == 10 and all(i % 4 == 0 for i in (h.id for h in de))
    idx.add(5, q)                                           # upsert: same id, new vector
    assert idx.search(q, 1)[0].id == 5 and len(idx) == 99


def test_ivf_recall_and_nprobe_tradeoff() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
    data = clustered(20_000, 64, 200, rng)
    flat, ivf = FlatIndex(64), IVFIndex(64, nlist=128, nprobe=8)
    for i, v in enumerate(data):
        flat.add(i, v); ivf.add(i, v)
    ivf.train()
    qs = clustered(100, 64, 200, np.random.default_rng(3))
    curve = {p: recall_at_k(ivf, flat, qs, 10, nprobe=p) for p in (1, 4, 8, 32)}
    assert curve[1] <= curve[4] <= curve[8] <= curve[32]
    assert curve[32] >= 0.95, curve                       # a quarter of the cells: near-exact
    t0 = time.perf_counter(); [flat.search(q, 10) for q in qs]; tf = time.perf_counter() - t0
    t0 = time.perf_counter(); [ivf.search(q, 10) for q in qs]; ti = time.perf_counter() - t0
    print(f"    recall@10 by nprobe {curve}; flat {tf*10:.2f} ms/q, ivf(8) {ti*10:.2f} ms/q")


def test_selective_filter_falls_back_to_exact() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
    data = clustered(10_000, 32, 100, rng)
    flat, ivf = FlatIndex(32), IVFIndex(32, nlist=64, nprobe=4)
    for i, v in enumerate(data):
        m = {"tenant": "small" if i % 500 == 0 else "big"}   # 'small' sees 0.2% of rows
        flat.add(i, v, m); ivf.add(i, v, m)
    ivf.train()
    qs = clustered(30, 32, 100, np.random.default_rng(5))
    assert recall_at_k(ivf, flat, qs, 10, where={"tenant": "small"}) == 1.0
    ivf.selective_below = 0.0                               # force naive post-filtering
    short = sum(len(ivf.search(q, 10, where={"tenant": "small"})) < 10 for q in qs)
    print(f"    naive post-filter returned < 10 results for {short}/30 queries")
    assert short > 0

11 · Edge cases

Zero vectors: reject; they have no direction. Wrong dimension: reject with a clear error naming the expected dimension. k larger than the number of live vectors: return all of them, sorted, not an error. Empty index or a filter that matches nothing: return an empty list. Duplicate ids: define upsert and test it. Searching before IVF is trained: fall back to exact search so the index is always correct. Ties in score: make ordering deterministic (a stable sort) so tests and pagination are repeatable. Floating-point precision: float32 halves memory against float64, and cosine scores agree to about six or seven digits, which is ample for ranking. Mixed embedding models: vectors from two models live in different spaces and are not comparable; store the model version as metadata and refuse to mix.

12 · Extensions the interviewer will ask for

Persistence: write the matrix with np.save or a memory-mapped file, the id map and metadata as JSON or SQLite, and append adds and deletes to a write-ahead log (a file of operations replayed on restart) between snapshots. Concurrency: a lock for the simple answer; for readers that never block, a small mutable buffer searched exactly plus a large immutable index swapped in by reference after background rebuilds. Hybrid search: combine vector scores with keyword (BM25) scores using reciprocal rank fusion. Batch queries: a matrix of queries times the matrix of vectors gives all scores in one multiply, which is how GPU search reaches high throughput. Range filters such as dates: keep a sorted list per field and use binary search to get matching rows. Reranking: retrieve 100 candidates cheaply and rescore them with a more expensive model.

13 · Complexity and memory

Flat: add O(d) amortised, delete O(1) plus metadata postings, search O(n·d) to score plus O(n) for the partition, memory n·d·4 bytes for float32. IVF: training O(iterations · n · nlist · d); add O(nlist · d) to find the cell; search O(nlist · d) for centroids plus O((nprobe / nlist) · n · d) for candidates; memory adds one integer per vector in the lists plus nlist·d for centroids. HNSW: search roughly logarithmic in n hops times M neighbour checks, insert similar times ef_construction, memory adds about M × 8 to 10 bytes per vector. The vectors dominate everything: 5 million 768-dimensional float32 vectors are 15.4 GB, against about 1.3 GB of HNSW links at M = 32. When memory is the problem, shrink the vectors.

Why filtered graph search dead-ends

Greedy graph search relies on being able to walk through the graph toward the query. If a filter is applied during the walk, so that non-matching nodes are not even visited, a selective filter removes most of the stepping stones: from the entry point there may be no matching neighbour at all, or the walk reaches a pocket where every neighbour fails the filter and stops, far from the true nearest matches. If the filter is applied only to the final candidates, a selective filter leaves almost none. Libraries handle the middle ground differently: hnswlib's knn_query accepts a filter that still traverses non-matching nodes but excludes them from results, which preserves connectivity at the cost of visiting many useless nodes. The robust system-level answer stays the same: estimate selectivity before searching, brute-force small subsets exactly, and reserve the approximate path for broad filters, possibly with a larger ef or nprobe when the filter is moderately selective.

Quantisation: where the memory goes and how to win it back

A float32 vector of dimension 768 is 3,072 bytes. Scalar quantisation maps each float to an 8-bit integer using a per-dimension minimum and scale, cutting memory four times with a small loss in ranking accuracy, and it is nearly free to implement. Product quantisation (PQ) goes much further: split the vector into m sub-vectors, run k-means separately on each sub-space to learn 256 centroids, and store each sub-vector as the one-byte id of its nearest centroid. A 768-dimensional vector with m = 96 becomes 96 bytes, 32 times smaller. Distances are computed from small lookup tables of query-to-centroid distances, which is fast. The cost is accuracy, so the standard pattern is two-stage: search compressed codes to find a few hundred candidates, then rerank those exactly with full-precision vectors kept on disk or in a memory-mapped file. FAISS's IVF-PQ index combines IVF cells with PQ codes, and the FAISS paper describes this budget at billion scale.

Training drift in IVF

IVF cells are only as good as the data they were trained on. If new vectors come from a different distribution, for example a new product line or a new language, they are forced into the nearest existing cells, which become crowded and incoherent, and queries for the new content probe cells full of unrelated vectors. Symptoms are rising recall loss for new content and unbalanced list lengths. Monitor list-size distribution and recall by segment, and retrain on a fresh sample periodically. HNSW does not need training, which is one of its practical advantages for growing corpora.

Why recall is not the end of evaluation

Recall@k compares the approximate top-k with the exact top-k, but in a retrieval-augmented system the real goal is that the answer-relevant chunk is retrieved. Exact nearest neighbours of a poorly embedded query can all be irrelevant, and an approximate index with recall 0.9 can be perfectly adequate if the missed neighbours were near-duplicates. So measure two things: recall against exact search, to know the index is doing its job, and end-task retrieval quality on labelled queries, to know the embeddings and chunking are doing theirs. Mixing them up leads teams to tune ef for weeks when the problem is the embedding model.

The LSM shape for concurrent writes

Graph inserts mutate shared neighbour lists, and IVF retraining reorganises everything, so both are awkward to update while queries run. The standard architecture borrows from log-structured merge trees: new writes go to a small mutable segment that is searched exactly (it is small, so that is cheap), large immutable segments are searched with their ANN indexes, results from all segments are merged by score, and a background process periodically merges segments into a new immutable one and swaps it in. Deletes become tombstones applied across segments and dropped at merge time. Lucene, and several vector databases, work this way. Recognising the shape is worth a mark because it shows you are applying a known solution rather than inventing locks.

IVF or HNSW: how to choose

The two families make different trades, and the interviewer will want a reasoned choice rather than "HNSW because everyone uses it". IVF is cheap in memory (one integer per vector in the lists plus a few thousand centroids), builds fast once trained, batches well on GPUs, and combines naturally with product quantisation, so it dominates at hundreds of millions to billions of vectors where memory is the constraint. Its weaknesses are the training step and drift. HNSW usually reaches a higher recall at the same latency on in-memory benchmarks such as ANN-Benchmarks, accepts inserts without training, and degrades gracefully as data grows, but costs extra memory per vector for links, builds slowly, and handles deletes poorly. A reasonable default: flat below a few hundred thousand vectors, HNSW for in-memory corpora up to tens of millions, IVF with quantisation beyond that or when memory is tight. Whichever you pick, the recall harness tells you whether it is meeting the target on your data.

Choosing k, thresholds and the score distribution

Top-k always returns k results, even when none is relevant. Downstream code often needs a threshold, such as "only use chunks with cosine above some value", but cosine scores are not calibrated probabilities and their distribution differs between embedding models: one model's typical relevant score might be 0.8, another's 0.4. Set thresholds per model from labelled data, return scores with every hit so callers can apply them, and expect to revisit them when the model changes.

How vector indexes fail in production

The defining failure is silent quality loss. Recall drops because tombstones accumulated, because data drifted away from IVF training, because someone lowered ef to fix latency, or because a new embedding model's vectors were mixed with old ones. Nothing errors; answers just get slightly worse. The defence is to sample live queries continuously, run them against exact search offline, and alert on recall by segment (tenant, language, content age), alongside the dead fraction and IVF list-size balance.

The second is memory exhaustion. Indexes are often sized for today's corpus and grow quietly until a node is killed. Capacity planning is arithmetic: vectors × dimension × bytes per number, plus graph links or list entries, plus metadata, plus headroom for a full rebuild, since compaction builds a new index while the old one serves.

The third is tail latency from filters and deletes. A broad filter with post-filtering may need several rounds of over-fetching; a moderately selective one forces a large ef. Track latency percentiles by filter type and log selectivity with each query so that slow queries can be explained.

Operations: rebuilds, re-embedding and versions

Two operations need planning. Compaction or retraining builds a new index from live data and swaps it in atomically; run it on a schedule or trigger it from the dead fraction, and keep the old index until the new one has passed a recall check. Changing the embedding model is a full migration: re-embed every item, build a new index, evaluate retrieval quality on labelled queries, then switch reads, because old and new vectors cannot be compared. Store the model name and version with each vector and in the index metadata, so a mismatch fails loudly instead of quietly returning nonsense.

What to measure and tune

Measure recall@k against exact search on sampled queries, latency percentiles, queries per second, memory, dead fraction, and filter selectivity. Tune nprobe or ef per use case from a recall versus latency curve measured on your own data, not from defaults; tune M and ef_construction only when building, guided by the hnswlib advice that if recall at ef equal to ef_construction is below 0.9 there is room to improve the graph. Quantise when memory, not latency, is the binding constraint.

Multi-tenancy

Products that serve many customers must decide between one shared index with a tenant filter and one index per tenant. A shared index is simpler to operate and uses memory efficiently, but every query is filtered, and small tenants hit the selectivity problem, which the brute-force fallback solves. Per-tenant indexes make isolation and deletion trivial (drop the tenant's index) and keep every search unfiltered, but thousands of small indexes waste memory on overhead and complicate operations. A common hybrid gives large tenants their own index and keeps small ones in a shared index where brute-forcing a tenant's subset is cheap. Whatever the layout, the tenant filter is a security boundary, so test that it is applied on every path, including the fallback and the pending, untrained rows.

The real case: IVF in FAISS

FAISS, Meta's similarity-search library, documents the IVF index in exactly the terms of this lesson. Its "Faster search" page explains that to speed up search the dataset is segmented into Voronoi cells (regions of space closer to one centroid than to any other), each database vector falls in one cell, and at search time only vectors in the query's cell and a few neighbouring ones are compared. This is the IndexIVFFlat index. It requires a training stage on vectors with the same distribution as the database, and it uses a second index, the quantizer, typically a flat index, to assign vectors to cells. Search has two parameters: nlist, the number of cells, and nprobe, the number visited, and the documentation notes that search time increases roughly linearly with the number of probes. The FAISS paper extends the same structure with product quantisation and GPUs to billion-vector collections. The interview version in this lesson is the same design in a hundred lines: a flat quantizer over centroids, inverted lists, exact scoring of candidates, and nprobe as the per-query knob, measured against an exact index.

Goal: run the full solution, then measure, break and extend it. You need Python 3.12 with NumPy.

  1. Save the full solution as vecindex.py and run it. Read the printed recall curve: recall@10 rises from about 0.44 at nprobe 1 to about 0.97 at 32, and IVF at nprobe 8 is several times faster than flat.
  2. Measure it. Plot or print recall against per-query time for nprobe in 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128. At 128 (every cell) recall must be exactly 1.0; if it is not, there is a bug in candidate gathering.
  3. Break it. Change the data to pure Gaussian noise (no clusters) with rng.normal(size=(n, 64)). Recall at the same nprobe collapses, because random high-dimensional data has no cell structure for IVF to exploit. Real embeddings are clustered, which is why ANN works on them.
  4. Break it again. Train IVF on the first 2,000 vectors only, then add the rest from a different set of cluster centres. Measure recall on queries from the new clusters: training drift in action.
  5. Extend it. Add int8 scalar quantisation: store per-dimension minimum and scale, keep an int8 matrix, score candidates approximately, and rerank the top 50 with the float32 vectors. Report memory saved and recall.
  6. Filter check. Set selective_below to 0, 0.01 and 0.05 and run the selective-tenant test each time; note where results start coming back short and how latency changes.

What to take away: the code is short, and the engineering is in the measurements. Every change above is judged by the same recall harness against the flat index.

Work it out

You have 5 million chunks, 768-dimensional float32 vectors, in an HNSW index with M = 32. Queries return the top 10, and 30 percent of vectors are tombstoned deletions.

(a) What does the index cost in memory? (b) Recall@10 measured against flat search is 0.72, but was 0.95 at launch. What happened and what do you do? (c) A user with access to 0.3 percent of the corpus gets poor results while everyone else is fine. Diagnose.

Answer

(a) Memory. Vectors: 5M × 768 × 4 bytes = 15.4 GB. Links: hnswlib documents memory for links as roughly M × 8 to 10 bytes per element, so about 256 to 320 bytes per node at M = 32 (the bottom layer stores up to 2M = 64 four-byte neighbour ids): about 1.3 to 1.6 GB. Total about 17 GB, plus metadata and headroom for a rebuild. The vectors dominate by roughly ten times, so if memory is the problem, quantise the vectors; tuning M attacks the small term.

(b) Recall fell from 0.95 to 0.72. The tombstones are the main suspect. Deleted nodes remain in the graph, occupy neighbour slots, and are visited during traversal; the search spends its ef budget on them and discards them at the end, so effective effort on live nodes has fallen by around 30 percent. Mitigation today: raise ef and over-fetch (ask for 3 to 4 times k before filtering), which buys recall back at a latency cost with no rebuild. Real fix: compact, rebuilding without the deleted nodes. Prevention: make the dead fraction a monitored metric and trigger compaction at a threshold such as 10 to 20 percent. Also rule out the other causes: drift in the data, or a mixed embedding model version. The failure raised no errors and changed no latency, which is exactly why recall sampling must run in production.

(c) The 0.3 percent user. Their filter matches about 15,000 of 5 million vectors, scattered across the graph. Post-filtering the approximate top results leaves few or no matches; filtering during traversal dead-ends because the walk must pass through nodes they cannot see. Users with broad access are unaffected. The fix is to switch strategy on selectivity: compute the matching set from a metadata index, and when it is small (here 15,000 vectors), brute-force it: 15,000 × 768 is one small matrix multiply, a few milliseconds, and exact. The user with the narrowest permissions should get the fastest and most accurate search. Tuning the graph instead would slow everyone else down and still not fix it.
