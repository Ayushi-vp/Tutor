A company builds a question-answering assistant over its policy handbook. Someone asks "how long do refunds take to appear on my card?" The system retrieves a passage, the model answers "five working days", and the customer complains a week later. The handbook said the refund decision takes five working days and the money takes three to five more. The retrieval step found text about refunds; the passage it found began halfway through a sentence, did not say which section it came from, and contained only one of the two numbers. No prompt could have fixed that answer, because the right text never reached the model in a usable shape.

That passage was produced by the chunker, the component that splits documents into pieces before they are embedded and indexed. In a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) system, the chunk is the unit of retrieval: the index stores one vector per chunk and returns whole chunks. If an answer is cut across two chunks, or a chunk loses the heading that says what it is about, or a table is separated from its header row, no retriever and no model downstream can put it back together.

Chunking looks like string manipulation, which is why it is often written in ten minutes: split every 1,000 characters, maybe with a little overlap. That version fails in every way that matters. Characters are not the unit models count, so chunks overflow limits or waste them. Fixed windows cut sentences and code in half. Structure (headings, tables, code fences) is ignored. Chunk ids are sequence numbers, so editing a document re-embeds all of it. And the overlap logic, written carelessly, can loop forever on a long unbroken string.

As a machine-coding round, the chunker is a good test of careful engineering. The algorithm is simple to describe and full of edge cases: the oversized code block, the 5,000-character URL with no spaces, the heading path longer than the budget, the overlap that prevents progress. Interviewers want to see a design that measures in tokens through a swappable tokenizer, follows the document's own structure, has explicit invariants (no chunk over budget, every sentence whole somewhere, guaranteed progress), and is tested on those invariants.

This lesson follows the round: clarify the requirements, name the pieces, build a naive character splitter and watch it fail, then build the structure-aware, token-aware chunker in stages (parse, split recursively, pack with a prefix budget, overlap safely, hash ids), test it with both a real BPE tokenizer and a stdlib stand-in, and finish with edge cases, the extensions interviewers ask for, complexity, and how a widely used published chunker, LangChain's recursive splitter, makes the same choices.

Where the chunker sits

A retrieval pipeline has two halves. At ingestion time, documents are converted to text, split into chunks, each chunk is turned into a vector by an embedding model, and the vectors are stored in an index together with the chunk text and metadata. At question time, the question is embedded, the index returns the nearest chunks, and those chunks are placed in the prompt for a generator model. The chunker is the only step that decides what a "passage" is. Everything after it, the embedding, the index, the reranker, the prompt, can only choose among the passages it produced, which is why small chunking decisions show up as large differences in answer quality.

Tokens, not characters

Language and embedding models do not read characters or words. They read tokens, pieces of text from a fixed vocabulary built by a method such as byte pair encoding (BPE), which merges frequent character sequences into single tokens. Common English words are usually one token, often with their leading space; rare words split into several; punctuation, digits and symbols are often a token each. Every limit that matters is in tokens: an embedding model's maximum input, a prompt's context window, and the price per call. A chunker that counts characters is measuring the wrong thing. In this lesson's test, using OpenAI's tiktoken library with the cl100k_base vocabulary, 1,000 characters of plain English were 186 tokens while 1,000 characters of JSON (short keys, ids, quotes and braces) were 473, about two and a half times as many. A character budget either wastes space on prose or overflows on data.

So the chunker takes a tokenizer as a dependency with two methods, encode(text) returning a list of token ids and decode(ids) returning text. In production you pass the tokenizer of the embedding model you use. In tests you can pass a simple stand-in. The rest of the code never needs to know which.

Follow the document's own structure

A well-written document already tells you where its ideas begin and end: headings start sections, blank lines separate paragraphs, sentence punctuation ends statements, code fences and tables mark blocks that only make sense whole. The idea of recursive splitting is to use the coarsest of these boundaries that produces small enough pieces, and to fall back to a finer boundary only for the pieces that are still too large. A short section stays one chunk. A long section splits between paragraphs. A huge paragraph splits between sentences. A monster sentence splits between words. And a string with no spaces at all, such as a long URL or a base64 blob, is sliced by raw tokens as a last resort.

The analogy: packing boxes

Think of moving house with boxes of fixed size. You pack whole shelves if they fit, then whole books, and only if a single item is too big for any box do you take it apart. You also label each box with the room it came from, so whoever unpacks it knows where it belongs. Chunking works the same way: the box is the token budget, the items are sections, blocks, sentences and words, and the label is the heading path, the chain of headings above the text, such as "Refunds > Process". The analogy breaks in one place: boxes do not overlap, but chunks may repeat a sentence or two from the previous chunk, so that a statement straddling the boundary appears whole somewhere.

Overlap, briefly

Overlap copies the last part of one chunk to the start of the next. It helps when an important sentence sits at a boundary or when a sentence depends on the one before it. It costs storage and embedding work, and it creates near-duplicate chunks that can crowd each other out of search results. A sensible chunker overlaps by whole sentences, up to a small budget, only inside a section, and drops the overlap whenever it would stop the chunker from making progress.

What the round expects

A chunk(document) -> list[Chunk] function or class with a token budget and an overlap budget; a tokenizer passed in; structure-aware splitting; heading-path prefixes counted inside the budget; content-addressed ids; and tests of the invariants: no chunk over budget, no sentence lost, tables keep their headers, the chunker terminates on pathological input, and ids are stable.

1 · Clarify the requirements

Ask what the input format is (Markdown, HTML, PDF text, code), which tokenizer and embedding model the chunks are for and its maximum input length, the target chunk size and overlap, whether chunks may cross section boundaries, how code and tables should be treated, what metadata each chunk needs (source document, heading path, position), whether documents are re-ingested and must be updated incrementally, and how big documents get.

A typical agreed scope: Markdown input; a tokenizer passed in; a maximum of N tokens per chunk including a heading-path prefix; overlap of up to M tokens of whole sentences; never split a sentence, code block or table if it fits; chunks never cross sections; stable ids for incremental re-ingestion. The non-functional requirement worth stating: the chunker must always terminate and must run in time roughly proportional to the document's length, because it runs over every document on every ingest.

2 · Entities and responsibilities

Five pieces. The Tokenizer protocol (encode, decode) is the only thing that knows what a token is. The block parser turns Markdown into a sequence of blocks (paragraph, code, table), each tagged with its heading path. The splitter breaks each block into pieces, each no larger than the budget: whole sentences, whole code blocks or tables, or the fallbacks (lines, rows with header, words, token slices). The packer groups consecutive pieces of one section into chunks under the budget, with overlap. A Chunk is the frozen output record: id, the text to embed (prefix plus body), the body alone, the heading path and the token count.

3 · Stage one: the naive splitter, and how it fails

def naive_chunks(text, size=1000, overlap=200):
    out, i = [], 0
    while i < len(text):
        out.append(text[i:i + size])
        i += size - overlap
    return out

Run it over the refund handbook in the drill and you can watch each failure. The window boundary lands mid-word ("refun" | "ds"), so neither chunk embeds the word correctly. The heading "Process" lands in the previous chunk, so the chunk with the five-day statements no longer says what process it describes. In a document with a code block, the opening fence lands in one chunk and the closing fence in another, so both render as broken code. In a document full of JSON, a 1,000-character chunk may be 470 tokens while a prose chunk is 190, so either the budget is wasted or the JSON overflows the embedding model. And if someone sets overlap equal to size, the step size - overlap is zero and the loop never ends. Every requirement in the clarified list exists because of one of these.

4 · Stage two: parse the Markdown into blocks

The parser walks the lines once, keeping the current heading path as a list. A heading line (# to ###### followed by text) of level L truncates the path to L-1 entries and appends the new heading, so "## Process" after "## Eligibility" under "# Refunds" yields the path Refunds, Process. Lines that start with a code fence begin a code block that runs, verbatim, to the matching closing fence; the parser does not look for headings inside it, because a # in Python is a comment, not a heading. Consecutive lines starting with | form a table. Blank lines end a paragraph. Remaining lines are joined into the current paragraph with spaces, which undoes the hard line wrapping common in Markdown source.

Each block is emitted as a triple: its kind, its text, and the heading path at that point. From here on, every piece remembers which section it belongs to, which is what lets the packer refuse to mix sections and lets every chunk carry its label.

5 · Stage three: split blocks into pieces under budget

The budget for a piece is the chunk budget minus the tokens of its heading-path prefix, because the prefix will be prepended to every chunk in that section. Counting the prefix inside the budget is a detail interviewers notice: a chunker that adds the prefix after checking the budget produces chunks a few tokens over the embedding model's limit, which then get silently truncated, losing their last sentence.

A paragraph is split into sentences with a regular expression that breaks after ., ! or ? followed by whitespace and a capital letter, digit, quote or bracket. That is a heuristic: it will occasionally split after an abbreviation such as "e.g. The". Say so, and note that production systems often use a trained sentence splitter. Each sentence that fits becomes one piece. A sentence that does not fit is split on spaces into word runs that fit. A single "word" that is itself too large (a long URL, a base64 string) goes to the hard cut: encode it, slice the token ids into budget-sized runs, and decode each run. This is the only place the chunker may cut through text, and it is reached only when no separator exists.

A code block that fits is one piece. One that does not is split by lines, accumulating lines while they fit, so no line is ever cut. A table that fits is one piece; one that does not is split by rows, and each piece starts with the table's header row and separator row so it is still a table a reader (human or model) can interpret. The test checks that every table chunk contains the header.

6 · Stage four: pack pieces into chunks

Pieces are grouped by section, and each section is packed independently, so a chunk never contains the end of one section and the start of the next. Packing is greedy: keep adding the next piece while the joined body still fits the budget; when it would not, emit the current chunk and start a new one. Pieces are joined with a blank line between blocks and a space between sentences of the same paragraph, so chunk text reads like the original.

Two invariants hold by construction and are checked by the tests. Every chunk is within the budget, because every piece is within the budget (stage three guarantees it) and the packer only adds a piece if the result fits. And every sentence of the source appears whole in at least one chunk, because sentences that fit are never split and every piece is emitted exactly once as new content. The test on a long handbook with three prose sections, a 60-line code block and an 80-row table checks both, with the real cl100k tokenizer and with the stand-in.

7 · Stage five: overlap that always makes progress

When the packer emits a chunk, it looks backwards through that chunk's pieces and collects trailing whole sentences while their total stays within the overlap budget; those become the start of the next chunk. Code, tables, word runs and token slices are never carried: repeating half a table helps nobody. Then comes the guard that the naive version lacked. If the carried sentences plus the next new piece would not fit, the overlap is dropped for this chunk. Because of the guard, every emitted chunk contains at least one new piece, so the packer moves forward through the list on every emission and must finish. The constructor also rejects an overlap budget of half the chunk budget or more, which would make most chunks mostly repetition.

8 · Stage six: heading-path prefix and content-addressed ids

Every chunk's text is the heading path joined with " > " on one line, then the body. The drill shows why: the Process section's chunk reads "Refunds > Process" followed by its sentences, so even in isolation it says it is about the refund process. Each chunk's id is the first 16 hex digits of the SHA-256 hash of the document id and the chunk text. Re-chunking an unchanged document yields identical ids, so an incremental ingest can compare id sets and do nothing. In the test, editing one sentence in the last prose section changed only one to three chunk ids out of dozens; everything else kept its id and would not be re-embedded.

The real tokenizer counts more tokens for the same text, especially for code ("SETTING_12 = 12 * 1024 # tuned" splits into many BPE tokens), so the same budget yields twice as many code chunks: exactly the reason to count with the embedding model's own tokenizer. 10 · Testing the invariants

The tests are written as invariants rather than expected outputs, because exact chunk boundaries change whenever the tokenizer or budget changes, while the properties must always hold. Budget: every chunk's token count, prefix included, is at most the maximum. Coverage: every sentence of the source appears whole in some chunk body, found by a regular expression over the source and a substring check over the joined bodies. Structure: every chunk of the table section contains the header row; no code chunk ends in the middle of a line. Overlap: in a prose section, most consecutive chunks share a sentence. Termination: a 5,000-character string with no spaces, followed by 300 short words, at a 40-token budget, finishes and loses no characters. Stability: chunking twice gives identical ids, and an edit to one sentence changes at most a few. Running every test with two different tokenizers is itself a test: it proves the chunker depends only on the interface.

11 · The full solution

The file below is the complete chunker with six tests. It runs on plain Python 3.12 using the stdlib RegexTokenizer, which treats a word or punctuation mark with its leading whitespace as one token (roughly how BPE tokenizers treat English), and it uses tiktoken's cl100k_base automatically if tiktoken is installed. All tests pass under both. The last test prints the characters-versus-tokens comparison.

"""A token-aware, structure-aware Markdown chunker.
Splits on headings, then blocks (paragraphs, fenced code, tables), then sentences, then words,
then raw tokens; packs pieces greedily under a token budget that includes a heading-path prefix;
adds sentence-level overlap; gives every chunk a content-addressed id.
Python 3.12 standard library; uses tiktoken if installed, else a regex tokenizer.
Run: python chunker.py"""
from __future__ import annotations

import hashlib
import re
from dataclasses import dataclass
from functools import lru_cache
from typing import Protocol


class Tokenizer(Protocol):
    def encode(self, text: str) -> list[int]: ...
    def decode(self, ids: list[int]) -> str: ...


class RegexTokenizer:
    """Stdlib stand-in: a word or punctuation mark with its leading whitespace is one token
    (roughly how BPE tokenizers treat English).
    Lossless (decode(encode(t)) == t). Real BPE tokenizers count differently; the chunker
    only relies on encode/decode, so swapping in tiktoken changes no other code."""
    _pat = re.compile(r"\s*\w+|\s*[^\w\s]|\s+")

    def __init__(self) -> None:
        self.vocab: dict[str, int] = {}
        self.inv: list[str] = []

    def encode(self, text: str) -> list[int]:
        out = []
        for piece in self._pat.findall(text):
            if piece not in self.vocab:
                self.vocab[piece] = len(self.inv)
                self.inv.append(piece)
            out.append(self.vocab[piece])
        return out

    def decode(self, ids: list[int]) -> str:
        return "".join(self.inv[i] for i in ids)


def default_tokenizer() -> Tokenizer:
    try:
        import tiktoken
        return tiktoken.get_encoding("cl100k_base")
    except Exception:                                   # not installed or no cached encoding file
        return RegexTokenizer()


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Chunk:
    id: str
    text: str                  # what gets embedded: heading path prefix + body
    body: str
    heading_path: tuple[str, ...]
    tokens: int


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class _Piece:
    text: str
    kind: str                  # "sent", "code", "table", "word", "cut"
    path: tuple[str, ...]
    joiner: str                # text placed before this piece when joined to the previous one


_FENCE = re.compile(r"^(```|~~~)")
_HEADING = re.compile(r"^(#{1,6})\s+(.*\S)\s*$")
_SENT = re.compile(r"(?<=[.!?])\s+(?=[A-Z0-9\"'(\[])")


class MarkdownChunker:
    def __init__(self, max_tokens: int = 512, overlap_tokens: int = 64,
                 tokenizer: Tokenizer | None = None, path_sep: str = " > ") -> None:
        if not 0 <= overlap_tokens < max_tokens // 2:
            raise ValueError("overlap must be non-negative and well below max_tokens")
        self.max, self.overlap, self.sep = max_tokens, overlap_tokens, path_sep
        self.tok = tokenizer or default_tokenizer()
        self.count = lru_cache(maxsize=100_000)(lambda s: len(self.tok.encode(s)))

    # ---------- 1. parse Markdown into blocks, each tagged with its heading path ----------
    def _blocks(self, md: str) -> list[tuple[str, str, tuple[str, ...]]]:
        lines, path, out, i = md.splitlines(), [], [], 0
        para: list[str] = []

        def flush_para() -> None:
            if para:
                out.append(("para", " ".join(l.strip() for l in para), tuple(path)))
                para.clear()
        while i < len(lines):
            line = lines[i]
            if _FENCE.match(line):                      # fenced code: keep verbatim
                flush_para()
                j = i + 1
                while j < len(lines) and not lines[j].startswith(line[:3]):
                    j += 1
                out.append(("code", "\n".join(lines[i:j + 1]), tuple(path)))
                i = j + 1
                continue
            m = _HEADING.match(line)
            if m:
                flush_para()
                level = len(m.group(1))
                del path[level - 1:]
                path.extend([""] * (level - 1 - len(path)))  # tolerate skipped levels
                path.append(m.group(2))
                path[:] = [p for p in path if p]
            elif line.lstrip().startswith("|"):         # table: consecutive pipe rows
                flush_para()
                j = i
                while j < len(lines) and lines[j].lstrip().startswith("|"):
                    j += 1
                out.append(("table", "\n".join(lines[i:j]), tuple(path)))
                i = j
                continue
            elif not line.strip():
                flush_para()
            else:
                para.append(line)
            i += 1
        flush_para()
        return out

    def _prefix(self, path: tuple[str, ...]) -> str:
        return (self.sep.join(path) + "\n") if path else ""

    # ---------- 2. break blocks into pieces no larger than the budget ----------
    def _pieces(self, kind: str, text: str, path: tuple[str, ...], budget: int) -> list[_Piece]:
        if kind == "code":
            if self.count(text) <= budget:
                return [_Piece(text, "code", path, "\n\n")]
            return self._split_lines(text, path, budget, header=None, kind="code")
        if kind == "table":
            if self.count(text) <= budget:
                return [_Piece(text, "table", path, "\n\n")]
            rows = text.splitlines()
            header = "\n".join(rows[:2])                # header row + separator row
            return self._split_lines("\n".join(rows[2:]), path, budget, header, "table")
        out = []
        for k, sent in enumerate(_SENT.split(text)):
            joiner = "\n\n" if k == 0 else " "
            if self.count(sent) <= budget:
                out.append(_Piece(sent, "sent", path, joiner))
            else:
                out.extend(self._split_words(sent, path, budget, joiner))
        return out

    def _split_lines(self, text, path, budget, header, kind) -> list[_Piece]:
        """Code by lines, tables by rows with the header repeated on every piece."""
        out, buf = [], []
        room = budget - (self.count(header + "\n") if header else 0)
        for line in text.splitlines():
            cand = "\n".join(buf + [line])
            if buf and self.count(cand) > room:
                body = "\n".join(buf)
                out.append(_Piece(f"{header}\n{body}" if header else body, kind, path, "\n\n"))
                buf = []
            buf.append(line)
        if buf:
            body = "\n".join(buf)
            out.append(_Piece(f"{header}\n{body}" if header else body, kind, path, "\n\n"))
        return [p for q in out for p in ([q] if self.count(q.text) <= budget
                                         else self._hard_cut(q.text, path, budget))]

    def _split_words(self, sent, path, budget, joiner) -> list[_Piece]:
        out, buf = [], ""
        for w in sent.split(" "):
            cand = f"{buf} {w}" if buf else w
            if buf and self.count(cand) > budget:
                out.append(_Piece(buf, "word", path, joiner if not out else " "))
                buf = w
            else:
                buf = cand
            if self.count(buf) > budget:                # one enormous "word": cut by tokens
                out.extend(self._hard_cut(buf, path, budget))
                buf = ""
        if buf:
            out.append(_Piece(buf, "word", path, joiner if not out else " "))
        return out

    def _hard_cut(self, text, path, budget) -> list[_Piece]:
        ids = self.tok.encode(text)
        return [_Piece(self.tok.decode(ids[i:i + budget]), "cut", path, "")
                for i in range(0, len(ids), budget)]

    # ---------- 3. pack pieces greedily, never across a section, with overlap ----------
    def chunk(self, md: str, doc_id: str = "doc") -> list[Chunk]:
        chunks: list[Chunk] = []
        sections: dict[tuple[str, ...], list[_Piece]] = {}
        for kind, text, path in self._blocks(md):
            budget = self.max - self.count(self._prefix(path))
            if budget < 16:
                raise ValueError(f"heading path too long for max_tokens: {path}")
            sections.setdefault(path, []).extend(self._pieces(kind, text, path, budget))
        for path, pieces in sections.items():
            prefix = self._prefix(path)
            budget = self.max - self.count(prefix)
            cur: list[_Piece] = []
            fresh = 0                                    # pieces in `cur` not shared with the previous chunk

            def body_of(ps: list[_Piece]) -> str:
                return "".join((p.joiner if k else "") + p.text for k, p in enumerate(ps))

            def emit() -> None:
                body = body_of(cur)
                text = prefix + body
                h = hashlib.sha256(f"{doc_id}\x00{text}".encode()).hexdigest()[:16]
                chunks.append(Chunk(h, text, body, path, self.count(text)))
            for p in pieces:
                if cur and self.count(body_of(cur + [p])) > budget:
                    emit()
                    keep: list[_Piece] = []             # carry trailing sentences as overlap
                    for q in reversed(cur):
                        if q.kind != "sent" or self.count(body_of([q] + keep)) > self.overlap:
                            break
                        keep.insert(0, q)
                    if keep and self.count(body_of(keep + [p])) > budget:
                        keep = []                        # overlap must never block progress
                    cur, fresh = keep, 0
                cur.append(p)
                fresh += 1
            if cur and fresh:
                emit()
        return chunks

DOC = """# Refunds

## Eligibility
Orders may be refunded within 30 days of delivery. Digital goods are
excluded unless faulty. Gift cards are never refundable.

## Process
Contact support with the order number. A decision is issued within
five working days. Approved refunds return to the original payment
method and take three to five days to appear.
"""


def long_doc() -> str:
    paras = []
    for s in range(1, 4):
        paras.append(f"## Section {s}")
        for p in range(6):
            paras.append(" ".join(
                f"Sentence {s}.{p}.{k} explains rule number {k} of section {s} in plain words."
                for k in range(5)))
            paras.append("")
    paras += ["## Config", "```python", *[f"SETTING_{i} = {i} * 1024  # tuned" for i in range(60)],
              "```", "", "## Prices", "| plan | price | seats |", "|---|---|---|",
              *[f"| plan-{i} | {i * 7} USD | {i} |" for i in range(80)]]
    return "# Handbook\n\n" + "\n".join(paras)


def check_all(ch: MarkdownChunker, md: str) -> list[Chunk]:
    out = ch.chunk(md)
    assert out, "no chunks"
    for c in out:
        assert c.tokens <= ch.max, (c.tokens, c.text[:80])
        assert c.text.startswith(" > ".join(c.heading_path))
    return out


def test_drill_example_two_chunks() -> None:
    for tok in (RegexTokenizer(), default_tokenizer()):
        ch = MarkdownChunker(max_tokens=60, overlap_tokens=10, tokenizer=tok)
        out = check_all(ch, DOC)
        assert [c.heading_path for c in out] == [("Refunds", "Eligibility"), ("Refunds", "Process")]
        assert out[1].text.startswith("Refunds > Process\nContact support")


def test_budget_structure_and_sentences() -> None:
    md = long_doc()
    for tok in (RegexTokenizer(), default_tokenizer()):
        ch = MarkdownChunker(max_tokens=120, overlap_tokens=30, tokenizer=tok)
        out = check_all(ch, md)
        # every sentence of the source appears whole in at least one chunk
        sentences = re.findall(r"Sentence \d+\.\d+\.\d+ [^.]*\.", md)
        bodies = "\n".join(c.body for c in out)
        assert all(s in bodies for s in sentences)
        # table pieces each repeat the header; code pieces stay inside the fence lines' content
        tables = [c for c in out if c.heading_path[-1] == "Prices"]
        assert len(tables) > 1 and all("| plan | price | seats |" in c.body for c in tables)
        code = [c for c in out if c.heading_path[-1] == "Config"]
        assert all(not re.search(r"SETTING_\d+ = \d+ \* 10\n", c.body) for c in code)  # no mid-line cut


def test_overlap_shares_a_sentence() -> None:
    ch = MarkdownChunker(max_tokens=80, overlap_tokens=25, tokenizer=RegexTokenizer())
    out = [c for c in ch.chunk(long_doc()) if c.heading_path == ("Handbook", "Section 1")]
    shared = 0
    for a, b in zip(out, out[1:]):
        last = _SENT.split(a.body.split("\n\n")[-1])[-1]
        shared += b.body.startswith(last)
    assert shared >= len(out) // 2, (shared, len(out))


def test_giant_word_and_tiny_budget_terminate() -> None:
    ch = MarkdownChunker(max_tokens=40, overlap_tokens=10, tokenizer=default_tokenizer())
    md = "# T\n\n" + "x" * 5000 + " end. " + "word " * 300

    assert sum(len(c.body) for c in out) >= 5000

12 · Edge cases

Empty document or only headings: return no chunks. Text before the first heading: it has an empty path and no prefix. Skipped heading levels (a level-3 heading directly under a level-1): the parser tolerates them. Heading path longer than the budget: raise a clear error rather than producing chunks that are all prefix. An unclosed code fence: the block runs to the end of the document; arguably correct, and worth a warning. A line inside a code block that is itself over budget, such as minified JavaScript: hard-cut it. Whitespace and Unicode: normalise line endings and Unicode form before hashing, or the same text from two systems produces different ids. Overlap greater than the budget: rejected in the constructor. Non-English text: the sentence regex assumes Latin punctuation and capitals; Chinese or Japanese text needs different sentence boundaries.

13 · Extensions the interviewer will ask for

Parent-document retrieval: emit small chunks for embedding and remember each chunk's section, so search matches on the small chunk and the model receives the whole section. Contextual descriptions: have a model write one or two sentences situating each chunk in its document and prepend them before embedding. Semantic chunking: split where the embedding similarity between consecutive sentences drops, to find topic shifts in unstructured text. Code-aware chunking: parse with tree-sitter and split on functions and classes, with the file path as the heading path. HTML and PDF: convert to a typed block structure first (title, paragraph, list, table), then reuse the same splitter and packer. Streaming very large documents: process section by section so memory stays bounded.

14 · Complexity

Parsing is one pass over the lines, O(n) in document length. Splitting tokenizes each block and sentence a constant number of times. Packing as written measures the growing chunk body each time a piece is added, which on its own would be quadratic in the number of pieces per chunk; the lru_cache on the count function and the small number of pieces per chunk keep it fast in practice, and the clean linear version keeps a running token total, adding each piece's count plus the joiner's. Memory is O(n) for the pieces and output. The tokenizer dominates the constant factor, which is why its speed matters on large corpora.

Choosing the chunk size

Size is a trade-off with two failure modes. A small chunk (a sentence or two) has a sharp embedding that matches specific questions precisely, but it may not contain enough context to answer, and an answer spanning three sentences is split across chunks of which retrieval may return only one. A large chunk (thousands of tokens) contains context but its single vector averages several topics, so it matches no specific question strongly, and it spends more of the generator's context window per retrieved chunk. The honest answer to "what size?" is a procedure: build a set of real questions with the passages that answer them, chunk at two or three sizes, and measure how often the answering passage is in the top k. The upper bound is hard: the embedding model's maximum input, since text beyond it is silently truncated by many APIs.

Structure beyond Markdown

Most real corpora are not clean Markdown. HTML has headings, lists and tables in tags; PDFs have only positioned text, from which headings must be inferred by font size and position; slide decks have one idea per slide; transcripts have speakers and timestamps. The robust architecture separates parsing from chunking: a format-specific parser emits a typed sequence of blocks (heading with level, paragraph, list item, table, code, caption), and one chunker consumes that sequence. The chunker in this lesson already works that way internally; its _blocks method is the Markdown parser, and replacing it with an HTML or PDF parser leaves splitting and packing unchanged.

Parent-document retrieval: separating search unit from context unit

The tension between precise matching and enough context dissolves if the unit you search and the unit you return differ. Index small chunks for matching, and when one matches, give the model its parent: the whole section, or a window of neighbouring chunks. The heading path and section grouping in this chunker make that easy, because every chunk already knows its section. The cost is more tokens per answer, so cap the parent size and deduplicate when several matched chunks share a parent.

Contextual retrieval

Anthropic's contextual retrieval technique goes beyond heading paths. For each chunk, a model is given the whole document and the chunk and asked for a short context that situates the chunk, such as which company and quarter a financial figure refers to. That context is prepended to the chunk before embedding and before keyword indexing. Anthropic reported that this reduced the top-20 retrieval failure rate from 5.7 percent to 3.7 percent with contextual embeddings, to 2.9 percent when combined with contextual BM25 keyword search, and to 1.9 percent with reranking added, and estimated the one-time cost at about one dollar per million document tokens using prompt caching, because the document is cached once and reused for all its chunks. It is the strongest cheap improvement in retrieval quality, and it sits naturally after this chunker: the chunker decides the boundaries, the model adds the context.

Why the overlap guard matters

Overlap bugs are the classic failure of hand-written chunkers. A character splitter that steps by size - overlap loops forever when the two are equal. A piece-based packer that always carries the overlap can emit a chunk that consists only of carried text plus nothing new, then carry the same text again: an infinite loop, or with a different guard an endless series of chunks each contained in its neighbour. The robust rule is that progress is measured in new pieces, and overlap is optional: if it would prevent adding at least one new piece, drop it. The test with a 5,000-character unbroken string and a 40-token budget exists to prove termination on exactly the input that breaks naive versions.

Sentence boundaries are harder than a regex

The regex used here splits after terminal punctuation followed by whitespace and an uppercase letter or digit. It fails on abbreviations ("Dr. Smith"), decimal numbers at sentence starts, quotes, lists without punctuation, and languages without spaces or with different punctuation. Production chunkers use trained sentence segmenters, or accept the heuristic because a bad split inside a large chunk costs little: the sentence is still whole in the chunk unless it falls exactly on a boundary, and overlap covers that case. The point to make is that sentence splitting is a dependency with a quality level, like the tokenizer.

Tokenizer mismatch

The tokenizer used to measure chunks must be the one the embedding model uses, or at least one that never undercounts it. Different vocabularies differ widely on the same text: code, numbers, non-English scripts and emoji are where they diverge most. A chunker that measures with one tokenizer and embeds with another can produce chunks that the embedding model truncates. If the exact tokenizer is unavailable, measure with a conservative one and leave a safety margin of, say, 10 percent of the limit, and verify on a sample by calling the provider's token-counting endpoint, where one exists. The same applies when chunks are later packed into a prompt for a generator model with a different tokenizer: budget the prompt with the generator's tokenizer, not the embedder's.

Idempotence, versions and normalisation

Content-addressed ids only help if the same document always yields byte-identical chunk text. Anything nondeterministic breaks that: different Unicode normal forms for the same accented character, Windows versus Unix line endings, timestamps inserted by a converter, or a PDF extractor whose output varies between runs. Normalise before chunking. And store the chunker's version with every chunk, because changing a rule (a new separator, a different overlap) legitimately changes ids; a version bump turns that into a planned full re-index instead of a mysterious mass re-embedding.

How chunking fails in production

The first failure is silent truncation. A chunk larger than the embedding model's input limit is often truncated without an error, so its final sentences are never embedded and can never be found. Symptoms are questions about the ends of sections failing while questions about their beginnings succeed. The fix is to count with the embedding model's own tokenizer, include every prefix in the budget, and assert the limit at ingestion.

The second is structural damage: tables split from their headers, code split mid-function, lists split from the sentence that introduces them, footnotes separated from their references. Answers built from these chunks are confidently wrong. Inspect samples of chunks from each document type, not just aggregate metrics; a chunk viewer that shows boundaries over the source document is one of the most useful internal tools a retrieval team can build.

The third is re-index churn. A pipeline that assigns sequential ids, or whose converter output varies slightly between runs, re-embeds the whole corpus every night. Cost and index write load go up, and caches keyed by chunk id are useless. Measure the fraction of chunks whose ids change per ingest; for mostly static corpora it should be near zero.

The fourth is near-duplicate crowding: heavy overlap or repeated boilerplate (headers, footers, legal notices on every page) produces many chunks with nearly the same text, and they occupy the top k results, pushing out the useful ones. Strip boilerplate before chunking, keep overlap modest, and deduplicate results before they reach the model.

What to measure

Chunk token-count distribution per document type (a spike at the maximum means many hard cuts or a too-small budget), the share of hard-cut and word-split pieces, the id churn rate per ingest, ingestion throughput, and most importantly retrieval quality on a labelled question set: how often the answering passage appears in the top k, broken down by document type. Chunking changes should be evaluated like model changes, with before-and-after numbers. A chunker change that improves the average while breaking one document type (say, tables) is common, so always look at the per-type breakdown before shipping.

Knobs

Chunk budget (bounded by the embedding model's limit), overlap budget, whether chunks may cross sections, the separator hierarchy per content type, whether to add heading paths or model-written context, and the parent size returned to the generator. Keep them in a versioned configuration so an experiment can run two chunkers side by side on the same corpus.

The real case: LangChain's recursive splitter

LangChain's RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter is one of the most widely used published chunkers, and its source shows the same design decisions, including the ones this lesson argues against as defaults. Its separators default to a paragraph break, a line break, a space and finally the empty string (which splits into single characters), and it recursively applies the next separator to any split that is still too long. Small splits are merged back up to chunk_size by a merge routine that keeps trailing splits as overlap up to chunk_overlap. The defaults in its base class are a chunk size of 4,000 and an overlap of 200, measured by a length_function that defaults to Python's len, which counts characters; a from_tiktoken_encoder constructor swaps in a tiktoken-based length so sizes are in tokens. The merge code even logs a warning when it creates a chunk longer than the specified size. Reading it is a good way to see why the interview version makes tokens the unit from the start, adds structure (headings, code, tables) that a generic text splitter cannot see, and makes the overlap guard explicit.

Goal: run the chunker, then break it, measure it and extend it. Python 3.12; tiktoken optional.

  1. Save the full solution as chunker.py and run it. With plain Python it uses the regex tokenizer; with tiktoken installed it uses cl100k_base. Note the printed comparison: 1,000 characters of JSON cost far more tokens than 1,000 characters of prose.
  2. Look at the chunks. Print every chunk of long_doc() at max_tokens=120 with its token count and heading path. Find a table chunk and confirm the header row is repeated; find two consecutive prose chunks and spot the shared sentence.
  3. Break it. Replace the guard line keep = [] with pass, disable the constructor check, and chunk long_doc() with max_tokens=60, overlap_tokens=55 using the regex tokenizer. Count chunks over 60 tokens: in our run 81 of 119 chunks broke the budget, because carried overlap plus the next piece no longer fit. Restore the guard.
  4. Measure it. Time chunking of a document built by repeating long_doc() 50 times, with and without the lru_cache on count. Then replace the repeated body_of measurement in the packer with a running token total and time it again.
  5. Extend it. Add a parent field to Chunk holding the full section text (or a section id) and a function that, given matched chunk ids, returns deduplicated parents in rank order.

Every change is checked by the same invariants: no chunk over budget, every sentence whole somewhere, termination, and stable ids. If one of them fails, the change is wrong, however good the chunks look.

Work it out

Chunk this Markdown with a 60-token limit and 10-token overlap, using the separator hierarchy heading, then blank line, then sentence, then word. Assume about one token per word or punctuation mark, and that the heading-path prefix counts toward the limit.

# Refunds

## Eligibility
Orders may be refunded within 30 days of delivery. Digital goods are
excluded unless faulty. Gift cards are never refundable.

## Process
Contact support with the order number. A decision is issued within
five working days. Approved refunds return to the original payment
method and take three to five days to appear.

(a) How many chunks, and where do the boundaries fall? (b) What does a query for "how long do refunds take to appear" retrieve, and is the answer safe? (c) What single change most improves this?

Answer

(a) The whole document is roughly 70 words plus punctuation, over 60, so it must split. Headings come first, giving two sections: Eligibility (about 24 words) and Process (about 35). Each plus its prefix ("Refunds > Eligibility", about 4 tokens) is under 60, so there are 2 chunks, split at the ## Process heading, and no overlap is applied because the split is at a section boundary. The full solution's first test asserts exactly this, with both tokenizers.

The splitter did not cut at 60 tokens; it cut where the document says a new topic starts, and the chunks are uneven. Uneven chunks aligned to structure beat even chunks that ignore it.

(b) The query matches chunk 2 strongly ("take three to five days to appear" is almost a lexical match), so retrieval succeeds. But chunk 2 contains two different durations: a decision within five working days, and three to five days for the money to appear. A model skimming it can answer "five working days" to a question about when the money arrives, which is the failure from the opening story. Without a prefix it would also never say that this is the refund process; a naive splitter that cut mid-section would leave "A decision is issued within five working days" with no subject at all.

(c) The single best change is the heading path, which this chunker already adds: "Refunds > Process" makes the chunk self-describing for the embedding and for the model, for four tokens. Beyond that, contextual retrieval would add a model-written line such as "This describes the steps and timings after a customer requests a refund: decision time, then time for funds to appear", which disambiguates the two durations. And an evaluation set should include this exact question, because it tests the answer, not just retrieval.
