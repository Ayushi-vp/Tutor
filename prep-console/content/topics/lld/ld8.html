Stand in the lobby of an office building at 8:55 in the morning. Forty people press the up button. Three cars are somewhere in the shaft, one heading down with passengers, one idle on the 12th floor, one stopped at the 3rd with its doors open. Which car should come? A car arrives and goes up, but on the way it stops at a floor where someone pressed down, opens, nobody gets on, and closes again. Another car passes your floor going the way you want and does not stop, because it was never told about you. Everyone has ridden elevators that behave like this, and every one of those behaviours is a software decision.

The elevator question is the second great object-modelling classic, after the parking lot. Candidates often hear it as an algorithm puzzle and start designing an optimal scheduler. The round is mostly not that. It tests whether you can model a system made of independent state machines (each car), coordinated by a pluggable policy (which car answers a call), with a clean line between what belongs to the building and what belongs to one car, and whether you can make that system testable.

The requirement that trips most candidates is that there are two different kinds of request. Someone on floor 7 pressing "down" has not chosen a destination, and any car may serve them: that is a dispatch problem for the system. Someone inside car B pressing "12" can only be served by car B: that is a scheduling problem inside one car. Designs with a single list of "requests" cannot express the difference, and their step function shows it immediately.

The second trap is ordering. A car that serves requests in the order they arrived bounces up and down the building. Real cars sweep: they keep going in one direction while there is work ahead, then reverse, an idea so well known that disk drives borrowed it and called it the elevator algorithm. The third trap is dispatching by distance alone, which ignores which way the car is heading.

Finally, elevators reward testability. A building cannot be used as a test bench, so the only way to know a policy works is to simulate it, and a design whose cars and dispatcher can run in a deterministic loop is a design that can be measured.

This lesson follows the round: the requirements to clarify, the entities and their responsibilities, the car's state machine and class diagram, a naive version and why it fails, then the correct build in stages (hall versus car calls, LOOK scheduling, the tick-driven step function, dispatchers as strategies, maintenance and reassignment), the full tested simulation, edge cases, the extensions interviewers ask for (capacity, starvation, destination dispatch), and complexity.

The mental model: cars are independent machines

Think of each car as a small robot with a very simple brain. It knows its floor, which way it is heading, whether its doors are open, and a list of floors where it has promised to stop. Each moment, it does one of a few things: wait, move one floor, open its doors, or keep them open a little longer. It does not know about other cars. A separate coordinator, the dispatcher, listens to the buttons on the landings and decides which robot should take each landing call, by adding a stop to that robot's list. That split, many simple independent machines plus one policy that assigns work, is the whole architecture, and it is the same shape as a fleet of delivery drivers, a pool of worker threads or a set of database replicas.

State machines

A finite-state machine is a model with a fixed set of states and rules for moving between them in response to events. A car has four states. Idle: no work, doors closed. Moving: travelling up or down, one floor per tick. Doors open: stopped at a floor for a fixed number of ticks. Maintenance: out of service, taking no calls. The rules connect them: an idle car with a stop elsewhere starts moving; a moving car that reaches a floor where it should stop opens its doors; doors close after their timer and the car either moves on or goes idle; maintenance can be entered from any state and left only to idle. Writing these down first turns most bugs into violations of a rule you already stated, such as "doors open while moving".

Hall calls and car calls

A hall call is the up or down button on a landing. It carries a floor and the direction the passenger wants, but not a destination. It is the building's problem: some car must come. A car call is a floor button inside a car. It carries only a floor, and it is that car's problem alone. Two consequences follow. Hall calls are assigned by the dispatcher, and the system should remember the assignment so it can change it later; car calls go straight to their car. And a hall call's direction matters for when the car stops: a car going up should not stop at floor 4 for someone who pressed "down", because that passenger would board a car going the wrong way.

Sweeping, not bouncing

Each car follows LOOK, a refinement of the classic elevator algorithm (SCAN). While there are stops ahead in the current direction, keep going, stopping at every floor that has a car call or a hall call in that direction. When nothing is ahead, reverse if there is work behind; otherwise go idle. LOOK differs from SCAN only in turning at the last request instead of travelling to the end of the shaft. The behaviour is fair (every request is reached within two sweeps) and efficient (no needless reversals), which is why it has survived from elevators to disk-arm scheduling and back.

Where the robot analogy breaks

Real cars are not fully independent: they share passengers' patience, the lobby's crowd and the building's energy budget, and a modern dispatcher plans across them. And real time is continuous, with acceleration, door sensors and people holding doors. The model deliberately simplifies to discrete ticks and fixed door times, which is enough to get the structure right and to compare policies; physics can be added inside the car without changing the architecture.

1 · Clarify the requirements

Ask how many cars and floors; whether all cars serve all floors or some are express or zoned; whether passengers press up and down on landings (conventional) or enter a destination in the lobby (destination dispatch); capacity limits; maintenance, fire-service or emergency modes; how long doors stay open; whether the goal is minimum waiting time, minimum journey time, or energy; and whether the deliverable is a controller for real hardware or a simulation. Each answer adds or removes a state, a field or a strategy.

A typical agreed scope: N cars, M floors, all cars serve all floors; conventional up and down hall buttons and car buttons; a car moves one floor per tick and keeps doors open for two ticks; maintenance mode; a pluggable dispatch policy; the system is a deterministic simulation driven by tick(). Invariants to state: a moving car always has a direction; a car never leaves the shaft; doors never open while moving; a hall call is served by a car travelling in the passenger's direction (or at its turnaround); no call waits forever.

2 · Entities and responsibilities

Dir and State are enums. HallCall is a frozen value (floor, direction), so two presses of the same button are equal and can be deduplicated in a dict. Car is an entity that owns its floor, direction, state, door timer and its set of stops, stored as (floor, direction) pairs where direction is None for a car call; it decides its own movement in step(). Dispatcher is a strategy interface with choose(call, cars). ElevatorSystem is the coordinator: it validates buttons, deduplicates hall calls, asks the dispatcher, records which car took each pending hall call and when, routes car calls, handles maintenance, advances time and records waiting times.

3 · Stage one: a naive version and why it fails

class Elevator:
    def __init__(self):
        self.floor, self.queue = 0, []          # requests in arrival order

    def request(self, floor):
        self.queue.append(floor)

    def step(self):
        if not self.queue:
            return
        target = self.queue[0]
        if target == self.floor:
            self.queue.pop(0)                    # open doors
        else:
            self.floor += 1 if target > self.floor else -1

Give it requests 9, 2, 8 from floor 0. It travels to 9, then all the way down to 2, then back up to 8, passing 8 on the first trip without stopping. Total travel: 9 + 7 + 6 = 22 floors instead of 8 floors going up (stopping at 2 and 8 on the way to 9). It has no notion of direction for landing calls, so it would happily stop at 4 for a "down" passenger while going up. It has one car and no dispatcher, so it cannot answer the question the round is about. And it has no states, so "doors open" is a single instant with no time for people to get in.

4 · Stage two: stops as (floor, direction) pairs

Each car stores its stops in a set of pairs: (7, Dir.DOWN) is a hall call at 7 for someone going down; (12, None) is a car call to 12. A set deduplicates automatically (pressing 12 twice adds nothing), membership is O(1), and the direction in the pair lets the car decide whether to stop. The rule for "should I open here?" while travelling in direction d is: open for car calls, and for hall calls in direction d. There is one exception, the turnaround floor: if there is no work further ahead, the car is about to reverse, so it also serves a hall call here for the opposite direction. Without that exception, a lone "down" call at the top of a sweep would never be served, because the car would arrive going up and then, with nothing ahead and the call behind it, oscillate.

5 · Stage three: LOOK in a tick-driven step

Car.step(tick) does at most one thing per tick. If in maintenance, nothing. If doors are open, count down the door timer and return until it reaches zero; then close and fall through to decide what next. To decide direction: if heading somewhere with work still ahead, keep going; if nothing is ahead but there is work behind, reverse; if idle with stops, head toward the nearest one; if no stops at all, go idle. Then, if there is something to serve at the current floor, open the doors (removing the served stops and recording the opening in a log) and return. Otherwise move one floor in the chosen direction, and if the new floor has something to serve, open there.

Trace the earlier example: car calls 5, 2 and 8 from floor 0. The nearest stop is 2, so the car heads up. It opens at 2 on tick 2, waits two ticks, continues to 5, opens, continues to 8, opens, and with no work left goes idle. The opening log reads 2, 5, 8, which is the first test. Now the direction rule: a car at floor 1 heading up to a car call at 9 receives a hall call (4, down). At floor 4 it is going up and there is work ahead (9), so 4-down is not served. It continues to 9, opens, finds work behind, reverses, and opens at 4 on the way down. The opening log reads 9, 4, which is the second test.

6 · Stage four: dispatch as a strategy

When a hall call arrives, ElevatorSystem.hall_call validates it (no "down" button on the ground floor, no "up" on the top), returns the existing assignment if the same call is already pending, filters out cars in maintenance, asks the dispatcher to choose, adds the stop to that car, and records the assignment with the current tick. Two dispatchers implement the interface.

NearestCar picks the car with the smallest absolute distance. It is the obvious first answer and it is weak, because it ignores direction. DirectionalCost estimates how far a car must travel before it reaches the caller moving in the caller's direction. An idle car costs its distance. A car already heading toward the caller, in the direction the caller wants, costs its distance: the call is on its way. Any other car must first finish its current sweep, to the farthest stop in its direction (or the caller's floor, if that is farther), and then come back: cost equals the distance to that far end plus the distance from there to the caller. Ties go to the car with the lower id, so results are deterministic.

The drill's scenario is a test: car A at floor 2 heading up with stops 5 and 8; car B at 9 heading down with stops 6 and 1; car C idle at 5; a hall call at 7, down. Directional costs are A 7 (up to 8, back down 1), B 2 (on its way), C 2 (idle, distance 2). B and C tie on cost and B wins on id; better still, B arrives already heading down, the way the passenger wants. Nearest-car sees B and C both at distance 2 and, with C listed first, picks C, which arrives and then has to choose a direction for a passenger it cannot yet read.

7 · Stage five: the system owns pending hall calls

The system keeps pending, a dict from each hall call to the car it was given and the tick it was placed. After each tick it checks which pending calls have disappeared from their car's stops, records their waiting time, and removes them. That ownership is what makes reassignment possible. When a car enters maintenance, set_maintenance removes its hall stops (keeping car calls, which only matter to passengers inside), deletes those calls from pending, and dispatches them again among the cars still in service. The maintenance test assigns a call to car A, puts A into maintenance, checks that the call now belongs to car B, runs the simulation and checks that B served it while A never opened its doors.

8 · Stage six: simulation and invariants

Each tick the system steps every car and calls car.check(floors), which asserts that the car is inside the shaft, every stop is a real floor, and a moving car has a direction. The randomised test runs three cars in a 15-floor building for 1,500 ticks with a hall call arriving with probability 0.15 per tick from a seeded random generator, each followed by a destination pressed inside the assigned car, then drains for 200 ticks. It asserts no call is left pending (no starvation) and prints the mean waiting time per dispatcher. Across five seeds, nearest-car averaged about 5 to 6.4 ticks per hall call and directional cost about 3.1 to 4.5: 25 to 45 percent shorter waits from a smarter cost function, with every other line of code unchanged. That comparison is the strongest possible argument for making dispatch a strategy.

9 · A tick-by-tick trace

Following one scenario tick by tick is the best way to check the step function, and interviewers often ask for it. One car at floor 0, idle, door time two ticks; at tick 0 the passenger inside presses 3, and at tick 1 someone at floor 2 presses down.

Had they pressed up instead, the car would have stopped at 2 on tick 1's move. Writing this table before coding catches most off-by-one errors in the door timer and the order of "decide, serve, move".

10 · Testing strategy

Tests for state machines work best at three levels. First, unit scenarios that pin one behaviour each: sweep order, direction filtering, turnaround service, maintenance handover, deduplication of repeated presses. They assert on the log of door openings, which is a compact, readable record of what the car did. Second, the cost function tested directly on hand-built car states, like the drill, without running a simulation at all, because a policy is just a function from state to a number. Third, a randomised simulation with fixed seeds, invariants checked every tick, and a final assertion that nothing was starved. The random test finds the bugs nobody thought of (the missing turnaround rule shows up as a car leaving the shaft); the seed makes any failure reproducible; and the scenario tests explain what broke. Avoid asserting exact mean waits in tests, since any policy tweak changes them; assert relationships instead, or just print them for comparison.

11 · The full solution

The file below contains the car state machine with LOOK, both dispatchers, the system with deduplication, maintenance and wait tracking, and five tests. Python 3.12, standard library only; runs in under a second.

"""Elevator system: per-car state machine with LOOK scheduling, hall calls vs car calls,
pluggable dispatchers (nearest vs directional cost), tick-driven simulation with invariants.
Python 3.12, standard library only. Run: python elevator.py"""
from __future__ import annotations

import random
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from enum import Enum
from typing import Protocol


class Dir(Enum):
    UP = 1
    DOWN = -1
    IDLE = 0


class State(Enum):
    IDLE = "idle"
    MOVING = "moving"
    DOORS_OPEN = "doors_open"
    MAINTENANCE = "maintenance"


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class HallCall:
    floor: int
    direction: Dir                       # UP or DOWN: which way the passenger wants to go


CAR = None                               # a car call has no direction of its own


@dataclass
class Car:
    id: str
    floor: int = 0
    direction: Dir = Dir.IDLE
    state: State = State.IDLE
    door_ticks: int = 2                  # ticks the doors stay open at a stop
    stops: set[tuple[int, Dir | None]] = field(default_factory=set)
    _open_left: int = 0
    log: list[tuple[int, int]] = field(default_factory=list)   # (tick, floor) of each door opening

    # ---- requests ----
    def add_car_stop(self, floor: int) -> None:
        self.stops.add((floor, CAR))

    def add_hall_stop(self, call: HallCall) -> None:
        self.stops.add((call.floor, call.direction))

    def floors(self) -> set[int]:
        return {f for f, _ in self.stops}

    # ---- LOOK: keep going while there is work ahead, then reverse ----
    def _work_ahead(self, d: Dir) -> bool:
        return any((f - self.floor) * d.value > 0 for f in self.floors())

    def _serve_here(self, d: Dir) -> set[tuple[int, Dir | None]]:
        """Stops at this floor we may serve travelling in direction d."""
        here = {s for s in self.stops if s[0] == self.floor}
        if d is Dir.IDLE:
            return here
        ok = {s for s in here if s[1] in (CAR, d)}
        if not self._work_ahead(d):              # turnaround floor: serve opposite hall call too
            ok |= here
        return ok

    def step(self, tick: int) -> None:
        if self.state is State.MAINTENANCE:
            return
        if self.state is State.DOORS_OPEN:
            self._open_left -= 1
            if self._open_left > 0:
                return
            self.state = State.IDLE                  # doors closed; decide below
        if self.direction is Dir.IDLE or not self._work_ahead(self.direction):
            # pick a direction: continue if work ahead, else reverse, else idle
            if self.direction is not Dir.IDLE and self._work_ahead(Dir(-self.direction.value)):
                self.direction = Dir(-self.direction.value)
            elif self.direction is Dir.IDLE and self.stops:
                nearest = min(self.floors(), key=lambda f: (abs(f - self.floor), f))
                self.direction = (Dir.UP if nearest > self.floor else
                                  Dir.DOWN if nearest < self.floor else Dir.IDLE)
            elif not self.stops:
                self.direction = Dir.IDLE
        served = self._serve_here(self.direction)
        if served:
            self._open(served, tick)
            return
        if self.direction is Dir.IDLE:
            self.state = State.IDLE
            return
        self.state = State.MOVING
        self.floor += self.direction.value
        served = self._serve_here(self.direction)
        if served:
            self._open(served, tick)

    def _open(self, served, tick: int) -> None:
        self.stops -= served
        if any(d is not CAR for _, d in served) and self.direction is Dir.IDLE:
            self.direction = next(d for _, d in served if d is not CAR)   # passenger's direction
        self.state, self._open_left = State.DOORS_OPEN, self.door_ticks
        self.log.append((tick, self.floor))

    def check(self, floors: int) -> None:
        assert 0 <= self.floor < floors, "left the shaft"
        assert all(0 <= f < floors for f in self.floors())
        if self.state is State.MOVING:
            assert self.direction is not Dir.IDLE, "moving with no direction"


# ------------------------------------------------------------------ dispatch strategies
class Dispatcher(Protocol):
    def choose(self, call: HallCall, cars: list[Car]) -> Car: ...


class NearestCar:
    def choose(self, call: HallCall, cars: list[Car]) -> Car:
        return min(cars, key=lambda c: abs(c.floor - call.floor))


class DirectionalCost:
    """Extra floors a car must travel to reach the caller heading the caller's way."""

    def cost(self, c: Car, call: HallCall) -> int:
        d = call.floor - c.floor
        if c.direction is Dir.IDLE or not c.stops:
            return abs(d)                                        # idle: straight there
        D = c.direction.value
        if c.direction is call.direction and d * D >= 0:
            return abs(d)                                        # on the way: no detour
        # otherwise: finish the sweep to its far end, then come back to the caller
        far = max([f * D for f in c.floors()] + [c.floor * D, call.floor * D]) * D
        return abs(far - c.floor) + abs(far - call.floor)

    def choose(self, call: HallCall, cars: list[Car]) -> Car:
        return min(cars, key=lambda c: (self.cost(c, call), c.id))


# ------------------------------------------------------------------ the system
class ElevatorSystem:
    def __init__(self, n_cars: int, floors: int, dispatcher: Dispatcher) -> None:
        self.floors, self.dispatcher, self.tick_no = floors, dispatcher, 0
        self.cars = [Car(chr(ord("A") + i)) for i in range(n_cars)]
        self.pending: dict[HallCall, tuple[str, int]] = {}      # call -> (car id, tick placed)
        self.waits: list[int] = []

    def hall_call(self, floor: int, direction: Dir) -> str:
        if not 0 <= floor < self.floors or direction is Dir.IDLE:
            raise ValueError("bad hall call")
        if (floor == 0 and direction is Dir.DOWN) or (floor == self.floors - 1 and direction is Dir.UP):
            raise ValueError("no such button")
        call = HallCall(floor, direction)
        if call in self.pending:                                 # pressing again changes nothing
            return self.pending[call][0]
        candidates = [c for c in self.cars if c.state is not State.MAINTENANCE]
        if not candidates:
            raise RuntimeError("no car in service")
        car = self.dispatcher.choose(call, candidates)
        car.add_hall_stop(call)
        self.pending[call] = (car.id, self.tick_no)
        return car.id

    def car_call(self, car_id: str, floor: int) -> None:
        if not 0 <= floor < self.floors:
            raise ValueError("no such floor")
        next(c for c in self.cars if c.id == car_id).add_car_stop(floor)

    def set_maintenance(self, car_id: str, on: bool) -> None:
        car = next(c for c in self.cars if c.id == car_id)
        if on:
            hall = {s for s in car.stops if s[1] is not CAR}
            car.stops -= hall
            car.state, car.direction = State.MAINTENANCE, Dir.IDLE
            for f, d in hall:                                     # hand its hall calls to others
                del self.pending[HallCall(f, d)]
                self.hall_call(f, d)
        else:
            car.state = State.IDLE

    def tick(self) -> None:
        for car in self.cars:
            car.step(self.tick_no)
            car.check(self.floors)
        for call, (cid, placed) in list(self.pending.items()):
            car = next(c for c in self.cars if c.id == cid)
            if (call.floor, call.direction) not in car.stops:     # served this tick
                self.waits.append(self.tick_no - placed)
                del self.pending[call]
        self.tick_no += 1

    def run(self, ticks: int) -> None:
        for _ in range(ticks):
            self.tick()


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- tests
def test_look_serves_in_sweep_order() -> None:
    s = ElevatorSystem(1, 10, NearestCar())
    for f in (5, 2, 8):
        s.car_call("A", f)
    s.run(30)
    assert [f for _, f in s.cars[0].log] == [2, 5, 8]


def test_opposite_direction_call_waits_for_the_return_sweep() -> None:
    s = ElevatorSystem(1, 10, NearestCar())
    s.car_call("A", 9)
    s.run(2)                                    # car is moving up
    s.hall_call(4, Dir.DOWN)                    # passenger at 4 wants to go DOWN
    s.run(40)
    assert [f for _, f in s.cars[0].log] == [9, 4]   # did not stop at 4 on the way up


def test_drill_directional_cost_prefers_b() -> None:
    a = Car("A", floor=2, direction=Dir.UP, stops={(5, CAR), (8, CAR)})
    b = Car("B", floor=9, direction=Dir.DOWN, stops={(6, CAR), (1, CAR)})
    c = Car("C", floor=5)
    call = HallCall(7, Dir.DOWN)
    cost = DirectionalCost()
    assert [cost.cost(x, call) for x in (a, b, c)] == [7, 2, 2]
    assert DirectionalCost().choose(call, [c, b, a]).id == "B"   # tie broken by id, B < C
    assert NearestCar().choose(call, [c, b, a]).id == "C"        # distance-only picks C first


def test_maintenance_hands_over_calls() -> None:
    s = ElevatorSystem(2, 10, NearestCar())
    s.cars[1].floor = 9
    assert s.hall_call(1, Dir.UP) == "A"
    s.set_maintenance("A", True)
    assert s.pending[HallCall(1, Dir.UP)][0] == "B"
    s.run(30)
    assert not s.pending and s.cars[0].log == []


def test_random_load_all_served_and_compare() -> None:
    results = {}
    for name, disp in (("nearest", NearestCar()), ("directional", DirectionalCost())):
        rng = random.Random(42)
        s = ElevatorSystem(3, 15, disp)
        for t in range(1_500):
            if rng.random() < 0.15:
                f = rng.randrange(15)
                d = Dir.UP if f == 0 else Dir.DOWN if f == 14 else rng.choice([Dir.UP, Dir.DOWN])
                car = s.hall_call(f, d)
                dest = rng.randrange(f + 1, 15) if d is Dir.UP else rng.randrange(0, f)
                s.car_call(car, dest)           # simplification: destination pressed at once
            s.tick()
        s.run(200)                              # drain
        assert not s.pending, f"{name}: starved calls {s.pending}"
        results[name] = sum(s.waits) / len(s.waits)
    print(f"    mean hall-call wait (ticks): {results}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    for t in (test_look_serves_in_sweep_order, test_opposite_direction_call_waits_for_the_return_sweep,
              test_drill_directional_cost_prefers_b, test_maintenance_hands_over_calls,
              test_random_load_all_served_and_compare):
        t()
        print("ok ", t.__name__)

12 · Edge cases

Invalid buttons: "down" on the ground floor or "up" on the top floor do not exist; reject. A call at the car's current floor while idle: open immediately. A call at the current floor while the car is leaving: the car has already moved; it will come back on a later sweep, which is what passengers see in real buildings. All cars in maintenance: raise a clear error, or queue calls until a car returns. Duplicate presses: deduplicated by value. A car call to the current floor: open the doors (people press their own floor by mistake). Simultaneous up and down calls at one floor: two separate hall calls, possibly served by different cars.

13 · Extensions the interviewer will ask for

Capacity: track load; a full car skips hall calls, which the system reassigns, and the cost function excludes nearly full cars. Starvation and fairness: add the age of a call to the cost, cap reassignments, alert on the longest wait. Door obstruction and timers: a door sensor event resets the door timer, a new state or a guard. Fire service and emergency recall: a mode that sends all cars to the ground floor and ignores calls, where the State pattern starts to pay. Zoning and express cars: each car has a set of floors it serves; the dispatcher filters by it. Destination dispatch: passengers enter their destination at the lobby kiosk; the interface becomes "trip requests" with origin and destination, and the dispatcher groups trips. Energy: idle cars park at likely-demand floors (the lobby in the morning), a policy plugged in when a car goes idle.

14 · Complexity

Each car's step scans its stops: O(S) for S stops, tiny in practice. Dispatch evaluates every car: O(N × S). A tick is O(N × S) plus O(P) for checking P pending calls. Memory is O(N × S + P). Real controllers have a handful of cars and tens of stops, so the design is far below any performance concern; the cost function's quality matters, not its speed.

Why the cost function is the real design decision

Expressing dispatch as "choose the car with the minimum cost" turns policy into a single function that can be unit-tested, compared in simulation, tuned, and later replaced by a learned model without touching the car. An if-tree spread across the dispatcher (if a car is idle and close, else if moving towards...) encodes the same policy in a form nobody can change safely. Good cost functions add terms beyond travel distance: each existing stop costs door time (perhaps 5 to 10 seconds), a nearly full car is penalised or excluded, and the age of the call is subtracted so old calls win and nothing starves. Writing the cost as a weighted sum makes these trade-offs explicit and tunable.

Why the car, not the system, decides its movement

A tempting alternative puts all logic in the system: one big function that, every tick, decides where each car goes. It works for one car and becomes unmanageable for several, because every rule about a car's doors, direction and stops is tangled with every rule about dispatch. Keeping movement inside the car and assignment inside the dispatcher gives two small pieces with a narrow interface between them (the dispatcher adds stops; the car serves them in sweep order), so each can be tested and changed alone. It also matches the physical system, where each car has its own controller and a group controller assigns calls.

Assignment versus reassignment

Committing a call to a car at the moment of the press is simple and makes the "your car is C" display honest. But conditions change: a car fills up, another becomes idle nearby, or a car goes into maintenance. Systems that never revisit assignments perform worse under changing load; systems that revisit them constantly confuse passengers and can oscillate, moving a call back and forth between two cars so that neither comes. A practical compromise re-evaluates assignments periodically and moves a call only if the improvement is large, with a cap on moves per call. Whatever the policy, it requires the system to own pending hall calls, which is why the data structure decision comes first.

SCAN, LOOK and their disk-scheduling twins

The elevator algorithm is old enough to have been simulated in Knuth's The Art of Computer Programming, volume 1, where it illustrates coroutines and linked lists with a single elevator in Caltech's mathematics building. Disk drives adopted it to schedule the read head: serve requests in one direction across the platter, then reverse, avoiding the long seeks of first-come, first-served. Variants trade fairness for efficiency. LOOK turns at the last request rather than the end of the shaft. C-SCAN and C-LOOK serve in one direction only and jump back, which equalises waiting time between floors near the ends and in the middle, at the cost of an empty return trip. Elevators use LOOK-like behaviour because an empty return trip in a building wastes energy and time.

Ticks versus events

The simulation here advances in fixed ticks, which is easy to reason about and test. Real controllers are event-driven: a car reports "approaching floor 7" and the controller decides within milliseconds whether to stop, because a car moving at speed must begin to decelerate well before the floor. A discrete-event simulation, jumping from event to event instead of tick to tick, models that more faithfully and runs faster for long simulations. The architecture is unchanged: the car's decision function receives the same inputs, so the tick-based model is a valid first version and its tests stay valid.

Concurrency: one control loop

Buttons are pressed on dozens of panels while cars report sensor events, so a real controller receives events concurrently. The simplest correct structure is a single control loop that owns all state and consumes events from one queue: button presses, sensor reports and timer ticks are all messages, processed one at a time. No locks are needed inside the model because only one thread ever touches it, and the order of events is recorded, which makes production incidents replayable in the simulator. The alternative, many threads calling hall_call and tick directly, needs a lock around each and still risks subtle interleavings (a call assigned to a car during the same tick that car enters maintenance). The event-loop design is the same idea as the batcher's single assembler: give mutable state one owner.

What are we optimising?

"Best" dispatch depends on the goal, and the goals conflict. Minimising average waiting time favours busy floors and can leave a quiet floor waiting a long time. Minimising the maximum wait is fairer but lowers average performance. Minimising journey time (wait plus ride) penalises picking up extra passengers on the way, which waiting-time policies encourage. Energy favours fewer, fuller trips. A cost function is the place where these goals are weighed, and saying out loud which one you are optimising, and what you give up, is a better answer than any particular formula.

Destination dispatch changes the interface

In tall buildings with many cars, conventional buttons waste capacity: a car leaves the lobby with ten people pressing ten different floors and stops at all of them. Destination dispatch asks passengers to enter their destination at a kiosk before boarding, and the controller groups passengers going to nearby floors into the same car, telling each person which car to take. Wikipedia's article traces the idea to Leo Port's 1961 patent and the first commercial system to Schindler's Miconic 10 in 1990. In software terms the change is fundamental: requests become trips with an origin and a destination known up front, cars have no internal buttons, and dispatch becomes an optimisation over groups of trips. Recognising that the class model must change, not just the algorithm, is a strong answer to the "how would this scale to a skyscraper" question.

How real elevator control differs and fails

Production elevator controllers are safety-critical embedded systems, and much of their logic is about failure. Doors must reopen when an obstruction sensor fires; a car must never move with doors open; a car that loses power must stop safely; fire service modes override all normal dispatch. These rules are typically enforced in hardware interlocks and certified safety controllers below the dispatch software, so the dispatch layer can make poor decisions without making unsafe ones. That layering, a simple, verified safety core with a smarter policy on top, is a pattern worth naming for any system where policies change but some invariants must never break.

The dispatch software fails in recognisable ways. Bunching: all cars end up travelling together because each decision was locally optimal, leaving long gaps between arrivals at any floor. Oscillation: a call reassigned back and forth so that no car commits. Starvation of upper or lower floors when the cost function favours busy middle floors. Morning and evening peaks overwhelm policies tuned for midday. Each has a cure in the cost function or in mode switching (an "up-peak" mode that returns cars to the lobby immediately after each trip in the morning).

Simulation before deployment

Building owners cannot experiment on tenants, so dispatch policies are tuned in simulation, fed with recorded traffic: when people arrived at each floor and where they went. The tick-driven model in this lesson is a miniature of that practice. Its value comes from being deterministic and fast: thousands of simulated days can be compared in minutes, and a policy change is accepted only if it improves the metric that matters without making any floor's tail waits worse. The same discipline, replaying recorded traffic against a candidate policy before shipping it, applies to load balancers, schedulers and cache eviction policies.

What to measure

Average and 95th-percentile waiting time per floor and time of day, journey time, the share of calls waiting more than 60 seconds, car round-trip time during peaks, load factor on departure from the lobby, number of reassignments per call, and energy per trip. These are the metrics building operators use to tune dispatch, and they map directly to the waiting times this lesson's simulation records.

Knobs

Door open time, the weights in the cost function (distance, stop penalty, load, age), the reassignment threshold, parking floors for idle cars by time of day, and peak-mode schedules. A simulation like the one in this lesson, fed with recorded traffic from the building, is how such knobs are tuned safely before they reach real passengers.

The real case: the elevator algorithm and destination dispatch

Two public references anchor this lesson. The elevator algorithm, documented with its variants in the standard references, is the LOOK and SCAN behaviour every car here follows: travel in one direction while requests remain, stop for those on the way, then reverse. Its earliest published treatment is Knuth's simulation of a single elevator, and its best-known reuse is disk-head scheduling, where the same sweep beat first-come, first-served by avoiding long back-and-forth seeks. The second is destination dispatch, which manufacturers such as Schindler and KONE now sell for large buildings. Its documented idea is the one the "scale to a skyscraper" question is fishing for: collect each passenger's destination before boarding, then allocate passengers to cars so that groups heading to nearby floors share a car and intermediate stops are avoided. Together they mark the boundaries of the interview design: a clean per-car sweep for the car's own schedule, a pluggable dispatcher for the building's choices, and the recognition that at large scale the request interface itself changes.

Goal: run the simulation, then break, measure and extend it. Python 3.12, standard library only.

  1. Save the full solution as elevator.py and run it. Five tests print ok; note the printed mean waits for the two dispatchers.
  2. Break it. In Car._serve_here, remove the turnaround exception (the line that adds opposite-direction calls when there is no work ahead). Run the tests: the random-load test stops on the "left the shaft" invariant. With nothing it may serve at the turnaround floor and no work strictly ahead or behind, the car keeps its direction and drives through the roof, a bug the per-tick invariant catches at once.
  3. Break it again. Replace the direction filter so the car serves every stop at its floor regardless of direction. The second test fails: the car stops at 4 on its way up for a passenger going down.
  4. Measure it. Run the random-load comparison for ten seeds and for arrival probabilities 0.05, 0.15 and 0.3. Plot mean wait per dispatcher. Where does the gap between them grow?
  5. Extend it. Add a per-call age term to DirectionalCost and a capacity limit per car (count passengers boarding at hall stops and leaving at car stops), and make full cars skip hall calls, which the system reassigns.
  6. Extend it again. Add an up-peak mode: between ticks 0 and 300, idle cars return to floor 0.

For every change, keep the invariant checks on and the seeds fixed, so differences come from the policy, not from luck, and write down what you expect before you run it.

Work it out

A building has 3 cars and 10 floors. Car A is at floor 2 moving up with stops {5, 8}; car B is at floor 9 moving down with stops {6, 1}; car C is idle at floor 5. A hall call arrives: floor 7, going down.

(a) Which car serves it under nearest-car? (b) Under a directional cost function? (c) A second call then arrives at floor 3 going up, before any car reaches floor 7. What changes?

Answer

(a) Nearest car. Distances to floor 7: A is 5 away, B is 2, C is 2. B and C tie, and a naive implementation picks whichever comes first in its list, possibly C. Raw distance cannot tell them apart, yet they are very different choices.

(b) Directional cost. Evaluate what each car would actually do:

CarReasoningCost (floors)
AMoving up; must finish its sweep to 8, then come back down to 72→8 is 6, 8→7 is 1: 7
BMoving down from 9, passing 7 in the passenger's direction9→7: 2, no detour
CIdle at 5; travels up to 72, but arrives heading up

B and C tie on travel, but B is the better choice: it adds 7 to its down sweep and stops on the way, arriving already heading down, which is what the passenger wants. C would arrive going up and must then turn, and if a car call arrives meanwhile it may carry the passenger the wrong way first. The full solution breaks cost ties by car id, which picks B here; a production cost function would add an explicit small penalty for arriving in the wrong direction, or for leaving the idle car C available for future calls near the middle of the building.

(c) The second call, floor 3 going up. A is at 2 or 3 by now, moving up, so it passes floor 3 in the requested direction and serves it at no extra cost; directional cost gives A a cost of 0 or 1. C, idle at 5, is 2 away but would arrive heading down. B is going down to 6 and 1, so 3-up is on its way down but in the wrong direction; it would serve it only after reaching 1 and turning. A takes it.

The subtle point: had car A already passed floor 3 (say it is at 4), the call is behind it in its own direction, and A's cost becomes a full sweep up to 8, down to the bottom of its next sweep and back up to 3. Then C, idle and two floors away, becomes the right answer. The cost of serving a call depends on where the car is in its sweep, not just how far away it is, which is exactly what a distance metric cannot express.
