Four friends go on a weekend trip. Anna pays for dinner, Ben for taxis that only three of them took, Chloe for concert tickets, and Dan for nothing yet. On Sunday night someone asks the dreaded question: who owes whom? Someone opens a spreadsheet. Twenty minutes later they have eight payments to make, a disagreement about whether Dan was in the second taxi, a missing penny from splitting a bill three ways, and a suspicion that one expense was entered twice.

Splitwise and its competitors exist to make that question trivial, and the design behind them is a classic machine-coding round because it combines several ideas every engineer needs. Money must be exact, so floating-point numbers are out and every split must account for every cent. History must be trustworthy, so expenses are never edited in place: they are recorded once and corrected by adding new entries, the same principle accountants have used for centuries. Balances are derived from that history rather than stored separately, so they can never disagree with it. And at the end there is a genuinely interesting algorithmic question: what is the smallest set of payments that settles everyone, and why is the honest answer "usually the greedy one, because the exact one is NP-hard"?

The common failure in this round is modelling debts directly: a dictionary from (debtor, creditor) to amount, updated as expenses come in. It works for three expenses and collapses under editing (how do you undo an expense that was netted against three others?), rounding (where did the penny go?) and auditing (why does Ben owe 21.67?). The design that works stores facts and derives conclusions.

The other failure is treating simplification as pure maths. Splitwise's own blog post introducing the feature spent as many words on its effect on people as on its effect on numbers: simplification can tell Anna to pay Chloe, someone she never shared a bill with, and users who did not expect that were confused. The company published rules to keep the feature predictable.

It also rewards honesty about limits. Interviewers often ask for "the minimum number of payments", and the best answers explain why the minimum is hard and what the product actually ships.

This lesson follows the round: clarify the requirements, find the entities and the invariant, build a naive debt table and see it break, then build the ledger design in stages (integer money and exact allocation, split strategies, multi-payer expenses, reversals, idempotent settlements, derived and cached balances), then simplification, greedy and exact, the full tested solution, edge cases, extensions such as multiple currencies and concurrent edits, complexity, and Splitwise's published rules.

Facts versus conclusions

The key idea is to separate what happened from what it means. What happened is a list of events: Anna paid 120.00 for a dinner shared equally by four; Ben paid 45.00 for taxis shared by three; Dan paid Anna 65.00. These are facts; they never change. What it means, who owes how much, is a conclusion computed from the facts by adding them up. If a fact was wrong (the dinner was 126.00), you do not rewrite the conclusion; you record a correction and recompute. This is the principle of an append-only ledger, the same principle as double-entry bookkeeping, bank statements and version control, and it makes the system auditable and hard to corrupt.

The one number per person

For each person, the conclusion is a single net balance: everything they paid minus everything that was their share. Positive means the group owes them; negative means they owe the group. Because every expense's payments equal its shares, the balances of a group always sum to exactly zero. That invariant is the most useful line of code in the solution: assert it, and every rounding bug or bookkeeping error is caught the moment it happens.

Note what the net balance does not say: it does not say whom you owe. "Dan owes 65.00" is the fact the group needs; "Dan owes Anna" is a choice about how to settle. That separation is what makes simplification possible.

Money is integers

Binary floating-point numbers cannot represent most decimal fractions: in Python, 0.1 + 0.2 is 0.30000000000000004. Money computed in floats drifts by fractions of a cent, and the zero-sum invariant breaks. The standard fix is to store amounts as integers in the currency's smallest unit (cents or pence, called minor units), or to use Python's Decimal. Integers have a second advantage: they force you to decide what happens to remainders. 1,000 cents split three ways is 333 each with 1 cent left over, and some person must get it. The rule here is the largest remainder method: floor every share, then give the leftover cents to the shares whose exact values had the largest fractional parts, breaking ties by user id so the result is always the same.

The same idea elsewhere

Facts-versus-conclusions is everywhere in serious software. A bank stores transactions and derives your balance. Git stores commits and derives the state of your files. A database's write-ahead log stores changes and can rebuild tables from it after a crash. An e-commerce order system stores order events (placed, paid, shipped, refunded) and derives the order's status. Recognising the pattern in a small interview problem, and naming it, tells the interviewer you will apply it in larger ones.

The analogy: a shared kitty

Imagine the group had a shared jar. Each time someone pays for something, it is as if they put that money in the jar and the participants each took their share back out. At the end, some people have put in more than they took (they are owed) and some have taken more than they put in (they owe). Settling up means moving money so everyone has put in exactly what they took. The jar never needs to know who paid for which dinner, only each person's net position. The analogy breaks in one respect: there is no real jar, so every settlement is a payment between two specific people, and choosing which pairs pay each other is the simplification problem.

1 · Clarify the requirements

Ask which split types are needed (equal, exact amounts, percentages, shares), whether an expense can have several payers, whether expenses can include people outside the group, how rounding should work and who absorbs leftover cents, whether expenses can be edited and deleted and whether history must be kept, how settlements are recorded and whether they can be partial, whether multiple currencies exist, whether the app should suggest simplified payments, and whether several people may edit a group at once.

A typical agreed scope: groups of users; expenses with one or more payers and a split strategy among group members; four split types; amounts in integer cents of one currency; leftover cents assigned deterministically; edits and deletes allowed with full history; settlements recorded as payments, safe to retry; per-person balances; suggested payments with as few transfers as practical. The invariants to state: each entry's paid amounts equal its owed amounts; every split's shares sum to the expense total; a group's balances sum to zero.

2 · Entities and responsibilities

A Group owns its member set and its ledger, appends entries, and derives balances. An Entry is an immutable value with an id, a kind (expense, reversal, settlement), a description, a tuple of (user, cents) paid, a tuple of (user, cents) owed, and, for reversals, the id it reverses; its constructor rejects any entry whose sides do not balance. A SplitStrategy turns a total into per-user shares that sum exactly to it, with four implementations. The free function allocate does exact proportional allocation for three of them. Simplification is a pair of pure functions over balances: greedy_transfers and, for small groups, min_transfers.

3 · Stage one: a naive debt table and why it breaks

debts = defaultdict(float)                  # (debtor, creditor) -> amount

def add_expense(payer, amount, participants):
    share = amount / len(participants)
    for p in participants:
        if p != payer:
            debts[(p, payer)] += share

Add Anna's 10.00 dinner shared by three: each other person owes 3.333... as a float, and the "balances" no longer add to zero exactly. Now Anna realises the dinner was 12.00. To edit, you must subtract the old shares from exactly the right pairs, and if anything else has been netted against those pairs (Ben also paid for something Anna shared), you cannot tell what to subtract from where. There is no record of what changed or when. A bug that adds a share twice leaves the table permanently wrong with nothing to recompute it from. And the table answers "who owes whom pairwise", which is the most complicated question, not "what is each person's position", which is the useful one.

4 · Stage two: exact allocation

allocate(total, weights) computes each user's exact share as a Fraction (Python's exact rational numbers), takes the integer floor of each, and counts the leftover cents, which is always fewer than the number of users. It then sorts users by the size of their dropped fraction, largest first, ties by user id, and gives one extra cent to each of the first leftover users. The result sums exactly to the total and is deterministic. Examples, each a test: 1,000 cents equally among three gives 334, 333, 333. 1,000 cents with shares 2 to 1 gives 667 and 333 (exact values 666.67 and 333.33; the larger remainder wins). 10,001 cents at 33.33, 33.33 and 33.34 percent sums to exactly 10,001.

Who absorbs the leftover cent is a product decision worth asking about. Alternatives include the payer (who is owed anyway), the first participant alphabetically, or a rotating choice so no one is always charged the extra cent. What matters for correctness is that the rule exists, is deterministic, and always produces shares that sum to the total.

5 · Stage three: split strategies

Each split type is a small frozen dataclass with a shares(total) method. Equal(users) calls allocate with weight 1 each. Shares(weights) passes its weights straight through. Percent(percents) takes percentages as strings such as "33.34" and converts them to exact fractions, rejecting any set that does not sum to exactly 100; floats would make "sums to 100" an approximate check. Exact(amounts) takes cents per user and rejects amounts that do not sum to the total. They share one interface because the group only ever needs "turn this total into shares", and because each has an obvious second variant a product might add, such as "equal except the payer" or "by nights stayed".

6 · Stage four: the ledger, multi-payer expenses and balances

Group.add_expense(description, paid, split) takes a dict of payers, because real expenses often have several ("I put 400 on my card, you put 200 on yours"). The total is the sum of payments, the split strategy turns it into owed shares, and an Entry is appended. The entry's constructor checks that paid and owed sides balance, so an unbalanced entry can never enter the ledger. balances() walks every entry, adding paid amounts to each payer and subtracting owed amounts from each participant, and asserts the total is zero. The multi-payer test: a cabin of 600.00 paid 400.00 by Anna and 200.00 by Ben, split three ways at 200.00 each, leaves Anna owed 200.00, Ben even and Chloe owing 200.00.

Here is the drill's ledger in cents. Dinner: Anna paid 12,000; each of four owed 3,000. Taxis: Ben paid 4,500; Ben, Chloe and Dan owed 1,500 each. Tickets: Chloe paid 8,000; each of four owed 2,000. Anna: 12,000 − 3,000 − 2,000 = +7,000. Ben: 4,500 − 3,000 − 1,500 − 2,000 = −2,000. Chloe: 8,000 − 3,000 − 1,500 − 2,000 = +1,500. Dan: −3,000 − 1,500 − 2,000 = −6,500. Sum: zero.

7 · Stage five: edits, deletes and settlements

Deleting an expense appends a reversal: an entry whose paid side is the original's owed side and vice versa, so adding it cancels the original exactly. A second delete of the same expense is refused. Editing is a delete followed by a new expense. The ledger then reads expense, reversal, expense, which is precisely the story of what happened, and balances need no special handling. The test edits a 30.00 lunch to 36.00 and checks both the balances (+18.00 and −18.00) and that the history has three entries.

A settlement is a real payment: the payer's "paid" side and the payee's "owed" side, so the payer's balance rises and the payee's falls. Payments are exactly the operation users retry on a flaky connection, so settle takes an idempotency key: the first call appends an entry and remembers the key; later calls with the same key return that entry and append nothing. The test pays 18.00 twice with key "pay-77" and checks that the same entry comes back and both balances are zero.

8 · Stage six: cached balances that cannot drift

Summing every entry on every page view is fine for a weekend trip and wasteful for a shared house with years of groceries. The group therefore keeps a cache of balances updated in _append, the single method through which every entry enters the ledger. Because there is exactly one write path, the cache cannot miss an update. As a safety net, balances() recomputes from the ledger and asserts the cache matches; in production that comparison runs periodically and alerts. This is how to have a cached value without a second source of truth: the cache is updated in the same step as the fact, and the fact remains the authority.

9 · Stage seven: simplifying debts

Given net balances, the greedy algorithm puts creditors in one max-heap and debtors in another (Python's heapq is a min-heap, so amounts are negated). It repeatedly takes the largest creditor and the largest debtor, records a transfer of the smaller of their two amounts, and pushes back whoever still has a remainder. Each transfer zeroes at least one person, so for n people with non-zero balances it makes at most n − 1 transfers, and everyone ends at zero. On the drill's balances it produces Dan pays Anna 65.00, Ben pays Chloe 15.00, Ben pays Anna 5.00: three transfers instead of eight.

Greedy is not always optimal. The insight is that a set of people whose balances sum to zero can settle among themselves; a group of k people needs k − 1 transfers. So the minimum number of transfers is the number of non-zero people minus the largest number of disjoint zero-sum subgroups the balances can be divided into. With balances a −7, b +4, c +6, d +7, e −10, the subgroups {a, d} and {b, c, e} each sum to zero, so 5 − 2 = 3 transfers suffice (a pays d 7; e pays c 6 and b 4). Greedy instead starts with the largest pair, e pays d 7, which mixes the two subgroups, and needs 4. Finding the largest zero-sum partition is NP-hard in general (the partition problem reduces to it), but for small groups min_transfers solves it exactly by dynamic programming over all 2^n subsets: for each subset, the best count is the best over its subsets with one member removed, plus one if the subset itself sums to zero. In 300 random groups of 3 to 9 people, greedy needed more transfers than the optimum in 4.

10 · Testing: examples, invariants and properties

Three kinds of tests cover this design. Examples pin specific numbers a human checked by hand: 1,000 cents three ways is 334, 333, 333; the drill's balances are +7,000, −2,000, +1,500, −6,500; an edited lunch leaves ±1,800. Invariants are asserted inside the code on every call: every entry balances (in the constructor), balances sum to zero and the cache equals the recomputation (in balances()). Properties are checked over many random inputs: for 300 random groups with random expenses, greedy's transfers always bring everyone to zero, never exceed n − 1, and are compared against the exact optimum. Property tests are what find the rare inputs where a hand-picked example would never look, which is how this lesson's counterexample to greedy was found: a search over random balances turned up −7, +4, +6, +7, −10 in a fraction of a second. When an interviewer asks "how would you test this?", naming all three kinds, and the invariant that ties them together, is the complete answer.

11 · Talking through the design

A strong spoken summary links each choice to a requirement: "Money is integer cents so every split is exact; leftover cents follow the largest-remainder rule so shares always sum to the total. Expenses, reversals and settlements are immutable entries in one ledger, so history and undo are free and balances are derived; the cache is updated only on the single append path and checked against a recomputation. Split types are strategies because each has an obvious variant. Settlement takes an idempotency key because payments get retried. Simplification is greedy, at most n − 1 transfers, not always minimal because the exact problem is NP-hard; for small groups I can compute the exact minimum by subset dynamic programming. And whether to simplify at all is a product choice, because it creates payments between people who never shared a bill."

12 · The full solution

The file below contains exact allocation, the four split strategies, the ledger with reversals, idempotent settlements and a checked cache, greedy and exact simplification, and five tests, including the drill's numbers and a randomised comparison of greedy with the optimum. Python 3.12, standard library only.

"""Splitwise-style expense sharing: integer cents, four split strategies with exact rounding,
an append-only ledger with derived balances, idempotent settlements, edits as reversals,
and debt simplification (greedy vs exact minimum for small groups).
Python 3.12, standard library only. Run: python splitwise.py"""
from __future__ import annotations

import heapq
import itertools
import random
from collections import defaultdict
from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from fractions import Fraction
from typing import Protocol


# ------------------------------------------------------------------ exact allocation
def allocate(total: int, weights: dict[str, Fraction | int]) -> dict[str, int]:
    """Split `total` cents in proportion to weights, exactly (largest remainder method).
    Floors every share, then gives the leftover cents to the largest fractional parts,
    ties broken by user id so the result is deterministic."""
    if total < 0:
        raise ValueError("negative total")
    w = {u: Fraction(x) for u, x in weights.items()}
    if not w or any(x < 0 for x in w.values()) or sum(w.values()) == 0:
        raise ValueError("weights must be non-negative and not all zero")
    s = sum(w.values())
    exact = {u: total * x / s for u, x in w.items()}
    out = {u: int(v) for u, v in exact.items()}               # floor (all non-negative)
    left = total - sum(out.values())
    for u in sorted(w, key=lambda u: (-(exact[u] - out[u]), u))[:left]:
        out[u] += 1
    return out


class SplitStrategy(Protocol):
    def shares(self, total: int) -> dict[str, int]: ...


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Equal:
    users: tuple[str, ...]

    def shares(self, total: int) -> dict[str, int]:
        return allocate(total, {u: 1 for u in self.users})


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Exact:
    amounts: dict[str, int]

    def shares(self, total: int) -> dict[str, int]:
        if sum(self.amounts.values()) != total:
            raise ValueError(f"exact amounts sum to {sum(self.amounts.values())}, not {total}")
        return dict(self.amounts)


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Percent:
    percents: dict[str, str]                                  # strings like "33.34" -> exact

    def shares(self, total: int) -> dict[str, int]:
        p = {u: Fraction(x) for u, x in self.percents.items()}
        if sum(p.values()) != 100:
            raise ValueError("percentages must sum to exactly 100")
        return allocate(total, p)


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Shares:
    weights: dict[str, int]                                   # e.g. Anna 2 shares, Ben 1

    def shares(self, total: int) -> dict[str, int]:
        return allocate(total, self.weights)


# ------------------------------------------------------------------ ledger
@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Entry:
    """One immutable ledger entry: who put money in (paid) and whose share it was (owed)."""
    id: int
    kind: str                                                 # expense | reversal | settlement
    description: str
    paid: tuple[tuple[str, int], ...]
    owed: tuple[tuple[str, int], ...]
    reverses: int | None = None

    def __post_init__(self) -> None:
        if sum(a for _, a in self.paid) != sum(a for _, a in self.owed):
            raise ValueError("paid and owed must balance")


@dataclass
class Group:
    members: set[str]
    entries: list[Entry] = field(default_factory=list)
    _ids: itertools.count = field(default_factory=lambda: itertools.count(1))
    _settled_keys: dict[str, int] = field(default_factory=dict)
    _cache: dict[str, int] = field(default_factory=lambda: defaultdict(int))

    def _append(self, e: Entry) -> Entry:
        for u, _ in e.paid + e.owed:
            if u not in self.members:
                raise KeyError(f"{u} is not in the group")
        self.entries.append(e)
        for u, a in e.paid:
            self._cache[u] += a
        for u, a in e.owed:
            self._cache[u] -= a
        return e

    def add_expense(self, description: str, paid: dict[str, int], split: SplitStrategy) -> Entry:
        total = sum(paid.values())
        if total <= 0:
            raise ValueError("an expense must be positive")
        owed = split.shares(total)
        return self._append(Entry(next(self._ids), "expense", description,
                                  tuple(sorted(paid.items())), tuple(sorted(owed.items()))))

    def delete_expense(self, entry_id: int) -> Entry:
        """Never mutate history: append the mirror image of the entry."""
        orig = next(e for e in self.entries if e.id == entry_id and e.kind == "expense")
        if any(e.reverses == entry_id for e in self.entries):
            raise ValueError("already deleted")
        return self._append(Entry(next(self._ids), "reversal", f"undo: {orig.description}",
                                  orig.owed, orig.paid, reverses=entry_id))

    def edit_expense(self, entry_id: int, description: str, paid: dict[str, int],
                     split: SplitStrategy) -> Entry:
        self.delete_expense(entry_id)
        return self.add_expense(description, paid, split)

    def settle(self, payer: str, payee: str, cents: int, key: str) -> Entry:
        """Payer hands payee real money. Idempotent per client key."""
        if key in self._settled_keys:
            return next(e for e in self.entries if e.id == self._settled_keys[key])
        if cents <= 0 or payer == payee:
            raise ValueError("bad settlement")
        e = self._append(Entry(next(self._ids), "settlement", f"{payer} paid {payee}",
                               ((payer, cents),), ((payee, cents),)))
        self._settled_keys[key] = e.id
        return e

    def balances(self) -> dict[str, int]:
        """Derived from the whole ledger: + means is owed money, - means owes."""
        net = {u: 0 for u in self.members}
        for e in self.entries:
            for u, a in e.paid:
                net[u] += a
            for u, a in e.owed:
                net[u] -= a
        assert sum(net.values()) == 0, "balances must sum to zero"
        assert all(net[u] == self._cache[u] for u in net), "cache drifted from ledger"
        return net


# ------------------------------------------------------------------ simplification
def greedy_transfers(net: dict[str, int]) -> list[tuple[str, str, int]]:
    """Match the largest debtor with the largest creditor; at most n-1 transfers."""
    cred = [(-a, u) for u, a in net.items() if a > 0]
    debt = [(a, u) for u, a in net.items() if a < 0]
    heapq.heapify(cred), heapq.heapify(debt)
    out = []
    while cred and debt:
        c_amt, c = heapq.heappop(cred)
        d_amt, d = heapq.heappop(debt)
        x = min(-c_amt, -d_amt)
        out.append((d, c, x))
        if -c_amt > x:
            heapq.heappush(cred, (c_amt + x, c))
        if -d_amt > x:
            heapq.heappush(debt, (d_amt + x, d))
    return out


def min_transfers(net: dict[str, int]) -> int:
    """Exact minimum for small groups: n - (max number of disjoint zero-sum subgroups).
    Each zero-sum subgroup of size k settles in k-1 transfers. O(2^n * n)."""
    vals = [a for a in net.values() if a != 0]
    n = len(vals)
    if n > 18:
        raise ValueError("exact search is exponential; use greedy")
    total = [0] * (1 << n)
    best = [0] * (1 << n)
    for m in range(1, 1 << n):
        low = (m & -m).bit_length() - 1
        total[m] = total[m & (m - 1)] + vals[low]
        best[m] = max(best[m & ~(1 << i)] for i in range(n) if m >> i & 1)
        if total[m] == 0:
            best[m] += 1
    return n - best[(1 << n) - 1]


def apply(net: dict[str, int], transfers: list[tuple[str, str, int]]) -> dict[str, int]:
    out = dict(net)
    for d, c, x in transfers:
        out[d] += x
        out[c] -= x
    return out


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- tests
def test_rounding_is_exact() -> None:
    assert Equal(("a", "b", "c")).shares(1000) == {"a": 334, "b": 333, "c": 333}
    assert sum(Percent({"a": "33.33", "b": "33.33", "c": "33.34"}).shares(10_001).values()) == 10_001
    assert Shares({"a": 2, "b": 1}).shares(1000) == {"a": 667, "b": 333}
    try:
        Exact({"a": 500, "b": 400}).shares(1000)
    except ValueError:
        pass
    else:
        raise AssertionError("exact split that does not sum was accepted")


def test_drill_balances_and_simplification() -> None:
    g = Group({"Anna", "Ben", "Chloe", "Dan"})
    everyone = ("Anna", "Ben", "Chloe", "Dan")
    g.add_expense("dinner", {"Anna": 12_000}, Equal(everyone))
    g.add_expense("taxis", {"Ben": 4_500}, Equal(("Ben", "Chloe", "Dan")))
    g.add_expense("tickets", {"Chloe": 8_000}, Equal(everyone))
    net = g.balances()
    assert net == {"Anna": 7_000, "Ben": -2_000, "Chloe": 1_500, "Dan": -6_500}
    t = greedy_transfers(net)
    assert t == [("Dan", "Anna", 6_500), ("Ben", "Chloe", 1_500), ("Ben", "Anna", 500)]
    assert all(v == 0 for v in apply(net, t).values()) and min_transfers(net) == 3


def test_delete_edit_and_idempotent_settle() -> None:
    g = Group({"a", "b"})
    e = g.add_expense("lunch", {"a": 3_000}, Equal(("a", "b")))
    g.edit_expense(e.id, "lunch (fixed)", {"a": 3_600}, Equal(("a", "b")))
    assert g.balances() == {"a": 1_800, "b": -1_800}
    assert [x.kind for x in g.entries] == ["expense", "reversal", "expense"]   # history kept
    s1 = g.settle("b", "a", 1_800, key="pay-77")
    s2 = g.settle("b", "a", 1_800, key="pay-77")                                # retried tap
    assert s1 is s2 and g.balances() == {"a": 0, "b": 0}


def test_multi_payer_expense() -> None:
    g = Group({"a", "b", "c"})
    g.add_expense("cabin", {"a": 40_000, "b": 20_000}, Shares({"a": 1, "b": 1, "c": 1}))
    assert g.balances() == {"a": 20_000, "b": 0, "c": -20_000}


def test_greedy_vs_exact_on_random_groups() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(3)
    worse = 0
    for _ in range(300):
        users = [f"u{i}" for i in range(rng.randint(3, 9))]
        g = Group(set(users))
        for _ in range(rng.randint(1, 12)):
            payer = rng.choice(users)
            part = tuple(rng.sample(users, rng.randint(2, len(users))))
            g.add_expense("x", {payer: rng.choice([1_000, 1_500, 2_000, 3_000, 4_500])}, Equal(part))
        net = g.balances()
        t = greedy_transfers(net)
        assert all(v == 0 for v in apply(net, t).values())
        nonzero = sum(1 for v in net.values() if v)
        assert len(t) <= max(0, nonzero - 1)
        worse += len(t) > min_transfers(net)
    print(f"    greedy used more transfers than the optimum in {worse}/300 random groups")
    net = {"a": -700, "b": 400, "c": 600, "d": 700, "e": -1_000}  # hidden groups {a,d}, {b,c,e}
    assert min_transfers(net) == 3 and len(greedy_transfers(net)) == 4


if __name__ == "__main__":
    for t in (test_rounding_is_exact, test_drill_balances_and_simplification,
              test_delete_edit_and_idempotent_settle, test_multi_payer_expense,
              test_greedy_vs_exact_on_random_groups):
        t()
        print("ok ", t.__name__)

13 · Edge cases

A payer who is not a participant (paying for others): handled naturally, since paid and owed are separate maps. A participant who is not a group member: rejected; extend to guests by adding them to the group. Zero or negative totals: rejected; refunds are reversals or negative-direction expenses with an explicit kind. Percentages like 33.333: exact fractions handle them, but they must sum to exactly 100. Leftover cents: always assigned deterministically, so recomputing gives identical balances. Removing a member with a non-zero balance: refuse until they settle, or the group's balances would no longer sum to zero. Deleting a settlement: allowed as a reversal, with an audit trail, because people mis-record payments.

14 · Extensions the interviewer will ask for

Multiple currencies: keep each entry in its currency, compute balances per currency, convert only at settlement with the rate recorded on the entry. Recurring expenses (rent every month): a schedule that appends an expense each period. Concurrent edits: give each expense a version and accept an edit only against the current version. Notifications: an observer on the append path. Simplify across groups: the same algorithm on a person's balances with everyone, which Splitwise also offers, with stronger surprise effects. Persistence: entries and entry lines in a database, a balances table updated in the same transaction as each insert.

15 · Complexity

Adding an expense is O(p) for p participants (allocation sorts them: O(p log p)). Deriving balances is O(L) over all ledger lines; the cache makes reading O(1) per user. Greedy simplification is O(n log n) for n people with non-zero balances. Exact minimisation is O(2^n · n), fine for n up to about 15 to 18 and hopeless beyond, which is the practical meaning of NP-hard here. Memory is O(L) for the ledger and O(n) for the cache; the subset table for exact search is O(2^n) integers, about 2 MB at n = 18, which is another reason to cap it.

Why the exact minimum is hard

Each transfer can zero at most one person's balance, except the last one in a subgroup, which zeroes two. So a subgroup of k people with zero total needs exactly k − 1 transfers, and splitting the whole group into as many independent zero-sum subgroups as possible minimises the total. Deciding whether even two disjoint zero-sum subgroups exist is at least as hard as the partition problem (can a set of numbers be split into two halves of equal sum?), a classic NP-complete problem: given numbers to partition, create creditors with those amounts and two debtors each owing half the total. So no algorithm is known that finds the minimum quickly for every input. In practice groups are small enough that exact search over subsets is feasible, and greedy, which is O(n log n) and needs at most n − 1 transfers, is within one or two transfers of optimal almost always; in the random test it was optimal in 296 of 300 groups.

The graph view

Debts form a directed graph: people are nodes and "A owes B x" is an edge with weight x. Paying back each expense separately keeps every edge; computing net balances collapses the graph to one number per node, the difference between its incoming and outgoing weights. Any set of transfers that gives every node the same net balance is a valid settlement, and simplification is choosing the smallest such edge set. Seen this way, cycles are pure waste: if A owes B 10, B owes C 10 and C owes A 10, all three edges cancel and no money needs to move. Greedy removes cycles automatically, because it never looks at the original edges at all, only at the net balances. The graph view also explains Splitwise's second rule: restricting transfers to edges that already exist turns simplification into finding a minimal flow on a fixed graph, which can need more transfers than the unrestricted problem.

Concurrency in the ledger

An append-only ledger is friendly to concurrency because appends do not conflict with each other: two members adding expenses at the same moment simply produce two entries, in either order, and the balances are the same either way, since addition is commutative. Conflicts arise only on operations that depend on current state: deleting or editing an expense (two people undoing the same expense) and idempotent settlement (two retries racing). Both are handled by uniqueness at the storage layer: a unique constraint on "reverses" so a second reversal of the same entry fails, and a unique idempotency key on settlements. The balance cache must be updated in the same transaction as the append, or a crash between them leaves it stale until reconciliation.

Other things to minimise

"Fewest transfers" is not the only reasonable goal. Users may prefer that nobody pays someone they do not know, which is Splitwise's second rule; that constrains transfers to pairs who shared an expense and turns the problem into one on a graph of relationships. Others prefer minimising the largest single payment, or the total money moved (which is fixed: it always equals the sum of positive balances, whatever the transfers). Clarify the objective before optimising, and notice that the cheapest objective to satisfy, total money moved, is constant, which is why counting transfers became the default.

Event sourcing in miniature

The ledger is an example of event sourcing: the state of the system (balances) is defined as the result of applying a sequence of immutable events (entries) in order. The benefits are the ones this lesson relies on: a complete audit trail, the ability to rebuild state from scratch, and corrections as new events. The costs are real too: reading state requires replaying or maintaining projections (here, the balance cache); schema changes to events must handle old events forever; and deleting personal data, which privacy laws can require, conflicts with "never change history", usually handled by storing personal details outside the ledger and referencing them by id.

Rounding is a product rule, not a maths fact

The largest remainder method is one defensible rule among several. Banker's rounding (round half to even, Python's default for round and the default mode of Decimal) reduces systematic bias in large sums. Assigning the extra cent to the payer means the person already owed money absorbs the difference. Rotating the extra cent across participants over time is fairest for groups that split many small bills. The engineering requirement is narrower than the product choice: shares must sum exactly to the total, the rule must be deterministic so recomputing the ledger gives identical results, and the rule must be documented so a user asking "why do I owe one cent more?" gets an answer.

Idempotency keys, precisely

An idempotency key works only if the client generates it once per user intention (one tap of "record payment") and reuses it on every retry of that intention, and if the server stores the key atomically with the effect. If the server appends the entry and then crashes before storing the key, a retry appends a second entry. In a database the key is a unique column on the settlement row itself, inserted in the same transaction, so "entry exists" and "key recorded" can never disagree. Keys should also expire eventually, and a reuse of a key with different parameters (a different amount) should be rejected as an error rather than silently returning the old result.

How expense-sharing systems fail

The classic failures are about trust. A balance that changes without explanation makes users suspect the app; a missing cent makes them suspect it more. Both come from mutable state and floating-point money, and the ledger design prevents both. A subtler failure is a cache that drifts from the ledger after a bug in an update path; the defence is a single write path and periodic reconciliation, with an alert on any difference. Another is double recording of payments from retries, which idempotency keys prevent, and duplicate expenses entered by two group members for the same bill, which no algorithm prevents but a "possible duplicate" warning (same amount, same day, same payer) catches.

Simplification produces its own support tickets. Users see a balance with someone they never shared a bill with and assume an error. Splitwise's post on the feature warned that simplifying across friends changes balances that users cannot see, and that friends who had never used it might be confused. Clear explanations ("Ben's 15.00 to you is part of settling the whole group") and making the feature opt-in per group are as important as the algorithm.

Privacy and deletion

An append-only ledger meets a hard requirement in many jurisdictions: users can ask for their personal data to be deleted. The usual answer is to keep personal details (names, emails, notes typed into descriptions) outside the ledger, referenced by an opaque user id, and to delete or anonymise those details on request while leaving the amounts in place, so the other members' balances still add up. Descriptions are the tricky part, because people type names into them; treat free-text fields as personal data from the start.

What to measure

Reconciliation differences between cached and recomputed balances (should be zero), entries rejected for imbalance (should be zero in production; non-zero means a client bug), duplicate settlement attempts caught by idempotency keys (a measure of network flakiness), the share of groups using simplification, and the number of support contacts about balances, which is the real quality metric for this product.

Operations

Because balances are derivable, a bug in the balance code can be fixed and every balance recomputed from the ledger, a property worth protecting. Schema changes to entries must keep old entries readable. Exports (a CSV of the group's history) come straight from the ledger. And because the ledger is append-only, it compresses and archives well, with old groups' entries moved to cheaper storage while their final balances stay cached.

The real case: Splitwise's simplified debts

Splitwise announced simplified debts in a 2012 blog post that is unusually candid about the design. Within a group, simplification means that if Anna owes Bob and Bob owes Cathy, Anna can pay Cathy directly: fewer payments, less waiting. The post also introduced simplifying a person's debts across all their friends at once, and warned that in order to reduce payments as much as possible it changes not only your balances but your friends' balances, including ones you cannot see, so friends who never used the feature may be confused. To keep simplification from making things worse, the post lists three rules it obeys: everyone owes the same net amount at the end; no one owes a person they did not owe before; and no one owes more money in total than before. In the comments, Splitwise's CTO clarified that simplification within groups follows the first and third rules but not necessarily the second: if you owe Betty 20 and Betty owes Carlos 20, the group simplification tells you to pay Carlos directly. Those three rules are a precise statement of the product constraints around the algorithm in this lesson: the first is the zero-sum invariant (net balances never change), the third bounds what greedy may do, and the second is the social constraint that turns a pure minimisation into a graph problem, and that the company chose to relax for groups.

Goal: run the solution, then break, measure and extend it. Python 3.12, standard library only.

Then try integer division without distributing the remainder: shares sum to 999 of 1,000 and the Entry constructor rejects the expense because paid and owed no longer balance.
  3. Break it again. Make settle ignore the key, retry a payment, and see the payee's balance go past zero.

Four friends. Anna paid 120.00 for dinner split equally among all four. Ben paid 45.00 for taxis split equally among Ben, Chloe and Dan. Chloe paid 80.00 for tickets split equally among all four. Dan has paid nothing.

(a) Compute each person's net balance. (b) How many payments does paying back each expense separately need, and how many does greedy simplification need? (c) Show the greedy result. (d) Is greedy always this good?

Check the invariant: 7,000 − 2,000 + 1,500 − 6,500 = 0. Anna is owed 70.00, Chloe 15.00; Ben owes 20.00, Dan 65.00.

(b) Paying back each expense separately: three people pay Anna for dinner, two pay Ben for taxis, three pay Chloe for tickets: 8 payments. Greedy needs at most n − 1 = 3, and achieves it.

(c) Greedy. Largest debtor Dan (−6,500) with largest creditor Anna (+7,000): Dan pays Anna 65.00; Anna is left owed 5.00. Now the largest creditor is Chloe (+1,500) and the only debtor Ben (−2,000): Ben pays Chloe 15.00; Ben still owes 5.00. Finally Ben pays Anna 5.00. Three payments, everyone at zero. Note that Ben paying Chloe is exactly the kind of transfer simplification creates: it settles the group even though the payment does not correspond to any single expense between them.

(d) Not always. Greedy is always within n − 1, but the true minimum is the number of non-zero people minus the most disjoint zero-sum subgroups. With balances −7, +4, +6, +7, −10, the subgroups {−7, +7} and {+4, +6, −10} settle in 1 + 2 = 3 payments; greedy first matches −10 with +7 and needs 4. Finding the best grouping is NP-hard in general, so the honest engineering answer is: ship greedy, use exact search for small groups if the extra payment matters, and say which you did.
