Suppose you are building a support assistant. It works beautifully in testing, then three complaints arrive in one week. A user asks how many r's are in "strawberry" and the model says two. An accountant pastes a column of invoice totals and the model adds them up wrong by a few hundred. And your finance team notices that customers writing in Hindi cost about three times as much per conversation as customers writing in English, and that their long conversations start "forgetting" the beginning much sooner.

These look like three unrelated weaknesses: bad counting, bad maths, an unfair pricing quirk. They have one root cause, and it sits in front of the model rather than inside it. A neural network cannot read text. It works on numbers, so before your user's message reaches the model, a component called the tokeniser chops the text into pieces called tokens and replaces each piece with an integer. The model never sees the letters of "strawberry"; it sees one number that stands for the whole word. It never sees "4,817" as four digits; it might see "4", ",", "817" or something else, depending on rules learned from frequency counts. And the rules were learned mostly from English, so Hindi text is chopped into many more, smaller pieces, and every piece costs money and context space.

The tokeniser is decided once, before the model is trained, and then it is frozen into the model forever: the model's first and last layers are indexed by token number. So every engineer who builds on language models inherits its choices, and the ones who understand it can predict costs, explain strange failures, avoid security holes and choose models sensibly.

This lesson starts from why text must become numbers at all, compares words, characters and subwords, then works through byte-pair encoding (the algorithm behind GPT, Llama and most modern models) merge by merge on a tiny corpus. From there it covers bytes and pre-tokenisation, how encoding and decoding work, vocabulary size and its cost in parameters, special tokens and chat templates, and finally each of the classic failures: spelling, arithmetic, multilingual cost and the strange "glitch tokens". The lab trains a real byte-level tokeniser on your own machine.

Models read integers, not text

A language model is a function that takes a sequence of symbols and outputs, for every symbol it knows, the probability that it comes next. Those symbols have to come from a fixed, finite list, because the model's last layer has one output per possible symbol, and its first layer has one learned vector per possible symbol. That fixed list is the vocabulary, each entry is a token, and each token's position in the list is its token ID. The tokeniser is the program that converts text into token IDs (encoding) and IDs back into text (decoding).

Inside the model, the first operation is a table lookup. The embedding table is a matrix with one row per vocabulary entry; token ID 41236 becomes row 41236, a vector of a few hundred or a few thousand numbers. Everything after that is arithmetic on vectors. At the other end, the model produces one score per vocabulary entry, a softmax turns the scores into probabilities, and the chosen ID is decoded back to text. So the vocabulary defines both what the model can read and what it can say.

An analogy: a phrasebook

Think of a traveller with a phrasebook. The most common phrases ("good morning", "how much?") have their own entries, so they are quick to look up. A rare word is not in the book, but every letter of the alphabet is, so the traveller can always spell it out, slowly. Subword tokenisation is exactly this: frequent words get a single entry, rare words are assembled from smaller entries, and the bottom layer (single bytes) guarantees nothing is ever impossible to write. The analogy breaks in one important way. A traveller can still see the letters inside a phrase. The model cannot: once "strawberry" is one entry, its letters are gone, and anything the model knows about the spelling it had to learn from elsewhere.

Why not words, why not characters?

A word-level vocabulary would need an entry for every word form in every language, including names, typos, URLs and code identifiers; that is millions of entries, and anything not in the list is an out-of-vocabulary word, which the model cannot read at all. A character-level vocabulary is tiny and never fails, but English text becomes about four times as many symbols as with subwords, and a model's cost grows with sequence length: attention compares every position with every other, and generation produces one symbol per step. Subword vocabularies are the compromise: tens of thousands to a couple of hundred thousand entries, where common words are a single token and everything else is spelled from pieces.

Some numbers to anchor on

GPT-2, the 2019 model from OpenAI whose tokeniser design most later models copied (GPT stands for generative pre-trained transformer), has a vocabulary of 50,257 tokens. The tokeniser used by GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, called cl100k_base, has about 100,000; the one used by GPT-4o, o200k_base, about 200,000; Meta's Llama 2 used 32,000 and Llama 3 about 128,000. With any of the modern ones, English prose comes out at roughly four characters, or three quarters of a word, per token, so a 500-word page is about 650-700 tokens and a 128,000-token context window holds a few hundred pages. Prices on LLM (large language model) APIs, context-window limits and generation speeds are all quoted in tokens, which is why engineers end up counting them constantly.

A reader who stops here has the correct picture: text becomes a short sequence of integers through rules learned from frequency, and the model's view of the world is limited to those integers. The rest of the lesson shows how those rules are learned and what follows from them.

1. What a tokeniser must guarantee

Before looking at any algorithm, it helps to list what we need. First, coverage: every possible input string must be encodable, including emoji, Chinese, code, typos and binary-looking junk, because a production model will receive all of them. Second, lossless round trip: decoding the IDs must give back exactly the original text, every space and newline included, because the model's output is decoded the same way and a code assistant that drops indentation is broken. Third, compression: the sequence should be short, because every token costs compute in every layer, attention cost grows with sequence length, the context window is measured in tokens, and generating text takes one model step per token. Fourth, consistency: the same string should tokenise the same way in the same context, so the model can learn stable meanings for its tokens.

Word vocabularies fail coverage. Character vocabularies satisfy coverage and round trip but fail compression. Subword vocabularies learned from data satisfy all four reasonably well, and byte-pair encoding is the most widely used way to learn one.

2. Byte-pair encoding, the idea

Byte-pair encoding (BPE) started life in 1994 as a data compression trick: find the most common pair of adjacent bytes in a file, replace it with a new unused byte, repeat. Sennrich, Haddow and Birch adapted it in 2015 for machine translation: instead of compressing a file, they used the merge procedure to build a vocabulary. The training algorithm is short enough to state in four lines:

  1. Split every word in the training corpus into its basic symbols (characters, or bytes) and count how often each word occurs.
  2. Count every pair of adjacent symbols across the corpus, weighting each by the frequency of the word it appears in.
  3. Take the most frequent pair, say (e, s), create a new symbol "es", replace every adjacent e, s in the corpus by it, and append the rule "e + s → es" to an ordered list of merges.
  4. Repeat from step 2 until you have done the chosen number of merges. The vocabulary is the base symbols plus one new symbol per merge.

The vocabulary size is therefore a dial you set in advance: GPT-2 used 256 byte symbols plus 50,000 merges plus one special end-of-text token, giving 50,257 entries. The output of training is two things: the vocabulary (a table from token to ID) and the ordered merge list. The merge list is what you apply later to tokenise new text.

3. Three merges by hand

Here is the exact toy example from Sennrich et al.'s paper. The corpus has four distinct words with their counts: "low" 5 times, "lower" 2 times, "newest" 6 times and "widest" 3 times. Each word is split into characters, with a special end-of-word marker </w> appended so that the tokeniser can tell "est" at the end of a word from "est" in the middle of one.

l o w </w>            × 5
l o w e r </w>        × 2
n e w e s t </w>      × 6
w i d e s t </w>      × 3

Merge 1. Count adjacent pairs, weighted by word count. The pair (l, o) occurs in "low" (5) and "lower" (2): 7. (o, w): also 7. (w, e) occurs in "lower" (2) and "newest" (6): 8. (n, e) 6, (e, w) 6. (e, s) occurs in "newest" (6) and "widest" (3): 9. (s, t): also 9. (t, </w>): 9. (w, </w>) 5, (e, r) 2, (r, </w>) 2, (w, i) 3, (i, d) 3, (d, e) 3. Three pairs tie at 9. The reference implementation takes the first one it encountered, (e, s). We merge it everywhere:

n e w es t </w>  × 6        w i d es t </w>  × 3     (rule 1: e + s → es)

Merge 2. Recount. (e, s) no longer exists; in its place (es, t) appears in "newest" and "widest": 6 + 3 = 9, the top count. Merge it: "newest" is now n e w est </w> and "widest" is w i d est </w> (rule 2: es + t → est).

Merge 3. Recount: (est, </w>) is 9, still the top. Merge it: n e w est</w>, w i d est</w> (rule 3: est + </w> → est</w>). The tokeniser has discovered the English superlative suffix "-est" purely from counting, with no linguistic knowledge.

Continuing, merges 4 and 5 are (l, o) and (lo, w), each with count 7, producing "low", and merge 6 is (n, e). Now encode a word that never appeared in the corpus, "lowest": start from l o w e s t </w>, apply rule 1 (e s → es), rule 2 (es t → est), rule 3 (est </w> → est</w>), rule 4 (l o → lo), rule 5 (lo w → low). Result: low + est</w>, two meaningful pieces. This is the essential property: an unseen word is decomposed into pieces the model has seen many times, so the model can still reason about it.

4. Starting from bytes

Sennrich's version starts from characters. That raises a problem at scale: Unicode defines well over a hundred thousand characters, so a base alphabet of "every character" is already enormous, and any character not seen in training is still unknown. The GPT-2 paper (Radford et al., 2019) points this out and switches to byte-level BPE. Text is first encoded as UTF-8, the standard variable-length encoding in which English letters take one byte, most accented Latin, Greek, Cyrillic, Arabic and Hebrew letters two, Devanagari, Chinese and Japanese three, and emoji four. The base alphabet is then the 256 possible byte values. Every possible string is a sequence of bytes, so every string can be encoded, and there is no out-of-vocabulary case at all.

The price is that a character the merges never learned is spelled as its raw bytes, so a single Chinese character may become two or three tokens, each a fragment of a character that means nothing on its own. A related approach, used by SentencePiece-based tokenisers such as Llama 1 and 2, keeps characters as the base alphabet but adds byte fallback: if a character is not in the vocabulary, it is encoded as its UTF-8 bytes rather than as an unknown token. The effect is the same guarantee of coverage.

5. Pre-tokenisation: where merges are not allowed

If BPE runs directly on a raw byte stream, its greed causes trouble. GPT-2's authors observed that it produced many versions of common words, such as "dog.", "dog!" and "dog?", each wasting a vocabulary slot, and it would happily merge across word boundaries. Their fix is pre-tokenisation: before BPE, a regular expression splits the text into chunks, and merges are only allowed inside a chunk. GPT-2's pattern, roughly, makes a chunk out of each of: a common English contraction suffix ('s, 't, 're, 've, 'm, 'll, 'd); an optional space followed by a run of letters; an optional space followed by a run of digits; an optional space followed by a run of other symbols; and runs of whitespace.

Two consequences are visible in every modern tokeniser. First, the space before a word is part of the word's chunk, so " strawberry" (with a leading space) and "strawberry" (at the start of a line, or after a quote) are different strings and usually different tokens: in cl100k, " strawberry" is the single token 73700, while "strawberry" is three tokens, "str", "aw", "berry". Second, letters, digits and punctuation never merge with each other, which keeps tokens interpretable. Later tokenisers changed the regex to handle digits differently: OpenAI's cl100k pattern matches numbers in runs of at most three digits, so a long number is always cut left to right into groups of three. Llama 1 and 2 went further and split every number into individual digits.

6. Encoding and decoding

To encode new text, the tokeniser pre-tokenises it into chunks, splits each chunk into bytes, and then repeatedly looks at all adjacent pairs in the chunk, finds the one whose merge rule was learned earliest, and applies it. It stops when no adjacent pair has a rule. The remaining symbols are the chunk's tokens, and their IDs are read from the vocabulary table. Applying merges in learned order, rather than greedily matching the longest vocabulary entry, is what makes encoding reproduce the segmentation seen during training. A decode is trivial: look up each ID's bytes, concatenate them, and decode the result as UTF-8. One subtlety matters for streaming chat interfaces: because a token can end in the middle of a multi-byte character, a client printing tokens one by one must buffer bytes until they form a complete character, or users see replacement-character junk.

Real numbers make the trade-offs concrete. In the lab you will train a byte-level BPE on about 12,700 characters of English documentation. With zero merges it produces one token per byte (12,719 tokens). With 50 merges it produces 8,797, with 200 merges 6,293, and with 400 merges 4,970: 2.56 bytes per token. The first merges learned are runs of spaces, "in", " a", " t", "he", which are exactly the most frequent patterns of English and code. Production tokenisers trained on trillions of bytes with 100K or more entries reach about four characters per token on English: Meta reports that Llama 3's 128K tokeniser encodes English at 3.94 characters per token, against 3.17 for Llama 2's 32K one.

7. Vocabulary size and what it costs

The vocabulary size, V, appears in two weight matrices. The input embedding has V rows of width d_model (the model's hidden width). The output layer, often called the unembedding or LM head, maps the final hidden vector to V scores, so it also has V × d_model weights. Some models share (tie) the two matrices; many large ones do not. Worked out: GPT-2 small has V = 50,257 and d_model = 768, so its embedding has 50,257 × 768 = 38,597,376 parameters, about 31% of its 124 million total. Llama 3 8B has V = 128,256 (128,000 regular tokens plus 256 reserved special tokens) and d_model = 4,096, so each matrix is 128,256 × 4,096 = 525,336,576 parameters, about 1.05 billion for the untied pair, roughly 13% of the model.

Compute grows too: the output layer does a V × d_model matrix-vector product for every token and the softmax runs over V entries, so at generation time the LM head is one of the larger single matrix multiplications in the model. Against this, a larger vocabulary makes sequences shorter, and every other layer's cost is proportional to sequence length. Moving from 32K to 128K cut Llama's English token count by about 20% (3.94 versus 3.17 characters per token), and much more for code and the languages the extra 28K tokens targeted. There is a quality side as well: entries that are rare in the training data get few gradient updates and poor embeddings, so blindly increasing V creates many weakly trained tokens.

8. Special tokens and chat templates

Besides tokens learned by merging, every vocabulary reserves a few special tokens: control signals that ordinary text should never produce. GPT-2 has exactly one, <|endoftext|>, which separates documents in training and tells the model to stop. Chat models add role markers. A chat request is a list of messages (system, user, assistant), but the model only reads a flat token sequence, so the chat template serialises the messages with special tokens marking where each turn starts and ends. The model was fine-tuned on exactly that serialisation, so at inference the template must match byte for byte: a missing end-of-turn token can make the model carry on and write the user's next message itself.

Special tokens are also a security boundary. If a user's message contains the literal text <|im_end|> and the tokeniser turns it into the real control token, the user has just closed their own turn and can forge a system message. For this reason tiktoken's encode raises an error by default when text contains a special-token string, and well-built servers construct templates from token IDs rather than by gluing strings together. (Prompt injection more broadly, where attackers use ordinary words rather than control tokens, is the subject of lm18.)

9. The failures that come from the vocabulary

Spelling and letter counting. The model receives " strawberry" as one ID. Its letters are not in the input. If the model knows that the word contains three r's, it learned it from training text where the word was spelled out or discussed, and for most words that information is weak. Asking it to write the word with separators first ("s-t-r-a-w-b-e-r-r-y") produces one token per letter, which it can then count. The same mechanism explains why models struggle to reverse a string, rhyme reliably or follow "use exactly 50 letters".

Arithmetic. Numbers tokenise inconsistently when merges decide. In GPT-2's tokeniser, " 1234" is " 12" + "34" and "12345" is "123" + "45": the same digit ends up in tokens of different lengths at different positions, so the model cannot learn one consistent column-by-column procedure. cl100k's three-digit chunks make the grouping regular, and Llama 1 and 2's single digits make it trivial, which helps; even so, exact arithmetic is better done by a calculator or code tool.

Multilingual cost. Merges are learned from a corpus, and a corpus dominated by English produces merges for English. With cl100k, "Hello world" is two tokens, while "नमस्ते दुनिया" (the same greeting in Hindi, 37 bytes of UTF-8) is 13 tokens; o200k, trained with more multilingual data, needs 5. Every extra token costs money, fills the context window and adds a decode step.

Glitch tokens. If the tokeniser is trained on one corpus and the model on another, some vocabulary entries may almost never appear in the model's training data. Their embedding rows stay near their random initialisation, so when one appears in a prompt the model behaves erratically: evasive answers, hallucinated words or refusals. The famous example is " SolidGoldMagikarp", a single token in GPT-2's vocabulary (ID 43453), learned because the name appeared often in the tokeniser's training data.

10. Other subword algorithms

BPE is not the only way to learn a subword vocabulary. WordPiece, used by BERT (Google's 2018 encoder model), follows the same bottom-up merging but picks the pair whose merge most increases the likelihood of the training data under a simple model, which in practice means preferring pairs whose parts rarely occur apart, rather than simply the most frequent pair. The Unigram tokeniser (Kudo, 2018) goes the other way: start from a large set of candidate pieces, fit a model where each piece has a probability and a word's segmentation is a sequence of independent pieces, then repeatedly prune the pieces whose removal hurts the corpus likelihood least. At encoding time it picks the most probable segmentation, and it can also sample alternative segmentations as a training-time regulariser. SentencePiece is a library rather than an algorithm: it implements both BPE and Unigram, treats the input as a raw stream with spaces represented by a visible marker (▁), and so needs no language-specific pre-tokeniser, which matters for languages such as Japanese and Thai that do not separate words with spaces.

Why merges are applied in learned order

A tempting shortcut is to encode by greedily matching the longest vocabulary entry at each position. It produces different segmentations from the ones seen in training. Take a vocabulary that learned "e s", "es t", "l o", "lo w" and also "w e": greedy longest-match from the left on "lowest" might produce "low" and then "est", or might grab "we" if "owe" style entries exist, depending on the table. BPE encoding instead replays the training process, always applying the merge with the lowest rank among the pairs present, so the segmentation of any word is the same one the model saw during training. Implementations make this fast with a priority queue of adjacent pairs keyed by merge rank, and cache the result per pre-tokenised chunk, because natural text repeats chunks constantly.

The regex is part of the model

Pre-tokenisation rules are easy to overlook because they are a single line of code, but they fix what tokens can exist. GPT-2's pattern, applied without case-insensitivity, treated "'S" differently from "'s"; it attached a single leading space to a word but left runs of spaces as separate whitespace chunks, which made Python indentation expensive (four spaces of indentation become separate whitespace tokens). cl100k's pattern changed three things visible in practice: case-insensitive contractions, digit runs capped at three, and whitespace handling that lets runs of spaces merge into single tokens, which roughly halved the token count of indented code. When comparing tokenisers for a code assistant, these regex details matter as much as vocabulary size.

Fertility, compression and fairness

Fertility is the average number of tokens per word; the cross-language version is the tokenisation premium, the ratio of token counts for the same content in two languages, measured on a parallel corpus such as FLORES-200, which has the same sentences translated into some two hundred languages. Premiums matter three ways: users pay per token, the effective context window shrinks by the premium, and generation latency grows by it. There is a quality effect as well: a language split into byte fragments forces the model to spend layers reassembling characters before it can work with meaning, and has fewer training examples per token. A sound multilingual tokeniser is trained on a corpus deliberately rebalanced towards under-represented languages, which is what Llama 3 did by adding 28K non-English tokens.

Glitch tokens, in mechanism

An embedding row moves away from its random initialisation only when its token appears in a training batch, because only then does the loss have a gradient with respect to that row. With weight decay (an optimiser setting that shrinks every weight a little at each step), rows that never appear shrink towards zero. Land and Bartolo use this to find under-trained tokens without prompting: they compare each output-layer row with the rows of tokens known to be unused (for example reserved placeholder tokens), flag tokens whose vectors look like those, and then confirm by asking the model to repeat the token. Their results show such tokens across many open model families. The defence is procedural: train the tokeniser on a sample of the same data mix as pretraining, deduplicate boilerplate before training the tokeniser (one repeated username can earn a token), and scan for under-trained tokens before release.

Tokenisation at prompt boundaries and token healing

Because the leading space belongs to the next word, prompts that end in a space put the model in an odd position. In training, " world" was a single token; a prompt "Hello " followed by a request to continue forces the model to generate "world" without its usual leading space, a token sequence it rarely saw. Outputs get subtly worse. The same happens when a prompt ends half-way through a word or a URL. Token healing, implemented in some inference libraries, backs the prompt up by one token and constrains the first generated token to start with the removed text, so the model can choose the natural tokenisation. The practical rule is simpler: do not end prompts with trailing whitespace, and keep few-shot examples' whitespace consistent.

Changing or extending a tokeniser

The embedding table and output layer are indexed by token ID, so a tokeniser cannot be replaced without retraining those layers, and changing it substantially means retraining the model. Extension is possible: learn new merges on the target language, append the new tokens at the end of the vocabulary so existing IDs keep their meaning, add rows to both matrices, initialise each new row sensibly (a common choice is the average of the embeddings of the old tokens that the new token replaces), and continue pretraining on the new language so the rows become useful. Vocabulary sizes are usually padded to a multiple of 64 or 128 so the matrix shapes stay efficient on GPU tensor cores (gpu4 explains why aligned shapes matter), which is one reason vocabularies often contain unused reserved tokens.

Beyond tokenisers

Byte-level models that drop the learned vocabulary and read raw bytes avoid every failure in this lesson, but they make sequences about four times longer, multiplying the cost of every layer. Research models reduce that cost by grouping bytes into patches inside the network (for example, using a small model to decide where patch boundaries go) so the large model runs over fewer, longer units. As of this writing, mainstream production LLMs still use subword tokenisers, so understanding BPE remains part of the job.

The public case: tokenisers make languages unequal (Petrov et al., NeurIPS 2023)

Petrov, La Malfa, Torr and Bibi measured the tokenisation premium of widely used tokenisers on FLORES-200, a parallel corpus in which the same sentences appear in about two hundred languages. For the tokeniser used by ChatGPT and GPT-4 (cl100k_base), the same content needs about 1.6 times as many tokens in Italian as in English, 2.6 times in Bulgarian, 3 times in Arabic and up to 15 times in Shan, a language of Myanmar's Shan State. They walk through one Shan word for "you": one consonant and three diacritics, four Unicode code points, which cl100k encodes as 9 tokens, while the English "you" is one token. Even the cheapest languages for this tokeniser, Portuguese among them, need about 50% more tokens than English. They also note that byte-level representations are not neutral: the UTF-8 bytes of the same text are over four times longer for Burmese or Tibetan than for Chinese.

The paper draws out three consequences that apply to any product. Cost: services that charge per token charge users of some languages at least 2.5 times more for the same task. Latency: more tokens mean more decode steps and slower responses. Capability: a fixed context window holds less content in high-premium languages, so long-document features degrade first for those users. The authors conclude that multilingual fairness has to be designed into the tokeniser's training data; it does not emerge from byte-level coverage.

Failure modes you will meet

Miscounted budgets. Teams estimate context use with "words × 1.3" and are surprised when code, JSON, logs or non-English text overflow the window. JSON with many short keys, quoted strings and braces is token-dense; base64 data is terrible, often below two characters per token. Count with the target model's tokeniser on real samples, and keep a safety margin for the chat template's own special tokens.

Truncation that cuts the wrong end. When input exceeds the context window, something gets cut. Libraries differ on whether they cut from the left or the right, and on whether system prompts are protected. The symptom is a model that suddenly ignores its instructions on long inputs. Log token counts per request and alert when requests come close to the limit.

Wrong tokeniser or template. Fine-tuned checkpoints sometimes ship with a tokeniser configuration that differs from the base model, or the serving stack applies a default chat template. Symptoms: the model never stops, emits role markers as text, or answers in the wrong format. Check by encoding a rendered conversation and comparing token by token with the format used in fine-tuning.

Streaming mojibake. A client that decodes each token's bytes independently prints replacement characters whenever a token boundary splits a multi-byte character, which happens constantly in Chinese, Hindi and emoji. Decode incrementally with a buffer that holds incomplete UTF-8 sequences.

What to measure

For a product, track input and output tokens per request by language and by feature, the characters-per-token ratio on your real traffic, the fraction of requests truncated, and cost per user by language. When evaluating a new model, run the same measurement with its tokeniser: a model with a cheaper per-token price can be more expensive per request if its tokeniser is less efficient on your data. When an output looks bizarre, look at the tokenised input first; tokenisation explains a surprising share of "the model is weird on this input" bugs.

def pair_counts(vocab):
    c = collections.Counter()
    for word, n in vocab.items():
        for a, b in zip(word, word[1:]): c[a, b] += n
    return c
def apply_merge(word, pair):
    out, i = [], 0
    while i < len(word):
        if i + 1 < len(word) and (word[i], word[i + 1]) == pair: out.append(word[i] + word[i + 1]); i += 2
        else: out.append(word[i]); i += 1
    return tuple(out)

def encode(s, rules):
    out = []
    rank = {p: i for i, p in enumerate(rules)}
    for ch in PAT.findall(s):
        w = tuple(bytes([b]) for b in ch.encode())
        while len(w) > 1:                                   # apply the earliest-learned merge first
            pairs = [(rank.get(p, 1e9), p) for p in zip(w, w[1:])]
            r, p = min(pairs)
            if r == 1e9: break
            w = apply_merge(w, p)
        out.extend(w)
    return out
print(f"\ncorpus: {len(text):,} characters, {len(text.encode()):,} bytes")
rules = train(text, 400)
print("first 10 merges:", [(a + b).decode(errors="replace") for a, b in rules[:10]])
print("last 5 merges:  ", [(a + b).decode(errors="replace") for a, b in rules[-5:]])
for n in (0, 50, 200, 400):
    toks = encode(text, rules[:n])
    print(f"{n:4d} merges (vocab {256+n}): {len(toks):6,} tokens, {len(text.encode())/len(toks):.2f} bytes/token")
for s in ["The subprocess module", "strawberry", "unhappiness", "12345678", "नमस्ते दुनिया"]:

Work it out — the multilingual bill

Your product answers support questions in English and Hindi. The system prompt is 900 English tokens. An average English question is 40 words; the Hindi equivalent carries the same meaning. Input is billed at £2.50 per million tokens. Estimate the input cost per question for each language, and say what that implies.

Answer

English: 40 words ÷ 0.75 words per token ≈ 53 tokens; plus 900 = 953 tokens. At £2.50 per million that is £0.0024.

Hindi: on an English-centric BPE vocabulary, Devanagari needs several times more tokens for the same content. The worked greeting was 13 tokens in cl100k against 2 for English; taking a conservative premium of about 3× for running text gives ≈ 160 tokens; plus 900 = 1,060 → £0.0027, about 11% more.

The implication is the interesting part. The gap looks small only because the shared English system prompt dominates. Strip it out and Hindi costs about 3× as much per question, and every Hindi user also fills the context window about 3× faster, so long-conversation truncation hits them first and their responses take longer to generate. Measure the real premium on your own traffic with the target model's tokeniser; consider a model whose tokeniser was trained with more Hindi (the same greeting is 5 tokens in o200k), which fixes cost, latency and context together, rather than a pricing tweak.

Estimate — what does vocabulary size cost?

A team proposes increasing their 1B-parameter model's vocabulary from 32,000 to 128,000 tokens. The model's width is d_model = 2,048, and its input and output matrices are untied. How many parameters does this add, and what do they get in return?

Answer

Each matrix grows by (128,000 − 32,000) × 2,048 = 196,608,000 parameters; two untied matrices add about 393 million parameters, which is roughly 39% on top of a 1B model, and the output softmax becomes four times wider, slowing every generated token. In return, sequences get shorter: Llama's move from 32K to 128K improved English compression from 3.17 to 3.94 characters per token, about 20% fewer tokens, and more for code and non-English text. For a small model the parameter cost is large relative to its size, so the trade is worth it mainly if the product is multilingual or code-heavy; tying the input and output matrices halves the cost.
