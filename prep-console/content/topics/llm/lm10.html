A user types "I forgot my login" into your help centre's search box. The article that answers it is titled "Resetting your password" and never contains the words "forgot" or "login". Keyword search, which matches words, returns nothing useful. A human would find it instantly, because a human compares meanings, not spellings. For decades that gap was the central problem of search, patched with hand-built synonym lists. Text embeddings closed much of it: they turn each text into a vector of numbers placed so that texts with related meaning sit close together, and "I forgot my login" lands right next to "Resetting your password".

Embeddings are now everywhere in AI engineering. They power the dense half of every RAG retriever (lm9), semantic search, recommendation ("users who read this also read"), deduplication of near-identical documents, clustering of support tickets into themes, and cheap classifiers built on top of a frozen model. Each of these turns into the same two engineering questions. First, which embedding model, and how do you know it understands your domain? Second, how do you find the nearest vectors among millions or billions quickly enough, when comparing a query with every stored vector would take too long and the vectors themselves no longer fit comfortably in memory?

The second question is where approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN) indexes come in, and where most production surprises live: indexes trade accuracy for speed through settings that are easy to leave at defaults and hard to reason about without measuring. A vector database that quietly returns 60% of the true nearest neighbours makes a RAG system worse in a way no prompt will fix.

There is also a modelling question underneath both: what does "similar" mean for your application? Two support tickets can be similar because they describe the same bug, because they come from the same customer, or because they are both angry. An embedding model encodes one notion of similarity, the one its training data rewarded, and choosing or adapting a model is really choosing that notion.

This lesson builds the idea of an embedding from the geometry of similarity (cosine versus dot product, worked by hand), explains how embedding models are trained with contrastive learning (one loss computed by hand), why bi-encoders made large-scale semantic search possible (the Sentence-BERT case), and then the index structures: brute force, IVF, HNSW and product quantisation, with memory arithmetic. The lab builds a 100,000-vector collection in NumPy and measures how recall changes with each index's settings.

Meaning as a location

Imagine a huge library where books are shelved not alphabetically but by subject, so finely that books on similar topics are always near each other: cookery next to nutrition, nutrition next to medicine. To find books like the one in your hand, you walk to where it belongs and look at its neighbours. An embedding model is the librarian who decides where each text goes; the shelf position is the vector; "nearby" is measured by similarity. The difference from a real library is the number of directions: instead of a corridor with two sides, the space has hundreds of dimensions (384, 768, 1,024 or more), so a text can be near "cooking" in some directions and near "chemistry" in others at once. That is what lets one vector represent several facets of meaning.

The analogy breaks in two useful ways. The librarian was trained on one notion of similarity, typically "questions near their answers, paraphrases near each other", and texts that are similar in other ways (same author, same date, one negates the other) may not be shelved together. And there is no reading the shelf labels: individual dimensions rarely mean anything nameable. Only distances are meaningful, and only within one model's space.

Three steps

Using embeddings always involves the same three steps. Encode every document (or chunk) once with the embedding model and store the vectors. Encode each incoming query with the same model. Search for the stored vectors most similar to the query vector and return their documents. Steps one and two are model questions; step three is a data-structure question, and at scale it is the hard part.

Why many dimensions?

Why does an embedding need hundreds of numbers? Because meaning has many independent facets. A sentence about "a refund for a damaged laptop delivered late" is about money, electronics, shipping and a complaint all at once, and it should be near other texts sharing any of those facets in proportion to how much they share. With two dimensions you could only arrange texts along two axes; with 768 there is room for hundreds of loosely independent directions, and a text's vector is a blend of them. The other side of the same coin is cost: every stored vector takes d numbers of memory, and every comparison takes d multiply-adds, so dimension is directly a memory and speed budget. Typical text-embedding models use 384 to 4,096 dimensions; 768 and 1,024 are common.

Where the numbers come from

An embedding model is a transformer encoder (lm3) that reads the whole text without a causal mask, produces a vector per token, and pools them into one vector, often by averaging. What makes it an embedding model rather than a language model is its training objective: it is trained on pairs that should be close, such as a question and the passage that answers it, and pushed to score them higher than unrelated pairs. Section 3 of the body works through that objective with numbers.

1. Vectors, dot products and cosine similarity

An embedding is a list of d numbers, a point (or arrow from the origin) in d-dimensional space. Two measures of similarity dominate. The dot product u·v = Σ uᵢvᵢ multiplies matching components and adds them. Cosine similarity divides it by both vectors' lengths: cos(u, v) = u·v/(|u||v|), which is the cosine of the angle between the arrows, 1 when they point the same way, 0 when perpendicular, −1 when opposite. Worked with q = [1, 2, 0] (length √5 = 2.236):

Document a points exactly the same way as q (it is 2q), so its cosine is 1; its large dot product partly reflects its length. If vectors are normalised to length 1 before storing, cosine and dot product become the same number, which is why many embedding models output unit vectors and why vector databases often just use dot products. Euclidean distance is the third common measure; for unit vectors, |u − v|² = 2 − 2·cos(u, v), so all three give the same ranking. When a model is not normalised, use the measure it was trained with.

2. From a transformer to one vector

An encoder produces one vector per token. To get one vector per text, embedding models pool: average the token vectors (mean pooling), take the vector of a special first token, or, for some decoder-based embedders, take the last token's vector. The result may be projected to a chosen dimension and normalised. Input length is limited by the encoder's context (often 512 to 8,192 tokens), and long texts are usually chunked (lm9) because one vector for a whole book cannot be specific about any page of it. The model's tokenizer matters too: rare identifiers split into fragments embed vaguely, one reason exact codes are better handled by keyword search.

3. Contrastive training, worked

Pretrained language models produce token vectors, but their pooled sentence vectors are poor at similarity out of the box. Embedding models are trained with a contrastive objective on pairs (query, positive passage). For a batch of B pairs, embed all queries and all passages, compute the B × B matrix of similarities, divide by a temperature τ, and apply a softmax cross-entropy in which each query's correct class is its own passage. Every other passage in the batch serves as a negative for free (in-batch negatives). The loss for query i is −log [exp(s_ii/τ) / Σ_j exp(s_ij/τ)].

Worked for one query whose similarities are 0.8 to its positive and 0.3 and 0.1 to two negatives. At τ = 1, the logits are 0.8, 0.3, 0.1; exponentials 2.226, 1.350, 1.105; the positive's probability 2.226/4.681 = 0.476; loss −ln 0.476 = 0.743. At τ = 0.1, the logits are 8, 3, 1; exponentials 2,981, 20.1, 2.72; probability 0.992; loss 0.0076. The same geometry is judged very differently: a small temperature means a gap of 0.5 in cosine is already a decisive win, so the model is pushed to make gaps of that size consistently and then stops pushing; a large temperature keeps pushing for bigger gaps. Typical values are around 0.01-0.1. Training data comes from many sources: question-answer pairs, search logs, title-body pairs, paraphrases, often hundreds of millions of pairs, plus hard negatives, passages that look relevant but are not, mined with an earlier retriever, which teach the fine distinctions that random negatives cannot.

4. Bi-encoders: why search became possible

Before sentence-embedding models, the most accurate way to compare two sentences with BERT was to feed both together into the model and read a similarity score: a cross-encoder. That is excellent for scoring one pair but hopeless for search. Reimers and Gurevych's Sentence-BERT paper made the point with numbers: finding the most similar pair in a collection of 10,000 sentences needs n(n − 1)/2 = 49,995,000 cross-encoder passes, about 65 hours on a V100 GPU. A bi-encoder embeds each sentence once (10,000 passes, about 5 seconds in their measurement) and then compares vectors with cosine similarity, about 0.01 seconds for all pairs. The accuracy cost is real, since the two texts never attend to each other, which is why retrieval systems use a bi-encoder to search and a cross-encoder to rerank a short list (lm9).

5. What embeddings capture, and what they miss

Embeddings capture what training rewarded. Models trained on question-passage pairs are good at topic and intent: "how do I cancel" matches "cancellation policy". They are weaker at things contrastive training rarely tested: negation ("refunds are not available" can sit close to "refunds are available"), numbers and dates ("2023 revenue" versus "2024 revenue"), exact identifiers, and long documents where one vector must summarise many topics. Domain matters: a general model may not know that two internal product names are related. And similarity scores are not calibrated probabilities: many embedding spaces are anisotropic, with vectors crowded into a narrow cone, so unrelated texts can still score 0.6-0.7, and a threshold that works for one model means nothing for another. Treat scores as rankings and calibrate any threshold on labelled data.

6. Choosing a model

The Massive Text Embedding Benchmark (MTEB) evaluates models on 58 datasets across 8 task types (retrieval, reranking, classification, clustering, pair classification, semantic similarity, summarisation and bitext mining) in 112 languages, and its authors' headline finding was that no single model dominates across tasks. Use the leaderboard to shortlist models strong on the task type closest to yours, then decide with your own evaluation: a few hundred real queries with labelled relevant documents, measuring recall@k and MRR (lm9). Practical constraints matter as much as scores: dimension (memory), speed (embedding a large corpus can take hours), maximum input length, languages, licensing and whether it is offered as an API or must be self-hosted.

7. Exact search

The simplest index stores all vectors in a matrix and, for each query, computes every similarity with one matrix-vector product, then selects the top k. It is exact, has no settings, updates instantly and parallelises perfectly on CPUs and especially GPUs (it is a matrix multiplication, gpu2). In the lab, NumPy searches 100,000 vectors of 64 dimensions for 200 queries in about 130 ms, under a millisecond per query. Cost is linear in N × d: 10 million vectors of 768 dimensions is 7.7 billion multiply-adds per query, tens of milliseconds on a GPU, too slow for a CPU at high query rates. For up to a few million vectors, brute force is often the right choice; beyond that, approximate indexes earn their complexity.

8. Measuring an approximate index

An ANN index is judged by recall@k: of the true k nearest neighbours (found by brute force), what fraction does the index return in its top k? Worked: the true top 3 for a query are {a, d, b}; the index returns {a, b, x}; recall@3 = 2/3 = 0.67. Average over a few hundred queries drawn from real traffic. This is a different recall from lm9's retrieval recall: there "relevant" means labelled by a person; here it means "nearest according to the embedding model". An index can have perfect ANN recall and still retrieve irrelevant documents if the embedding model is poor, and vice versa, so measure both.

9. IVF: search only the nearby clusters

The inverted file (IVF) index applies k-means (ml12) to the collection, producing, say, 256 centroids, and stores each vector in the list of its nearest centroid. A query is compared with the 256 centroids, the nprobe nearest lists are selected, and only their vectors are compared exactly. With 100,000 vectors and 256 lists, each list holds about 391 vectors, so nprobe = 1 compares the query with 256 centroids plus about 391 vectors instead of 100,000. The catch is the boundaries: a true neighbour can fall in an adjacent cluster. The lab measures the trade-off on clustered synthetic data: recall@10 is 0.373 with nprobe 1 (0.4% of vectors scanned), 0.616 with 4 (1.6%), 0.856 with 16 (6.3%) and 0.981 with 64 (25%). The curve is the whole story of ANN: each additional point of recall costs more work, and the setting must be chosen against a measured target.

10. HNSW: navigating a graph

The most widely used in-memory index is HNSW (hierarchical navigable small world, Malkov and Yashunin, 2016). Each vector is a node linked to up to M of its near neighbours (M is often 16-64). The graph has layers: every node is in the bottom layer, a random fraction also in the layer above, a smaller fraction in the one above that, so upper layers are sparse with long links, like motorways, and lower layers dense with short links, like local streets. Search starts at an entry node in the top layer and repeatedly moves to whichever neighbour is closer to the query until no neighbour is closer; then it drops a layer and continues from there. In the bottom layer it keeps a list of the best ef candidates, a parameter that trades recall for speed exactly as nprobe does for IVF. The authors report logarithmic complexity scaling. HNSW gives high recall at low latency, but each node stores M or more links (extra memory, often 100+ bytes per vector), building is slower than IVF, and deletions are awkward, typically handled by marking nodes deleted and rebuilding periodically.

11. Product quantisation: shrinking the vectors

At large scale the vectors themselves are the problem: 100 million 1,024-dimensional float32 vectors take 410 GB. Product quantisation (PQ) compresses them. Split each vector into m sub-vectors; for each sub-vector position, learn a codebook of 256 centroids with k-means; store each vector as m one-byte codes, the index of the nearest centroid for each piece. In the lab, 64 dimensions become 16 sub-vectors of 4 dimensions, so each vector is 16 bytes instead of 256, 16 times smaller. Search does not decompress anything: for a query, compute the dot product of each query piece with all 256 centroids of that piece (a 16 × 256 table), and the approximate score of any stored vector is the sum of 16 table lookups. Because each piece is approximated, scores are noisy: recall@10 from the codes alone is 0.42 in the lab. The standard remedy is to shortlist with PQ (top 200) and re-rank the shortlist with exact vectors kept on disk or in cheaper memory, which brings recall@10 to 0.975. IVF and PQ combine (IVF-PQ): search a few lists, score with codes, re-rank, which is how FAISS handles billion-scale collections.

Simpler compressions are also common. Scalar quantisation stores each component in int8 (4× smaller than float32) with little recall loss. Binary quantisation keeps one bit per component (32× smaller), searchable with fast bit operations, usually followed by re-ranking. Matryoshka embeddings (Kusupati et al., 2022) are trained so that the first m dimensions form a usable embedding on their own; their authors report up to 14× smaller embeddings at the same accuracy on ImageNet classification, and the idea is now offered by several text-embedding models, letting you truncate 1,024 dimensions to 256 for a first-stage search and use the full vector to re-rank.

13. Vector databases in practice

A vector database wraps these indexes with the features applications need: storing metadata alongside vectors, filtered search ("nearest vectors where tenant = 42 and year ≥ 2025"), inserts, deletes and updates, persistence, replication and sharding across machines. Filtering is subtle: filtering after an ANN search can leave too few results when the filter is selective, while filtering before search may defeat the index; good systems integrate filters into the search. Hybrid search (vectors plus keywords, lm9) is a standard feature. Whatever the product, the same checks apply: measure ANN recall at your settings, check latency at your query rate and filter selectivity, and plan for re-embedding the whole collection when the model changes.

14. A complete search, with numbers

Put the pieces together for a help centre with 2 million article chunks embedded at 768 dimensions in float32 (6.1 GB). A user types "I forgot my login". The query is embedded by the same model, taking a few milliseconds, and normalised to unit length. Brute force would take 2 million × 768 ≈ 1.5 billion multiply-adds, about 10-20 ms on a modern CPU core with vectorised code, a little less on a GPU: acceptable at low traffic. At 500 queries per second the team uses HNSW instead: with M = 32 and ef = 100, a query touches a few thousand vectors and returns in about a millisecond, and measured recall@10 against brute force on 1,000 logged queries is 0.97. The top 10 chunks go to a reranker and then into the RAG prompt (lm9). The index adds about 0.5 GB of links; the team also keeps a nightly job that measures ANN recall on the same 1,000 queries, because recall can drift as the collection grows and the graph is updated.

15. Beyond retrieval

The same vectors serve other jobs. Clustering support tickets with k-means on embeddings surfaces recurring themes; deduplication finds near-identical documents by thresholding similarity; classification can train a logistic regression (ml6) on frozen embeddings with a few hundred labels; recommendation embeds users and items in one space; and semantic caching reuses an LLM answer when a new question embeds very close to a previously answered one (with care, because near-identical wording can carry different meaning). Each is cheap once embeddings exist, which is why a well-chosen embedding model is reusable infrastructure.

Why high-dimensional search is hard

In low dimensions, tree structures such as k-d trees prune the search space efficiently. In hundreds of dimensions they degrade to near brute force, because of the effects ml11 calls the curse of dimensionality: distances between random points concentrate around the same value, so a query's nearest and farthest neighbours differ relatively little, and any partition of space has many neighbours near its boundaries. Real embeddings are not random: they lie near lower-dimensional structures (clusters, manifolds), which is what IVF's clusters and HNSW's neighbour graph exploit. The lab's data is clustered for this reason; on uniformly random vectors every ANN index performs much worse, a good reminder to benchmark on real embeddings.

HNSW's design in a little more depth

HNSW descends from navigable small-world graphs, in which greedy routing reaches any node in few hops because the graph mixes short and long links. A single-layer version gets stuck in local minima on large graphs and its routing scales polylogarithmically; the hierarchy fixes both by doing coarse routing in sparse upper layers, where each hop covers a large distance, before refining in the dense bottom layer. Node levels are drawn from an exponentially decaying distribution, so the expected number of layers grows logarithmically with N. A neighbour-selection heuristic keeps diverse links (not just the M closest, which might all lie in one direction), which preserves connectivity across clusters. The construction-time parameter efConstruction controls graph quality; the query-time parameter ef controls the recall-latency trade.

Asymmetric distance computation in PQ

PQ search uses "asymmetric" distances: the query is kept exact and only the database vectors are quantised. This halves the error compared with quantising both sides, and the lookup-table trick makes scoring a vector cost m table reads and additions instead of d multiplications. FAISS's GPU implementation (Johnson, Douze and Jégou) pushed this further with a k-selection algorithm running at up to 55% of theoretical peak performance, a nearest-neighbour implementation 8.5× faster than the prior GPU state of the art, and the construction of a k-nearest-neighbour graph over one billion vectors in under 12 hours on four GPUs. Optimised PQ variants rotate the space first so that information is spread evenly across sub-vectors, reducing quantisation error.

Contrastive training details that matter

Three details separate good embedding models from mediocre ones. Batch size: with in-batch negatives, a batch of B gives each query B − 1 negatives, so larger batches give a harder, more informative task; techniques that share negatives across devices make effective batches of thousands. Hard negatives: random negatives are easy, so the model learns little from them late in training; mined hard negatives teach it to separate "about the topic" from "answers the question", but must be filtered for false negatives (passages that are actually relevant but unlabelled). Instructions and asymmetry: queries and documents differ in form (short question versus long passage), so many models prepend task instructions or use different prefixes for queries and passages; using the wrong prefix at inference quietly lowers quality.

Fine-tuning embeddings for a domain

If a general model's recall on your evaluation set is poor, fine-tuning on in-domain pairs often helps substantially. Pairs can come from logs (query, clicked document), from document structure (heading, section), or from an LLM generating plausible questions for each chunk. Train contrastively with hard negatives from your current retriever, hold out a labelled evaluation set, and watch for overfitting to the synthetic question style. Remember that a new model means re-embedding the corpus and rebuilding the index, so fine-tuning is a release, not a tweak.

Hubness and why some documents are always retrieved

In high-dimensional spaces a few points become "hubs": they appear among the nearest neighbours of a disproportionate number of queries, simply because of where they sit relative to the overall distribution (often near the centre of the data). In retrieval this shows up as the same generic chunk (a boilerplate paragraph, a table of contents, a very short chunk) appearing in many unrelated results. Remedies include removing boilerplate during ingestion, centring embeddings (subtracting the mean vector before normalising), penalising chunks that are retrieved for too many queries, and reranking. Logging which chunks are retrieved most often is a cheap way to find hubs.

Embeddings and privacy

Embeddings feel anonymous because they are just numbers, but research has shown that text can be partially or largely reconstructed from its embedding by training an inversion model, especially for short texts. Treat stored embeddings with the same access controls as the source text, and do not assume that sending embeddings rather than documents to a third party removes privacy risk.

The public case: Sentence-BERT (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019) and FAISS (Johnson et al., 2017)

In 2019 BERT was the state of the art on sentence-pair tasks such as semantic textual similarity, but only as a cross-encoder: both sentences went into the model together. Reimers and Gurevych showed what that means for search: finding the most similar pair in 10,000 sentences requires 49,995,000 inference computations, about 65 hours on a V100 GPU; finding the most similar of over 40 million Quora questions to a new one would mean 40 million forward passes per query. Worse, BERT's own pooled sentence vectors, compared by cosine, performed poorly on similarity benchmarks, worse than simply averaging older word embeddings. Sentence-BERT fine-tuned BERT in a siamese (shared-weights, two-tower) set-up on natural-language-inference and similarity data so that the pooled vectors themselves were good for cosine comparison. The same 10,000-sentence task then took about 5 seconds to embed and about 0.01 seconds to compare, and its large variant reached a Spearman correlation of 79.23 on the STS benchmark. The bi-encoder recipe, with contrastive training on much larger datasets, became the template for modern embedding models.

The other half of the problem, searching the vectors, was addressed at scale by FAISS. Johnson, Douze and Jégou's 2017 paper described GPU implementations of exact search, IVF and product quantisation, with a k-selection algorithm at up to 55% of theoretical peak and a nearest-neighbour implementation 8.5× faster than the previous GPU state of the art, and built a k-nearest-neighbour graph over a billion vectors in under 12 hours on four GPUs. The open-source library became a standard component, and its index taxonomy (flat, IVF, PQ, HNSW and combinations) is the vocabulary vector databases still use.

Failure modes in production

Silent recall loss from index settings. A vector database with default parameters returns half of the true neighbours, and RAG quality is mediocre for reasons nobody can see. Measure ANN recall against brute force on a sample of real queries and tune ef or nprobe.

Mixed embedding versions. Part of the corpus was re-embedded with a new model and part was not; similarities across the two are meaningless. Version the model in the index and rebuild atomically.

Selective filters starving results. A tenant filter that matches 0.1% of vectors returns two results instead of ten after post-filtering. Use indexes with integrated filtering or per-tenant partitions.

Memory blow-up. An HNSW index sized for 10 million float32 vectors plus links exceeds the instance's RAM as the collection grows. Plan memory per vector including index overhead, and use quantisation or sharding before you need it.

Threshold drift. A similarity threshold tuned for one model (for semantic caching or deduplication) behaves differently after a model change. Recalibrate thresholds on labelled pairs with every model change.

A note on cost

Embedding is usually cheap per item but adds up at corpus scale: embedding 50 million chunks of 300 tokens is 15 billion tokens, a large one-off batch job whether run on your own GPUs or through an API, and it must be repeated in full for every embedding-model change. Query-time embedding is a small, latency-sensitive model call. Index memory, not compute, tends to dominate the steady-state bill for large collections, which is why quantisation and dimension reduction are worth evaluating early rather than after the index has outgrown its machines.

What to monitor

ANN recall@k on a fixed query sample, latency percentiles at the production query rate, index memory per vector, ingestion lag (time from document change to searchable), and the distribution of top similarity scores per query, whose shift often signals data drift or an embedding mismatch.

Goal: compute similarities by hand, then build a 100,000-vector "embedding" collection (clustered synthetic unit vectors standing in for real embeddings, since no model is downloaded) and measure exact search, an IVF index at four nprobe settings, and product quantisation with and without re-ranking. NumPy only; about a minute, mostly k-means.

import numpy as np, time
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# ---- 1. cosine similarity by hand
q = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 0.0]); docs = {"a": [2.0, 4.0, 0.0], "b": [0.0, 1.0, 1.0], "c": [-1.0, 0.0, 1.0], "d": [3.0, 1.0, 0.0]}
cos = lambda u, v: float(u @ v / (np.linalg.norm(u) * np.linalg.norm(v)))
for k, v in docs.items():
    v = np.array(v); print(f"{k}: dot {q @ v:5.1f}   cosine {cos(q, v):6.3f}")

# ---- 2. a synthetic 'embedding' collection: 100,000 unit vectors in 64 dims, clustered like real embeddings
n, d, n_topics = 100_000, 64, 500
centres = rng.normal(size=(n_topics, d))
X = centres[rng.integers(0, n_topics, n)] + 0.6 * rng.normal(size=(n, d))
X = (X / np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1, keepdims=True)).astype(np.float32)
Q = X[rng.choice(n, 200, replace=False)] + 0.3 * rng.normal(size=(200, d)).astype(np.float32)
Q /= np.linalg.norm(Q, axis=1, keepdims=True)
k = 10

t = time.perf_counter(); S = Q @ X.T; exact = np.argpartition(-S, k, axis=1)[:, :k]; t_exact = time.perf_counter() - t
print(f"\nbrute force: {len(Q)} queries x {n:,} vectors in {t_exact*1e3:.0f} ms ({t_exact/len(Q)*1e3:.2f} ms/query)")
def recall(found):
    return np.mean([len(set(f) & set(e)) / k for f, e in zip(found, exact)])

# ---- 3. IVF: cluster with k-means, search only the nprobe nearest clusters
def kmeans(data, c, iters=10):
    C = data[rng.choice(len(data), c, replace=False)].copy()
    for _ in range(iters):
        a = np.argmax(data @ C.T, axis=1)                     # nearest centroid by cosine (unit vectors)
        for j in range(c):
            m = data[a == j]
            if len(m): C[j] = m.mean(0) / np.linalg.norm(m.mean(0))
    return C, np.argmax(data @ C.T, axis=1)
n_lists = 256
C, assign = kmeans(X, n_lists)
lists = [np.where(assign == j)[0] for j in range(n_lists)]
print(f"IVF: {n_lists} lists, average {n/n_lists:.0f} vectors per list")
for nprobe in (1, 4, 16, 64):
    found, scanned = [], 0
    for qv in Q:
        probe = np.argpartition(-(C @ qv), nprobe)[:nprobe]
        cand = np.concatenate([lists[j] for j in probe]); scanned += len(cand)
        s = X[cand] @ qv; found.append(cand[np.argsort(-s)[:k]])
    print(f"  nprobe {nprobe:3d}: recall@10 {recall(found):.3f}, scanned {scanned/len(Q)/n:6.1%} of vectors")

# ---- 4. product quantisation: 64 dims -> 16 sub-vectors x 1 byte = 16 bytes per vector (vs 256 bytes float32)
m, ksub = 16, 256; ds = d // m
codebooks, codes = [], np.zeros((n, m), np.uint8)
for i in range(m):
    sub = X[:, i*ds:(i+1)*ds]
    cb = sub[rng.choice(n, ksub, replace=False)].copy()
    for _ in range(8):
        a = np.argmin(-2 * sub @ cb.T + (cb ** 2).sum(1), axis=1)     # nearest code (squared distance, constant dropped)
        for j in range(ksub):
            mm = sub[a == j]
            if len(mm): cb[j] = mm.mean(0)
    codebooks.append(cb); codes[:, i] = a
def pq_search(qv):
    table = np.stack([codebooks[i] @ qv[i*ds:(i+1)*ds] for i in range(m)])   # m x 256 partial dot products
    approx = table[np.arange(m), codes].sum(1)                                  # approximate q . x for every vector
    top = np.argpartition(-approx, 200)[:200]                                   # shortlist, then re-rank exactly
    return top[np.argsort(-(X[top] @ qv))[:k]], np.argpartition(-approx, k)[:k]
res = [pq_search(qv) for qv in Q]
print(f"\nPQ codes: {codes.nbytes/1e6:.1f} MB vs float32 {X.nbytes/1e6:.1f} MB ({X.nbytes/codes.nbytes:.0f}x smaller)")
print(f"  recall@10 from PQ scores alone {recall([r[1] for r in res]):.3f}; with exact re-rank of top 200 {recall([r[0] for r in res]):.3f}")

# ---- 5. memory for a real collection
for name, nvec, dim in [("10M x 768", 10e6, 768), ("100M x 1024", 100e6, 1024)]:
    print(f"{name}: float32 {nvec*dim*4/1e9:6.1f} GB, float16 {nvec*dim*2/1e9:6.1f} GB, int8 {nvec*dim/1e9:6.1f} GB, PQ 64 B {nvec*64/1e9:5.1f} GB")

What to look for.

  1. Section 1 reproduces the table: dot products 10, 2, −1, 5 and cosines 1.000, 0.632, −0.316, 0.707.
  2. Section 2: brute force over 100,000 × 64 vectors at well under a millisecond per query, giving the exact top-10 used as ground truth.
  3. Section 3: IVF with 256 lists; recall@10 of about 0.37, 0.62, 0.86 and 0.98 at nprobe 1, 4, 16 and 64, scanning 0.4%, 1.6%, 6.3% and 25% of the vectors.
  4. Section 4: PQ codes 16× smaller (1.6 MB against 25.6 MB); recall@10 about 0.42 from codes alone and about 0.975 after exactly re-ranking the top 200.
  5. Section 5: memory for 10M × 768 and 100M × 1,024 collections in each encoding.

Design — size a vector index

You must serve semantic search over 50 million document chunks with 1,024-dimensional embeddings, at 200 queries per second with p95 latency under 50 ms and recall@10 of at least 0.95 against brute force. Machines have 256 GB of RAM. Propose an index and check the memory.

Answer

Raw size: 5 × 10⁷ × 1,024 × 4 bytes = 205 GB in float32, too close to 256 GB with index overhead and the OS. In float16, 102 GB; in int8, 51 GB.

Option A, HNSW on int8 vectors: 51 GB of vectors plus links (say M = 32, about 256 bytes per vector with two-layer overhead, about 13 GB) fits comfortably on one machine; replicate for throughput and availability. Tune ef until recall@10 ≥ 0.95 on sampled queries; HNSW typically meets 50 ms at this scale.

Option B, IVF-PQ with re-ranking: about 8,000 lists, 64-byte PQ codes (3.2 GB), keep full or int8 vectors for re-ranking the top 100-200 from disk or RAM; cheapest memory, more tuning, slightly higher latency.

Either way: consider Matryoshka truncation to 512 dimensions for the first stage if the model supports it (halves memory), measure recall against brute force on a few thousand real queries, load-test at 200 QPS with realistic filters, and plan a full rebuild path for model upgrades.

Compute — contrastive loss

A query's similarities are 0.6 to its positive and 0.5, 0.2 and 0.1 to three in-batch negatives. Compute the loss at τ = 0.05 and say what it implies.

Answer

Logits are 0.6/0.05 = 12, 10, 4, 2. Relative to the largest: exp(0) = 1, exp(−2) = 0.135, exp(−8) = 0.00034, exp(−10) = 0.000045. Probability of the positive = 1/(1 + 0.135 + 0.00034 + 0.000045) = 0.881; loss = −ln 0.881 = 0.127. The near-miss negative at 0.5 contributes almost all of the loss: with a low temperature, training focuses on the hardest negatives, which is exactly why mined hard negatives are so valuable.
