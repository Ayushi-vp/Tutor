A team has a 7B open model that is almost right for their product. It extracts fields from invoices correctly most of the time, but it wraps the JSON in chatty sentences, invents a field name now and then, and stumbles on a few supplier formats. Prompt engineering got them to 90%; the last 10% is costing support time every day. The obvious move is to train the model on a few thousand examples of exactly the output they want. Then they look at the bill: training every weight of a 7B model with the standard optimiser needs on the order of 100 GB of GPU memory just for the model's training state, several large GPUs, and produces a 13.5 GB checkpoint per experiment. If they want separate variants for ten customers, that is ten copies of the model to store and serve.

In 2021, Hu and colleagues at Microsoft showed a different way. Instead of changing all the weights, freeze them, and next to each chosen weight matrix learn a tiny correction made of two thin matrices whose product is a low-rank update. On GPT-3 175B, this cut training memory from 1.2 TB to 350 GB and the checkpoint from 350 GB to 35 MB, about 10,000 times smaller, with quality that matched full fine-tuning on their benchmarks. Two years later QLoRA stored the frozen model in 4-bit precision and fine-tuned a 65-billion-parameter model on a single 48 GB GPU. Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) and its quantised variant are now the default way most teams customise open models.

For an AI engineer the questions are practical. Should we fine-tune at all, or use prompting and retrieval? If we fine-tune, full or LoRA, and with what rank on which layers? How much memory and data will it take? What will it fail to learn, and what might it break? Interviewers ask all of these, usually with a request to count LoRA's parameters for a given layer.

This lesson starts with when fine-tuning is the right tool, then the memory cost of full fine-tuning, LoRA's low-rank update with its parameter count worked by hand, why low rank suffices (and when it does not), initialisation and scaling, merging and multi-adapter serving, QLoRA's three memory tricks, and a practical recipe including data and evaluation. The lab trains a LoRA adapter on a toy layer in NumPy and shows rank deciding what it can learn.

What fine-tuning changes

Fine-tuning means continuing to train a pretrained model on your own examples. For an LLM, this is usually supervised fine-tuning on (prompt, ideal response) pairs with the ordinary next-token loss (lm6), sometimes followed by preference tuning. It shifts the model's behaviour towards your examples: the format it answers in, the style, which of its existing skills it applies, how it treats your domain's conventions. It is a poor way to add facts: a fact seen in a few examples is learned shallowly, and models fine-tuned on facts they did not already know can become more prone to confident errors. Knowledge that changes or must be cited belongs in retrieval (lm9).

An analogy: tailoring, not a new suit

Full fine-tuning is like re-cutting an entire suit to fit you: every seam can move, which gives the best possible fit, but it takes a lot of fabric and work, and you end up with a separate suit for each person. LoRA is like tailoring with a few well-placed adjustments, taking in the waist and shortening the sleeves: most of the suit stays exactly as it was, the alterations are small and cheap, and the same base suit can be adjusted differently for different people, with each set of alterations stored separately. The analogy captures the trade-off too: small adjustments fix fit, not style; if you need a completely different garment, adjustments are not enough. In LoRA terms, a low-rank update is ideal for steering existing abilities and weaker when the task needs genuinely new ones.

Three ways to fine-tune

There are three families, and the rest of the lesson compares them. Full fine-tuning updates every weight: the most expressive, the most memory-hungry, and it produces a complete new copy of the model. LoRA freezes the original weights and trains a small low-rank correction next to some or all of them: a fraction of a percent of the parameters, far less memory, and the correction can be stored and swapped separately. QLoRA is LoRA with the frozen original weights stored in 4-bit precision during training, which shrinks memory again so that models with tens of billions of parameters fit on a single GPU. All three use the same data and the same loss; they differ in which parameters are allowed to move and how the frozen ones are stored. In interviews, "LoRA versus full fine-tuning" is really a question about this trade-off between expressiveness, memory, forgetting and operational convenience.

The one equation

For a weight matrix W₀ (say 4,096 × 4,096 in an attention projection), LoRA keeps W₀ frozen and learns two thin matrices, B (4,096 × r) and A (r × 4,096), with r small such as 8 or 16. The layer computes

h = W₀ x + (α / r) · B A x

Only A and B are trained. Their product BA has the full 4,096 × 4,096 shape, so it can change the layer's behaviour everywhere, but it has rank at most r, so it can only change it along r directions. At r = 16 that is 131,072 trainable numbers instead of 16.8 million, 0.78%.

1. Decide whether to fine-tune at all

Fine-tuning is the third tool, not the first. Prompting (clear instructions, examples, output schemas, constrained decoding from lm8) is cheapest and fastest to change. Retrieval (lm9) supplies knowledge, keeps it fresh and lets the model cite it. Fine-tuning is worth its cost when the remaining gap is behaviour that prompting cannot make reliable, or that costs too many tokens to prompt every time: a strict format, a house style, a narrow classification or extraction task, specialised conventions (legal drafting, a codebase's idioms), tool-calling patterns, or teaching a smaller and cheaper model to do what a larger one does with a long prompt ("distillation" of prompts into weights). It is also the route to preference tuning (lm6). A quick test: if you can write a few hundred good examples of the desired output and a prompt cannot get there, fine-tuning is a candidate.

One common and underrated use is cost reduction. A large model with a long, carefully engineered prompt may solve a task well but expensively; generating a few thousand of its outputs and fine-tuning a much smaller model on them often reproduces most of the quality at a fraction of the inference cost and latency. The smaller model no longer needs the long prompt, because the behaviour now lives in its weights. Check the provider's terms before training on another model's outputs, and evaluate the small model on fresh inputs, not on the large model's training-time examples.

2. What full fine-tuning costs

Full fine-tuning updates every weight, which needs memory for more than the weights. With mixed-precision AdamW (gpu9 derives this), each parameter needs 2 bytes for the bf16 weight, 2 for its gradient, 4 for an fp32 master copy, and 8 for the two Adam moment estimates: about 16 bytes per parameter. For Llama 2 7B (6.74 billion parameters) that is 6.74 × 10⁹ × 16 ≈ 108 GB before activations, which grow with batch size and sequence length. So full fine-tuning of a 7B model needs at least two 80 GB GPUs with sharding (ZeRO or FSDP), and a 70B model needs over a terabyte of model state, a multi-node job. Each experiment also produces a full checkpoint, 13.5 GB for 7B in bf16, and each fine-tuned variant is a separate model to serve. Compute per token is roughly three times the forward pass, about 6N FLOPs (lm7).

3. The LoRA idea

Hu et al. proposed freezing W₀ and representing the fine-tuning change as a product of two thin matrices: ΔW = BA, with B of shape d_out × r and A of shape r × d_in. The forward pass computes W₀x + (α/r)·B(Ax): first squeeze x to r numbers with A, then expand back with B. Gradients flow through W₀ (so the model's behaviour is fully differentiated) but W₀ itself receives no update, no gradient storage and no optimiser state. Only A and B are trained.

Why should a rank-r update be enough? The paper's hypothesis is that the change a pretrained model needs to adapt to a task has low "intrinsic rank": it amplifies or redirects a few directions the model already represents rather than rewriting the whole matrix. A large pretrained model already contains most of the skills a task needs; fine-tuning selects and reshapes them. The authors measured the learned updates on GPT-3 and found they concentrate in a few directions, and that tiny ranks (r = 1 to 8) already matched full fine-tuning on their benchmarks.

4. Counting LoRA parameters

For one matrix with d_in inputs and d_out outputs, LoRA trains r·(d_in + d_out) parameters. Worked for a 4,096 × 4,096 attention projection at r = 16: 16 × (4,096 + 4,096) = 131,072, against 4,096² = 16,777,216, so 0.78%. For Llama 2 7B (32 layers; q, k, v, o are 4,096 × 4,096; the MLP's gate and up are 4,096 → 11,008 and down 11,008 → 4,096):

q and v only:   32 × 2 × 16 × (4,096 + 4,096)                      =  8.4M  (0.12% of 6.74B)
all seven:      32 × 16 × [4 × 8,192 + 3 × (4,096 + 11,008)]       = 40.0M  (0.59%)

Those 40M parameters need about 16 bytes each in training (bf16 weight, gradient, fp32 master, Adam moments): 0.64 GB. The frozen base still has to sit in memory once, 13.5 GB in bf16, but needs no gradients or optimiser states. Total model state is about 14 GB instead of 108 GB, which fits one 24-48 GB GPU with room for activations. The saved adapter is 40M × 2 bytes = 80 MB.

5. Initialisation and the α/r scale

A is initialised with small random values and B with zeros, so BA = 0 and the model starts exactly as the pretrained model; training then grows the update from nothing. Both cannot be zero, because then neither would ever receive a gradient (the gradient of B depends on A's output and vice versa). The update is multiplied by α/r. The reason is practical: as you change r, the typical magnitude of BA changes, and so would the effective learning rate; dividing by r keeps it comparable, so a learning rate tuned at one rank carries over. Hu et al. set α to the first r they tried and did not tune it further. Common practice is r between 8 and 64 with α equal to r or 2r; QLoRA's experiments used r = 64 and α = 16 on all linear layers.

6. Rank in action

The lab makes the low-rank argument concrete. A 64 × 64 "pretrained" layer must be adapted to a task whose true change ΔW has rank 4 (four non-zero singular values, 4.26, 3.37, 2.70 and 2.45), with a little noise in the targets. The frozen layer's loss is 0.68. LoRA with r = 1 trains 128 parameters and reaches only 0.40; r = 2 reaches 0.21; r = 4 (512 parameters, an eighth of the layer's 4,096 weights) reaches 0.0001, the noise floor; r = 8 does no better. Rank is capacity: below the change's rank, LoRA can only approximate it with its best low-rank version; at or above it, LoRA can represent it exactly. Real fine-tuning updates are not exactly low-rank, but their singular values decay, so a modest r captures most of the change.

7. Which matrices to adapt

The original paper adapted only the attention query and value projections of GPT-3 and found that adapting more matrix types with lower rank beat adapting fewer with higher rank at the same parameter budget. Later work, including QLoRA, found that applying LoRA to all linear layers (attention and MLP) is what closes the gap to full fine-tuning, and that with all layers adapted the exact rank matters little. The MLP holds two thirds of each layer's weights (lm3) and much of its stored knowledge, so leaving it frozen limits what the adapter can change. Embedding and LM-head matrices are usually left frozen unless new tokens are added.

8. Merging and multi-adapter serving

After training, the adapter can be merged: compute W = W₀ + (α/r)·BA once and replace W₀. The result has exactly the original shape and inference speed; the lab verifies the merged layer's outputs equal the base-plus-adapter outputs. Alternatively the adapter is kept separate, which enables a powerful serving pattern: one copy of the base model in GPU memory and many adapters beside it, each tens to hundreds of megabytes, with batched kernels that apply each request's own adapter. A platform can host hundreds of customer-specific fine-tunes on the GPUs that would otherwise hold one model. Hu et al. made the storage argument for GPT-3: 100 adapted models need about 350 GB + 100 × 35 MB ≈ 354 GB, instead of 100 × 350 GB = 35 TB.

9. QLoRA: the frozen model in 4 bits

With LoRA the trainable part is tiny, so the frozen base dominates memory: 13.5 GB for 7B, 130 GB for 65B in bf16. Dettmers et al.'s QLoRA attacks exactly that, with three techniques. 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4): the frozen weights are stored in 4 bits per parameter, in blocks of 64 weights that each have their own scaling constant, using 16 levels spaced to suit normally distributed weights (lm12 covers quantisation formats and why spacing matters). Before each matrix multiplication, a block is dequantised to bf16; computation and gradients are in bf16. Double quantisation: the per-block scaling constants (32-bit, one per 64 weights, 0.5 bits per parameter) are themselves quantised to 8 bits, reducing the overhead to about 0.127 bits per parameter, a saving of about 0.37 bits per parameter, roughly 3 GB on a 65B model. Paged optimisers: optimiser states live in memory that can be paged to the CPU automatically during spikes, such as a long sequence, instead of crashing. Gradients flow through the frozen 4-bit model into 16-bit LoRA adapters on all linear layers.

The memory arithmetic for 7B: about 6.74 × 10⁹ × 0.5 bytes ≈ 3.4 GB of weights plus a few percent for constants, plus 0.64 GB for 40M LoRA parameters' training state: about 4 GB of model state. That is why QLoRA fine-tuning of 7B-13B models runs on a single consumer GPU, and why QLoRA reported fine-tuning a 65B model on one 48 GB GPU, down from over 780 GB, while matching 16-bit fine-tuning performance. The cost is speed: dequantising weights at every step makes QLoRA steps slower than LoRA on a bf16 base, a fair trade when the alternative is not fitting at all.

10. What LoRA learns less of, and forgets less of

LoRA's low-rank constraint is also a regulariser. Biderman et al. (2024) compared LoRA and full fine-tuning on programming and mathematics in two regimes: instruction fine-tuning on 100K prompt-response pairs, and continued pretraining on 20B tokens of domain text. In standard low-rank settings LoRA substantially underperformed full fine-tuning on the target domain. But it better preserved the base model's performance on tasks outside that domain, more so than common regularisers such as weight decay and dropout, and kept generations more diverse. Their spectral analysis found that full fine-tuning learned weight changes with rank 10-100 times greater than typical LoRA configurations, which explains the gap: the domain shift they studied was not low-rank. The practical reading: for narrow behaviour changes LoRA is usually sufficient and safer; for substantial domain adaptation, use higher ranks on all layers or full fine-tuning, and measure both target gains and regressions.

11. Data: the part that decides quality

The method matters less than the data. A good fine-tuning set is representative of real inputs (including messy and edge cases), consistent in the output it demands (two annotators who format differently teach the model to be inconsistent), deduplicated, and free of leakage into the evaluation set (split by customer, document or time, not randomly, when near-duplicates exist). Size depends on the task: a format or style change can work with a few hundred examples; a narrow extraction or classification task often wants a few thousand; domain adaptation wants far more. Quality beats quantity, as LIMA showed for SFT (lm6). Format training examples with the model's chat template (lm1) and compute the loss on response tokens only.

12. Training settings that usually work

Starting points for LoRA SFT on a 7B-13B model: adapters on all linear layers, r = 16, α = 16-32, learning rate around 1-2 × 10⁻⁴ (higher than for full fine-tuning, because only the small adapters move), a short warm-up then cosine decay, 1-3 epochs, and a batch size in the tens of sequences (via gradient accumulation if needed). Watch the held-out loss and, more importantly, task metrics; more epochs often improve training loss while worsening generalisation and general abilities. Full fine-tuning uses lower learning rates (around 10⁻⁵) for the same reason in reverse: every weight moves.

13. Evaluating a fine-tune

Evaluate on three fronts. Target task: the metric that matters (field-level exact match, classification F1, human or judge preference), on a held-out set drawn like production traffic. Regressions: a fixed suite of general tasks (instruction following, reasoning, safety refusals) compared against the base model, to catch forgetting. Behaviour: output format validity, length, refusal rate, and calibration of "I don't know" or "not present". Compare against the strongest non-fine-tuned baseline (best prompt plus retrieval), so you know the fine-tune earns its maintenance cost: every base-model upgrade means re-running the fine-tune and the evaluation.

14. A worked project timeline

To make the costs concrete, here is a typical small project: adapting an 8B instruction-tuned model to extract 20 fields from purchase orders. Week one: write the schema, collect and label 3,000 examples (often the slowest part), hold out 500 split by supplier, and measure the baseline: a careful prompt with constrained JSON decoding gets 88% field-level accuracy. Day one of week two: a QLoRA run on one 24 GB GPU, r = 16 on all linear layers, two epochs over 2,500 examples of about 1,500 tokens each, roughly 3.75 million tokens per epoch; at a few thousand tokens per second it finishes in well under an hour. Held-out accuracy rises to 96%, the general regression suite drops by less than a point, and the adapter is 80 MB. The team serves it on a shared base model next to two other adapters. The expensive part was the data, not the GPU time, which is the usual shape of LoRA projects and the reason data quality deserves most of the effort.

15. Other parameter-efficient methods

LoRA is the dominant parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) method, but not the only one. Adapters insert small bottleneck MLP layers between existing ones (they add inference latency unless merged, which they cannot be in general). Prefix and prompt tuning learn virtual tokens prepended to the input, using very few parameters but usually underperforming LoRA on harder tasks. LoRA variants change details: DoRA separates the update's magnitude and direction, others vary rank across layers or initialise A and B from the weight's singular vectors. All share the idea that a small, well-placed set of trainable parameters can steer a large frozen model.

The gradient view: what LoRA can and cannot reach

In full fine-tuning, the gradient for W is Σ δ xᵀ over the batch (output errors times inputs), a sum of rank-one matrices. LoRA constrains the cumulative update to the column space of B and row space of A, which are themselves learned. Early in training, with B = 0, the gradient for B is δ(Ax)ᵀ, so B moves towards the projection of the full gradient onto the r random directions A picks out, and A then rotates towards useful input directions. LoRA therefore follows a low-rank approximation of the full fine-tuning trajectory. When the full update is dominated by a few directions this approximation is good; when the task requires many independent changes, as in large domain shifts, it is not, which matches Biderman et al.'s spectral finding.

Why the adapter is a regulariser

Forgetting happens when fine-tuning changes directions in weight space that other abilities rely on. A full fine-tune can move any direction; a rank-r adapter can move only r per matrix, and those directions are chosen by the target data. Directions the target data never touches cannot change, so abilities that depend on them survive. This is a stronger constraint than weight decay (which shrinks all changes uniformly but allows any direction) and explains why Biderman et al. found LoRA mitigated forgetting more than weight decay or dropout. The same property makes LoRA attractive for sequential customisation: many independent adapters on one base rarely interfere with each other.

NF4 in a little more detail

Pretrained weights within a block are approximately normally distributed around zero. A uniform 4-bit grid wastes levels in the sparse tails and has too few near zero, where most weights are. NF4 places its 16 levels at quantiles of a standard normal distribution, so each level is used about equally often, which minimises expected quantisation error for normal data; the block's absolute-maximum value scales the grid. Because QLoRA only stores the frozen weights in NF4 and computes in bf16, quantisation error affects the forward pass slightly but the adapter can learn to compensate for it during training, one reason QLoRA matches 16-bit LoRA so closely in their experiments.

Activation memory and long sequences

LoRA and QLoRA shrink model state but not activations: the frozen model's forward pass still produces the same activations that backpropagation needs. For long sequences (8K-32K tokens) activations can exceed the 4-14 GB of model state. Gradient checkpointing (recompute activations in the backward pass) cuts them substantially at about one extra forward pass of compute, and FlashAttention (gpu7) removes the quadratic attention matrix from memory. Paged optimisers handle the occasional spike from an unusually long example. Planning memory for fine-tuning means planning for the longest sequence in the dataset, not the average.

Serving many adapters efficiently

Serving one base model with many LoRA adapters in a single batch requires computing, for each request, its own BAx alongside the shared W₀x. Specialised kernels batch these small, request-specific multiplications (each a skinny matrix product) so that a batch mixing dozens of adapters runs nearly as fast as a batch with one. Adapters can be cached on the GPU and swapped from CPU memory on demand. The engineering limits are adapter memory when thousands are active and the extra latency from per-request adapter multiplications; merged models avoid both but give up sharing.

Learning rates, rank and stability

LoRA uses learning rates roughly ten times higher than full fine-tuning because each adapter parameter must carry a larger share of the change and because B starts at zero. Too high a learning rate shows up as loss spikes or a model that quickly overfits the training format. The α/r scaling interacts with this: increasing r with α fixed shrinks the effective step, so practitioners either keep α proportional to r or retune the learning rate when changing rank. Some variants scale by α/√r instead, arguing that this keeps learning stable at high ranks. These details matter mostly when pushing LoRA towards full fine-tuning territory with large ranks; for typical r = 8-64 the defaults in common libraries work.

Fine-tuning, safety and evaluation risk

Fine-tuning can undo safety behaviour learned in post-training: researchers have shown that small amounts of fine-tuning data, even benign-looking data, can reduce refusal of harmful requests. Teams that fine-tune instruction-tuned models should include safety evaluations in the regression suite, and providers that offer fine-tuning APIs screen training data. A related risk is evaluation contamination: if benchmark or test examples leak into fine-tuning data, measured gains are illusory, so data deduplication against evaluation sets is part of the pipeline.

The public case: LoRA (Hu et al., Microsoft, 2021) and QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023)

LoRA's paper set out to make fine-tuning of the largest model then available, GPT-3 175B, practical. Its abstract claims LoRA "can reduce the number of trainable parameters by 10,000 times and the GPU memory requirement by 3 times", performing "on-par or better than fine-tuning in model quality on RoBERTa, DeBERTa, GPT-2, and GPT-3". The concrete numbers: on GPT-3 175B, training VRAM fell from 1.2 TB to 350 GB, because no optimiser state is kept for frozen weights; with r = 4 and only the query and value projections adapted, the checkpoint shrank from 350 GB to 35 MB, roughly 10,000×; and training ran about 25% faster because most gradients need not be computed for weights. They noted the deployment benefit: 100 adapted models need about 354 GB rather than 35 TB, and switching tasks means swapping 35 MB rather than reloading a model. Because the adapter can be merged into the weights, it adds no inference latency, unlike earlier adapter layers.

QLoRA (Dettmers, Pagnoni, Holtzman and Zettlemoyer) pushed memory further: by storing the frozen base in 4-bit NF4 with double quantisation and using paged optimisers, they reduced the memory to fine-tune a 65B model from more than 780 GB to less than 48 GB, a single GPU, "while preserving full 16-bit finetuning task performance". They fine-tuned over 1,000 models to study instruction-tuning data, and their Guanaco family reached 99.3% of ChatGPT's performance level on the Vicuna benchmark (as judged in their evaluation) after 24 hours of fine-tuning on one GPU. They also found that adapting all linear layers was critical to match full fine-tuning, and that dataset quality mattered far more than size. Together the two papers turned fine-tuning from a cluster job into something a single engineer can run on one GPU.

What goes wrong

Fine-tuning for knowledge. A team fine-tunes on product documentation expecting the model to answer product questions; it learns the tone and still hallucinates details. Use retrieval for knowledge, fine-tuning for behaviour.

Over-training and forgetting. Many epochs on a small set drive training loss near zero; general abilities and output diversity drop. Fewer epochs, LoRA, mixed-in general data, and a regression suite.

Template and masking bugs. Training on raw text without the chat template, or computing loss on prompt tokens, produces models that ramble or imitate users. Inspect a few tokenised training examples with the mask before launching.

Adapter-base mismatch. An adapter trained on one base checkpoint applied to another (a newer version, a different quantisation) degrades or breaks. Version adapters with their exact base.

Evaluation leakage. Near-duplicate examples across train and test inflate results. Split by source and deduplicate.

Operational cost of owning a fine-tune

A fine-tune is a model you now maintain. When the base model is upgraded, the adapter does not transfer; the data must be re-run on the new base and the evaluation repeated. Training data must be stored, versioned and governed (it often contains customer information). And every fine-tune is another artefact to serve, monitor and roll back. These costs are why teams keep a strong prompted baseline alive and re-check, at each base-model upgrade, whether the fine-tune is still worth it.

What to track

Training and held-out loss, target-task metrics at each checkpoint, general regression scores against the base, output format validity, response length and refusal rate, and for QLoRA the GPU memory high-water mark per batch to catch long-sequence spikes before they become out-of-memory errors.

Goal: count LoRA parameters for real layer shapes, estimate fine-tuning memory for full, LoRA and QLoRA, then train a LoRA adapter on a toy 64 × 64 layer in NumPy and watch rank decide what it can learn, and verify merging. NumPy only, a few seconds.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# ---- 1. LoRA parameter counts by hand
d_in = d_out = 4096; r = 16
full, lora = d_in * d_out, r * (d_in + d_out)
print(f"one 4096x4096 projection: full {full:,} params, LoRA r={r}: {lora:,} ({lora/full:.2%})")
L, d, dff, kv = 32, 4096, 11008, 4096                      # Llama 2 7B shape
per_layer = {"q": (d, d), "k": (d, kv), "v": (d, kv), "o": (d, d), "gate": (d, dff), "up": (d, dff), "down": (dff, d)}
for targets in (["q", "v"], list(per_layer)):
    n = L * sum(r * (a + b) for k, (a, b) in per_layer.items() if k in targets)
    print(f"  LoRA r={r} on {'+'.join(targets):24s}: {n/1e6:6.1f}M trainable = {n/6.74e9:.3%} of 6.74B")

# ---- 2. memory for fine-tuning a 7B model (bytes per parameter, mixed-precision AdamW)
P = 6.74e9
full_ft = P * (2 + 2 + 4 + 4 + 4)                           # bf16 weights + bf16 grads + fp32 master + Adam m, v
n_lora = 40e6
lora_ft = P * 2 + n_lora * (2 + 2 + 4 + 4 + 4)              # frozen bf16 weights + small trainable set
qlora_ft = P * 0.5 * 1.03 + n_lora * 16                     # ~4-bit weights (+ ~3% quantisation constants)
for name, b in [("full fine-tune", full_ft), ("LoRA (bf16 base)", lora_ft), ("QLoRA (4-bit base)", qlora_ft)]:
    print(f"{name:20s} ~{b/1e9:6.1f} GB before activations")

# ---- 3. LoRA in action: adapt a 'pretrained' layer to a new task whose change is low-rank
n_in, n_out, true_rank = 64, 64, 4
W0 = rng.normal(0, n_in ** -0.5, (n_out, n_in))                       # frozen pretrained weight
delta = rng.normal(size=(n_out, true_rank)) @ rng.normal(size=(true_rank, n_in)) * 0.05
X = rng.normal(size=(2000, n_in)); Y = X @ (W0 + delta).T + 0.01 * rng.normal(size=(2000, n_out))
def train_lora(rank, alpha=None, steps=1500, lr=0.05):
    alpha = alpha or rank
    A = rng.normal(0, 1 / np.sqrt(n_in), (rank, n_in)); B = np.zeros((n_out, rank))   # B = 0: start exactly at W0
    s = alpha / rank
    for t in range(steps):
        idx = rng.integers(0, len(X), 128); x, y = X[idx], Y[idx]
        h = x @ A.T                                      # (batch, r)
        err = x @ W0.T + s * h @ B.T - y                 # prediction error
        gB = s * err.T @ h / len(x); gA = s * (B.T @ err.T) @ x / len(x)
        B -= lr * gB; A -= lr * gA
        if t == 0: first = float((err ** 2).mean())
    loss = float(((X @ (W0 + s * B @ A).T - Y) ** 2).mean())
    return loss, first, s * B @ A
base = float(((X @ W0.T - Y) ** 2).mean())
print(f"\nlayer {n_out}x{n_in} ({n_out*n_in:,} weights); task change has rank {true_rank}; loss with frozen W0: {base:.4f}")
for rank in (1, 2, 4, 8):
    loss, first, dW = train_lora(rank)
    print(f"  LoRA r={rank}: {rank*(n_in+n_out):5d} trainable  first-step loss {first:.4f}  final loss {loss:.5f}  rank(dW)={np.linalg.matrix_rank(dW, tol=1e-6)}")
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(delta)
print("singular values of the true change:", np.round(S[:6], 3), "(only 4 are non-zero)")

# ---- 4. merging: after training, fold the adapter into the weight; no extra inference cost
loss, _, dW = train_lora(4)
x = rng.normal(size=(5, n_in))
print("\nmerged W0 + BA gives identical outputs:", np.allclose(x @ (W0 + dW).T, x @ W0.T + x @ dW.T))

What to look for.

  1. Section 1: 131,072 LoRA parameters (0.78%) for a 4,096 × 4,096 projection at r = 16; 8.4M (0.124%) on q and v of Llama 2 7B; 40.0M (0.593%) on all seven projections.
  2. Section 2: about 108 GB of model state for full fine-tuning, 14 GB for LoRA on a bf16 base and 4 GB for QLoRA, before activations.
  3. Section 3: the frozen layer's loss is 0.68; LoRA with r = 1 and 2 stalls at about 0.40 and 0.21, while r = 4 and r = 8 reach 0.0001, the noise floor, because the true change has rank 4 (four non-zero singular values).
  4. Section 4: the merged weight W₀ + BA gives identical outputs: no inference overhead after merging.
Estimate — can we fine-tune this on our hardware?

You have one 48 GB GPU and want to fine-tune Llama 2 13B (d = 5,120, 40 layers, MLP width 13,824, 13.0B parameters) for a classification-style task. Compare full fine-tuning, LoRA and QLoRA, with LoRA r = 16 on all linear layers.

Answer

LoRA parameters: per layer, four 5,120 × 5,120 projections give 4 × 16 × 10,240 = 655,360; three MLP matrices give 3 × 16 × (5,120 + 13,824) = 909,312; total 1,564,672 per layer × 40 = 62.6M (0.48%).

Full fine-tuning: 13.0B × 16 bytes ≈ 208 GB: impossible on one 48 GB GPU.

LoRA (bf16 base): 26 GB of frozen weights + 62.6M × 16 bytes ≈ 1 GB = 27 GB, leaving about 20 GB for activations: feasible with moderate sequence lengths and gradient checkpointing.

QLoRA: about 13.0B × 0.5 × 1.03 ≈ 6.7 GB + 1 GB ≈ 8 GB, leaving ample room for longer sequences or larger batches, at some speed cost from dequantisation.

Choose LoRA if it fits your sequence lengths (faster steps), QLoRA if memory is tight, and confirm with a held-out evaluation and a regression suite. Also check whether a well-prompted base model plus retrieval already meets the target.

Diagnose — the fine-tune that got worse at everything else

After full fine-tuning a 7B chat model for 5 epochs on 3,000 support transcripts, it answers support questions in the right style but now writes poor code and refuses fewer unsafe requests. What happened and what do you change?

Answer

Catastrophic forgetting and safety drift: a small, narrow dataset trained for many epochs with every weight free to move overwrote directions the general and safety behaviours depended on. Changes: switch to LoRA (which forgets less), cut to 1-2 epochs with a lower learning rate, mix in general instruction and safety data, and add a regression suite (coding, reasoning, safety refusals) gating release. If the base model must stay strong for other uses, serve the fine-tune only for support traffic and route everything else to the base model.
