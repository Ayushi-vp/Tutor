A team wants to run a 70-billion-parameter open model for an internal assistant. In the bf16 format it was released in, its weights take 141 GB, more than the largest single GPU in their cloud account. Two GPUs would do, but they would also need room for the KV cache (lm5), the bill doubles, and each generated token still takes about 40 ms because every step must read all 141 GB of weights from memory. Someone suggests "just quantise it to 4 bits". Suddenly the model is 36 GB, fits on one GPU with room for a large cache, and generates tokens roughly three times faster. Is that too good to be true? What did they give up?

Quantisation, storing numbers with fewer bits, is the single most important lever for the cost of serving LLMs and for running them on modest hardware, from laptops to phones. It is also full of traps. Naively rounding the weights of a large model to 8 bits works; doing the same to its activations destroys it, because of a few huge "outlier" values that appear in models above a certain size. Rounding to 4 bits works only with care. Speed-ups appear in some regimes and vanish in others. And the accuracy cost, usually small, can be concentrated in exactly the abilities your product needs.

For an AI engineer the questions are concrete and frequent: how much memory does this model need in each format; will it fit; how fast will it decode; which method should we use; how do we check we did not break it? Interviewers love the memory arithmetic because it is quick to compute and reveals immediately whether a candidate understands where inference cost comes from.

This lesson builds quantisation from scratch: integer codes and scales worked by hand at 8 and 4 bits, why one outlier ruins a shared scale and how per-channel and per-group scales fix it, the memory and speed arithmetic for 7B and 70B models, then the three landmark methods: LLM.int8()'s outlier decomposition, GPTQ's error compensation and AWQ's salient-channel scaling. It covers activation and KV-cache quantisation, formats such as FP8 and NF4, and how to evaluate a quantised model. The lab implements all of it in NumPy on a layer with realistic outliers.

Fewer bits per number

A model's weights are usually stored as 16-bit floating-point numbers (bf16, 2 bytes each). Quantisation replaces each weight with a small integer code, 8 bits (256 possible values) or 4 bits (16 values), plus a shared scale: the real-valued size of one step between codes. To use a weight, multiply its code by the scale. The model now needs a half or a quarter of the memory, and every time it reads its weights, which it does once per generated token, it moves a half or a quarter of the bytes.

An analogy: measuring with a coarser ruler

Imagine recording the heights of everyone in a building with a ruler marked only every 10 cm instead of every millimetre. Each height is now one of a few dozen marks, so the list is much shorter to write down, and for most purposes (who is taller than whom, roughly how tall) it is still useful. The ruler's range must cover the tallest person; if one visitor is 5 metres tall, you would need a ruler 5 metres long with the same number of marks, so marks would be 50 cm apart and everyone else's height would round to the same two or three marks. That is the outlier problem in one picture, and much of modern quantisation is about rulers per group of people (per-group scales), measuring the giants separately (mixed precision), or bending the ruler (non-uniform formats). The analogy breaks in that a model's errors compound: a slightly wrong weight feeds into the next layer, and a few hundred layers of small errors can add up, which is why careful methods matter more at 4 bits than at 8.

Two moments when quantisation happens

Quantisation can be done after training or during it. Post-training quantisation takes a finished model and converts it, sometimes using a few hundred example inputs ("calibration data") to choose scales well; it takes minutes to hours and is how almost every quantised model you download was made. Quantisation-aware training simulates low precision while training or fine-tuning so the model adapts to it; it costs training compute but works at lower bit widths. This lesson focuses on post-training methods, because that is what engineers deploying existing models use. There is also a choice about what to quantise: the weights only, the activations flowing between layers as well, or the KV cache. Each has a different payoff and a different difficulty, and section 3 of the body compares them.

1. Integer codes and scales

The simplest scheme is symmetric absmax quantisation. For b bits, the integer codes run from −(2^(b−1) − 1) to 2^(b−1) − 1: −127 to 127 for int8, −7 to 7 for int4 (one code is left unused to keep the range symmetric). Choose the scale so that the largest absolute value in the group maps to the largest code: s = max|x| / (2^(b−1) − 1). Quantise by q = round(x / s) and dequantise by x̂ = q · s.

Worked, int8: x = [0.5, −1.2, 3.0, 0.1]. The largest magnitude is 3.0, so s = 3.0/127 = 0.02362. Divide and round: 0.5/0.02362 = 21.2 → 21; −1.2/0.02362 = −50.8 → −51; 3.0 → 127; 0.1/0.02362 = 4.2 → 4. Codes [21, −51, 127, 4]. Dequantise: [0.496, −1.205, 3.000, 0.094]. Errors of 0.004 to 0.006, about 0.2% of the range: invisible in practice. Worked, int4: s = 3.0/7 = 0.4286; codes round([1.17, −2.80, 7, 0.23]) = [1, −3, 7, 0]; dequantised [0.429, −1.286, 3.0, 0]. Errors up to 0.1, and the 0.1 has vanished entirely. With only 15 levels, each step is a large fraction of the range, so how the levels are placed and how the scale is chosen matter much more.

Asymmetric quantisation adds a zero point so the codes cover [min, max] rather than a symmetric range, useful for values that are mostly positive (such as outputs of a ReLU). Weights of LLMs are roughly symmetric around zero, so symmetric schemes are common for weights.

2. Granularity: one scale for how many numbers?

A scale is shared by a group of numbers, and the choice of group is the main accuracy lever. Per-tensor: one scale for a whole weight matrix; cheapest, but the single largest value anywhere sets the step for everyone. Per-channel: one scale per output row; rows differ in range, and adapting to each costs almost nothing (4,096 scales for a 4,096 × 4,096 matrix). Standard for int8 weights. Per-group: one scale per block of consecutive weights within a row, typically 32, 64 or 128; needed for good 4-bit accuracy. The cost is overhead: a 16-bit scale per 128 weights adds 16/128 = 0.125 bits per weight; per 32 weights, 0.5 bits. A "4-bit" model with group size 128 actually stores about 4.125 bits per weight, or about 0.52 bytes.

The lab measures the effect on a vector of 256 normal values with one outlier of 60. int8 with one scale for all 256: RMS error 0.135. int8 with groups of 32: 0.047. int4 with one scale: 1.01, which is as large as the values themselves, so the vector is destroyed; int4 with groups of 32: 0.30. The outlier only damages the 31 values that share its group.

3. What gets quantised: weights, activations, KV cache

Three kinds of numbers can be stored in low precision, with different consequences. Weights are fixed after training, so they can be quantised once, offline, with as much care (and calibration data) as you like. Activations, the inputs to each matrix multiplication, change with every input, so they must be quantised on the fly with cheap rules, and they contain the outliers discussed below. The KV cache (lm5) stores keys and values for every past token; quantising it to 8 bits halves the memory that limits batch size and context length. Notation: W4A16 means 4-bit weights, 16-bit activations (weight-only quantisation); W8A8 means both in 8 bits.

Weight-only quantisation saves memory and speeds up memory-bound decoding: the kernel reads 4-bit weights, dequantises them in fast on-chip memory, and multiplies in 16-bit. Weight-and-activation quantisation lets the multiplication itself run on low-precision tensor cores, which doubles or quadruples peak throughput on GPUs that support it (gpu4), helping compute-bound prefill and large batches.

4. The memory arithmetic, in full

Serving memory = weights + KV cache + activations and overhead. For Llama 2 70B (about 70.6B parameters, 80 layers, 8 KV heads, head dimension 128):

On one 80 GB GPU, bf16 does not fit at all; int8 fits with only a few GB left for cache; int4 leaves about 35-40 GB for cache and activations, enough for dozens of concurrent 4K-token sequences with an 8-bit cache. This arithmetic, done before choosing hardware, prevents most capacity surprises.

5. The speed arithmetic

At batch size 1, each decode step reads every weight once and performs about 2 FLOPs per weight, far below what a GPU can do per byte (gpu2), so time per token ≈ bytes of weights ÷ memory bandwidth. On an H100 (3.35 TB/s): 70B in int4, 36 GB → about 11 ms per token at peak bandwidth; in bf16 across two GPUs with tensor parallelism, 70.5 GB per GPU → about 21 ms plus communication. Real kernels reach perhaps 60-80% of peak bandwidth. GPTQ's authors measured end-to-end speed-ups of about 3.25× on A100 GPUs and 4.5× on A6000 GPUs over fp16 for their 3-4-bit 175B models, the memory-bound regime at work. As the batch grows, weight reads are shared across sequences and the step becomes compute- or KV-bound, so weight-only quantisation's speed advantage shrinks; then W8A8 or FP8 compute and an 8-bit KV cache are the levers.

6. Outlier features

Dettmers et al. studied the hidden states of transformers as they scaled and found something surprising: a few feature dimensions carry values up to 20 times larger than the rest, and they are systematic, the same dimensions across tokens. In smaller models they appear in some layers; in their analysis, outliers first appear in about 25% of layers and then spread, and at around 6.7B parameters a "phase shift" occurs: all layers and about 75% of sequence positions are affected. They are highly concentrated: at the 6.7B scale, about 150,000 outlier values occur per sequence but in only 6 feature dimensions across the entire transformer. And they matter: setting those dimensions to zero reduced the top-1 attention softmax probability mass by more than 20% and degraded validation perplexity by 600-1000%, although they are about 0.1% of all input features, while removing the same number of random features had little effect. Their consequence for quantisation is the ruler problem: quantise an activation row with one absmax scale and the outlier dimensions stretch it, leaving the other values with a handful of usable levels. Regular 8-bit methods that worked on small models failed on larger ones exactly at this scale.

7. LLM.int8(): compute the outliers separately

LLM.int8() handles this with a mixed-precision decomposition. For a matrix multiplication XWᵀ, it finds the input feature dimensions where any activation exceeds a threshold (6.0 in the paper), and splits the computation: those few columns of X (and the matching columns of W) are multiplied in 16-bit; everything else is quantised to int8 with per-row (vector-wise) scales for activations and per-column scales for weights, multiplied in int8 and dequantised; the two partial results are added. More than 99.9% of values go through the 8-bit path. The result preserved full 16-bit accuracy for models up to 175B parameters, which could then be loaded from 16-bit checkpoints, converted and used immediately on half the memory. In the lab, a layer with 6 outlier input channels out of 512: quantising activations to int8 per token gives 3.4% relative output error; keeping the outlier columns (1.2% of channels) in 16-bit cuts it to 0.8%.

8. GPTQ: compensate rounding errors

Rounding each weight to its nearest code independently (round-to-nearest, RTN) ignores that errors in different weights can cancel or compound. GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2022) quantises a layer's weight matrix one input column at a time. After rounding column i, it computes the error that rounding introduced and adjusts all not-yet-quantised columns to compensate, so that the layer's output on calibration inputs changes as little as possible. How much to adjust each remaining column comes from the inverse of the Hessian H = XᵀX of the layer's calibration inputs: if input features i and j are correlated, an error in weight i can be partly absorbed by changing weight j. The algorithm processes columns in order, uses a Cholesky factorisation of H⁻¹ for numerical stability, and batches updates for speed. It quantised 175B-parameter models to 3-4 bits per weight in about four GPU hours with negligible accuracy loss, more than doubling the compression of earlier one-shot methods, and made it possible to run a 175B model on a single GPU for generation.

The lab implements a compact version of the update. On a 512 × 512 layer whose inputs are correlated (as real activations are) and have outlier channels, int4 RTN with groups of 128 gives 11.7% relative output error; GPTQ-style compensation gives 3.8%, three times lower, using only 1,024 calibration vectors. The improvement depends on correlation: with independent inputs there is little room to shift error and GPTQ approaches RTN.

9. AWQ: protect the salient channels

AWQ (activation-aware weight quantisation, Lin et al., 2023) starts from an observation: not all weights matter equally, and protecting only about 1% of salient weight channels greatly reduces quantisation error. Which channels are salient is decided by the activations, not the weights: an input channel that regularly carries large values amplifies any error in the weights that multiply it. Keeping those channels in 16-bit would need awkward mixed-precision kernels, so AWQ instead multiplies the salient input channels of W by a factor s > 1 before quantising (so their weights use more of the integer range and get relatively finer steps) and divides the corresponding activations by s at run time, which can be folded into the previous layer. In full precision this is an identity; under quantisation it shifts precision towards the weights that matter. Scales are chosen from activation statistics on a small calibration set, with no backpropagation or reconstruction, which makes AWQ fast and robust to the choice of calibration data. In the lab, a simple version (s proportional to the square root of mean activation magnitude) reduces int4 error from 11.7% to 9.8%; the real method searches the scaling exponent per layer. The AWQ authors' TinyChat inference framework reported more than 3× speed-up over a 16-bit Hugging Face implementation on desktop and mobile GPUs.

10. SmoothQuant and W8A8

To quantise activations as well as weights to 8 bits without LLM.int8()'s mixed precision, SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2022) applies the same mathematical trick in the opposite direction: divide each activation channel by a factor and multiply the matching weight rows by it, choosing factors that "smooth" the activation outliers by moving part of their magnitude into the weights, which are easier to quantise. Both can then be int8, and the matrix multiplication runs on int8 tensor cores. This W8A8 path, and its floating-point cousin FP8, is the common choice for high-throughput serving on hardware with fast 8-bit units.

11. Formats beyond integers

Integers space their levels evenly. Weights are roughly normally distributed, with most values near zero, so evenly spaced levels waste resolution in the tails. NF4 (used by QLoRA, lm11) places its 16 levels at quantiles of a normal distribution, so each level is used about equally often. FP8 formats (E4M3 and E5M2, gpu4) give each number its own exponent, providing far more range than int8 at the same byte, which tolerates outliers better; recent GPUs compute in FP8 natively, making it attractive for activations and KV caches. 4-bit floating-point formats with small per-block scales (for example the microscaling formats on the newest GPUs) extend the same idea. Local-inference tools such as llama.cpp's GGUF format use a family of block-wise schemes ("k-quants") that mix bit widths across layers. The principle is constant: spend bits where the values and the errors matter.

12. A worked comparison on one layer

The lab's layer shows how much the method matters at 4 bits. The same 512 × 512 weight matrix, the same group size of 128, the same calibration inputs: round-to-nearest leaves 11.7% relative error in the layer's output; AWQ-style scaling, which only changes which weights get finer steps, cuts it to 9.8%; GPTQ-style compensation, which uses the input correlations to cancel errors, cuts it to 3.8%. For comparison, int8 per-channel weights give 0.75%. Errors at this level in a single layer compound through dozens of layers, which is why naive 4-bit models are noticeably worse while GPTQ and AWQ models are close to the original. The ordering in a real model depends on its activations, and the published methods are more refined than the lab's versions, but the lesson holds: at 8 bits, almost any sensible method works; at 4 bits, the method is a large part of the quality.

13. Evaluating a quantised model

Quantisation is lossy, so it must be evaluated, and the headline benchmark is not enough. Measure perplexity on held-out text from your domain (a sensitive, cheap signal: increases of a few percent are typical at 4 bits for large models). Run task evaluations that matter for the product: accuracy, long-context retrieval, code, maths, multilingual inputs, and compare outputs of the 16-bit and quantised models on a sample of real prompts. Check speed with the actual serving engine and batch sizes, because speed depends on kernel support for the exact format. If a scheme hurts a capability, options include a larger group size or smaller one, a better method (GPTQ or AWQ instead of RTN), 8 bits instead of 4, keeping sensitive layers (often the first and last, embeddings and LM head) in higher precision, or quantisation-aware fine-tuning.

Why weights tolerate quantisation

Quantisation adds a small, roughly random error ε to each weight. A layer's output y = Wx then changes by εx, a sum of many independent small terms, whose size grows only with the square root of the number of terms while the signal grows linearly with it. Large layers therefore average quantisation noise away relative to their outputs, and large models have more redundancy (many weights and many paths can carry the same information). That is why 8-bit weights are nearly lossless and 4-bit weights are acceptable for large models, while small models, with less redundancy, lose more. The averaging argument fails for systematic errors, such as those caused by outliers or by consistently biased rounding, which is what careful methods target.

The GPTQ update, derived in one line

For a single output row w and calibration inputs X, the output error from changing w by δ is ‖Xδ‖² = δᵀHδ with H = XᵀX. If column q is forced to its quantised value (changing it by e_q), the optimal adjustment of the remaining weights to minimise δᵀHδ is δ = −e_q · H⁻¹[:, q] / H⁻¹[q, q], the classic optimal-brain-surgeon result. Applying it column by column, with H⁻¹ updated as columns are fixed, is OBQ; GPTQ's contributions were to quantise columns in a fixed order for all rows at once (so H⁻¹ is shared across rows), process them in blocks for GPU efficiency, and use a Cholesky decomposition of H⁻¹ for numerical stability. The lab's loop is exactly this with the Cholesky factor U: e = (w_i − q_i)/U[i,i], then w[:, i:] −= e·U[i, i:].

Where outliers come from

Why would a trained network concentrate huge values in a few dimensions? One account connects outliers to attention sinks and "no-op" behaviour (lm2): to let an attention head do nothing, the model routes large values through specific dimensions so that softmax puts weight on a sink token, and normalisation layers (lm3) with learned per-dimension scales amplify them. Whatever the cause, they are consistent across inputs, which is what makes them detectable from calibration data and handleable by per-channel methods. Training-side remedies (modified attention or normalisation, or rotating the hidden space with an orthogonal matrix before quantisation, which spreads outliers across all dimensions) are active research and some are used in practice.

Kernels decide the real speed

A quantised model is only fast if a kernel exists that reads the packed low-bit format, dequantises it in registers or shared memory, and feeds tensor cores without extra trips to main memory (gpu6 explains fusion). Without such kernels, a framework may dequantise whole matrices to 16-bit in memory before multiplying, which costs more bandwidth than it saves. Speed also depends on batch size: weight-only kernels shine at batch 1-16 and can lose to plain 16-bit at large batches, where dequantisation overhead lands on a compute-bound path. Always benchmark the exact format, engine and batch size you will deploy.

Rotation tricks

Outliers are a property of the basis: they live in a handful of coordinates. Multiplying the hidden states by a random orthogonal matrix (and the adjacent weights by its transpose, so the network computes the same function) spreads each outlier's energy across all dimensions, leaving a distribution with no extreme coordinates, which quantises well with simple per-token scales. Recent methods apply fixed Hadamard rotations or learned rotations at points where they can be folded into existing weights, making 4-bit weights and activations (W4A4) and 4-bit KV caches more accurate. The idea is the same as SmoothQuant's and AWQ's: transform the problem so that the numbers to be quantised are well-behaved, using operations that cancel exactly in full precision.

Quantisation-aware training and low-bit frontiers

Post-training methods hit diminishing returns below about 4 bits. Quantisation-aware training simulates quantisation in the forward pass during training or fine-tuning (using a straight-through gradient estimator for the rounding step) so the model learns weights that survive it; it recovers much of the loss at 2-3 bits but costs training compute. Research models trained natively with ternary weights (−1, 0, 1) suggest that very low precision is possible when designed in from the start. For engineers deploying existing models, the practical frontier remains 4-bit weights with good post-training methods, 8-bit or FP8 activations and caches, and 16-bit where sensitivity demands it.

The public case: LLM.int8() (Dettmers et al., 2022), then GPTQ and AWQ

In 2022 the largest open models, such as OPT-175B and BLOOM-176B, needed about 350 GB in 16-bit, a multi-GPU server just to load. Existing 8-bit methods worked on small models but failed on large ones, and nobody knew why. Dettmers, Lewis, Belkada and Zettlemoyer traced the failure to emergent outlier features: dimensions with magnitudes up to 20× larger than others, which first appear in about 25% of transformer layers and, around 6.7B parameters, undergo a phase shift to affect all layers and 75% of sequence positions. Regular quantisation, they showed, fails exactly where these appear. Their LLM.int8() method uses vector-wise quantisation plus a mixed-precision decomposition that multiplies the outlier dimensions (magnitude above 6.0) in 16-bit while more than 99.9% of values are multiplied in 8-bit. A 175B model could be loaded from a 16/32-bit checkpoint, converted to int8 and used immediately without performance degradation, halving memory and making OPT-175B and BLOOM usable on a single server with consumer GPUs. The method shipped in the open-source bitsandbytes library and became the standard 8-bit loader.

GPTQ (Frantar, Ashkboos, Hoefler and Alistarh) went further on weights: one-shot quantisation using approximate second-order information, quantising 175B models to 3 or 4 bits per weight in about four GPU hours with negligible accuracy degradation, enabling generation with a 175B model on a single GPU and inference speed-ups of about 3.25× on A100 and 4.5× on A6000 GPUs over FP16. AWQ (Lin et al.) showed that protecting about 1% of salient weight channels, chosen by activation magnitude and protected by scaling rather than mixed precision, gives strong 4-bit quality without backpropagation, and its TinyChat framework reported more than 3× speed-up over a 16-bit implementation and deployment of Llama 2 70B on mobile GPUs. Together these three papers are why "download a 4-bit version" is now a routine step.

Failure modes in production

No speed-up, or a slow-down. A quantised model running without fused kernels, or at large batch with weight-only quantisation. Benchmark with the deployment engine at realistic batch sizes before switching.

Localised quality loss. Perplexity barely moves but a specific capability (code, maths, a language, long context) degrades. Evaluate on task suites that cover your traffic and compare outputs side by side.

Calibration mismatch. GPTQ calibrated on generic English text applied to a code or multilingual workload performs worse than expected. Calibrate on representative data.

Format and engine mismatch. A checkpoint quantised for one library loads but runs dequantised in another, or a scale convention differs. Use formats your serving engine supports natively.

KV-cache quantisation surprises. 8-bit caches are usually safe, but some models' keys have outlier channels that need per-channel scales; check long-context tasks after enabling.

A typical rollout

A sensible sequence for a team moving a 70B model to 4-bit: produce GPTQ and AWQ variants with calibration data drawn from production prompts; compare perplexity on a domain sample and the task evaluation suite against the bf16 model; run a side-by-side human or judge comparison on a few hundred real prompts; benchmark throughput and latency with the production engine at expected batch sizes; then canary the quantised model on a small share of traffic, watching user feedback and task metrics before switching fully. Keeping the bf16 model available for rollback is cheap insurance.

What to monitor

Tokens per second and latency per token at production batch sizes, GPU memory headroom for KV cache, perplexity on a fixed domain sample, task metrics against the 16-bit reference, and output-diff rates on a canary set of prompts when changing quantisation settings.

Goal: quantise by hand at 8 and 4 bits, see an outlier ruin a shared scale, then quantise a realistic layer (correlated inputs with six outlier channels) with round-to-nearest, AWQ-style scaling and a GPTQ-style column-by-column update, and reproduce LLM.int8()'s mixed-precision trick. import numpy as np
np.set_printoptions(precision=3, suppress=True)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def quant(x, bits, axis=None):
    """Symmetric absmax quantisation; returns the de-quantised values (what the matmul effectively uses)."""
    qmax = 2 ** (bits - 1) - 1
    s = np.abs(x).max(axis=axis, keepdims=axis is not None) / qmax
    s = np.where(s == 0, 1, s)
    return np.clip(np.round(x / s), -qmax, qmax) * s

# ---- 1. by hand
x = np.array([0.5, -1.2, 3.0, 0.1])
for bits in (8, 4):
    qmax = 2 ** (bits - 1) - 1; s = 3.0 / qmax
    print(f"int{bits}: scale {s:.4f}, codes {np.round(x / s).astype(int)}, back {quant(x, bits)}, error {quant(x, bits) - x}")

# ---- 2. one outlier ruins a shared scale; smaller groups contain the damage
v = rng.normal(0, 1, 256); v[17] = 60.0                                  # one outlier, like LLM.int8()'s features
err = lambda a, b: np.sqrt(np.mean((a - b) ** 2))
print(f"\nint8 RMS error, one scale for 256 values: {err(quant(v, 8), v):.4f}")
print(f"int8 RMS error, groups of 32:             {err(quant(v.reshape(-1, 32), 8, axis=1).ravel(), v):.4f}")
print(f"int4 RMS error, one scale / groups of 32: {err(quant(v, 4), v):.4f} / {err(quant(v.reshape(-1, 32), 4, axis=1).ravel(), v):.4f}")

# ---- 3. a layer: activations with a few outlier channels, weights normal
n_in, n_out, n_tok = 512, 512, 1024
W = rng.normal(0, n_in ** -0.5, (n_out, n_in))
X = rng.normal(size=(n_tok, 64)) @ rng.normal(0, 0.125, (64, n_in)) + 0.3 * rng.normal(size=(n_tok, n_in))   # correlated features, like real activations
outl = rng.choice(n_in, 6, replace=False); X[:, outl] *= 20
Y = X @ W.T
rel = lambda Yq: np.linalg.norm(Yq - Y) / np.linalg.norm(Y)
def group_quant(W, bits, g=128):                                         # per output row, groups of g inputs
    return quant(W.reshape(n_out, -1, g), bits, axis=2).reshape(n_out, n_in)
print("\nrelative output error of the layer (weights quantised, activations exact):")
print(f"  int8 per output channel        {rel(X @ quant(W, 8, axis=1).T):.4f}")
print(f"  int4 round-to-nearest, g=128   {rel(X @ group_quant(W, 4).T):.4f}")

# AWQ-style: scale up weight columns that meet large activations, quantise, fold the inverse scale into x
a = np.abs(X).mean(0); s = (a / a.mean()) ** 0.5
print(f"  int4 AWQ-style scaling, g=128  {rel((X / s) @ group_quant(W * s, 4).T):.4f}")

# GPTQ-style: quantise one input column at a time, push its error onto the not-yet-quantised columns
def gptq(W, X, bits, g=128):
    W = W.copy(); Q = np.zeros_like(W)
    H = X.T @ X / len(X); H += 0.01 * np.mean(np.diag(H)) * np.eye(n_in)
    U = np.linalg.cholesky(np.linalg.inv(H)).T                            # upper Cholesky factor of H^-1
    qmax = 2 ** (bits - 1) - 1
    for i in range(n_in):
        if i % g == 0:                                                    # group scale from the (updated) weights
            scale = np.abs(W[:, i:i + g]).max(1) / qmax
        q = np.clip(np.round(W[:, i] / scale), -qmax, qmax) * scale
        e = (W[:, i] - q) / U[i, i]
        W[:, i:] -= np.outer(e, U[i, i:]); Q[:, i] = q
    return Q
print(f"  int4 GPTQ-style, g=128         {rel(X @ gptq(W, X, 4).T):.4f}")

# ---- 4. LLM.int8(): quantising activations too; keep outlier feature columns in 16-bit
Xq_naive = quant(X, 8, axis=1)                                           # per-token int8
big = np.abs(X).max(0) > 6.0
X_mixed = np.where(big, X, quant(np.where(big, 0, X), 8, axis=1))
Wq = quant(W, 8, axis=1)
print(f"\nint8 weights and activations, per-token scales: {rel(Xq_naive @ Wq.T):.4f}")
print(f"same, outlier columns kept in 16-bit ({big.sum()} of {n_in} = {big.mean():.1%}): {rel(X_mixed @ Wq.T):.4f}")

# ---- 5. memory
for name, P in [("7B", 6.74e9), ("70B", 70.6e9)]:
    row = ", ".join(f"{fmt} {P * b / 1e9:6.1f} GB" for fmt, b in [("bf16", 2), ("int8", 1), ("int4+g128 scales", 0.5 + 2 / 128)])
    print(f"{name:4s}: {row}")

What to look for.

  1. Section 1: int8 codes [21, −51, 127, 4] with errors under 0.01; int4 codes [1, −3, 7, 0] with errors up to 0.1.
  2. Section 2: one outlier of 60 among 256 values: int8 RMS error 0.135 with one scale, 0.047 with groups of 32; int4 1.01 (destroyed) versus 0.30.
  3. Section 3: relative output error of a 512 × 512 layer: int8 per channel 0.0075; int4 round-to-nearest 0.117; AWQ-style 0.098; GPTQ-style 0.038.
  4. Section 4: int8 weights and activations 0.034, falling to 0.008 when the 6 outlier columns (1.2%) stay in 16-bit.
  5. Section 5: 13.5 / 6.7 / 3.5 GB for 7B and 141 / 71 / 36 GB for 70B in bf16 / int8 / int4 with group-128 scales.
Estimate — does it fit, and how fast?

You must serve a 34B model (48 layers, 8 KV heads, head dimension 128) on one 48 GB GPU with 0.96 TB/s of memory bandwidth, for up to 8 concurrent users with 4,096-token contexts. Which weight and cache formats work, and what time per token should you expect?

Answer

Weights: bf16 68 GB (no), int8 34 GB, int4 (g=128) about 17.6 GB.

KV cache: 2 × 48 × 8 × 128 × 2 bytes = 192 KiB per token in bf16; 8 users × 4,096 tokens = 32,768 tokens → 6 GiB in bf16, 3 GiB in 8-bit.

Fit: int8 weights (34 GB) + bf16 cache (6.4 GB) + a few GB overhead ≈ 42-44 GB: tight but possible; int4 weights + 8-bit cache ≈ 21 GB leaves ample headroom (room for more users or longer context).

Speed at small batch: each step reads weights plus caches. int8: (34 + 6.4) GB / 0.96 TB/s ≈ 42 ms at peak, perhaps 55-65 ms in practice, about 16-18 tokens per second per user. int4 + 8-bit cache: (17.6 + 3.2) / 0.96 ≈ 22 ms, perhaps 30 ms in practice, about 33 tokens per second. Choose int4 (GPTQ or AWQ) if evaluation on your tasks shows acceptable quality; otherwise int8 with the tighter memory budget.

Diagnose — the 8-bit model that falls apart

A colleague quantises both weights and activations of a 13B model to int8 with one scale per tensor. Small models quantised the same way were fine; this one produces gibberish. Why, and what are the fixes?

Answer

Emergent outlier features: models above roughly 6.7B parameters have a few activation dimensions with values up to 20× larger than the rest, in all layers. A per-tensor (or even per-token) int8 scale is stretched by them, leaving ordinary activations with a handful of levels, so outputs degrade catastrophically. Fixes: keep weight quantisation per channel and quantise activations with outlier handling, either LLM.int8()'s mixed-precision decomposition (outlier columns in 16-bit), SmoothQuant (migrate the outliers into the weights, then W8A8), or FP8 activations, whose exponent gives the range; or use weight-only int8/int4 and keep activations in 16-bit.
