A team changes the system prompt of their support assistant. Someone tries ten questions, the answers look better, and the change ships. A week later escalations to human agents are up 15%. The new prompt made answers friendlier and longer, which looked better on the ten questions, but it also made the assistant more willing to guess at refund rules it did not know. Nobody measured that, because nobody had a way to measure anything beyond "try a few and see". Every LLM team goes through some version of this, and the ones that improve steadily are the ones that build evaluation early.

Evaluating LLM systems is genuinely harder than evaluating a classifier. There is rarely one correct output; a right answer can be phrased a hundred ways; quality has several dimensions that trade off (correct, complete, concise, safe, in the right format); results change with the prompt, the retrieved documents and the decoding settings; and public benchmarks, the obvious shortcut, may have leaked into the model's training data or may not resemble your users' questions at all. Meanwhile the cheapest scalable evaluator, another LLM acting as a judge, has its own biases.

The result is that "we evaluated it" can mean anything from rigorous to theatre. For an AI engineer, being able to design an evaluation that answers a decision question, "is this change better, by how much, and are we sure?", is one of the most valuable skills there is, and interviewers probe it hard: how big should the eval set be, how do you compare two prompts, how do you trust an LLM judge, what is contamination?

Evaluation is also how teams move fast safely. With a trusted suite, a prompt change or a model upgrade can be tested in an hour and shipped with confidence; without one, every change is a gamble, so teams either freeze or break things. Investment in evaluation pays back in speed.

This lesson covers building eval sets from real traffic, choosing metrics by output type, the statistics of comparing systems (confidence intervals and paired comparisons worked by hand), LLM judges with their measured biases and how to control them, contamination of benchmarks, and how offline evaluation connects to online experiments. The public case is the MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena study of LLM judges, with the GSM1k contamination study alongside. The lab simulates all of it in NumPy so you can see noise, bias and contamination with your own eyes.

An eval is a test suite for behaviour

Software engineers already know the core idea: a test suite. An eval set is a fixed collection of inputs, each with a way to decide whether the output is good: an expected answer, a set of checks, or a rubric for a grader. You run the system over it, compute metrics, and compare against the last version. The differences from unit tests are that outputs are fuzzy (so grading needs judgement), systems are stochastic (so scores have noise), and the set must represent what real users do (so it is built from data, not from imagination).

An analogy: tasting a recipe

A chef changing a recipe does not ask one friend whether it tastes better. They taste it on the dishes customers actually order, compare old and new side by side, ask several tasters who do not know which is which, and check specific things (is it too salty?) as well as the overall impression. Evaluation of LLM systems has the same ingredients: realistic test cases, paired comparison, multiple and blinded graders, specific criteria, and enough tastings that one fluke does not decide. The analogy breaks in that tasters are expensive and LLM graders are cheap but opinionated in odd ways, such as preferring whichever dish was served first, which is why much of this lesson is about checking the graders.

What can go wrong with a number

Every evaluation produces numbers, and there are four classic ways a number can mislead. It can be noisy: measured on too few examples, so a different sample would have given a different answer. It can be biased: produced by a grader that systematically prefers something irrelevant, such as longer answers. It can be unrepresentative: measured on inputs unlike what users send, so it is precise about the wrong thing. And it can be contaminated: the system has seen the test items before, so it is measuring memory instead of skill. Each has a remedy you will meet in this lesson: enough items and paired statistics for noise; validated, bias-controlled graders for bias; eval sets drawn from real traffic for representativeness; and private, fresh or perturbed items for contamination. When you read anyone's evaluation, including your own, ask which of the four it has ruled out.

Three kinds of grader

Outputs are graded in one of three ways. Programmatic checks compare against a known answer or run a test: exact match, a JSON schema validator, unit tests for code. They are cheap, fast and objective, but only work when correctness is checkable by rule. Human graders handle anything, including subtle quality judgements, but are slow, expensive and disagree with each other. Model graders (LLM judges) sit in between: nearly as flexible as humans, nearly as cheap as code, but with their own biases that must be measured. Good evaluation uses programmatic checks wherever possible, model graders for the rest, and humans to calibrate the model graders.

Layers of evaluation

The rest of the lesson goes through these layers, with the statistics that tell you when a difference is real.

1. Start from the decision

An evaluation exists to support a decision: ship this prompt or not, choose model X or Y, is the fine-tune worth maintaining, did the provider's model update break us? Writing the decision down first determines what to measure and how precisely. "Is the new model at least as good on refund questions and not worse on safety?" leads to a specific eval set, specific metrics and a non-inferiority comparison; "is it better?" with no definition leads to vibes. Each decision also implies a minimum detectable difference: if switching models saves 40% of cost, you may accept a 1-point quality drop but need to be confident it is not 5.

2. Building the eval set

The best eval sets are built from reality. Pull inputs from production logs or a beta, grouped by intent; add edge cases domain experts know about (ambiguous questions, adversarial phrasing, inputs in other languages, very long inputs); and add every failure found in testing or production, so each becomes a regression case. For each item, write what a good output must do: an expected answer, required facts, prohibited content, a format. Start small, 50-200 well-chosen items labelled carefully, rather than thousands of synthetic ones; synthetic generation (asking an LLM to write test questions) helps fill coverage gaps but tends to produce cleaner, easier inputs than real users send. Keep the set versioned, and keep it out of anything the model is trained or prompted on.

3. Metrics by output type

The metric must suit the output. Short factual answers: exact match after normalisation (lower-casing, stripping punctuation and articles) or answer extraction, sometimes token-level F1 for partial credit. Classification and extraction: accuracy, precision, recall and F1 per class or per field (ml7), and schema validity for structured output. Code: execution against tests; pass@1 for a single attempt and pass@k for the probability that one of k samples passes (lm8). Retrieval: recall@k and MRR (lm9). Open-ended text (summaries, explanations, chat): rubric-based grading by humans or an LLM judge, on criteria such as correctness, completeness, faithfulness to sources, concision and tone; overlap metrics such as BLEU or ROUGE correlate poorly with quality for modern generative outputs and are mostly used for translation and as rough signals. Safety: refusal rate on harmful requests and over-refusal rate on benign-but-edgy ones, both, because improving one often worsens the other. Cost and latency are metrics too, and every comparison should report them.

4. Noise: how precise is a score?

An accuracy measured on n items is an estimate. If the true rate is p, the standard error is √(p(1 − p)/n). Worked: 82% on 500 items gives √(0.82 × 0.18/500) = √0.000295 = 0.0172, so a 95% confidence interval of about ±1.96 × 1.72 = ±3.4 points. Turned around, to measure an 80% accuracy to within ±3 points you need n = (1.96/0.03)² × 0.8 × 0.2 ≈ 683 items; ±1 point needs about 6,100; ±5 points about 250. Two further sources of noise add to this: sampling randomness (if temperature is above 0, the same item can pass or fail on different runs, so run several samples per item and average) and grader noise (human or judge disagreement). Report intervals, not bare numbers.

5. Comparing two systems: pair them

When comparing prompt A with prompt B, run both on the same items and look at the pairs. Items both get right or both get wrong say nothing about which is better; only the discordant items, where one is right and the other wrong, carry information. In the lab, two variants on 500 items score 82.6% and 82.0%, and they disagree on 75 items: 39 won by A, 36 by B. With 75 discordant items split 39/36, there is no evidence either is better (McNemar's test, which looks only at those 75, agrees). The lab's variant B was in fact constructed to be about 2 points better in expectation, which shows how easily a real small improvement is invisible, or even reversed, at this sample size.

Pairing also makes the comparison more precise. Because items differ in difficulty, the two systems' errors are correlated; analysing the difference per item removes the shared difficulty. A paired bootstrap resamples items (keeping each item's pair together) thousands of times and recomputes the difference: in the lab its 95% interval was [−4.0, +2.8] points, against [−5.4, +4.0] for an unpaired bootstrap that ignores the pairing, about 30% narrower from the same data. Always compare on the same items, fix decoding settings, and analyse pairs.

6. Human evaluation

For open-ended quality, people remain the reference. Make human evaluation reliable the same way as any measurement: write a rubric with explicit criteria and examples of each level; blind raters to which system produced which output and randomise order; prefer pairwise comparisons ("which is better?") to absolute scores, because people are more consistent at comparing (lm6); use domain experts where correctness needs expertise; and measure inter-rater agreement on an overlap sample. If two experts agree only 70% of the time, no automated metric can be expected to do better than that, and the rubric probably needs work. A small, carefully labelled human set, a golden set, is also what you need to validate cheaper evaluators.

7. LLM-as-a-judge

Human evaluation is slow and expensive, so teams use a strong LLM as a grader: give it the input, the output (or two outputs), a rubric and ideally a reference answer, and ask for a score or a preference with reasoning. Zheng et al. studied this systematically with MT-Bench, 80 multi-turn questions graded by GPT-4, and Chatbot Arena, a crowdsourced platform of pairwise human votes. They found strong judges such as GPT-4 matched both controlled expert and crowdsourced human preferences, "achieving over 80% agreement, the same level of agreement between humans". That made LLM judging a standard tool. The same paper documented its failure modes, which every user of a judge must control.

8. Judge biases, measured

Position bias: a preference for the answer in one slot. Zheng et al. swapped the two answers and found GPT-4 with its default prompt gave a consistent verdict only 65.0% of the time; in 30.0% of cases it favoured the first answer whichever it was. Weaker judges were worse. Verbosity bias: a preference for longer answers, even when the extra length adds nothing (they showed judges could be fooled by answers padded with repetitive content). Self-enhancement bias: a tendency to favour one's own outputs. Limited reasoning: judges can be misled on maths and reasoning questions they could not solve themselves. Controls follow directly: run each pairwise comparison in both orders and count only consistent verdicts (or score inconsistent ones as ties); use reference answers for factual and maths items; instruct on length explicitly or compare length-controlled outputs; use a judge from a different model family; and ask for reasoning before the verdict.

The lab simulates a judge that follows the true human preference 70% of the time and otherwise favours the first slot. Shown each pair in one order only, it agrees with humans on 86.8% of pairs and reports that response X wins 61.0% of the time, although humans prefer X in 55%: position bias has inflated X's win rate by 6 points. Run in both orders, it is consistent on 71.8% of pairs, close to GPT-4's 65% in the paper, and on those consistent pairs it agrees with humans 98.6% of the time and reports a 53.3% win rate. Discarding inconsistent verdicts costs coverage but removes most of the bias.

9. Validating a judge

Before trusting a judge, measure it against humans on a golden set from your own task. Report raw agreement and Cohen's kappa, which corrects for chance. Worked: a judge agrees with human labels on 85 of 100 pairs; the judge prefers X 60% of the time and humans 55%. Chance agreement is 0.60 × 0.55 + 0.40 × 0.45 = 0.51, so kappa = (0.85 − 0.51)/(1 − 0.51) = 0.69, "substantial" agreement, noticeably less impressive than the raw 85%. Compare with human-human agreement on the same items: a judge at that level is as good as adding another human rater. Validate per criterion (a judge may be reliable on tone and unreliable on factual accuracy in your domain), and re-validate whenever the judge model or prompt changes.

10. Benchmarks and contamination

Public benchmarks (multiple-choice knowledge tests, grade-school maths, coding problems) are useful for shortlisting models and for comparing on a common yardstick. Their weaknesses: they measure narrow skills in fixed formats; scores are sensitive to prompt format and decoding (lm8); and their test items are on the public internet, so they can end up in training data. Contamination lets a model recall answers instead of solving problems, inflating scores. Zhang et al. built GSM1k, 1,000 new grade-school maths problems designed to match GSM8k in difficulty and style, and found accuracy drops of up to 8% for some models, with several model families showing systematic overfitting at almost all sizes, and a positive relationship between how likely a model was to generate GSM8k examples and how much it dropped. Many frontier models showed little or no overfitting, so contamination is not universal, but it must be checked. The lab's simulation: a model with 60% true skill that has memorised 15% of a test set scores about 66% on it and about 61% on fresh equivalent items.

11. Contamination in your own evals

Your private eval set can be contaminated too: test items pasted into few-shot examples, used as fine-tuning data, or present in documents the RAG system retrieves. Keep eval items in a separate store with access rules, deduplicate training and prompt data against them, and when an eval score jumps unexpectedly, check for leakage before celebrating. Rotate in fresh items over time, because a set used to make many decisions gradually overfits: teams tune prompts until they pass the specific items.

12. From offline to online

Offline evals are proxies; the outcome you care about happens in production. Online metrics depend on the product: task completion, resolution without escalation to a human, user ratings and thumbs-down rates, edits to generated text, retention, and of course latency and cost. The decision tool is the A/B test (ml16): randomly assign users or sessions to variants and compare outcome metrics with appropriate statistics and enough traffic. Offline evals then act as a gate (a change must not regress on the suite before it is allowed into an experiment) rather than the final verdict. When offline and online disagree, it is usually the offline set that is unrepresentative or the offline metric that measures the wrong thing, and the fix is to refresh the set from live traffic.

13. Monitoring in production

Evaluation continues after launch. Sample live traffic regularly and grade it with the validated judge and periodic human review; track the distribution of intents (new intents appear), refusal and escalation rates, response length, latency and cost; and log enough context (prompt version, retrieved documents, model version, decoding settings) to reproduce any output. Provider model updates deserve special attention: hosted models change, and the eval suite should be re-run on any new version before switching.

14. Prompt sensitivity and evaluation settings

LLM scores can move several points with changes that should not matter: the wording of the instruction, the order of few-shot examples, whether options are labelled A-D or 1-4, a trailing space (lm1), or the decoding temperature. This sensitivity has two consequences. First, comparisons are fair only when both systems are evaluated under identical, reported settings, or each under its own best settings found on a separate development split. Second, a robust system should not depend on fragile phrasing, so evaluation sets benefit from paraphrased variants of the same item: if a model passes one phrasing and fails another, the item is measuring luck. Benchmark harnesses exist largely to pin down these settings so that numbers from different teams are comparable.

15. Evaluating safety and refusals

Safety needs two-sided measurement. A set of genuinely harmful requests measures the refusal rate you want to be high; a set of benign requests that look superficially similar ("how do I kill a stuck process?", "what household chemicals should never be mixed?") measures over-refusal, which you want low. Optimising one alone produces a model that refuses everything or nothing. Adversarial testing (red-teaming) adds prompts designed to bypass safeguards, including indirect injections through documents and tool outputs (lm18). Safety metrics belong in the regression suite like any other, because fine-tuning and prompt changes can shift them silently (lm11).

16. Multi-turn and long-horizon behaviour

Many failures only appear over several turns: the assistant forgets an instruction given earlier, contradicts itself, or drifts in tone. Single-turn eval items cannot catch these. Multi-turn evals script a conversation (or let a simulated user, another LLM following a persona and a goal, drive it) and grade the whole transcript against criteria such as goal achieved, constraints respected throughout and consistency. Simulated users are convenient but behave more politely and predictably than real ones, so replaying anonymised real conversations, where possible, remains the stronger test.

17. A worked evaluation plan

For a support assistant deciding between the current model and a cheaper one: (1) Decision: switch if quality is not worse by more than 2 points on resolution correctness and not worse on safety. (2) Eval set: 800 real tickets across 12 intents plus 100 adversarial and policy-sensitive cases. (3) Metrics: rubric-graded correctness (LLM judge validated at kappa 0.75 against two support experts on 150 items), policy compliance, escalation appropriateness, length, latency and cost per ticket. (4) Run both models with fixed settings, three samples per item, both judge orders. (5) Analyse paired differences with bootstrap intervals per metric and per intent. (6) If the cheaper model passes, run a two-week A/B on 10% of traffic measuring escalation rate and customer satisfaction. That plan answers the question with known uncertainty, which is all an evaluation can do.

Statistical power and sample size

Whether a comparison can detect a difference depends on the number of discordant items, not the total. If two systems disagree on a fraction d of items and the better one wins a fraction w of those, a paired test needs roughly n ≈ (z_α + z_β)²/(d·(2w − 1)²) items. With d = 0.15 and a true win share of 0.6 among discordant items (a 1.5-point accuracy difference), 80% power at 5% significance needs about (1.96 + 0.84)²/(0.15 × 0.04) ≈ 1,300 items. This is why small eval sets cannot distinguish close variants, and why it is better to target the eval at items where variants are likely to differ (hard cases) than to add more easy ones that both systems pass.

Multiple comparisons

Evaluating ten prompt variants on twenty metrics and reporting the best-looking result guarantees false discoveries: at a 5% significance level, about one in twenty null comparisons looks significant by chance. Pre-register the primary metric and decision, correct for multiple comparisons when testing many variants, and confirm a winner on a fresh held-out slice. The same trap applies to selecting checkpoints or hyperparameters on the eval set: the selected score is optimistically biased, so keep a final test slice untouched until the end.

Elo and Bradley–Terry for model rankings

Chatbot Arena ranks models from many pairwise human votes. The natural model is Bradley–Terry (lm6): P(A beats B) = σ(r_A − r_B), with a strength r per model fitted by maximum likelihood; Elo ratings are an online approximation from chess. Pairwise designs scale to many models without every pair being compared, and confidence intervals come from bootstrapping the votes. Caveats: the population of prompts and voters defines what "better" means (casual chat prompts, not your domain), style (length, formatting) influences votes, and close ratings within the intervals are ties. Arena-style rankings are informative about general preference and should not replace task-specific evaluation.

Evaluating stochastic and agentic systems

With sampling, each item has a probability of success rather than a fixed outcome. Running k samples per item and averaging estimates it; reporting pass@1 and pass@k separately distinguishes reliability from capability. For agents (lm16), outcomes depend on multi-step trajectories, tool results and environment state; evaluation needs reproducible sandboxes, success criteria on the final state, and multiple runs per task, because a 60% success rate can easily look like 100% or 20% in a handful of tries. Trace-level checks (did it confirm before an irreversible action?) are often more important than the final answer.

Measuring faithfulness and hallucination

For grounded tasks (RAG, summarisation), a key question is whether each claim in the output is supported by the source. Automated approaches split the output into atomic claims and check each against the source with a natural-language-inference model or an LLM judge, reporting the fraction supported. These checks are themselves imperfect (claim splitting is noisy and judges may accept paraphrases that change meaning), so they need human validation like any judge, but they scale much better than reading every output. Faithfulness to a source is also different from factual correctness: an answer can faithfully repeat a wrong document. Evaluate both when both matter (lm15).

Inter-rater reliability as a ceiling

If two expert humans agree on 75% of pairwise judgements for a criterion, a judge cannot be meaningfully "90% accurate" against either of them: the labels themselves are noisy. Measuring human-human agreement on an overlap sample gives the ceiling for any evaluator and often reveals that the criterion is ill-defined. The fix is usually a better rubric (concrete criteria, worked examples of each level, tie-break rules) rather than a better judge. Agreement statistics such as Cohen's kappa for two raters, or Krippendorff's alpha for several, quantify progress as the rubric improves.

Calibration as an evaluation target

For many products it matters not only whether the model is right but whether it knows when it might be wrong: an assistant that says "I'm not sure, please check with HR" on the questions it gets wrong is far more useful than one that is equally accurate but always confident. Measuring this means collecting a confidence signal (a stated confidence, the probability of the answer, or agreement across several samples) and checking calibration (ml15): among answers given with 90% confidence, are about 90% correct? Selective-prediction curves, accuracy as a function of how many low-confidence answers are withheld, show whether abstaining can buy reliability.

Goodhart's law for evals

Any eval that becomes a target is optimised against: teams tune prompts to its items, judges get gamed by formatting, and models trained with judge-based rewards learn the judge's biases (lm6's reward hacking in another guise). Defences are rotation (refresh items), diversity of evaluators (several judges, periodic humans), hidden test slices, and tying decisions to online outcomes. An eval suite is a living instrument that needs maintenance, not a fixed exam.

The public case: "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena" (Zheng et al., 2023), with GSM1k

By 2023, open-source chat models were multiplying and benchmarks such as multiple-choice knowledge tests failed to capture what users noticed: whether a model followed instructions well in open-ended, multi-turn conversation. Zheng and colleagues at LMSYS built two evaluation resources: MT-Bench, a set of 80 challenging multi-turn questions across writing, roleplay, reasoning, maths, coding, extraction and knowledge, and Chatbot Arena, a crowdsourced platform where users chat with two anonymous models and vote for the better one. They then asked whether a strong LLM could replace the humans as the grader. Their answer was a qualified yes: GPT-4 as a judge matched both controlled expert votes and crowdsourced preferences, "achieving over 80% agreement, the same level of agreement between humans". They released the MT-Bench questions, 3,000 expert votes and 30,000 conversations with human preferences.

Equally important was their catalogue of judge failures, with numbers. Position bias: with the default prompt, GPT-4 gave the same verdict after swapping the two answers only 65.0% of the time, favouring the first answer in 30.0% of cases; Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 were less consistent. Verbosity bias: judges could be swayed by answers lengthened with repetitive content. Self-enhancement: some judges favoured their own outputs. Limited capability: judges mis-graded maths and reasoning they could not do themselves, which reference-guided judging (giving the judge a correct answer) reduced. Their mitigations, swapping positions, few-shot examples, chain-of-thought and reference answers, are now standard practice. A year later, the GSM1k study showed the other side of evaluation risk: on freshly written problems matched to the GSM8k benchmark, some models dropped by up to 8%, evidence that public benchmark scores can overstate ability through contamination.

Failure modes in practice

Vibe checks. Decisions made on a handful of hand-picked examples; regressions surface in production. Build a small eval set first.

Unrepresentative sets. An eval of clean, synthetic questions passes while real, messy questions fail. Source from logs and refresh regularly.

Noise mistaken for signal. A 2-point gain on 200 items celebrated and shipped. Report intervals and use paired comparisons.

Unvalidated judges. A judge that prefers longer answers drives the team to make answers longer. Validate against humans, control for length and position.

Silent upstream changes. A hosted model update changes behaviour; nobody notices until users complain. Pin versions where possible and re-run evals on any change.

A note on cost

Evaluation has a budget like everything else. A judge call per item per variant per sample adds up: 1,000 items × 3 samples × 2 orders × 2 variants is 12,000 judge calls per comparison. Teams manage this by using programmatic checks first, a cheaper judge for screening and a stronger one for close calls, caching outputs of unchanged variants, and reserving human review for the golden set and disagreements. The cost is small next to the cost of shipping a regression, but it should be planned.

What to keep running

A regression suite on every change (prompt, model, retrieval, decoding), with paired comparisons against the last release; a weekly graded sample of live traffic; judge validation refreshed quarterly or on judge changes; dashboards of online outcomes alongside offline scores; and a process that turns every production failure into a new eval case.

Goal: see how noisy accuracy estimates are, why paired comparisons are more precise, how position bias distorts an LLM judge and how a swap test controls it, and how contamination inflates a score. NumPy simulations, instant.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# ---- 1. how precise is an accuracy measured on n items?
n, p = 500, 0.82
se = np.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n)
print(f"accuracy {p:.0%} on {n} items: standard error {se:.4f}, 95% CI ±{1.96*se:.1%}")
for target in (0.05, 0.03, 0.01):
    print(f"  items needed for a ±{target:.0%} 95% interval at p=0.8: {int(np.ceil((1.96/target)**2 * 0.8*0.2)):,}")

# ---- 2. comparing two models on the SAME items: paired beats unpaired
difficulty = rng.normal(size=n)                                   # shared item difficulty makes errors correlated
a = (rng.normal(size=n) * 0.6 + difficulty < 1.0).astype(int)     # model A correct?
b = (rng.normal(size=n) * 0.6 + difficulty < 1.08).astype(int)    # model B slightly better
print(f"\nmodel A {a.mean():.3f}, model B {b.mean():.3f}, difference {b.mean()-a.mean():+.3f}")
B = 10_000
idx = rng.integers(0, n, (B, n))
paired = (b[idx] - a[idx]).mean(1)                                # resample items, keep the pairing
unpaired = b[rng.integers(0, n, (B, n))].mean(1) - a[rng.integers(0, n, (B, n))].mean(1)
for name, d in [("paired bootstrap", paired), ("unpaired bootstrap", unpaired)]:
    lo, hi = np.percentile(d, [2.5, 97.5])
    print(f"  {name:18s} 95% CI [{lo:+.3f}, {hi:+.3f}]  -> {'significant' if lo > 0 else 'not significant'}")
only_a, only_b = int(((a == 1) & (b == 0)).sum()), int(((a == 0) & (b == 1)).sum())
print(f"  items only A got right {only_a}, only B got right {only_b} (the only items that carry information)")

# ---- 3. an LLM judge with position bias, and agreement with humans
m = 400
true_pref = rng.random(m) < 0.55                                   # humans prefer response X in 55% of pairs
def judge(x_first):                       # judge follows the true preference 70% of the time; otherwise it
    correct = rng.random(m) < 0.70           # picks whichever response is in slot 1 with probability 0.75
    first = rng.random(m) < 0.75
    return np.where(correct, true_pref, np.where(first, x_first, ~x_first))
j1 = judge(np.ones(m, bool)); j2 = judge(np.zeros(m, bool))        # X shown first, then second
consistent = j1 == j2
print(f"\njudge consistent across both orders: {consistent.mean():.1%}")
def kappa(x, y):
    po = (x == y).mean(); pe = x.mean() * y.mean() + (1 - x.mean()) * (1 - y.mean())
    return po, (po - pe) / (1 - pe)
for name, verdict in [("single order (X first)", j1), ("both orders, keep consistent", j1[consistent])]:
    hum = true_pref if len(verdict) == m else true_pref[consistent]
    po, k = kappa(verdict, hum)
    print(f"  {name:30s} agreement with humans {po:.1%}, Cohen's kappa {k:.2f}, P(judge prefers X) {verdict.mean():.1%}")

# ---- 4. contamination: memorising part of the test set inflates the score
test = 1000; true_skill = 0.60; leaked = 0.15
mem = rng.random(test) < leaked
score = np.where(mem, 1, rng.random(test) < true_skill).mean()
fresh = (rng.random(test) < true_skill).mean()
print(f"\nreported accuracy {score:.1%} with {leaked:.0%} of the test set memorised; on fresh, equivalent items {fresh:.1%}")

What to look for.

  1. Section 1: 82% on 500 items has a standard error of 0.0172 and a 95% interval of ±3.4 points; ±5, ±3 and ±1 points need about 246, 683 and 6,147 items.
  2. Section 2: A scores 82.6% and B 82.0%; they disagree on 75 items (39 vs 36). The paired bootstrap interval [−4.0, +2.8] is narrower than the unpaired [−5.4, +4.0]; neither shows a significant difference, although B was built to be about 2 points better in expectation.
  3. Section 3: a biased judge is consistent across orders on 71.8% of pairs; in a single order it agrees with humans 86.8% (kappa 0.73) and inflates X's win rate to 61.0% against the true 55%; keeping only consistent verdicts gives 98.6% agreement and 53.3%.
  4. Section 4: memorising 15% of the test set raises a 61% model to about 66% on that set.
Design — is the cheaper model good enough?

Your team wants to replace the model behind a document-QA feature with one that costs a third as much. You have 300 labelled questions. The current model scores 86%. How do you decide?

Answer

Define the decision: switch if the new model is not more than about 2 points worse on correctness, not worse on faithfulness to sources and refusal of unanswerable questions, and within latency targets.

Check the precision available: at 86% and n = 300, the standard error is √(0.86 × 0.14/300) = 0.020, so each score alone is ±3.9 points. A paired comparison does better, but to resolve a 2-point non-inferiority margin you likely need more items: extend the set to 800-1,000 from logs, especially hard and unanswerable questions.

Run: both models, same retrieval, fixed decoding, several samples per item; grade with exact-match or rubric judging validated against a human-labelled subset (report kappa); run pairwise judgements in both orders.

Analyse: paired bootstrap interval for the difference in each metric overall and per question type; inspect discordant items to understand failure patterns.

Confirm online: if the lower bound of the difference is above −2 points, A/B test on a slice of traffic measuring user-reported answer quality and follow-up question rates before full rollout.

Diagnose — the judge says we keep getting better

Over three months of prompt iterations, your LLM-judge score rose from 7.1 to 8.4 out of 10, but user satisfaction is flat and responses have doubled in length. What happened?

Answer

The team has been optimising against the judge's biases, most likely verbosity bias: the judge rewards longer, more elaborate answers, so iterations that added length "won" even though users did not benefit. It may also be overfitting to the fixed eval items. Fixes: validate the judge against fresh human preferences on current outputs (agreement and kappa) and check whether its scores correlate with length; add explicit length guidance or compare length-controlled outputs; run pairwise judgements in both orders; refresh the eval set from recent traffic; and make an online metric (satisfaction, resolution) the decision criterion, with the judge as a gate rather than the target.
