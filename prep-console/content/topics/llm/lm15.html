In 2023 two New York lawyers filed a brief in a personal-injury case against the airline Avianca. It cited several earlier court decisions supporting their argument, complete with case names, courts, dates and quotations. The airline's lawyers could not find them. Neither could the judge. The decisions did not exist: one of the lawyers had asked ChatGPT for supporting cases, and it had produced convincing ones from nothing. When he asked it whether one case was real, it said yes and that the cases could be found in standard legal databases. The court sanctioned the lawyers and their firm. The story became famous because it was so legible, but the same failure happens every day in less visible ways: a support bot quoting a refund policy that does not exist, a coding assistant calling a function no library provides, a summary attributing a statement the document never made.

These are hallucinations: fluent, confident outputs that are false or unsupported. They are not glitches in an otherwise truthful system; they are what a system trained to produce plausible text does when it lacks the knowledge to be accurate, especially when its training and evaluation have rewarded answering over admitting uncertainty. Better models hallucinate less, but every model does it, and the rate depends heavily on the task: short questions about famous facts are mostly fine, long answers about obscure entities are often riddled with errors.

For an AI engineer, hallucination is the defining reliability problem of LLM products. You need to understand why it happens, so you know which fixes can work; how to measure it, so you know whether a fix worked; and how to design systems in which the inevitable remaining errors are caught or contained, so a fluent mistake does not become a sanctioned brief or a wrong refund. Interviewers ask about all three, and the weakest answers are the ones that treat it as a prompt-wording problem.

This lesson defines the kinds of hallucination, then traces their causes through pretraining (plausibility over truth, rare facts and the singleton rate), post-training (imitation and approval) and evaluation (binary grading that rewards guessing, worked with numbers). It then covers detection (source checking, atomic-fact scoring, self-consistency) and the controls that work in practice, from grounding and abstention to verification and interface design. The public case is Mata v. Avianca. The lab simulates the guessing incentive, the singleton rate and a self-consistency detector in NumPy.

Three kinds of hallucination

It helps to separate cases by what the output should have been checked against. A closed-book hallucination is a false statement of world knowledge with no source in play: "the Eiffel Tower was completed in 1899". With a source in the context, such as a document to summarise or retrieved passages in RAG (lm9), there are two more kinds. An intrinsic hallucination contradicts the source: the document says revenue fell and the summary says it rose. An extrinsic hallucination adds something the source does not support, which may even be true but cannot be verified from it. Fabricated citations are a special and especially harmful case, because they borrow the authority of a source that does not exist.

An analogy: the confident student

Picture a student who has read an enormous amount and is sitting an exam where every blank answer scores zero and every wrong answer also scores zero. For questions they know, they answer correctly. For questions they half-know or do not know, the rational strategy is to write something plausible, because a guess sometimes scores and a blank never does. After years of such exams the student becomes very good at producing plausible answers in the right style and format, whether or not they know them. Language models are that student. Pretraining taught them what correct-looking answers look like; post-training and benchmarks rewarded answering; and nothing in either required them to know the difference between knowing and guessing. The analogy breaks in one place: a human student usually knows when they are guessing. A model has no direct access to whether a fact was well represented in its training data, although, as this lesson shows, signals such as consistency across samples can partly recover it.

How often does it happen?

There is no single hallucination rate, because it depends on what is asked. Short questions about well-known facts are answered correctly by strong models most of the time. Long, detailed answers about obscure subjects are another matter: when FActScore checked biographies written by 2023-era models against Wikipedia, only 42-58% of individual facts were supported for the closed-book systems. Summaries of a provided document are usually much more faithful, but rarely perfect. Questions that require precise identifiers (citations, URLs, version numbers, account details) are among the riskiest, because a plausible-looking wrong identifier is easy to generate and hard for a reader to spot. A useful habit is to ask, for any feature, "what is the cost of one confident error here, and how would a user notice it?", and design controls in proportion.

Why it cannot be fixed by asking nicely

Instructions such as "do not make things up" help a little, because they shift the model towards hedged answers, but they cannot give the model knowledge it lacks or a reliable sense of which of its beliefs are well-founded. The lawyer in Mata v. Avianca did ask whether the cases were real, and the model confirmed its fabrications. Reliable control therefore comes from outside the model's own say-so: sources it must cite, checks against those sources, incentives that make abstaining acceptable, and people responsible for verification where errors are costly.

1. Pretraining optimises plausibility

Pretraining minimises next-token cross-entropy on a huge corpus (lm6). The loss rewards assigning high probability to whatever text actually came next, and the corpus contains truths, falsehoods, fiction, jokes and errors, all presented in the same confident prose. The model therefore learns the shape of text extremely well: what a citation looks like, how a biography is structured, which kinds of numbers appear in which contexts. For facts that appear many times consistently, the most probable continuation is the true one, and the model is reliable. For facts that appear rarely, the model has learned the format but not the content, so the most probable continuation is a plausible filler: a name that fits, a year in the right decade, a case citation in the right style. Nothing in the objective distinguishes "this is what the text said" from "this is what text like this usually says".

2. Rare facts and the singleton rate

Kalai et al. (2025) made this precise. They show that generating valid statements is at least as hard as classifying statements as valid or not, so hallucinations can be understood as ordinary classification errors that arise "through natural statistical pressures" whenever true and false statements cannot be told apart from the training data. For arbitrary facts with no learnable pattern, such as individual people's birthdays, the relevant quantity is the singleton rate: the fraction of such facts that appear exactly once in the training data. The idea goes back to Alan Turing and I. J. Good's estimate of the probability of unseen events from the number of events seen once. Their example: if 20% of birthday facts appear exactly once in the pretraining data, one expects base models to hallucinate on at least 20% of birthday facts. The lab simulates a corpus where 50,000 people's birthdays are mentioned with a skewed (Zipf) frequency: 43.9% appear exactly once. Facts about the long tail of people, places, products and papers are mostly singletons, which is why questions about obscure entities fail so much more often than questions about famous ones.

3. Post-training rewards answering

Supervised fine-tuning (lm6) imitates demonstrations. If a demonstration answers a question with a fact the model does not know, imitation teaches the model to produce confident answers in that situation, not the fact. Gekhman et al. studied this directly: fine-tuning examples introducing new knowledge were learned much more slowly than examples consistent with what the model already knew, and as they were eventually learned they linearly increased the model's tendency to hallucinate. Preference tuning adds a second push: raters, especially without time or sources to check facts, tend to prefer complete, confident answers to hedged ones, so the reward model learns to reward confidence, and the policy learns to give it. The same mechanism produces sycophancy, agreeing with a user's false premise because agreement is preferred. Careful post-training counters both by including examples where the right answer is "I don't know" or "that premise is false" and rewarding them.

4. Evaluation rewards guessing, worked

Most benchmarks grade answers as right (1 point) or wrong (0 points), and an abstention also scores 0. Under that rule, guessing never hurts and sometimes helps. Kalai et al. argue this creates an "epidemic" of penalising uncertainty: models, and the teams tuning them to leaderboards, are optimised to be good test-takers who always guess. The lab makes it concrete. A model knows 60% of answers; when unsure, a guess is right 20% of the time. Always guessing: accuracy 0.60 + 0.40 × 0.20 = 68% (67.9% in the simulation), with 32% confident errors. Abstaining when unsure: accuracy 60%, zero confident errors. A leaderboard ranks the guesser higher. Now penalise each wrong answer 3 points: the guesser scores 0.68 − 3 × 0.32 = −0.28, the abstainer 0.60. The ranking flips, and the incentive now favours honesty.

Kalai et al. propose stating an explicit confidence target t in evaluation instructions: "Answer only if you are > t confident, since mistakes are penalized t/(1 − t) points, while correct answers receive 1 point, and an answer of 'I don't know' receives 0 points." For t = 0.75 a mistake costs 3 points; for t = 0.9, 9 points. Answering is worthwhile exactly when the probability of being right exceeds t, because the expected value p × 1 − (1 − p) × t/(1 − t) is positive only when p > t. Their argument is that changing the scoring of the mainstream benchmarks, rather than adding yet more hallucination tests, is what would change model behaviour.

5. Decoding and context effects

Some hallucinations are introduced at generation time. Sampling from the low-probability tail (lm8) occasionally picks a wrong token, and the model then continues consistently with its own mistake, elaborating a false detail. Long generations compound the risk, which is one reason FActScore found errors more frequent later in biographies. Ambiguous or false premises in the prompt ("Why did Einstein win two Nobel Prizes?") invite the model to play along. And in long contexts, relevant information buried in the middle may be under-used (lm4, lm9), so the model falls back on memory. Lower temperature and truncation reduce tail-sampling errors, but they cannot fix beliefs the model holds confidently.

6. Measuring hallucination: against a source

When a source is defined, as in summarisation or RAG, hallucination is measured as unfaithfulness: the share of claims not supported by the source. The standard procedure splits the output into individual claims and checks each against the source, using a natural-language-inference (NLI) classifier, which decides whether a premise entails, contradicts or is neutral towards a hypothesis, or an LLM judge validated against human labels (lm14). Citation checks are a special case: every citation must resolve to a real retrieved document, and the cited passage must support the sentence it is attached to. These checks are automatable and should run on every response in high-stakes applications, not only in offline evaluation.

7. Measuring hallucination: atomic facts

For long-form outputs without a provided source, FActScore (Min et al., 2023) splits a generation into atomic facts (short statements with one piece of information each) and checks each against a knowledge source, reporting the percentage supported. On biographies of people, checked against Wikipedia, InstructGPT scored 42.5%, ChatGPT 58.3% and the search-augmented PerplexityAI 71.5%. Worked on a toy example in the lab: a fictional chemist's biography contains 10 atomic facts, 6 of which a reference source supports, so the factual precision is 60%, and the unsupported claims (a Nobel Prize, three children, a death date, a career as a violinist) are exactly the kind of plausible, specific details models invent. The method needs a reliable knowledge source and a good claim splitter, both automatable with care; it measures precision (how much of what was said is true), not completeness.

8. Detecting hallucination without a source: self-consistency

If the model knows an answer, independent samples tend to agree; if it is guessing, they scatter. SelfCheckGPT (Manakul et al., 2023) exploits this: sample several extra responses, and for each sentence of the main response, check whether the samples support it; sentences the samples contradict or do not mention are flagged. It needs no external database and works with black-box APIs. The lab simulates a model that knows 60% of 2,000 answers: with 5 samples, mean agreement is 0.91 for known answers and 0.36 for unknown ones; flagging answers whose agreement falls below 0.6 catches 91% of unknown answers with 99% precision, and a 0.8 threshold catches 99% at 90% precision. Real models are messier, and the method cannot catch errors the model makes consistently (a firmly learned falsehood), but consistency is a useful, cheap signal, especially combined with other checks. Its cost is several extra generations per answer.

9. Prompt-level practices: modest but real

Prompting cannot add knowledge, but it shifts behaviour at the margin. Telling the model that "I don't know" is an acceptable and preferred answer when unsure, and showing an example of it, reduces guessing. Asking it to quote the supporting passage before answering (so the answer is anchored to text it must reproduce) reduces unsupported claims in grounded tasks. Breaking a task into steps and asking for intermediate results exposes errors that would otherwise be hidden in a fluent paragraph. Asking it to flag uncertainty inline ("I'm not sure about the year") gives users and verifiers something to check. These practices are cheap and worth using, but they should be measured like any other change, and they are a complement to grounding and verification, never a substitute.

10. Grounding: the most effective control

The single most effective control for knowledge questions is to stop asking the model to remember: retrieve authoritative sources and require the answer to be based on them (lm9). Grounding converts closed-book hallucination into a faithfulness problem, which is easier to check because the evidence is in hand. Practices that make it work: instructions to answer only from the sources and to cite them; placing sources where the model uses them well; giving the model explicit permission to say the sources do not contain the answer; and, for citations, constraining the output so that only identifiers of retrieved documents can be cited. FActScore's comparison is suggestive: the search-augmented system had markedly higher factual precision than the closed-book ones, though far from perfect.

11. Abstention and calibrated uncertainty

A system that can say "I don't know" or "the documents don't cover this" turns many potential hallucinations into honest non-answers. Enabling it takes three things. Permission and examples in the prompt or fine-tuning data. A signal of uncertainty: token probabilities of the answer (often reasonably informative for short answers from base models, less so after preference tuning), agreement across samples, retrieval scores, or a verifier's judgement. And incentives in evaluation that reward abstaining when unsure, as in section 4. The threshold should reflect the cost of errors: a medical-information service might require high confidence (t = 0.9, a mistake worth nine correct answers), a brainstorming tool almost none. Showing calibrated uncertainty to users ("I'm not certain; here is the source to check") is part of the same control.

12. Verification passes

A second pass can check the first. Chain-of-Verification (Dhuliawala et al., 2023) prompts the model to draft an answer, plan verification questions about its claims, answer those questions independently (so the answers are not biased by the draft) and then revise; the authors report fewer hallucinations on list questions, closed-book question answering and long-form generation. More robust in production are external verifiers: a separate model or NLI classifier checking each claim against sources, code execution for computational claims, database lookups for structured facts, and validators for formats. Verification costs latency and money, so apply it in proportion to the stakes, and measure how many errors it catches and how many correct answers it wrongly blocks.

13. Constraining what can be said

Many hallucinations can be prevented structurally. Constrained decoding (lm8) can restrict an output to valid JSON, an enumeration of allowed values, or identifiers that exist in a list, so a model cannot invent a product code or a document ID. Tool use (lm16) moves exact work out of the model: arithmetic to a calculator, dates to a calendar library, account details to an API call, so the model reports results instead of recalling them. Templates for high-risk statements (prices, legal terms, medical doses) can be filled from data rather than generated. The general principle is to let the model do what it is good at, understanding and phrasing, and let deterministic systems supply facts.

14. Interface and process design

Some errors will get through, so the product should make them easy to catch. Show sources next to claims and make them one click away; highlight unsupported or low-confidence statements; avoid presenting generated text as authoritative in contexts where users will not check (Mata v. Avianca is partly a story of a tool used as if it were a database); and keep humans accountable for outputs with real consequences, with workflows that make verification the default rather than an afterthought. User feedback ("this is wrong") should flow into the evaluation set (lm14).

15. Summaries, extraction and translation

Tasks that transform a provided text are easier to control than open questions, because the source is in hand, but they have their own patterns. Summaries add connective claims ("as a result", "the company plans to") that the source does not make, merge numbers from different sections, or attribute a statement to the wrong speaker. Extraction tasks fill a field that the document leaves empty with a plausible value instead of null. Translation can smooth over an untranslatable term by inventing an equivalent. The controls are specific: instruct and evaluate for "not stated" outputs, check numbers and names against the source programmatically, and use claim-level faithfulness checks on summaries. These tasks are where automated verification works best, because every claim has a definite place to be checked.

16. Setting an acceptable error rate

Zero hallucination is not achievable, so each feature needs an explicit target tied to the cost of errors and the presence of human review. A brainstorming tool can tolerate invented ideas, since users treat output as suggestions. A drafting assistant whose output a professional reviews can tolerate occasional errors if they are easy to spot and sources are shown. An autonomous system that acts on its outputs (sending emails, changing records, advising patients) needs near-zero high-severity errors and therefore strong grounding, verification and abstention, or a human approval step. Writing the target down, measuring against it per release and per intent (lm14), and choosing controls to meet it turns hallucination from a vague worry into an engineering requirement.

17. Putting the controls together

A layered design for a knowledge assistant, in order: retrieve authoritative sources; generate with instructions to use only the sources, cite them and abstain when they are silent; constrain citations to retrieved documents; verify claims against cited passages and drop or flag unsupported ones; expose sources and uncertainty in the interface; and log everything for evaluation. Each layer catches some of what the previous ones miss. Measured end to end, such stacks reduce unsupported claims dramatically compared with a bare model, but the residual rate is never zero, which is why the final layer, a person who verifies where it matters, remains part of the system.

Generation is harder than classification

Kalai et al.'s central reduction runs like this. Suppose we could generate only valid statements. Then we could classify any candidate statement as valid or not by checking whether a good generator would produce it, which is at least as hard. Turning it around, the error rate of any generator is bounded below by a multiple of the error rate of the best classifier of validity ("is-it-valid"). For facts with structure that can be learned (grammar, arithmetic patterns, common knowledge repeated many times) the classifier can be accurate, so generation errors can be low. For arbitrary facts with no pattern, classification error is high whenever the training data contains too few examples, which is where the singleton rate enters. The argument explains why hallucination persists even with perfect training data: errors come from the statistics of learning, not only from errors in the data.

Why post-training can damage calibration

A pretrained model is trained with cross-entropy, a proper scoring rule, which rewards probabilities matching frequencies, so base models are often fairly well calibrated on short factual questions. Preference tuning optimises a reward, not a scoring rule: if confident answers earn more reward, the policy concentrates probability on single answers regardless of how sure it should be. Reports from model developers have shown base models better calibrated than their chat-tuned versions. Practical consequence: token probabilities from a chat model are a weaker uncertainty signal than they look, and sampling consistency or external verification is more reliable.

Detecting knowledge from inside the model

Research has found that a model's internal activations often contain a signal of whether it knows an answer: a small linear classifier (a probe) trained on hidden states can predict whether the model's answer will be correct better than chance, sometimes better than the model's verbalised confidence. This suggests the information needed to abstain is partly present and not being used by the output. Probing requires access to model internals and labelled data, so it is mainly a research and open-model tool, but it supports the view that hallucination is partly an incentive problem, not only a knowledge problem.

Reasoning models and hallucination

Models that generate long chains of reasoning before answering can catch some of their own errors, for example by checking a computation or noticing a contradiction, and they often do better on tasks with verifiable answers. But reasoning text can itself contain fabricated intermediate facts, and a longer chain gives more opportunities to introduce and then build on an error. Whether reasoning reduces hallucination depends on the task and on whether training rewarded correct final answers (which can be checked) or plausible-looking reasoning (which cannot). Evaluate reasoning models on factual precision directly rather than assuming the extra thinking helps.

Faithfulness versus factuality

Two distinct properties are often conflated. Faithfulness is consistency with the provided source; factuality is consistency with the world. A summary can be faithful to a wrong document, and a closed-book answer can be factual but unsupported by any provided source. RAG systems should optimise faithfulness (and trust their sources), while open-domain assistants care about factuality. Evaluation should name which it measures; mixing them leads to confusing results, such as penalising a correct, well-known fact because the retrieved passage did not mention it.

Hallucination in code and tools

In code, hallucination shows up as calls to functions, parameters or packages that do not exist. The last is a security risk: attackers can publish malicious packages under names that models commonly invent, so a developer who installs a suggested package without checking pulls in malware. Execution and static checks catch non-existent functions quickly; dependency suggestions should be verified against registries and lock files. In agents (lm16), a model may report a tool result it never received or claim an action succeeded; traces should record actual tool outputs, and claims about actions should be checked against them.

Retrieval can also introduce errors

Grounding shifts risk rather than removing it. If the retrieved document is outdated, wrong or adversarial, a faithful model faithfully repeats it; if two retrieved documents conflict, the model may blend them into a statement neither supports. And documents can carry instructions that hijack the model (lm18). So grounded systems also need source quality controls: authoritative and current corpora, version metadata, conflict detection, and treating retrieved text as untrusted input.

The limits of self-verification

Asking the same model to check its own answer helps when the check is easier than the original task (verifying a citation exists, checking arithmetic step by step) or when the check is done independently of the draft, as in Chain-of-Verification. It helps little when the model's error comes from a confidently held false belief, because the checker shares the belief. Independent evidence, from retrieval, tools, other models with different training or people, is what breaks the shared-belief problem.

The public case: Mata v. Avianca, Inc. (S.D.N.Y., 2023)

Roberto Mata sued the airline Avianca over a personal injury on a flight. When Avianca moved to dismiss, Mata's lawyers filed an opposition on March 1, 2023 that cited a series of court decisions. Avianca's counsel reported that they could not locate several of them, and the court could not either. In his Opinion and Order on Sanctions of June 22, 2023, Judge P. Kevin Castel found that the lawyers had "submitted non-existent judicial opinions with fake quotes and citations created by the artificial intelligence tool ChatGPT, then continued to stand by the fake opinions after judicial orders called their existence into question". The fabricated decisions included opinions attributed to real judges under names such as "Varghese", "Shaboon", "Petersen", "Martinez", "Durden" and "Miller".

The record shows the mechanism of hallucination and the failure of self-verification. The lawyer testified that he had believed ChatGPT was "like a super search engine". Screenshots he submitted showed him asking it "Is Varghese a real case" and whether the other cases it provided were fake; it responded that it had supplied real authorities that could be found through Westlaw, LexisNexis and the Federal Reporter. The court stressed that "there is nothing inherently improper about using a reliable artificial intelligence tool for assistance", but that existing rules impose a gatekeeping duty on lawyers to ensure the accuracy of their filings. It imposed a $5,000 penalty jointly and severally on the respondents and ordered them to send letters to their client and to each judge falsely identified as the author of a fake opinion. For engineers the lessons are direct: a model's fluent output, including its answers about its own reliability, is not evidence; systems used for factual work must ground and verify; and interfaces must not invite users to treat a generator as a database.

Failure patterns in deployed systems

Fabricated references. Citations, URLs, case names, paper titles. Constrain citations to retrieved documents and validate every link before display.

Policy and price inventions. Support bots stating rules that do not exist. Ground in the knowledge base, use templates for numbers, and test with questions about policies that do not exist.

Confident answers to false premises. The model explains why something untrue is true. Include false-premise questions in evaluation and reward correcting the premise.

Summary drift. Summaries adding plausible but unsupported details. Measure faithfulness with claim-level checks.

Invented tool results. Agents reporting actions they did not take. Verify claims against tool logs.

Other publicised incidents

Mata v. Avianca was followed by many similar court filings containing fabricated citations, in several countries, leading courts and bar associations to issue guidance on disclosing and verifying AI-assisted work. Outside law, there have been widely reported cases of support chatbots stating policies their companies did not have, and of tribunals holding companies responsible for what their chatbots told customers. The common thread is not that the model erred, which is expected, but that the system around it treated generated text as authoritative without verification.

What to monitor

Unsupported-claim rate on sampled traffic (by intent and severity), citation validity, refusal rate on unanswerable questions, user corrections and thumbs-down linked to specific claims, and retrieval recall (because missed retrieval is a leading cause). Review high-severity incidents individually and add them to the evaluation set.

Goal: quantify why binary grading rewards guessing and how a confidence target flips the incentive, see the singleton rate on a skewed corpus, test a self-consistency detector, and compute a FActScore-style precision. NumPy simulations, instant.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# ---- 1. why binary grading rewards guessing
n = 10_000
knows = rng.random(n) < 0.60                       # the model knows 60% of answers
guess_right = rng.random(n) < 0.20                 # when guessing, it is right 20% of the time
def score(answer_when_unsure, wrong_penalty):
    correct = knows | (answer_when_unsure & guess_right)
    wrong = ~knows & answer_when_unsure & ~guess_right
    return correct.mean(), wrong.mean(), correct.mean() - wrong_penalty * wrong.mean()
for name, policy in [("always guess", np.ones(n, bool)), ("abstain when unsure", np.zeros(n, bool))]:
    acc, hall, _ = score(policy, 0)
    _, _, s3 = score(policy, 3)
    print(f"{name:20s} accuracy {acc:.1%}  confident errors {hall:.1%}  score with 3-point penalty {s3:+.3f}")
for t in (0.5, 0.75, 0.9):
    print(f"  confidence target t={t}: wrong answers cost t/(1-t) = {t/(1-t):.0f} points; answering is worth it only if P(correct) > {t}")

# ---- 2. the singleton argument: facts seen once in training cannot be reliably recalled
n_people = 50_000
mentions = rng.zipf(1.6, n_people)                  # how often each person's birthday appears in the corpus
singleton_rate = np.mean(mentions == 1)
print(f"\n{n_people:,} birthdays, Zipf-distributed mentions: {singleton_rate:.1%} appear exactly once (singletons)")
print(f"Kalai et al.: a base model's hallucination rate on such facts is expected to be at least about the singleton rate")

# ---- 3. self-consistency as a hallucination detector
qs = 2_000
known = rng.random(qs) < 0.6
k = 5
def samples(is_known):
    if is_known: return rng.choice(3, k, p=[0.9, 0.05, 0.05])          # mostly the same (true) answer
    return rng.integers(0, 10, k)                                          # scattered guesses
agree = np.array([np.bincount(samples(kn)).max() / k for kn in known])
print(f"\nself-consistency with {k} samples: mean agreement known {agree[known].mean():.2f}, unknown {agree[~known].mean():.2f}")
for thr in (0.6, 0.8):
    flag = agree < thr                                                     # low agreement -> flag as possible hallucination
    prec = (flag & ~known).sum() / max(flag.sum(), 1); rec = (flag & ~known).sum() / (~known).sum()
    print(f"  flag if agreement < {thr}: precision {prec:.2f}, recall {rec:.2f}, flagged {flag.mean():.0%} of answers")

# ---- 4. FActScore-style precision for one long answer (a fictional biography checked against a fictional source)
facts = ["born in 1931", "Swedish chemist", "studied in Uppsala", "worked on catalysts", "led a lab in Lund",
         "won the Nobel Prize", "had three children", "wrote a textbook", "died in 2004", "was a professional violinist"]
supported = np.array([1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 1, 0, 0])      # what the reference source confirms
print(f"\natomic facts {len(facts)}, supported {supported.sum()}: factual precision {supported.mean():.0%}; unsupported: "
      + "; ".join(f for f, s in zip(facts, supported) if not s))

Design — a medical-information assistant

A health company wants an assistant that answers patients' questions about their medications. Wrong answers can harm people. Outline how you would control hallucination.

Answer

Scope and grounding: answer only from an approved corpus (drug labels, clinical guidelines, the company's leaflets); retrieve passages and require citations to them; refuse questions outside scope and route to a pharmacist.

Constrain: doses, interactions and contraindications come from structured data via tools or templates, never generated freely; citations restricted to retrieved document IDs.

Abstain: set a high confidence target (for example t = 0.9); combine retrieval scores, sample consistency and a verifier's judgement; when below threshold, say so and escalate.

Verify: a claim-level faithfulness check against cited passages before display; block or flag unsupported claims.

Evaluate: expert-labelled test set including unanswerable and false-premise questions; track unsupported-claim rate (target near zero for high-severity claims), refusal appropriateness and citation validity; red-team with tricky phrasings.

Interface and accountability: show sources, state that the assistant does not replace a clinician, make escalation one click, and review flagged conversations.

Compute — should the model answer?

Your evaluation uses a confidence target of t = 0.8. The model estimates it is 70% likely to be right on a question. What is the expected score of answering versus abstaining, and what should it do?

Answer

A wrong answer costs t/(1 − t) = 0.8/0.2 = 4 points. Expected score of answering = 0.7 × 1 − 0.3 × 4 = 0.7 − 1.2 = −0.5. Abstaining scores 0. It should abstain; answering is worthwhile only when P(correct) > 0.8. Under binary grading the same answer would be worth +0.7 in expectation, which is exactly the incentive that produces confident guessing.
