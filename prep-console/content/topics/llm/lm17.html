Scaling laws (lm7) say bigger models trained on more data reach lower loss, smoothly and predictably. But in a dense transformer every parameter is used for every token, so doubling the parameters doubles the compute per token, in training and in serving. By 2020 the largest dense models were running into hard limits: training budgets measured in tens of millions of dollars and serving costs that grew with every parameter. Is there a way to have more parameters, and the knowledge they can store, without paying for all of them on every token?

A mixture of experts (MoE) is that way. Keep attention as it is, but replace each layer's single MLP with several parallel MLPs, the experts, plus a small router that sends each token to only one or two of them. A model with eight experts per layer has roughly four times the MLP parameters of a dense model with the same per-token compute. Google's Switch Transformer used this to train models up to 1.6 trillion parameters and reported up to 7× faster pre-training than dense baselines with the same compute per token. In 2023 Mistral released Mixtral 8x7B, an open MoE model with 47B parameters of which only 13B are used per token, which matched or outperformed the dense Llama 2 70B on the benchmarks its authors reported. Several of the most capable models since then are reported or widely believed to be MoE models.

The idea is also older than deep learning. Mixtures of experts appeared in early-1990s neural-network research as a way to divide a problem among specialised networks; what is new is applying sparse routing inside each layer of very large transformers, with the systems engineering to make it fast.

MoE is not free, and the costs are exactly what interviewers probe. Every expert must sit in memory even though most are idle for any given token. The router has to be kept from sending everything to a favourite expert. Experts on different GPUs must exchange tokens at every layer. And small-batch inference does not get the compute savings you might expect. An AI engineer choosing between an MoE and a dense model, or sizing hardware for one, needs to reason about all of these.

This lesson builds the MoE layer from the transformer block you know, works the routing computation by hand, counts Mixtral's total and active parameters from its configuration, explains load balancing (with Switch Transformer's auxiliary loss computed for three cases) and capacity factors (with token dropping simulated), then covers expert parallelism, inference behaviour and newer designs. The lab implements a small MoE layer and these calculations in NumPy.

Conditional computation

The core idea is conditional computation: different inputs use different parts of the network. A dense model is like a company where every employee works on every request. An MoE model is like a company with many specialist teams and a receptionist who routes each request to one or two of them. The company can employ far more people (parameters) without each request costing more work, as long as routing is cheap and the teams share the load sensibly.

The analogy needs two corrections. First, the routing happens per token and per layer: a single word in a sentence may visit experts 2 and 5 in layer 1, experts 0 and 7 in layer 2, and so on, so there is no fixed "team" for a request. Second, the "specialities" are not human topics like maths or French; they are whatever division of labour training finds useful, which analyses mostly describe in terms of token types and syntax. The useful mental model is mechanical: a learned switch in front of several MLPs.

Two numbers per model

Every MoE model has two sizes, and confusing them is the most common mistake. Total parameters count everything stored, every expert in every layer. Active parameters count what one token actually uses: the shared parts (attention, embeddings, norms) plus the k experts it is routed to in each layer. Total parameters determine how much memory you need to hold the model and, roughly, how much it can know. Active parameters determine how much arithmetic each token costs in training and inference. A model card that says "47B parameters, 13B active" is telling you it needs the memory of a 47B model and the compute of a 13B one. Names like "8x7B" are shorthand and can mislead: Mixtral is not 56B, because only the MLPs are replicated eight times.

Where it sits in the model

Recall the transformer block (lm3): x ← x + Attention(norm(x)), then x ← x + MLP(norm(x)). An MoE model keeps the attention sub-layer, the norms, the residual stream and the embeddings unchanged, and replaces the MLP with an MoE layer: router plus experts. Each expert is itself an ordinary MLP (SwiGLU in Mixtral). Because the MLP holds about two thirds of a dense layer's weights, replicating it is where most of an MoE model's extra parameters come from.

A small example

Imagine a toy model with 4 experts per layer and top-1 routing. The token "def" in a Python file might go to expert 3 in layer 1; "return" to expert 1; the indentation spaces to expert 3 again. In layer 2 the same tokens might go to entirely different experts. Over billions of training tokens, each expert sees a quarter of them (if balancing works) and learns whatever transformation helps those tokens most. No one told expert 3 to handle Python; routing and gradient descent together produced the division of labour, which is why it is hard to predict and hard to name. It also means routing can shift when the input distribution shifts, for example when a model trained mostly on English is fine-tuned on code, which is one reason MoE fine-tuning needs care.

Hold on to one sentence: an MoE model's compute per token follows its active parameters, while its memory follows its total parameters.

1. The router

The router is a single linear layer, W_r of shape d_model × E for E experts (4,096 × 8 = 32,768 parameters in Mixtral, negligible next to an expert). For a token vector h it computes E logits, h·W_r, one score per expert, and a softmax turns them into probabilities. The top k experts by probability are selected. Their probabilities are renormalised to sum to one (Mixtral applies the softmax over only the top-k logits, which is equivalent), giving the gating weights. The layer's output is Σ gᵢ·Expertᵢ(h) over the selected experts.

Worked: in the lab, one token's router probabilities over four experts are [0.349, 0.176, 0.356, 0.119]. Top-2 selects expert 2 (0.356) and expert 0 (0.349). Renormalised: 0.356/0.705 = 0.505 and 0.349/0.705 = 0.495. The output is 0.505 × Expert₂(h) + 0.495 × Expert₀(h); experts 1 and 3 are never run for this token. The selection (which experts) is a hard, non-differentiable choice, but the gating weights are differentiable, so gradients flow to the router through them: if expert 2's contribution would reduce the loss, the router learns to give it more weight.

2. Experts

Each expert is an ordinary MLP with its own weights, the same shape as a dense model's MLP or smaller. In Mixtral, each expert is a SwiGLU MLP with hidden size 14,336: three matrices of 4,096 × 14,336, 176.2 million parameters per expert, eight per layer. Experts in the same layer have identical structure; they become different only through training, because each sees a different subset of tokens. There is nothing in the architecture that assigns them topics.

3. Top-k: why one or two

The choice of k sets the trade-off between compute and flexibility. Shazeer et al.'s 2017 sparsely gated MoE used k of at least two, reasoning that the router needs to compare experts to learn. Switch Transformer (Fedus, Zoph and Shazeer, 2021) simplified routing to k = 1: each token goes to a single expert. This halves expert compute and communication relative to k = 2 and, the authors found, still trains well, with routing that is simpler and more stable. Mixtral uses k = 2, combining two experts per token. Newer designs use many small experts and larger k (DeepSeek-V3 selects 8 of 256 routed experts). With k fixed, adding experts adds parameters but not per-token compute; that is the knob MoE exposes.

4. Counting Mixtral's parameters by hand

Mixtral's paper lists its configuration: model width 4,096, 32 layers, 32 query heads and 8 key-value heads of dimension 128 (grouped-query attention, lm5), expert hidden size 14,336, vocabulary 32,000, 8 experts, top-2. Counting:

attention per layer   2 × 4,096 × 4,096 + 2 × 4,096 × 1,024     =    41.9M
one expert            3 × 4,096 × 14,336                         =   176.2M
router                4,096 × 8                                  =     0.03M
total per layer       41.9M + 8 × 176.2M (+ router, norms)       = 1,451.3M
active per layer      41.9M + 2 × 176.2M (+ router, norms)       =   394.3M
embeddings + LM head  2 × 32,000 × 4,096                         =   262.1M
TOTAL                 32 × 1,451.3M + 262.1M                     ≈ 46.7B
ACTIVE                32 × 394.3M + 262.1M                       ≈ 12.9B

That matches the paper's description: "each token has access to 47B parameters, but only uses 13B active parameters during inference". The total is not 8 × 7B = 56B because attention, embeddings and norms are shared rather than replicated. Converting with lm3's rules: a forward pass costs about 2 × 12.9B ≈ 26 GFLOP per token, like a 13B dense model, but the weights take 46.7B × 2 bytes ≈ 93 GB in bf16, like a 47B dense model.

5. Why MoE improves quality per FLOP

Scaling laws say loss falls with parameters. In a dense model, parameters and compute per token are tied together; MoE unties them. For a fixed compute per token, an MoE model has several times more parameters, and its experts can store more knowledge and represent more specialised functions, each trained on the tokens routed to it. Empirically this pays: Switch Transformer reported up to 7× increases in pre-training speed over T5 baselines with the same computational resources, gains across all 101 languages in a multilingual setting, and a 4× speed-up over T5-XXL with its trillion-parameter models. The gains per FLOP are real; the costs move to memory and communication.

6. Load balancing

Training a router by gradient descent has a failure mode. Early on, by chance, some experts receive slightly more tokens; they improve faster; the router learns that they give lower loss and sends them even more tokens; the others receive few tokens and stay poor. This rich-get-richer dynamic, expert collapse, wastes most of the parameters and, when experts sit on different GPUs, overloads some devices while others idle. Every MoE design includes a counter-measure.

Switch Transformer's is an auxiliary load-balancing loss added to the language-model loss: loss_aux = α·N·Σᵢ fᵢ·Pᵢ, where N is the number of experts, fᵢ the fraction of the batch's tokens dispatched to expert i, and Pᵢ the average router probability given to expert i over the batch. f is not differentiable (it counts discrete choices) but P is, so the gradient pushes router probabilities down for over-used experts. Under perfectly uniform routing every fᵢ and Pᵢ is 1/N, and N·Σ (1/N)(1/N) = 1, which is the minimum. Worked for four experts: with f = [0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1] and P = [0.35, 0.30, 0.20, 0.15], Σ fᵢPᵢ = 0.140 + 0.090 + 0.040 + 0.015 = 0.285, times N = 4 gives 1.14; with near-collapse, f = [1, 0, 0, 0] and P = [0.9, 0.04, 0.03, 0.03], the loss is 4 × 0.9 = 3.6. Switch uses α = 10⁻², large enough to keep balance, small enough not to overwhelm the main objective; they swept values from 10⁻¹ to 10⁻⁵.

7. Capacity and dropped tokens

For efficient batched computation, especially across devices, each expert processes tokens in a fixed-size buffer. Its size is the expert capacity: (tokens per batch ÷ number of experts) × capacity factor. With 1,024 tokens, 8 experts and capacity factor 1.25, each expert accepts up to 160 tokens. If more tokens choose an expert than its capacity, the overflow tokens are dropped for this layer: they skip the expert computation and their vectors pass unchanged through the residual connection to the next layer. That is a quality loss, not a crash, but it should be rare. The capacity factor trades waste for safety: above 1.0, buffers have padding that costs memory and compute; below or near 1.0, any imbalance drops tokens.

The lab simulates top-1 routing of 1,024 tokens to 8 experts. With a balanced router the largest expert receives 151 tokens: factor 1.0 (capacity 128) drops 3.6% of tokens, factor 1.25 (160) drops none. With a skewed router the largest expert receives 247 tokens: factor 1.0 drops 26.5%, 1.25 drops 17.1%, 2.0 (256) drops none. Load balancing and capacity are two halves of one design: good balancing lets you use a small capacity factor without dropping. Inference systems typically do not enforce capacity at all, processing every token with its chosen experts.

8. Expert parallelism and all-to-all

A large MoE model's experts do not fit on one GPU, so they are distributed: with 8 GPUs and 8 experts per layer, each GPU holds one expert of every layer (expert parallelism), alongside the usual data, tensor and pipeline parallelism (dl9). At each MoE layer, every GPU has a batch of tokens whose chosen experts live on various GPUs. Tokens are sent to their experts with an all-to-all collective (gpu8), in which every GPU sends a different slice to every other GPU; experts run; a second all-to-all returns the outputs. Two all-to-alls per MoE layer per forward pass, and two more in the backward pass, make communication a first-class cost: within a node with NVLink it is manageable, across nodes it can dominate step time. Load imbalance across devices also matters, because the slowest GPU sets the pace. GShard (Lepikhin et al., 2020) established much of this machinery for training very large sparse models.

9. MoE at inference time

For serving, MoE changes the arithmetic of lm5 and lm13. Memory must hold all experts: Mixtral's 93 GB of bf16 weights need two 80 GB GPUs (or quantisation, lm12: about 47 GB in 8-bit, about 25 GB in 4-bit), plus KV cache, which in Mixtral is modest thanks to grouped-query attention. Compute per token is that of the active parameters, about 26 GFLOP. Bandwidth is subtle. Decoding is memory-bound; at batch size 1, each step reads only the two selected experts per layer plus shared weights, about 13B parameters' worth, so it is fast. But as the batch grows, different tokens choose different experts, and soon every expert in every layer is read each step, so the step reads nearly the full 47B parameters while doing compute for only the active part per token. MoE is therefore most efficient at large batch, where the arithmetic per expert fills up, and its advantage over a dense model of equal total size is mainly compute; its disadvantage against a dense model of equal active size is mainly memory. Prefill, which is compute-bound, benefits from the reduced FLOPs.

10. What experts learn

Researchers have looked for interpretable specialisation. The Mixtral authors analysed which experts were selected for text from different domains (for example ArXiv papers, biology, philosophy, code) and found no obvious pattern of experts by subject; they did observe that routing showed structure related to syntax, with tokens such as indentation in code often going to the same experts, and that consecutive tokens often shared experts. Other studies find specialisation by token type and position. The practical upshot: you cannot route "maths questions to the maths expert" or prune experts by topic without measurement; experts are an efficiency device, not a modular knowledge base.

11. Newer designs

Since Switch and Mixtral, MoE design has moved towards fine-grained experts (many small experts, so each token can use a more specific combination) and shared experts (one or more experts every token always uses, capturing common knowledge so routed experts can specialise). DeepSeek-V3 is a prominent public example: each MoE layer has 1 shared expert and 256 routed experts, each token uses 8 routed experts, and the model has 671B total parameters with 37B activated per token. It also replaces the auxiliary loss with auxiliary-loss-free balancing: each expert has a bias added to its routing score, used only for selection, and the bias is decreased when the expert is overloaded and increased when it is under-loaded, steering balance without a competing gradient. Its report notes training on 14.8 trillion tokens with stable training and no irrecoverable loss spikes.

12. Quantisation and offloading of experts

Because memory is the binding constraint for serving MoE models, the memory techniques of lm12 matter even more. Quantising experts to 8 or 4 bits shrinks the dominant part of the model, and experts tolerate quantisation about as well as dense MLPs. A second technique exploits sparsity in time: at small batch only a few experts are active per step, so systems can keep the shared layers and recently used experts in GPU memory and stream others from CPU memory as needed. This makes large MoE models usable on hardware that could not hold them, at the cost of latency whenever a non-resident expert is needed. It works best for single-user, local inference; at high batch nearly every expert is used every step and offloading stalls.

13. Worked serving comparison

Compare serving Mixtral 8x7B with a dense 13B model and a dense 47B-class model on 80 GB GPUs. The dense 13B fits on one GPU with plenty of room for KV cache and computes about 26 GFLOP per token. Mixtral in bf16 needs two GPUs for its 93 GB of weights, also computes about 26 GFLOP per token, and at batch 1 reads only about 26 GB of weights per step (active parameters), so single-stream speed is similar to the dense 13B's; at batch 32 or more it reads close to all 93 GB per step but serves many more tokens per step. A dense 47B model needs two GPUs, reads 94 GB per step at any batch size and computes 94 GFLOP per token. In quality per serving dollar, Mixtral sits between the two dense models in cost and close to or above the larger one in quality on the benchmarks its authors report, which is the case for MoE in one sentence.

14. Training cost, worked

Training compute follows active parameters (lm7): C ≈ 6 × N_active × D. For a Mixtral-sized model trained on, say, 8 trillion tokens, that is 6 × 12.9 × 10⁹ × 8 × 10¹² ≈ 6.2 × 10²³ FLOPs, the same order as a dense 13B model on the same data and about a quarter of what a dense 47B model would need. The extra costs are not FLOPs: memory for all experts' weights, gradients and optimiser states (16 bytes per total parameter with mixed-precision Adam, gpu9, so about 750 GB of model state for 47B parameters, sharded across many GPUs), the all-to-all communication, and engineering effort for stable routing. That is the MoE bargain at training time: far fewer FLOPs for a given quality, paid for with memory, bandwidth and complexity.

15. When to choose MoE

For pretraining at large scale with abundant accelerators and fast interconnect, MoE gives more quality per FLOP and is often the better use of a compute budget. For serving at high throughput, MoE's lower FLOPs per token help, provided you have the memory for all experts and batch enough to use them efficiently. For small deployments (a single GPU, edge devices, low traffic) a dense model of similar active size is usually simpler and cheaper to serve, and for fine-tuning on small datasets dense models are less finicky. Choosing an open MoE model for a product means planning memory for total parameters, quantising if necessary, and benchmarking throughput at your real batch sizes.

The gradient through a discrete choice

Top-k selection is not differentiable, so how does the router learn? Through the gating weights: the output Σ gᵢEᵢ(h) depends smoothly on gᵢ, which depends on the router logits, so the loss's gradient tells the router to raise the weight of selected experts whose outputs help and lower the weight of those that hurt. Experts not selected receive no gradient for that token, and the router receives no direct signal about how unselected experts would have done. That missing counterfactual is why routers can lock into early choices and why exploration aids exist: noise added to router logits during training (noisy top-k gating, from Shazeer et al.), auxiliary balancing, and in some designs random routing for a fraction of tokens.

Training instability and the router z-loss

Large MoE models have been reported to suffer more training instability than dense ones, partly from router logits growing large, which makes the softmax saturate and routing flip abruptly. One remedy, introduced in follow-up work on stable sparse models, is a router z-loss: a small penalty on the squared log-sum-exp of the router logits, which keeps them in a moderate range. Switch Transformer also found it helpful to compute the router in float32 even when the rest of the model used bfloat16 ("selective precision"), and to initialise weights with a reduced scale. These are small details with large effects at scale, typical of MoE engineering.

Token-choice versus expert-choice routing

In standard token-choice routing, each token picks its top-k experts and experts may overflow. In expert-choice routing, each expert picks its top-c tokens from the batch, which guarantees perfect load balance by construction, at the cost that some tokens may be chosen by no expert (and others by several). Expert choice works well in training but is awkward for autoregressive generation, because an expert's choice depends on the whole batch, including tokens from other sequences, which leaks information between requests and breaks causality within a sequence. Most decoder LLMs therefore use token-choice routing with balancing.

Upcycling dense checkpoints

Instead of training an MoE from scratch, a dense checkpoint can be "upcycled": copy its MLP into each of the E experts, add a freshly initialised router, and continue training. The experts start identical and diverge as routing sends them different tokens. This reuses the dense model's training compute and often reaches good MoE quality faster than training from scratch, and it explains why some open MoE models are released alongside dense models of the same family. The analogy with lm5's GQA uptraining is direct: convert an existing architecture, then train briefly to adapt.

Communication-aware design

Because all-to-all traffic scales with tokens × hidden size per MoE layer, designs reduce it in several ways: placing experts so that common routes stay within a node, restricting each token's experts to a limited number of devices (node-limited routing), compressing dispatched activations to low precision, and overlapping communication with computation of other micro-batches. These considerations, not only model quality, determine how many experts and how large k a production MoE uses, and they are why MoE is as much a systems design as an architecture.

Load balance across devices, not just experts

Balancing the number of tokens per expert is necessary but not sufficient in expert-parallel training: if the busiest experts happen to sit on the same device, that device becomes the bottleneck. Systems therefore track load per device, place experts to spread expected load, sometimes replicate popular experts across devices, and in some designs add device-level balancing terms. The goal is that every GPU finishes its expert computation at about the same time, because the all-to-all that follows waits for the slowest one.

MoE and scaling laws

Scaling studies of MoE models find that, at fixed compute per token, adding experts lowers loss with diminishing returns, and that an MoE behaves roughly like a dense model with some effective parameter count between its active and total sizes. For planning, the useful rules are: training FLOPs ≈ 6 × active parameters × tokens; memory ∝ total parameters; and quality per FLOP improves with sparsity up to a point set by communication costs and routing efficiency. Compute-optimal token counts (lm7) should be reasoned about with these distinctions in mind rather than with total parameters.

The public case: Switch Transformer (Fedus, Zoph and Shazeer, 2021) and Mixtral (Jiang et al., 2024)

Mixture of experts is an old idea, revived for deep learning by Shazeer et al.'s 2017 sparsely gated MoE layer in LSTM language models. Switch Transformer brought it to transformers at scale with deliberate simplifications. Each token is routed to a single expert (top-1, the "switch"), which halves routing compute and communication relative to top-2 and simplified the implementation. Load is balanced with the auxiliary loss α·N·Σ fᵢPᵢ at α = 10⁻². Each expert has a capacity set by a capacity factor, with overflow tokens passed through the residual connection. Training was stabilised with selective float32 precision in the router and smaller initialisation. The results: up to 7× increases in pre-training speed over T5-Base and T5-Large baselines with the same computational resources, gains over mT5-Base across all 101 languages tested, and trillion-parameter models (Switch-C, with 1.6 trillion parameters and 2,048 experts) pre-trained on the C4 corpus with a 4× speed-up over T5-XXL.

Mixtral 8x7B, released by Mistral AI with an Apache 2.0 licence, made a high-quality MoE something engineers could download and run. Each of its 32 layers has 8 SwiGLU experts and uses 2 per token, giving 47B total and 13B active parameters, with a 32K-token context. The paper reports that Mixtral outperforms or matches Llama 2 70B and GPT-3.5 across all its evaluated benchmarks, with particularly large margins over Llama 2 70B on mathematics, code generation and multilingual tasks, and that its instruction-tuned version surpassed GPT-3.5 Turbo, Claude-2.1, Gemini Pro and Llama 2 70B chat on human evaluation benchmarks at the time. It is the standard worked example for total versus active parameters, and its serving profile (fast per token, heavy in memory) taught many teams the MoE trade-off first-hand.

Failure modes in practice

Out-of-memory at deployment. Teams size GPUs by active parameters and discover the model needs memory for all experts. Plan memory from total parameters; quantise if needed.

Disappointing throughput at low batch. Each step reads many experts' weights while doing little compute. Benchmark at production batch sizes; MoE rewards high batch.

Imbalanced experts under fine-tuning. Routing shifts and some experts dominate, hurting quality. Keep balancing terms, use lower learning rates, consider freezing the router.

Communication bottlenecks in training. Cross-node all-to-all dominates step time. Keep expert-parallel groups within fast interconnect domains and limit cross-node routing.

Token dropping in training. A capacity factor that is too low silently drops tokens. Monitor drop rates per layer and expert load statistics.

Why MoE became mainstream

Three developments made MoE practical beyond research labs: training recipes (balancing losses or biases, router precision, initialisation) that made routing stable; systems support for expert parallelism and all-to-all in training frameworks and for ragged expert batches in serving engines; and open releases such as Mixtral that let engineers measure the trade-offs themselves. The result is that "how many parameters does it have?" is no longer a sufficient question about a model; you also need "how many are active, and how are they routed?"

What to monitor

In training: per-layer expert load distribution (fraction of tokens per expert), auxiliary loss, dropped-token rate, router logit magnitudes, and step time split between compute and all-to-all. In serving: memory headroom, throughput and latency versus batch size, and expert activation patterns on real traffic, which can reveal whether quantising or offloading rarely used experts is viable. Compare these patterns before and after fine-tuning or a traffic shift, because a change in routing is often the first sign of a change in behaviour.

Goal: count Mixtral's total and active parameters from its configuration, run a small top-2 MoE layer by hand, compute the Switch load-balancing loss for balanced, skewed and collapsed routing, and simulate how the capacity factor controls dropped tokens. NumPy only, instant.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# ---- 1. Mixtral 8x7B: total versus active parameters (config from the Mixtral paper, Table 1)
d, L, dff, heads, kv_heads, hd, vocab, E, k = 4096, 32, 14336, 32, 8, 128, 32000, 8, 2
attn = d * heads * hd * 2 + d * kv_heads * hd * 2          # W_Q, W_O full; W_K, W_V for 8 KV heads (GQA)
expert = 3 * d * dff                                        # one SwiGLU expert
router = d * E
per_layer_total = attn + E * expert + router + 2 * d
per_layer_active = attn + k * expert + router + 2 * d
emb = 2 * vocab * d
total, active = L * per_layer_total + emb + d, L * per_layer_active + emb + d
print(f"attention/layer {attn/1e6:.1f}M, one expert {expert/1e6:.1f}M, router {router:,}")
print(f"total {total/1e9:.2f}B parameters, active per token {active/1e9:.2f}B ({active/total:.0%})")
print(f"bf16 weights to hold in memory: {total*2/1e9:.0f} GB; forward FLOPs per token ~2 x active = {2*active/1e9:.1f} GFLOP")

# ---- 2. one MoE layer forward pass, top-2 routing, for a few tokens
d_small, E_small, T = 8, 4, 6
x = rng.normal(size=(T, d_small))
W_r = rng.normal(0, 0.5, (d_small, E_small))
experts = [rng.normal(0, d_small ** -0.5, (d_small, d_small)) for _ in range(E_small)]
logits = x @ W_r
probs = np.exp(logits - logits.max(1, keepdims=True)); probs /= probs.sum(1, keepdims=True)
top2 = np.argsort(-probs, axis=1)[:, :2]
gates = np.take_along_axis(probs, top2, 1); gates /= gates.sum(1, keepdims=True)      # renormalise over the chosen two
y = np.zeros_like(x)
for t in range(T):
    for j in range(2):
        y[t] += gates[t, j] * (x[t] @ experts[top2[t, j]])
print("\nrouter probabilities, token 0:", probs[0].round(3), "-> experts", top2[0], "with weights", gates[0].round(3))
print("experts chosen per token:", top2.tolist())

# ---- 3. the Switch Transformer load-balancing loss: N * sum_i f_i * P_i  (1.0 when perfectly balanced)
def aux_loss(f, P): return len(f) * float(np.dot(f, P))
print(f"\naux loss balanced:   {aux_loss(np.full(4, .25), np.full(4, .25)):.3f}")
print(f"aux loss imbalanced: {aux_loss(np.array([.4, .3, .2, .1]), np.array([.35, .3, .2, .15])):.3f}")
print(f"aux loss collapsed:  {aux_loss(np.array([1., 0, 0, 0]), np.array([.9, .04, .03, .03])):.3f}")

# ---- 4. capacity factor and dropped tokens (top-1 routing, 1,024 tokens, 8 experts)
tokens, n_exp = 1024, 8
for skew, label in [(0.0, "balanced router"), (0.6, "skewed router")]:
    pref = np.exp(skew * rng.normal(size=n_exp)); pref /= pref.sum()
    choice = rng.choice(n_exp, tokens, p=pref)
    counts = np.bincount(choice, minlength=n_exp)
    for cf in (1.0, 1.25, 2.0):
        cap = int(cf * tokens / n_exp)
        dropped = np.maximum(counts - cap, 0).sum()
        print(f"{label:15s} capacity factor {cf:4.2f}: capacity {cap:3d} per expert, max load {counts.max():3d}, dropped {dropped/tokens:5.1%}")

Estimate — sizing an MoE deployment

You plan to serve an MoE model with 64 layers, d = 6,144, 16 experts per layer of 3 × 6,144 × 16,384 weights each, top-2 routing, and attention of 2 × 6,144² + 2 × 6,144 × 1,024 per layer (GQA). Ignore embeddings. Compute total and active parameters, bf16 memory and FLOPs per token, and say whether 8 × 80 GB GPUs suffice.

Answer

One expert: 3 × 6,144 × 16,384 = 302.0M. Attention: 2 × 37.7M + 2 × 6.3M = 88.1M per layer.

Total per layer: 88.1M + 16 × 302.0M = 4,920M; × 64 ≈ 315B parameters. Active per layer: 88.1M + 2 × 302.0M = 692M; × 64 ≈ 44B active.

Memory: 315B × 2 bytes ≈ 630 GB in bf16: more than the 640 GB of eight 80 GB GPUs once KV cache and overheads are included, so plan for 8-bit weights (about 315 GB) or FP8, or more GPUs.

Compute: about 2 × 44B ≈ 88 GFLOP per token, like a 44B dense model. Expect good throughput at large batch; at small batch, steps will read most experts' weights and be bandwidth-limited, so benchmark at production batch sizes before committing.

Diagnose — the experts that stopped learning

During MoE pre-training, monitoring shows 3 of 16 experts in several layers receiving almost no tokens, and the loss improving more slowly than a dense baseline's per FLOP. What is happening and what do you change?

Answer

Expert collapse: the router has concentrated tokens on a subset of experts, so the starved ones receive almost no gradient and their parameters are wasted, reducing the effective model size. Check the auxiliary loss weight (it may be too small or missing in those layers), router logit magnitudes (large logits make routing brittle), and whether capacity limits are dropping tokens from the popular experts. Remedies: increase the balancing coefficient or switch to bias-based balancing, add router noise early in training, apply a router z-loss and float32 router computation, and monitor per-expert load continuously so collapse is caught early.
