A team builds a support assistant on a frontier model. The prompt is 800 tokens of instructions and examples, and the quality is excellent. Then traffic grows to two million tickets a day, and finance points out the bill: at illustrative API prices of $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output tokens, 800 tokens in and 150 out per ticket comes to about $9,300 a day, roughly $3.4 million a year, for a task that is, honestly, narrow: classify the ticket into one of forty intents and write a three-sentence summary. Latency is also a complaint: the big model takes over a second to start answering, and the agents waiting for the summary notice.

The obvious idea is to use a small model, say an 8-billion-parameter open model running on the team's own GPUs. Prompted with the same 800 tokens, it is visibly worse: it confuses neighbouring intents, misses the customer's actual request in long tickets, and ignores the formatting rules half the time. The big model clearly knows how to do the task; the small one does not, and the team does not have a hand-labelled dataset of a hundred thousand tickets to train it from scratch.

But they do have something nearly as good: a model that does the task well and can be asked to do it as many times as they like. Knowledge distillation is the family of techniques for turning that into training signal: a large teacher model produces outputs, probabilities or internal states, and a smaller student model is trained to imitate them. Done well, the student recovers most of the teacher's quality on the task, costs a few hundred dollars a day of GPU time instead of thousands, answers faster, and runs where the data has to stay.

The same idea, applied at pretraining scale, is how many of the small open models you can download today were made: Meta's Llama 3.2 1B and 3B, Google's Gemma 2 2B and 9B and NVIDIA's Minitron models were trained to match a bigger sibling's predictions rather than only the raw next token.

This lesson builds distillation from the ground up. It starts with what kind of signal a teacher can give, depending on whether you have an API or the weights; then logit distillation and why temperature matters; the storage arithmetic that decides whether you can afford it; sequence-level distillation, the workhorse of production; exposure bias and on-policy distillation; what to do when the teacher and student use different tokenizers; pruning plus distillation for building small base models; and how to run a distillation project in production without fooling yourself. The reasoning-specific version, distilling long chains of thought into small models, has its own lesson (rs6); here the focus is general LLM distillation.

The mental model: an apprentice who can watch the master

Think of an apprentice chef learning from a master. There are different levels of access. The apprentice can taste the finished dishes (the teacher's outputs). With more access, they can hear the master think aloud: "this could take a little more salt, maybe lemon, definitely not sugar" (the teacher's probabilities over choices). With full access, they can watch the master's hands at every step (the teacher's internal states). The more they can observe, the faster they learn, but each level needs more cooperation from the master.

The analogy also shows the main trap. An apprentice who has only ever watched the master cook never practises recovering from their own mistakes. On their first solo night, one wrong move leads to a situation the master never got into, and they have no idea what to do. That is exposure bias, and the fix, letting the apprentice cook while the master comments on their dish, is on-policy distillation.

Where the analogy breaks: a model's "taste" is a probability distribution over tens of thousands of tokens at every position, and the student may literally speak a different vocabulary from the teacher (a different tokenizer), which has no kitchen equivalent.

What the teacher can give you

The first question in any distillation project is what access you have to the teacher, because it decides which methods are available.

Reading the figure left to right: with text only, you can generate outputs and fine-tune the student on them (sequence-level distillation). Some APIs also return log-probabilities of the top few candidate tokens at each position, a partial view of the teacher's distribution. With the teacher's weights, you can compute its full output distribution (logits) at any position of any text, including text the student wrote, which enables logit and on-policy distillation. And with weights you can also read internal activations, which some methods match directly, or even cut the teacher down and use it as the student's starting point.

The big picture in four moves

Almost every distillation project, from a support classifier to a pretrained 1B model, follows the same four moves. First, choose prompts: the inputs the student must handle, which should look like real traffic. Second, get the teacher's signal on those prompts: text, top-k probabilities or full logits. Third, train the student to match that signal, with a loss suited to the signal. Fourth, evaluate the student on fresh inputs and decide whether it can replace the teacher, for which traffic, with what fallback. The rest of the lesson is how to do each move well, and the mistakes that make a student look good in the lab and fail in production. Keep the four moves in mind: almost every failure story later in the lesson is a mistake in one of them, most often the first or the last.

1. The deal you are making

Distillation trades a one-time training cost for a recurring serving saving. The teacher's knowledge already exists; you pay once to transfer the part your task needs into a model small enough to serve cheaply. That framing tells you when it is worth it. If traffic is low, prompting the big model is simpler and the bill is small. If the task changes weekly, the student will keep going stale. If the task is broad (general chat in any domain), a small student cannot hold everything the teacher does, and it will lose quality in places you did not test. Distillation pays best for a narrow, stable, high-volume task: classification, extraction, routing, summarisation in a fixed format, a specific coding style, a fixed tool-calling pattern.

The worked economics from the opening: 2M tickets a day × 800 input tokens = 1.6 billion input tokens, at $3 per million = $4,800; 2M × 150 output tokens = 300 million output tokens, at $15 per million = $4,500. Total about $9,300 a day. Suppose (an assumption for illustration) that the 8B student serves this load on four rented GPUs at $3 per GPU-hour: 4 × 24 × $3 = $288 a day. The distillation itself is cheap by comparison: generating 50,000 teacher outputs costs about 50,000 × (800 × $3 + 300 × $15) / 1,000,000 ≈ $345, and a LoRA fine-tune of an 8B model on them takes a few GPU-hours. The engineering time, the evaluation suite and the ongoing refreshes are the real cost, which is why the decision is about stability and volume, not the training bill.

2. Soft targets and temperature, worked

The original form of distillation (Hinton, Vinyals and Dean, 2015) trains the student to match the teacher's whole output distribution, not just its top answer. Lesson rs6 introduces the idea; here we work through the details you need to implement it for a language model.

At every position, the teacher outputs a logit, a raw score, for every token in its vocabulary. Take a toy four-token vocabulary at one position in a ticket summary: "refund", "return", "exchange", "banana", with teacher logits z = [4.0, 2.0, 1.0, −1.0]. Softmax at temperature 1 gives probabilities proportional to ez: e4 = 54.60, e2 = 7.39, e1 = 2.72, e−1 = 0.37, total 65.07, so p = [0.839, 0.114, 0.042, 0.006]. A hard label would say only "refund". The soft distribution adds that "return" is a plausible alternative, "exchange" less so, and "banana" is nonsense. That extra structure, which Hinton called dark knowledge, is what the student gains.

Notice that most of the structure is squashed into small numbers: 0.042 versus 0.006 matters, but in a loss dominated by the 0.839, it barely moves the gradient. Temperature fixes this. Divide every logit by T before the softmax, for teacher and student alike. At T = 2 the logits become [2.0, 1.0, 0.5, −0.5]; exponentiating gives 7.39, 2.72, 1.65, 0.61, total 12.36, so pT=2 = [0.598, 0.220, 0.133, 0.049]. The order is unchanged, but the second and third choices now carry real weight in the loss, so the student is pushed to get the shape of the distribution right, not only the winner.

The distillation loss at one position is the KL divergence from the teacher's softened distribution to the student's softened distribution:

L_soft = T² · KL( softmax(z_teacher / T) ‖ softmax(z_student / T) )
L      = α · L_soft + (1 − α) · CE(hard label, softmax(z_student))

Two details deserve explanation. The T² factor: softening both distributions makes their differences, and so the gradients, smaller; for large T the gradient with respect to the student's logits shrinks roughly as 1/T². Multiplying by T² keeps the soft loss on the same scale as the hard loss whatever T you choose, so changing T does not secretly change the learning rate. The mixture with α: when ground-truth tokens exist (pretraining text, labelled data), combining the soft loss with ordinary cross-entropy on the true token keeps the student anchored to reality where the teacher is wrong. When the only data is teacher output, the hard-label term is the teacher's own sampled token and adds little.

For language models, this is applied at every token position of every training sequence and averaged. With a large vocabulary, temperatures near 1 to 2 are common; very high T flattens the targets over 100,000 tokens into near-uniform mush.

3. The storage arithmetic of logit distillation

Logit distillation has an obvious cost: you need the teacher's distribution at every position you train on. There are two ways to get it, and the arithmetic decides between them.

Online: load the teacher next to the student and run a teacher forward pass on each training batch. Nothing is stored, but every training step pays for a forward pass through a model that may be ten times bigger than the student, and both must fit in GPU memory. For a 70B teacher in bf16 that is 140 GB of weights before activations.

Offline: run the teacher once over the training data and save its outputs. Here the size bites. A vocabulary of 128,256 tokens (Llama 3's) at 2 bytes per logit is about 256 KB per token. One billion training tokens would need about 256 TB; ten billion about 2.6 PB. Nobody stores that. The standard solution is top-k storage: keep only the k most likely tokens and their probabilities at each position. With k = 64, a 4-byte token id and a 2-byte probability, that is 64 × 6 = 384 bytes per token, about 668 times smaller: 384 GB per billion tokens. At training time you renormalise the k stored probabilities to sum to 1 and compute the KL over those tokens only.

Truncation is not free. If the top 64 tokens cover 97% of the teacher's probability at some position, renormalising inflates each by 1/0.97, and the student is never told anything about the remaining 3%. In practice the top few dozen tokens cover nearly all of the mass at most positions of ordinary text, and the bias is small; on high-entropy positions (the first word of a creative answer, an arbitrary name) it is larger. Store a measurement of the covered mass, so you can see how much you dropped.

API log-probabilities are a much weaker version of the same idea: providers that expose them usually cap the count at about 20 alternatives per position, and they are often available only for the generated tokens. They can be used as an approximate soft target, but most API-based projects simply use the text.

4. Sequence-level distillation: the production workhorse

Sequence-level distillation (Kim and Rush, 2016) is the simplest method: generate complete outputs with the teacher, then fine-tune the student on (prompt, teacher output) pairs with ordinary supervised fine-tuning, exactly as in lm6. It looks like a crude approximation of logit distillation, and per token it is: the student sees one sampled token where it could have seen a distribution. But it has four practical advantages that make it the default.

First, it needs only text, so any teacher works, including closed API models. Second, it is tokenizer-agnostic: the student tokenizes the teacher's text with its own tokenizer. Third, it costs nothing extra at training time: it is ordinary SFT, with the usual tools (LoRA, QLoRA, any trainer). Fourth, and most important, whole outputs can be checked. A probability vector at position 37 cannot be marked right or wrong, but a complete JSON object can be validated, a classification can be compared across five teacher samples, a summary can be scored by a judge, a code answer can be run against tests. Filtering lets the dataset be better than the teacher's average output.

A typical pipeline for the ticket task, step by step:

  1. Collect prompts from real traffic: 60,000 tickets sampled across intents, languages, lengths and months, with personal data scrubbed (ev11 covers the curation). Hold out 5,000 for evaluation, never to be used for training.
  2. Generate with the teacher using the existing production prompt, sampling each ticket three times at a moderate temperature.
  3. Filter: drop outputs that fail the JSON schema; keep tickets where at least two of three samples agree on the intent; send disagreements to a human queue or drop them; have a judge model check summaries for faithfulness on a sample.
  4. Train the student on the surviving pairs, without the 800-token prompt: the instructions now live in the weights, which also saves serving cost on every call.
  5. Evaluate on the held-out tickets, by slice, against the teacher and any human labels.

A useful refinement is to distil the teacher's reasoning as well as its answer. Hsieh et al.'s "distilling step-by-step" (2023) asked the teacher for a short rationale with each label and trained the student on both, as two tasks. The rationales act as extra supervision about why a label is right; their 770M-parameter T5 student (T5 is Google's encoder-decoder model) outperformed few-shot prompted 540B PaLM on one benchmark using 80% of the available data. At serving time the student can skip the rationale and output only the label, so the extra cost is only at training time.

5. Exposure bias, and why the student must practise on its own text

In methods A and B the student is always trained on a prefix someone else wrote: at position 50, it predicts token 51 given the teacher's (or the corpus's) first 50 tokens. This is called teacher forcing. At inference there is no teacher: token 51 is predicted from the student's own first 50 tokens. If the student slipped at token 20, say it started summarising the wrong part of the ticket, it is now in a state that never appeared in training, and it has never learned how to get back on track. Errors compound, which is why distilled students often look fine on short outputs and wander on long ones. This mismatch is exposure bias.

On-policy distillation fixes it by changing who writes the text. In each training step, the student generates responses to a batch of prompts; the teacher then runs one forward pass over each response and produces its full next-token distribution at every position of the student's text; and the student is trained to move its distribution towards the teacher's at those positions. The teacher pass is cheap compared with generation, because it processes all positions in parallel, like a prefill (lm13). Generalized Knowledge Distillation (GKD, Agarwal et al., 2024) packages this as a recipe: mix fixed data and student-generated data in some ratio, and choose the divergence to minimise.

The result is dense, per-token feedback on exactly the situations the student gets itself into: at position 20, where it started drifting, the teacher's distribution tells it which tokens would have been better. Compare reinforcement learning (RL; rs3, rs4), which also trains on the student's own samples but gives one reward for the whole response; on-policy distillation gives a full distribution at every token. The costs are real: the teacher has to be runnable in your training loop (so, usually, open weights), it must share the student's tokenizer or be aligned to it, and every step pays for student sampling plus a teacher forward pass. A common schedule is cheap sequence-level distillation first, then a shorter on-policy stage to fix the long-generation behaviour.

6. Forward versus reverse KL, worked

When the student is too small to imitate everything the teacher does, the direction of the KL decides what it gives up. rs6 introduces the two directions; a worked example makes the difference concrete.

Suppose at the first token of a summary the teacher spreads its probability between two good openings and two bad ones: p = [0.45, 0.45, 0.05, 0.05] over "Customer", "The", "Banana", "Purple". Imagine the student can only produce one of two shapes: a spread student A with q = [0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25], or a focused student B with q = [0.85, 0.05, 0.05, 0.05].

StudentForward KL(p ‖ q)Reverse KL(q ‖ p)
A, spread0.3680.511
B, focused0.7030.431

Working the first cell: KL(p ‖ qA) = 2 × 0.45 × ln(0.45/0.25) + 2 × 0.05 × ln(0.05/0.25) = 0.9 × 0.588 + 0.1 × (−1.609) = 0.529 − 0.161 = 0.368. For student B, the term 0.45 × ln(0.45/0.05) = 0.45 × 2.197 = 0.989 is large, because the teacher puts 45% on "The" and B gives it only 5%, which forward KL punishes heavily. So forward KL prefers A, the student that covers every token the teacher might say, even though A says "Banana" or "Purple" half the time. Reverse KL prefers B: it weights each term by the student's probability, so it mainly asks "is what the student says plausible to the teacher?", and B's 0.85 on a good token scores well while A's 0.25 on each nonsense token is penalised.

For text generation, where averaging two good answers often produces a bad one, the mode-seeking behaviour of reverse KL usually reads better, which was the argument of MiniLLM (Gu et al., 2023). The catch is that reverse KL is an expectation over the student's samples, so it needs on-policy generation. In practice people mix: forward KL (or plain SFT on teacher text) for a stable start, then on-policy training with reverse KL or a blend.

7. When teacher and student speak different vocabularies

Logit distillation compares two distributions over the same vocabulary at the same position. Change the tokenizer and both assumptions break. Suppose the teacher splits "unbelievable" as ["un", "believ", "able"] and the student as ["unbel", "ievable"]. The teacher's distribution at its second position is over the teacher's 128K-token vocabulary and predicts "believ"; the student's second position predicts "ievable" from a different 32K-token vocabulary. There is no position-by-position, token-by-token correspondence to compute a KL over.

You have four options, from simplest to most involved:

  • Use sequence-level distillation. Text is the universal interface: the student re-tokenizes the teacher's output. This is why API-based and cross-family distillation is almost always sequence-level.
  • Choose a teacher from the student's family. Model families usually share a tokenizer across sizes: Meta used Llama 3.1 8B and 70B as teachers for Llama 3.2 1B and 3B, and Google's Gemma 2 small models learned from a larger Gemma-family teacher.
  • Align by text spans. Several methods map positions where both tokenizations hit the same character boundary, and compare probabilities of whole spans (or the first-token probabilities of each) there. It works, at the cost of complexity and some lost signal.
  • Use a vocabulary-free loss. Universal Logit Distillation (Boizard et al., 2024) compares the two distributions after sorting each one's probabilities, using an optimal-transport distance, so it measures whether the student is as confident and as spread as the teacher at aligned positions without needing the tokens to match.

The general lesson: if you plan to do logit or on-policy distillation, pick the student's tokenizer and the teacher together, early.

8. Distilling inside the network: hidden states and pruning

With open weights you can also distil what the teacher computes internally. Feature distillation adds a loss that makes some of the student's hidden states match some of the teacher's, through a learned linear projection when their widths differ. DistilBERT (Sanh et al., 2019) is the classic example: a 6-layer student initialised by taking every other layer of 12-layer BERT, trained with a soft-target loss, the ordinary masked-language-model loss (BERT is Google's 2018 encoder model, trained to fill in masked words) and a cosine loss aligning hidden states. It was 40% smaller and 60% faster while keeping 97% of BERT's language-understanding score.

That idea, start the student from the teacher's own weights, has become the standard recipe for small open LLMs. Pruning removes parts of a trained network: whole layers (depth pruning), or attention heads and MLP (feed-forward layer) channels judged least important by their activations on sample data (width pruning). A pruned model is damaged, but it keeps much of the teacher's structure, so a relatively short retraining with distillation from the unpruned teacher recovers most of the quality. NVIDIA's Minitron (2024) pruned Nemotron-4 15B to 8B and 4B and retrained with distillation on less than 3% of the original training data: deriving each smaller model took up to 40× fewer training tokens than training it from scratch, about 1.8× less compute for the whole family, and the derived models scored up to 16% higher on MMLU (a broad multiple-choice knowledge benchmark) than same-size models trained from scratch. Meta's Llama 3.2 1B and 3B were structurally pruned in one shot from Llama 3.1 8B and then trained with logits from Llama 3.1 8B and 70B as token-level targets. Google trained Gemma 2's 2B and 9B models with knowledge distillation instead of plain next-token prediction.

Why does pretraining-scale distillation help so much? A next-token label gives the student one observed token per position, a noisy sample of the true distribution of continuations. The teacher's distribution is a far better estimate of that distribution, so each training token carries more signal, and a small model given fewer tokens of better targets ends up ahead of one given more tokens of raw labels.

9. A special case: distilling a draft model

Speculative decoding (lm13) speeds up a big model by letting a small draft model propose several tokens that the big model verifies in one pass; the speedup depends on how often the big model accepts the draft's tokens. That makes the draft model's job exactly a distillation objective: match the target model's distribution as closely as possible. DistillSpec (Zhou et al., 2023) distilled draft models on-policy towards their target and reported 10-45% speedups over speculative decoding with an undistilled draft. It is a neat example of a student whose only purpose is agreement with its teacher.

10. Choosing the student, and evaluating it

Choose the student size by working backwards from the serving budget (latency, cost, hardware, memory after quantisation, lm12), then check whether the task fits in that size. A very large gap between teacher and student can make the teacher's distribution too hard to imitate (the capacity gap, discussed in rs6 and in the deep section): a 1B student may learn more from an 8B teacher than from a 400B one. Run a quick pilot with two or three student sizes on a few thousand examples before committing.

Evaluation decides whether the project worked, and the most common mistake is evaluating on data like the training data. A sound evaluation for the ticket student has five parts:

  1. Fresh, held-out production inputs, sampled after the training set was collected, so the evaluation sees drift.
  2. Task quality against ground truth where it exists (human-labelled intents), not only agreement with the teacher, so you notice if the student copied the teacher's systematic mistakes.
  3. Agreement with the teacher by slice: per intent, language, ticket length and customer tier. Averages hide the rare slices the synthetic data underrepresented.
  4. Long-generation behaviour: summaries of the longest tickets, read or judged, since exposure bias shows up with length.
  5. Safety and refusals: the student inherits the teacher's behaviour only where the data showed it. If the training prompts contained no abusive tickets, the student has not learned how to handle them.

Then roll out behind the teacher: shadow traffic first (both run, only the teacher's answer is used), then a fraction of real traffic, with a router that falls back to the teacher when the student's confidence is low or the input is from a slice where it underperforms.

Why soft targets carry more signal per token

A hard label from data is a single sample from the true next-token distribution, and its cross-entropy gradient pushes the observed token up and every other token down in proportion to the student's current probability. The gradient of the soft loss instead pushes each token towards the teacher's probability: for logits, ∂L/∂zi = qi − pi. If the teacher is a good estimate of the true distribution, that is a much lower-variance estimate of the gradient you would get from infinitely many samples of the same context. This is the mechanism behind the sample-efficiency gains at pretraining scale, and it also explains the failure: if the teacher is badly miscalibrated on some region of text, the student faithfully learns the wrong shape there, with no ground-truth samples to contradict it unless you mix in the hard-label loss.

The capacity gap and when distillation pays

Students do not improve monotonically as the teacher gets stronger. Past some ratio, the teacher's distribution contains distinctions the student cannot represent, and forward-KL training spends capacity chasing them. Mirzadeh et al. (2020) showed this in vision and proposed a teacher assistant, an intermediate-sized model distilled first and then used as the teacher for the small model. For LLMs the practical reading is to try a mid-sized teacher when a giant one gives disappointing results. Busbridge et al. (2025) fitted scaling laws for distillation and found that it beats ordinary training of the student when a teacher already exists or will be amortised over many students; if the teacher must be trained just to produce one student, spending that compute on the student directly is often better. That is consistent with industry practice: distillation is used by labs that already train large models.

On-policy distillation as dense reinforcement learning

Reverse KL on student samples can be written as reinforcement learning where the reward at each token is the teacher's log-probability of that token minus the student's. Viewed this way, on-policy distillation is RL with a dense, per-token reward and no reward model to hack: the teacher's log-probabilities are the reward. That explains its properties: it is far more sample-efficient than RL with a single end-of-sequence reward, it stays stable because the target is a fixed distribution rather than a learned scorer, and it cannot teach behaviour beyond the teacher. It also explains a known failure: a mode-seeking student can collapse onto a narrow, safe subset of the teacher's behaviour (short, generic answers), so diversity and length should be monitored, and mixing in some forward-KL data is common.

Top-k truncation is a biased estimator, and how to bound it

Renormalising the top-k probabilities and computing KL over those tokens alone changes the target. The student is pushed to put all its mass on the stored tokens, when the teacher put (1 − covered mass) elsewhere. Two common fixes: add a single "everything else" bucket with the residual mass, and give the student a matching bucket equal to its probability outside the stored tokens, which keeps the total correct; or store the teacher's log-sum-exp so the stored logits can be turned into exact probabilities without renormalising. Either way, logging the covered mass per position lets you check that truncation is not concentrated in exactly the positions that matter, such as the first token of an answer or the choice of a tool name.

Synthetic prompt distribution is the hidden variable

In sequence-level distillation the teacher's outputs are usually good; what silently fails is the distribution of inputs. If prompts are generated by an LLM rather than sampled from traffic, they tend to be cleaner, shorter, more grammatical and less diverse than real users' inputs, and the student is evaluated in exactly the conditions it was trained in. Mode collapse in prompt generation (many near-duplicates of the same few patterns) shrinks the effective dataset. ev10 covers the tools: seed with real examples, measure diversity with embeddings or n-gram statistics, deduplicate (ev11), and always hold out real inputs for evaluation.

Distilled behaviour includes the teacher's flaws and its alignment

A student trained on a teacher's outputs inherits the teacher's biases, refusal style, hallucination patterns and blind spots, all filtered through what the dataset happened to cover. Safety behaviour transfers only where examples demonstrated it, so a student distilled on benign task data can be noticeably easier to misuse than its teacher. Conversely, a teacher's undesirable patterns can be filtered out of the data. Treat the dataset as the spec of the student's behaviour, and include deliberate examples for refusals, uncertainty ("I can't tell from this ticket") and out-of-scope inputs.

The legal layer is part of the design

Distillation from someone else's model is constrained by contracts, not only technique. Several commercial providers' terms prohibit using outputs to develop models that compete with them; open-weight licences may require attribution, naming conventions for derivatives or restrictions on use. Data you generate also carries the input's privacy obligations: tickets with personal data used as prompts end up, in transformed form, in the training set. Clearing this before generation is far cheaper than discovering it after the student ships, and it often decides the choice of teacher: an open-weight teacher with a permissive licence may be preferred over a stronger closed one.

The public cases

Three public examples show distillation at different scales. DistilBERT (Hugging Face, 2019) made BERT practical on CPUs and phones: 40% smaller, 60% faster, 97% of the language-understanding score. NVIDIA's Minitron (2024) showed that pruning plus distillation could derive 8B and 4B models from a 15B one with up to 40× fewer training tokens each. Meta's Llama 3.2 1B and 3B (2024) brought the same recipe, pruning from Llama 3.1 8B and distillation from the 8B and 70B models, to a widely used model family designed for phones and edge devices.

What goes wrong

The student is great in the lab and worse in production. The symptom is a gap between offline agreement (95%) and online metrics (escalations rising). The cause is usually distribution shift between the prompts used for distillation and live traffic: newer products, other languages, longer tickets, or synthetic prompts that were cleaner than reality. Fix with real-traffic sampling, slice metrics and a periodic refresh.

Long outputs degrade. Summaries of long tickets repeat themselves or drift to the wrong topic, while short ones are fine. This is exposure bias; confirm by plotting quality or the teacher's per-token log-probability of the student's output against length, and fix with an on-policy stage or more long examples.

The student copied the teacher's systematic mistakes. If the teacher always confuses two intents, so will the student, and agreement metrics will look perfect. Only ground-truth labels on a sample reveal it. Filtering by multi-sample agreement does not help either, because the teacher makes the same mistake consistently.

Safety and refusal regressions. The student follows instructions in abusive or out-of-scope tickets that the teacher would have refused, because those cases were not in the data. Add red-team prompts to both the training set and the evaluation suite.

Silent teacher changes. An API teacher is updated by its provider; regenerating data six months later produces subtly different outputs, and the refreshed student behaves differently. Pin the teacher version where possible, and store the generated dataset so you can reproduce a student.

What to track

  • Task quality on fresh, held-out real inputs, by slice, against ground truth where available.
  • Agreement rate with the teacher, by slice, and its trend over weeks (drift detection).
  • Quality versus output length, and the teacher's mean log-probability of student outputs.
  • Router statistics: the share of traffic falling back to the teacher, and the student's confidence distribution.
  • Cost per request and latency percentiles (p50, p95), the reason the project exists.
  • For logit distillation: the top-k covered mass per position and the soft and hard losses separately.

Operating the student

A distilled model is not a one-off artefact; it is the output of a pipeline that must be rerun when the product, the input mix or the teacher changes. Keep the prompt sampler, generation scripts, filters, training configuration and evaluation suite versioned together, so a refresh is a routine job, not a project. Budget a regular cadence, monthly or quarterly, and rerun the evaluation against the current teacher, because the gap you accepted at launch can grow as traffic changes.

    m = p > 0; return float(np.sum(p[m] * np.log(p[m] / q[m])))

# 1. Temperature: the soft target's shape
z = np.array([4.0, 2.0, 1.0, -1.0])        # refund, return, exchange, banana
for T in (1, 2, 4):
    print(f"T={T}:", np.round(softmax(z, T), 3))

# 2. Forward vs reverse KL for a limited student
p = np.array([0.45, 0.45, 0.05, 0.05])
A = np.full(4, 0.25); B = np.array([0.85, 0.05, 0.05, 0.05])
for name, q in [("A spread", A), ("B focused", B)]:
    print(f"{name}: forward {kl(p, q):.3f}  reverse {kl(q, p):.3f}")

# 3. Top-k storage on a long-tailed 128K-token distribution
V = 128_256
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
logits = rng.gumbel(size=V) - 1.2 * np.log(np.arange(1, V + 1))   # Zipf-like tail
probs = softmax(logits)
order = np.argsort(probs)[::-1]
for k in (8, 32, 64, 256):
    covered = probs[order[:k]].sum()
    print(f"k={k:4d}: covered mass {covered:.3f}, {k*6} bytes/token "
          f"vs full bf16 {V*2/1024:.0f} KB")

What to look for:

  1. Step 1 reproduces the lesson's numbers: [0.839, 0.114, 0.042, 0.006] at T = 1 and [0.598, 0.220, 0.133, 0.049] at T = 2. At T = 4 the "banana" token is no longer negligible, which is what too high a temperature looks like.
  2. Step 2 prints forward 0.368 and reverse 0.511 for A, forward 0.703 and reverse 0.431 for B: each direction prefers a different student.
  3. Step 3 shows covered mass rising with k while storage stays in hundreds of bytes per token, against about 250 KB for full logits. Change the 1.2 exponent to 0.8 (a flatter, higher-entropy position) and watch the covered mass fall: those are the positions where truncation bias is largest.

Extend it: train a 4-logit student by gradient descent towards p with forward KL and with reverse KL from a few different starting points, and see which one ends up spread and which one picks a mode.

Design — replace an expensive API call with a distilled student

Your company calls a frontier model 2M times a day to classify support tickets into 40 intents and write a three-sentence summary (800 input tokens, 150 output). It costs about $9,300 a day. You may use the API outputs (the terms allow it for internal models) and an open 8B model on your own GPUs. Plan the project: data, method, evaluation and rollout.

Answer

Data: sample about 60,000 real tickets across intents, languages, lengths and the last few months, scrub personal data, and hold out 5,000 fresh ones (plus a later sample) for evaluation only. Get a few hundred human-labelled intents for ground truth.

Generation and filtering: run the teacher with the production prompt three times per ticket; keep tickets where the intent agrees in at least two samples and the JSON validates; send disagreements to human review; judge-check a sample of summaries for faithfulness. Optionally ask for a one-line rationale for the intent (distilling step-by-step).

Training: sequence-level distillation: SFT the 8B student on (ticket, teacher output) without the 800-token instructions, LoRA first; move to full fine-tuning if a measurable gap remains on hard intents. If long summaries drift and an open teacher with a compatible tokenizer is available, add a short on-policy stage.

Evaluation: on fresh tickets, measure intent accuracy against human labels and agreement with the teacher per slice (intent, language, length), judge summaries of the longest tickets, test abusive and out-of-scope tickets, and measure p95 latency and cost at real load.

Rollout: shadow the student for a week, then route a growing share of traffic to it, with fallback to the teacher for low-confidence predictions and weak slices. Track agreement drift weekly and schedule a quarterly re-distillation.

Diagnose — the logit-distilled model that got worse at names

You distil a 1B model from an 8B teacher of the same family with offline top-k logits, k = 16. The student is good at ordinary prose but noticeably worse than a same-size baseline at producing correct rare names, product codes and the first word of creative answers. What is happening and what do you change?

Answer

Those are high-entropy positions, where the teacher's probability is spread over many tokens, so the top 16 cover a small share of the mass. Renormalising the stored tokens tells the student to put all its probability on 16 candidates, and it learns nothing about the remaining mass, including the correct rare token whenever the teacher ranked it 17th or below. Check by logging covered mass per position and comparing it on the failing cases. Fixes: raise k (64-256 is still only hundreds of bytes to a few KB per token), add an "everything else" bucket for residual mass, and keep a hard-label cross-entropy term on the ground-truth corpus token so rare correct tokens are still pushed up.
