Read this sentence: "The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too big." What was too big? You know immediately: the trophy. Change one word, "because it was too small", and "it" now means the suitcase. To understand "it", you had to look back at other words, decide which ones were relevant, and pull their meaning into your reading of "it". Every word in a sentence is like this to some degree: "bank" means different things next to "river" and "loan", and a verb's meaning depends on its subject several words away.

Before 2017 the dominant language models were recurrent neural networks (RNNs), which read text one token at a time and carried a single running summary vector forward (dl7 covers them). They had two problems. Information from far back had to survive being squeezed through hundreds of summary updates, so long-range links were weak. And because step 500 needed the result of step 499, training could not use the thousands of parallel arithmetic units of a GPU; it crawled along the sequence. Researchers had already found a patch, attention: let the model, at each step, look directly back at all earlier positions and take a weighted average of what it finds, with the weights decided by how relevant each position is.

In 2017 Vaswani and colleagues at Google asked what happens if you keep the attention and throw the recurrence away. The answer, the transformer, trained faster and translated better, and every large language model since is built on it. If you work with LLMs, self-attention is the operation you will be asked to explain, to compute by hand, to size in memory and to debug. It is also the source of the quadratic cost that limits context length, and of the KV cache that dominates inference memory.

This lesson builds self-attention from scratch: the idea of a soft lookup, queries, keys and values, the formula, one complete computation for three tokens with 2-dimensional vectors done by hand, why the scores are divided by √d_k, the causal mask that makes training parallel, multi-head attention, the cost, and what trained heads are found to do. The lab implements it in NumPy and checks it against PyTorch.

A soft lookup table

Think of a Python dictionary. You give it a key, it finds the exactly matching entry and returns that entry's value. Attention is a soft version of this. You give it a query vector. Instead of finding one exact match, it compares the query with every stored key vector, scores how similar each is, turns the scores into weights that sum to 1, and returns a weighted average of all the stored value vectors. A key that matches well contributes a lot of its value; a key that matches badly contributes almost nothing. Because everything is made of weighted sums, the whole lookup is differentiable, so the model can learn what to ask for and what to advertise by gradient descent.

In self-attention, the queries, keys and values all come from the same sequence of tokens. Every token produces all three, from its own current vector, using three learned matrices: the query says "what kind of information am I looking for?", the key says "what kind of information do I have, for others searching?", and the value is "what I will hand over if someone attends to me". Then every token runs the soft lookup against all tokens (in a language model, all earlier tokens and itself). The result is a new vector per token that mixes in information from the tokens most relevant to it.

The analogy, and where it breaks

Picture a conference where everyone wears a badge describing their expertise (the key) and carries notes they will share (the value). You walk in with a question (the query), glance at every badge, and spend your time with people in proportion to how well their badge matches your question, coming away with a blend of their notes. Everyone does this at once. The analogy breaks in two useful ways. First, the "badge" and the "notes" are learned transformations of the same person, not separate things they bring. Second, the room has no seating plan: nothing in the lookup itself knows who arrived first, which is why transformers need separate positional information (lm4).

The whole thing in one line

Stack the n tokens' queries as rows of a matrix Q, keys as rows of K and values as rows of V. Then

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q Kᵀ / √d_k ) V

Read it from the inside out. Q Kᵀ computes every query's dot product with every key: an n × n table of similarity scores. Dividing by √d_k, where d_k is the length of the query and key vectors, keeps the scores at a sensible size (section 5 of the body explains exactly why). The softmax, applied to each row, turns each query's scores into weights that are positive and sum to 1. Multiplying by V replaces each row of weights by the weighted average of the value vectors. Everything else in this lesson is detail around this line.

That is the correct mental model: a learned, content-based, weighted average over the sequence, computed for every position at once.

1. The vectors attention starts from

Attention operates on one vector per token. In the first layer of a language model these are the token embeddings from lm1 (plus positional information, which lm4 covers; ignore it for now). In later layers they are the outputs of the previous layer. Each vector has the model's width, d_model: 512 in the original transformer, 4,096 in a typical 7-billion-parameter model. We write the n token vectors as the rows of a matrix X of shape n × d_model. Nothing about attention depends on what the vectors "mean"; it is a general operation that lets rows of X exchange information.

2. Three projections: query, key, value

A single vector per token cannot play three roles well at once. What a token is looking for ("I am a pronoun; I need my antecedent") is different from what it advertises ("I am a singular noun, an animal") and different again from the information it should pass along when chosen. So attention learns three weight matrices, W_Q and W_K of shape d_model × d_k, and W_V of shape d_model × d_v, and computes for every token

q = x W_Q      (query, length d_k)
k = x W_K      (key,   length d_k)
v = x W_V      (value, length d_v)

or, for all tokens at once, Q = XW_Q, K = XW_K, V = XW_V. These are ordinary matrix multiplications, and the three matrices are the attention layer's learned parameters (along with an output projection, section 7). Queries and keys must have the same length d_k because they will be dotted together; values can in principle have a different length, though in practice d_v = d_k.

3. Scores, weights, output

The score of query i against key j is their dot product, q_i · k_j: the sum of element-wise products, which is large and positive when the two vectors point in a similar direction. For every pair of positions, that is the matrix product QKᵀ, of shape n × n: row i holds query i's scores against every key. Each score is divided by √d_k (section 5). Then, for each row separately, the softmax turns scores s_1 … s_n into weights

w_j = exp(s_j) / Σ_m exp(s_m)

which are positive and sum to 1, with larger scores getting exponentially more weight. Finally the output for position i is the weighted average of the values, o_i = Σ_j w_ij v_j, which for all positions at once is the matrix product of the n × n weight matrix with V. That is the whole formula: softmax(QKᵀ/√d_k)V.

4. One complete attention step by hand

Take a three-token sequence, "the cat sat", with toy 2-dimensional vectors (d_model = d_k = 2) so the arithmetic fits on paper:

x₁ ("the") = [1, 0]      W_Q = [[1, 0],      W_K = [[1, 1],      W_V = [[1, 0],
x₂ ("cat") = [0, 1]             [1, 1]]             [0, 1]]             [0, 2]]
x₃ ("sat") = [1, 1]

Project. A row vector times a matrix: q₁ = [1, 0]W_Q = first row of W_Q = [1, 0]; q₂ = second row = [1, 1]; q₃ = sum of both rows = [2, 1]. Likewise k₁ = [1, 1], k₂ = [0, 1], k₃ = [1, 2], and v₁ = [1, 0], v₂ = [0, 2], v₃ = [1, 2].

Score. Every query against every key. For the third token, q₃ = [2, 1]: q₃·k₁ = 2·1 + 1·1 = 3; q₃·k₂ = 2·0 + 1·1 = 1; q₃·k₃ = 2·1 + 1·2 = 4. The full matrix QKᵀ is

          k₁   k₂   k₃
q₁  [     1    0    1 ]
q₂  [     2    1    3 ]
q₃  [     3    1    4 ]

Scale. d_k = 2, so divide by √2 ≈ 1.414. Row 3 becomes 2.121, 0.707, 2.828.

Mask. In a language model, a token may not look at tokens after it (section 6). So in row 1, the scores for k₂ and k₃ are replaced by −∞, and in row 2 the score for k₃ is. Row 3 is untouched: "sat" may look at all three.

Softmax. Row 3: exp(2.121) = 8.342, exp(0.707) = 2.028, exp(2.828) = 16.919; the sum is 27.289; the weights are 8.342/27.289 = 0.306, 2.028/27.289 = 0.074 and 16.919/27.289 = 0.620. Row 2 has only two allowed scores, 1.414 and 0.707: exp gives 4.113 and 2.028, sum 6.141, weights 0.670 and 0.330. Row 1 has a single allowed score, so its weight is 1: the first token can only attend to itself.

Weighted sum. Output for "sat": 0.306·[1, 0] + 0.074·[0, 2] + 0.620·[1, 2] = [0.306 + 0.620, 0.148 + 1.240] = [0.926, 1.389]. For "cat": 0.670·[1, 0] + 0.330·[0, 2] = [0.670, 0.660]. For "the": [1, 0], its own value. Each token now carries a mixture of information from the tokens its query matched; with trained weights, the mixture would be the context it needs.

5. Why divide by √d_k

The scaling factor has an exact justification, given in a footnote of the original paper. Suppose, as at initialisation, the components of a query and a key are independent random numbers with mean 0 and variance 1. Their dot product is a sum of d_k products, each with mean 0 and variance 1, so the sum has mean 0 and variance d_k, and standard deviation √d_k. With d_k = 64 the raw scores typically range over about ±8 and beyond; with d_k = 512, over about ±23.

Softmax is very sensitive to the spread of its inputs. If one score exceeds the others by 10, its weight is e¹⁰ ≈ 22,000 times larger, so the output is effectively one-hot: the query attends to a single key. A one-hot softmax is saturated: small changes to the scores barely change the output, so the gradient flowing back through it is close to zero (the same failure as a saturated sigmoid in dl3). The lab measures this: with 8 keys and unit-variance components, the largest weight averages 0.53 at d_k = 4, 0.88 at d_k = 64 and 0.96 at d_k = 512 without scaling, but stays near 0.35-0.37 at every d_k with scaling. Dividing by √d_k restores unit standard deviation regardless of head size, so the softmax starts training in its responsive range and the model can learn sharper patterns where it needs them.

6. The causal mask and parallel training

A decoder-only language model, the GPT design used by nearly all chat LLMs, is trained to predict each token from the tokens before it. If position t could attend to position t + 1, it could simply read the answer. The causal mask prevents this: before the softmax, every score where the key's position is after the query's is set to −∞, and exp(−∞) = 0, so those weights are exactly zero. The weight matrix becomes lower-triangular, as in the figure.

The mask is what makes training efficient. A single forward pass over a 2,048-token training sequence produces, at every position, a prediction of the next token that used only legitimate context, so one pass yields 2,048 training examples, all computed in parallel as large matrix multiplications. An RNN would need 2,048 sequential steps for the same thing. At generation time the model produces one token at a time anyway, and future tokens do not exist yet; the new token's query attends over the keys and values of all previous tokens, which are stored rather than recomputed (the KV cache, lm5). Encoder models such as BERT, which read a whole text to classify or embed it, use no causal mask: every token sees every other. A padding mask, applied the same way, stops tokens attending to the padding added to make sequences in a batch the same length.

7. Multi-head attention

A single attention computation gives each query one set of weights, so one head must average every relationship the token cares about into one distribution. "Sat" might need its subject ("cat"), the previous word, and the tense of an earlier verb, at the same time. Multi-head attention runs h attention computations in parallel, each with its own W_Q, W_K, W_V projecting to a smaller size d_k = d_model / h, so each head can learn a different pattern. The h outputs, each of length d_k, are concatenated back to length d_model and multiplied by an output matrix W_O (d_model × d_model) that mixes them.

In the original transformer, d_model = 512 and h = 8, so each head has d_k = d_v = 64. Because the heads split the width rather than copy it, the projection matrices together are still d_model × d_model each, and the attention layer has 4·d_model² weights (W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O), about 1 million at d_model = 512 and 67 million at d_model = 4,096. In code, the "split into heads" step is just a reshape of the n × d_model projection into h × n × d_k, which is why multi-head attention is no more expensive than single-head attention of the same width.

8. Self-attention versus cross-attention

Everything above is self-attention: Q, K and V are all computed from the same sequence. The original transformer was an encoder–decoder for translation. Its decoder also had cross-attention layers, in which the queries come from the target sentence being generated and the keys and values come from the encoder's output for the source sentence, so each target word can look up the relevant source words. Decoder-only LLMs dropped the encoder and use only causal self-attention: the "source" (your prompt) is simply the beginning of the same sequence. Cross-attention survives in some multimodal and speech models, where text queries look up image or audio features.

9. What it costs

For one head with n tokens, the score matrix QKᵀ needs n² dot products of length d_k, and the weighted sum another n² × d_v multiply-adds: O(n²·d) arithmetic for the layer, and O(n²) memory if the score matrix is stored. At 8,192 tokens, one head's score matrix in 16-bit floating point (2 bytes per number) is 8,192² × 2 bytes = 128 MiB; at 32,768 tokens, 2 GiB, per head, per layer. A naive implementation writes this matrix to GPU memory, reads it back for the softmax, writes the weights, and reads them again to multiply by V, so it is limited by memory bandwidth rather than arithmetic (gpu2 explains this "memory-bound" regime). FlashAttention (gpu7) computes exactly the same result in tiles that stay in fast on-chip memory, using a running maximum and sum so the softmax can be computed in pieces, and never stores the full matrix.

The quadratic term is not the whole story. Per token, the four projections cost 4·d_model² multiply-adds and the feed-forward block that follows (lm3) another 8·d_model², while attention's scores and weighted sum cost about 2·n·d_model. The attention term only dominates once n is several times d_model, about 24,000 tokens for d_model = 4,096. That is why attention's cost matters most for long-context models, and why short-context workloads are dominated by the matrix multiplications in the projections and MLPs.

10. From formula to code: shapes and batching

Real implementations process a batch of B sequences at once and all heads at once, so the tensors carry four dimensions. Writing the shapes out is the fastest way to catch bugs, and interviewers often ask for it:

X                    (B, n, d_model)          batch of token vectors
Q = X W_Q            (B, n, d_model)          one big projection for all heads
reshape + transpose  (B, h, n, d_k)           split the width into h heads
S = Q Kᵀ / √d_k      (B, h, n, n)             one score matrix per head per sequence
S + mask             (B, h, n, n)             −∞ above the diagonal (and on padding)
W = softmax(S, -1)   (B, h, n, n)             normalise along the key axis
O = W V              (B, h, n, d_k)
transpose + reshape  (B, n, d_model)          concatenate the heads
Y = O W_O            (B, n, d_model)          mix the heads

Three details trip people up. The softmax runs along the last axis, the keys: each query's weights sum to 1, not each key's. The three projections are usually fused into one d_model × 3·d_model matrix multiplication and split afterwards, which is faster on a GPU because one big multiplication keeps the hardware busier than three small ones. And the transpose of K swaps only the last two axes, so each head's queries meet only that head's keys. In PyTorch the middle five lines collapse into one call, torch.nn.functional.scaled_dot_product_attention(q, k, v, is_causal=True), which picks a fused FlashAttention-style kernel when it can; the lab checks a NumPy implementation against it.

11. Attention during generation

When a trained model generates text, it produces one token per step. At step t, the newest token is the only new query; it needs the keys and values of every earlier token, but those were already computed at earlier steps and do not change, because a token's key and value depend only on itself and (through earlier layers) on tokens before it. So inference engines keep each layer's past keys and values in memory and, at each step, compute just one new query, key and value, append the new key and value, and run one row of attention: a 1 × t score vector, a softmax, and a weighted sum of t values. That stored memory is the KV cache, the subject of lm5. It turns each generation step's attention from O(t²) into O(t) work, at the price of memory that grows with every token generated, and it is the main reason serving long conversations is expensive.

12. What trained heads turn out to do

Nothing in the architecture tells a head what to look for; roles emerge from training. Researchers inspecting trained models have found recurring types. Positional heads attend mostly to the previous token or a fixed offset, which lets later layers combine neighbouring tokens into phrases. Syntactic heads link verbs to their subjects or objects, or modifiers to the nouns they modify, with accuracy well above simple baselines when compared against grammatical annotations. Coreference heads connect pronouns to their antecedents, as the transformer paper's own visualisations of "its" suggest. Induction heads, described in the deep section, find an earlier occurrence of the current token and copy what followed it. Many other heads have no clean interpretation, and some can be removed after training with little effect. These findings are useful intuition, but they come with the caveat in this lesson's myths: a weight pattern is a hint about information flow, not proof of what the model used.

13. Why it won

Vaswani et al. compared layer types on three properties: arithmetic per layer, how many operations must happen one after another, and the longest path a signal travels between two positions. Self-attention needs O(1) sequential operations and has path length O(1): any two tokens interact directly in every layer. A recurrent layer needs O(n) sequential steps and has path length O(n). Short paths make long-range dependencies easier to learn, and the absence of sequential steps lets training use the full parallel width of a GPU. Those two properties, more than any single accuracy number, are why attention displaced recurrence.

Attention as a kernel smoother, and its limits

Formally, each output is a weighted average of values with weights given by a similarity kernel, exp(q·k/√d_k), normalised over the allowed positions. This explains two properties. First, an attention output always lies in the convex hull of the value vectors: it can interpolate between them but never extrapolate beyond them, so a single head cannot, for example, compute a sum that grows with the number of matches; the averaging normalises it away. Second, because weights are normalised, attention struggles to say "nothing here is relevant": the weights must go somewhere. Trained models handle this by dumping weight onto a fixed position, usually the first token, whose value vector is near zero. These attention sinks are why streaming implementations that drop the oldest tokens must keep the first few, or quality collapses.

Multi-head as low-rank factorisation

For one head, the score between positions i and j is x_i W_Q W_Kᵀ x_jᵀ: a bilinear form with the d_model × d_model matrix W_Q W_Kᵀ, whose rank is at most d_k. Likewise the value path W_V W_O (restricted to that head's slice) is a rank-d_k map. So a head is a low-rank "which positions" circuit plus a low-rank "what to move" circuit, and h heads give h independent such circuits for the price of one full-rank one. This view, from mechanistic interpretability, explains why heads specialise and why pruning many heads after training often barely hurts: several are redundant. It also motivates sharing keys and values across heads to save inference memory, which lm5 develops as multi-query and grouped-query attention.

Why queries and keys need separate projections

One could imagine using the same projection for queries and keys, so the score is just the similarity of two tokens' vectors. That score would be symmetric: token i would attend to j exactly as strongly as j to i, and every token would score itself highest (a vector is most similar to itself). Real relationships are asymmetric. A pronoun should attend strongly to its antecedent noun, while the noun has no particular reason to attend back to the pronoun, and a verb looks for its subject but the subject is not looking for the verb in the same way. Separate W_Q and W_K make the bilinear form W_Q W_Kᵀ non-symmetric, so "what I am looking for" and "what I offer" can be different features. The separate value projection decouples a third thing: the features used to find a token need not be the ones worth copying from it, so a head can locate the subject of a sentence by its grammatical role while moving its meaning.

Logit growth and stabilisation at scale

The √d_k argument holds at initialisation, but during training the norms of queries and keys can grow, pushing scores back into the saturated regime; in large models this shows up as training-loss spikes. Two common remedies: QK-norm, which applies a normalisation (lm3 explains layer normalisation) to queries and keys before the dot product so their scale is controlled, and logit soft-capping, which squashes scores with c·tanh(s/c) so they never exceed ±c. Both trade a small amount of expressiveness for stability. In mixed precision (gpu4), large scores also risk overflow in 16-bit formats, which is why kernels subtract the row maximum before exponentiating.

Induction heads and in-context learning

Olsson et al. identified a two-layer circuit that appears suddenly during training. A previous-token head in an early layer copies into each position some information about the token before it. An induction head in a later layer forms its query from the current token and its keys from that "previous token" information, so it attends to positions that came right after an earlier occurrence of the current token, and copies what is there. On "Mr Dursley ... Mr", it attends to "Dursley" and boosts it as the next prediction. The authors found that the appearance of induction heads coincides with a sharp improvement in the model's ability to use earlier context, and argue they are a major mechanism of in-context learning. This is the kind of concrete answer interviewers are looking for when they ask "what do attention heads learn?".

Faster and approximate attention

Many variants trade exactness for cost. Sliding-window attention lets each token attend only to the previous w tokens, making cost O(n·w); stacking layers still lets information travel further, w positions per layer. Sparse patterns combine local windows with a few global tokens. Linear attention replaces exp(q·k) with a product of feature maps φ(q)·φ(k), so the sum over keys can be precomputed and cost becomes O(n); it behaves like a recurrent state of fixed size and is weaker at exact recall of earlier tokens. FlashAttention is different in kind: it is exact, and wins by reducing memory traffic rather than arithmetic. In production LLMs, exact attention with FlashAttention-style kernels plus memory savings in the KV cache has been the default, sometimes interleaved with sliding-window layers.

Numerics of the masked softmax

Implementations never compute exp of raw scores: they subtract each row's maximum first, which leaves the softmax unchanged (the factor cancels) and avoids overflow. Masking uses −∞ or a large negative number; with −∞, a row in which every entry is masked would produce 0/0 = NaN, which happens with left-padding in a batch if a padded query row is fully masked. Libraries guard this case, and it is a classic source of NaN losses in hand-written attention code. FlashAttention's online softmax keeps a running maximum m and running sum l per row and rescales the partial output whenever m increases, which is the same trick applied block by block.

The public case: "Attention Is All You Need" (Vaswani et al., 2017)

The transformer paper is short, and its claims are concrete enough to check. The base model uses d_model = 512, h = 8 heads with d_k = d_v = 64, 6 encoder and 6 decoder layers, and has 65 million parameters; the big model uses d_model = 1,024 with 16 heads and has 213 million. The authors justify the √d_k scaling in a footnote with exactly the variance argument above. They trained on one machine with 8 NVIDIA P100 GPUs: the base model for 100,000 steps, about 12 hours, and the big model for 300,000 steps, 3.5 days.

On the WMT 2014 English-to-German translation benchmark the big model scored 28.4 BLEU (a 0–100 measure of overlap with human reference translations), more than 2 BLEU above the best previous results including ensembles of models, and 41.8 BLEU on English-to-French, a new single-model state of the art, at a small fraction of the training cost of competing models. The paper's Table 1 is the argument in miniature: self-attention has O(1) sequential operations and O(1) maximum path length, against O(n) for recurrent layers. Its attention visualisations (figures 3-5) show heads in layer 5 following long-distance dependencies and apparently resolving which noun "its" refers to. Within two years the encoder half became BERT and the decoder half became GPT; the attention formula in every modern LLM is the one on page 4.

Failure modes in practice

NaNs from fully masked rows. Custom attention code that pads sequences on the left and masks with −∞ produces NaN for any query row with no allowed key. Symptom: loss becomes NaN only for certain batches. Fix: guard the row, or use library kernels such as PyTorch's scaled_dot_product_attention.

Mask bugs that leak the future. An off-by-one in the causal mask (allowing the diagonal plus one) gives a model that reaches suspiciously low training loss and then generates nonsense, because at inference the "future" it learned to copy is not there. A quick test, as in the lab: change only the last token and assert that all earlier outputs are unchanged.

Memory blow-ups at long context. A model that runs at 4K tokens and fails with out-of-memory errors at 32K is often using an attention path that materialises the n × n matrix, for example because a custom mask disabled the fused kernel. Check which attention backend is actually running; profilers (gpu11) show a huge allocation per layer.

Precision issues. Computing the softmax in 16-bit precision without max subtraction overflows; computing it in FP16 with long rows loses precision in the sum. Kernels accumulate in 32-bit; hand-written code should too.

What to watch

During training, track the maximum attention logit per layer: steady growth precedes loss spikes, and is the signal for adding QK-norm or lowering the learning rate. Attention entropy per head (how spread out the weights are) helps spot heads that have collapsed to one position. At serving time, attention's share of step time grows with context length; latency that rises with prompt length faster than linearly is a sign that attention, not the projections, has become the bottleneck.

Goal: implement causal scaled dot-product attention and multi-head attention in NumPy, reproduce the hand-worked example, check against PyTorch, and verify three properties: causality, order-blindness without positions, and the saturation that √d_k prevents. NumPy only; the cross-check runs if torch is installed. Takes about a second.

import numpy as np
np.set_printoptions(precision=4, suppress=True)

def softmax(z):
    z = z - z.max(axis=-1, keepdims=True)                 # subtract the row max: same result, no overflow
    e = np.exp(z)
    return e / e.sum(axis=-1, keepdims=True)

def attention(Q, K, V, causal=True):
    d = Q.shape[-1]
    S = Q @ np.swapaxes(K, -1, -2) / np.sqrt(d)           # (..., n, n) scores
    if causal:
        n = S.shape[-1]
        S = np.where(np.triu(np.ones((n, n), bool), 1), -np.inf, S)
    W = softmax(S)
    return W @ V, W

# ---- 1. the lesson's worked example: 3 tokens, 2-dim vectors
X = np.array([[1., 0], [0, 1], [1, 1]])
WQ = np.array([[1., 0], [1, 1]]); WK = np.array([[1., 1], [0, 1]]); WV = np.array([[1., 0], [0, 2]])
out, W = attention(X @ WQ, X @ WK, X @ WV)
print("attention weights (causal):\n", W, "\noutputs:\n", out)

# ---- 2. multi-head attention on random data, checked against PyTorch if it is installed
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, d_model, h = 6, 16, 4; dk = d_model // h
X = rng.normal(size=(n, d_model))
Wq, Wk, Wv, Wo = (rng.normal(0, d_model ** -0.5, (d_model, d_model)) for _ in range(4))
def mha(X, causal=True):
    split = lambda M: (X @ M).reshape(n, h, dk).transpose(1, 0, 2)   # (h, n, dk)
    o, W = attention(split(Wq), split(Wk), split(Wv), causal)
    return o.transpose(1, 0, 2).reshape(n, d_model) @ Wo, W
Y, Wh = mha(X)
print("\nmulti-head output shape", Y.shape, " weights shape", Wh.shape, " rows sum to 1:", np.allclose(Wh.sum(-1), 1))
try:
    import torch, torch.nn.functional as F
    t = lambda M: torch.tensor((X @ M).reshape(n, h, dk).transpose(1, 0, 2))
    ref = F.scaled_dot_product_attention(t(Wq), t(Wk), t(Wv), is_causal=True).numpy()
    print("matches torch scaled_dot_product_attention:", np.allclose(ref.transpose(1, 0, 2).reshape(n, d_model) @ Wo, Y))
except ImportError:
    print("(torch not installed - skipped cross-check)")

# ---- 3. causality and permutation
X2 = X.copy(); X2[-1] += 10                                   # change only the last token
print("earlier outputs unchanged when the last token changes:", np.allclose(mha(X2)[0][:-1], Y[:-1]))
perm = rng.permutation(n); Yb = mha(X, causal=False)[0]
print("without mask or positions, permuting inputs just permutes outputs:", np.allclose(mha(X[perm], causal=False)[0], Yb[perm]))

# ---- 4. why divide by sqrt(d_k): score spread and softmax saturation
print("\n d_k   std(q.k)  max weight unscaled  max weight scaled")
for d in (4, 64, 512):
    q = rng.normal(size=(2000, d)); k = rng.normal(size=(2000, 8, d))
    s = np.einsum("bd,bjd->bj", q, k)
    print(f"{d:4d}  {s.std():8.2f}  {softmax(s).max(-1).mean():19.3f}  {softmax(s / np.sqrt(d)).max(-1).mean():17.3f}")

# ---- 5. memory of the full score matrix for one head, one layer, fp16
for ctx in (2048, 8192, 32768):
    print(f"context {ctx:6d}: score matrix {ctx*ctx*2/2**20:8.0f} MiB per head")

Work it out — head size and memory

A model has d_model = 4,096 and 32 attention heads. Compute (a) the per-head dimension, (b) the scaling factor, (c) the attention layer's parameter count, and (d) the size of the score matrix for one head at an 8,192-token context in fp16, and for all heads of one layer.

Answer

(a) d_k = 4,096 / 32 = 128.

(b) √128 ≈ 11.3. Without it, scores at initialisation would have a standard deviation of about 11 and the softmax would be nearly one-hot.

(c) Four d_model × d_model matrices: 4 × 4,096² = 67.1 million parameters per attention layer, independent of the number of heads.

(d) 8,192² = 67.1 million entries × 2 bytes = 128 MiB per head, × 32 heads = 4 GiB for one layer of one sequence. That is the matrix FlashAttention never stores: it tiles the computation in on-chip memory so the memory cost is linear in n.

Diagnose — the model that was too good

A colleague's hand-written GPT reaches a training loss far below a reference implementation within a few hundred steps, but at generation it produces repetitive nonsense. What do you suspect, and how do you test it in one minute?

Answer

A broken causal mask. If positions can see the next token, the model learns to copy it, so training loss collapses; at generation the next token does not exist yet, so the learned shortcut has nothing to copy and the output is garbage. Common causes: building the mask with triu(..., 0) instead of triu(..., 1) in the wrong convention, applying it after the softmax, or broadcasting it along the wrong axis. Test: run the model on a sequence, change only the last token, rerun, and assert that the outputs (logits) at all earlier positions are unchanged, exactly as the lab's section 3 does. If they change, information is flowing backwards.
