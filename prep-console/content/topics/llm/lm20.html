Picture a team six months into fine-tuning. They have an open 7B base model and a growing pile of experiments: a LoRA adapter that writes good SQL, another that extracts JSON from invoices reliably, a third that answers in the company's support tone, and a fourth, trained with DPO, that refuses fewer harmless requests. Each was trained separately, and now the product needs a single model that does all of it. Retraining one model on everything means re-gathering four datasets and paying for a long run, and the last time they tried, the combined model was worse at SQL than the specialist.

Meanwhile a second team is stuck at the other end of the pipeline. Their legal-research assistant hallucinates case details and misuses legal terms, and no amount of instruction tuning fixes it: the base model simply has not read enough law. They have twenty billion tokens of legal text but no idea how to train on it without the model forgetting how to write ordinary English or follow instructions.

A third team collects thumbs-up and thumbs-down on chat answers. They read that DPO needs pairs of responses to the same prompt, which they do not have, and that it keeps a second copy of the model in memory, which they cannot afford on their GPUs.

LoRA (lm11) and DPO (lm6) are the foundation of modern post-training, but these three situations need tools beyond them. This lesson covers four groups. Model merging combines fine-tunes in weight space without training: model soups, task arithmetic, TIES and DARE. Newer parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods fix specific weaknesses of LoRA: DoRA, rsLoRA, LoRA+ and PiSSA. Newer preference methods remove the reference model or the need for pairs: SimPO, ORPO and KTO. And continued pretraining adapts a base model to a new domain or language with billions of tokens of raw text, without destroying what it already knew. For each, you will learn the mechanism with worked numbers, what problem it solves, and when it is the wrong tool.

The mental model: weight space as a map

A model's weights are one long list of numbers, billions of them. Think of that list as a single point on an enormous map, where every possible setting of the weights is a location. Pretraining walks a long way across the map to a good region: the base model. Fine-tuning then takes a short walk from that point in some direction: towards SQL, towards invoices, towards a support tone. Because the walks are short and all start from the same place, the fine-tuned models are neighbours, and the arrows from the base to each of them, the task vectors, can be added, scaled and combined like arrows on a real map.

The map also explains continued pretraining: it is a long walk, deliberately moving the base itself towards a region that knows law or Hindi. It is long enough to leave some old abilities behind, which is why it needs care, and long enough that afterwards the old fine-tunes no longer fit the moved base.

The newer PEFT methods are about how you take the short walk: which directions you are allowed to move in (LoRA's low-rank constraint), how big each step is (scaling and learning rates), and where you start (initialisation). DoRA adds the ability to change a weight's length and its direction separately, like being allowed to turn without changing speed.

The preference methods are about what tells you which direction is better. DPO compares the walker against a frozen copy of itself at the start (the reference model). SimPO and ORPO drop the copy and use another yardstick; KTO accepts single "good" or "bad" signals instead of comparisons.

Where the map analogy breaks: real weight space has billions of dimensions, and the low-loss region is not a neat ellipse. Two arrows that look independent on a page can collide on the same weights, which is the interference that TIES and DARE exist to manage.

Three questions that pick the tool

Before reaching for any of these methods, ask three questions. First, what do I already have? Existing fine-tunes of one base point to merging; a large pile of unlabelled domain text points to continued pretraining; logs of user feedback point to a preference method that fits their shape. Second, how far does the model need to move? Behaviour and style are short walks that adapters and merges handle well; new knowledge or a new language is a long walk that needs full training on lots of tokens. Third, what can I afford to keep around? A reference model, several adapters, a router, a second training stage: each method removes one of these costs and usually adds a hyperparameter or a risk in exchange. Keep the three questions in mind as you read; the last section of the body returns to them as a decision guide.

1. Why merging works at all

Take two networks trained from scratch on the same data with different random seeds and average their weights: the result is usually useless. The reason is that the networks arranged their internal units differently; unit 17 in one does the job of unit 341 in the other, and averaging mixes unrelated features. Fine-tunes of a pretrained model do not have that problem. They all start from the same weights, they move only a little, and so unit 17 means the same thing in every fine-tune. Experiments show that the straight line in weight space between two such fine-tunes stays at low loss, a property called linear mode connectivity. That shared starting point is the precondition for every method in this section; you cannot merge a Llama fine-tune with a Mistral fine-tune.

Merging is attractive because it is almost free: no training data, no GPUs beyond loading the weights, minutes of CPU time, and the result is an ordinary model with no serving overhead. The price is that you can only combine what already exists, and quality depends on how much the fine-tunes interfere.

2. Model soups: averaging fine-tunes of one task

The simplest merge is the average. Wortsman et al. (2022) fine-tuned the same pretrained model many times on the same task with different hyperparameters (learning rate, augmentation, seed), and instead of keeping only the best run, averaged the weights: a model soup. The uniform soup averages everything; the greedy soup sorts runs by validation accuracy and adds each to the average only if it improves validation accuracy. Their greedy soup of ViT-G image models reached 90.94% top-1 accuracy on ImageNet, a new state of the art at the time, with no extra inference cost, and soups were also more robust under distribution shift than the best single run.

Why does averaging help? Each run lands at a slightly different point in the same low-loss basin, each with its own idiosyncratic errors. Their average sits nearer the centre of the basin, which tends to be flatter and generalise better, much as averaging checkpoints along a single training run does. Soups combine runs of the same task; combining different tasks needs task vectors.

3. Task vectors and task arithmetic

Ilharco et al. (2023) defined the task vector of a fine-tune as τ = θfine-tuned − θbase: the change in every weight. With task vectors, editing a model becomes arithmetic. A worked example on a four-weight model makes it concrete:

base       θ₀ = [ 1.0,  2.0, -1.0,  0.5]
SQL        θA = [ 1.3,  2.0, -1.2,  0.5]   →  τA = [ 0.3,  0.0, -0.2,  0.0]
tone       θB = [ 0.9,  2.4, -1.0,  0.8]   →  τB = [-0.1,  0.4,  0.0,  0.3]

add (λ=1):    θ₀ + τA + τB       = [1.2, 2.4, -1.2, 0.8]
average:      θ₀ + (τA + τB)/2   = [1.1, 2.2, -1.1, 0.65]

Three operations matter. Addition combines skills: weights 2 and 4 were changed only by the tone fine-tune and weight 3 only by SQL, so their sum keeps both changes intact. Scaling by λ controls strength; note that plain averaging is addition with λ = 0.5, which halves every task's change, so each skill gets weaker. Negation removes a behaviour: fine-tune on toxic text, then subtract that task vector from the base, and Ilharco et al. found the model generated much less toxic text with little loss elsewhere.

Now look at weight 1: SQL wants +0.3, tone wants −0.1. Adding gives +0.2, so SQL loses a third of its change and tone's change is reversed entirely. That is interference, and with billions of weights and several task vectors it is everywhere. The next two methods are about reducing it.

4. TIES-Merging: trim, elect, merge

Yadav et al. (2023) identified two sources of interference: many small, noisy changes that add up and swamp the important ones, and sign conflicts where tasks push the same weight in opposite directions. TIES-Merging handles each in a step. Take three task vectors over six weights:

τ1 = [ 0.40, -0.02,  0.30,  0.01, -0.25,  0.03]
τ2 = [ 0.35,  0.03, -0.20,  0.02, -0.30, -0.01]
τ3 = [-0.10,  0.01,  0.25, -0.02,  0.05,  0.40]

Step 1, Trim: in each task vector keep only the largest-magnitude values (here the top 50%, three of six; in practice often the top 20%) and set the rest to zero. Small changes are mostly noise from training, and dropping them costs the task little.

τ1' = [ 0.40, 0,  0.30, 0, -0.25, 0   ]
τ2' = [ 0.35, 0, -0.20, 0, -0.30, 0   ]
τ3' = [-0.10, 0,  0.25, 0,  0,    0.40]

Step 2, Elect sign: for each weight, choose the sign with the larger total magnitude across tasks. Weight 1: 0.40 + 0.35 positive against 0.10 negative, so +. Weight 3: 0.30 + 0.25 against 0.20, so +. Weight 5: 0.55 negative against 0, so −. Weight 6: +. Weights 2 and 4: nothing left, so no change.

Step 3, Disjoint merge: for each weight, average only the values that agree with the elected sign, ignoring zeros and dissenters.

Plain averaging dilutes every change by the number of models: weight 6, which only task 3 cares about, gets 0.14 instead of 0.40, and weight 3 is dragged down by task 2's disagreement. TIES keeps each task's important changes at full strength and settles conflicts by majority. The merged vector is then added to the base with a scale λ, usually tuned on validation data (values near 1 are common).

5. DARE: drop most of the delta and rescale

Yu et al. (2023) made a surprising observation: the changes a supervised fine-tune makes, the delta parameters, are typically tiny (they report values within about 0.002) and extremely redundant. DARE (Drop And REscale) randomly sets a fraction p of each task vector's values to zero and multiplies the survivors by 1/(1 − p). With p = 0.9, a delta value of 0.002 becomes either 0 (90% of the time) or 0.02 (10% of the time). Its expected value is 0.1 × 0.02 = 0.002, unchanged, which is why the model's behaviour survives. The authors found that 90%, sometimes even 99%, of deltas could be dropped without hurting the fine-tuned model.

For merging, DARE matters because sparse task vectors rarely touch the same weights. If each of three task vectors keeps a random 10% of its entries, two of them overlap on only about 1% of weights, so most interference disappears. In practice DARE is used as a preprocessing step before task arithmetic or TIES ("DARE-TIES"). It works for fine-tunes whose deltas are small; for models that moved far from the base, such as after continued pretraining, the drop rate must be much lower or the merge breaks.

6. Merging in practice: SLERP and mergekit

For exactly two models, a common alternative to linear averaging is SLERP, spherical linear interpolation: it blends the two weight vectors along an arc rather than a straight line, which keeps the merged vector's norm from shrinking when the two point in fairly different directions. Most practitioners use mergekit, an open-source toolkit that implements linear, SLERP, task arithmetic, TIES, DARE and other methods from a YAML file:

models:
  - model: org/llama-8b-sql-ft
    parameters: {density: 0.5, weight: 0.5}   # density = share of deltas kept
  - model: org/llama-8b-json-ft
    parameters: {density: 0.5, weight: 0.5}
merge_method: ties
base_model: org/llama-8b
parameters: {normalize: true}
dtype: bfloat16

LoRA adapters can be merged too: either merge each adapter into the base and treat the result as a full model, or combine the adapters' low-rank products directly. The open-model community has used merges heavily, and Sakana AI showed that the merge recipe itself (which layers, which weights, which method) can be searched automatically with evolutionary algorithms. The discipline that matters is evaluation: test the merge on every source task and on general benchmarks, because a merge can look strong on a leaderboard and quietly lose one of its parents' skills.

7. DoRA: separate a weight's length from its direction

Now the second group: improvements to LoRA itself. Recall from lm11 that LoRA learns W = W₀ + BA. Liu et al. (2024) analysed how full fine-tuning changes each weight matrix by splitting every column into a magnitude (its length) and a direction (the column divided by its length). Full fine-tuning tended to change magnitude and direction somewhat independently, sometimes a large turn with a small change of length, and vice versa. LoRA's updates instead coupled the two: changes in direction came with proportional changes in magnitude. DoRA (Weight-Decomposed Low-Rank Adaptation) removes the coupling by making the split explicit:

W = m · (W₀ + BA) / ‖W₀ + BA‖_c

Here ‖·‖c means the length of each column, so the fraction is a matrix of unit-length directions, LoRA's low-rank update BA steers those directions, and m is a trainable vector with one magnitude per column, initialised to the columns' original lengths so training starts exactly at W₀.

Worked by hand: a column of W₀ is [3, 4], length 5, so m starts at 5 and the direction is [0.6, 0.8]. Suppose the LoRA product adds [0.3, −0.4] to it, giving [3.3, 3.6]. Plain LoRA uses [3.3, 3.6] directly: its length is √(10.89 + 12.96) = √23.85 = 4.88, so the column both turned and shrank. DoRA normalises it to the direction [0.676, 0.737] and multiplies by m = 5, giving [3.38, 3.69]: the same turn, the original length. If shrinking is actually useful, gradient descent can lower m on its own.

Liu et al. reported consistent gains over LoRA on LLaMA-family models for commonsense reasoning and on vision-language tasks, often at the same or lower rank. The costs: computing column norms adds memory and time during training (the paper reduces this by treating the norm as a constant in the backward pass), and the magnitude vector adds a tiny number of parameters. After training, m · direction is computed once and merged into an ordinary weight matrix, so serving is identical to LoRA's merged form. In Hugging Face PEFT it is one flag, use_dora=True.

8. Three smaller fixes: rsLoRA, LoRA+ and PiSSA

rsLoRA (rank-stabilised LoRA, Kalajdzievski, 2023) changes one number. LoRA multiplies BA by α/r. As rank r grows, that factor shrinks, and Kalajdzievski showed it shrinks too fast: the update and its gradients get smaller with rank, so high-rank adapters learn slowly and end up little better than low-rank ones. Scaling by α/√r keeps the update's size stable as r grows, so a rank-256 adapter genuinely uses its extra capacity. This matters when you raise rank for harder adaptation (lm11 explains when that is). In PEFT it is use_rslora=True.

LoRA+ (Hayou et al., 2024) changes the learning rates. A and B play different roles: B starts at zero and A at random values, and the analysis shows that using one learning rate for both is inefficient for wide models. LoRA+ gives B a learning rate several times larger than A's (ratios around 16 are a common starting point); the authors reported about 1-2% better results and up to about 2× faster fine-tuning at the same cost.

PiSSA (Meng et al., 2024) changes the starting point. LoRA initialises B to zero, so the adapter starts with nothing and must discover the important directions. PiSSA computes the weight matrix's singular value decomposition, puts the top r singular directions (the "principal" part of W, ml13) into A and B, and freezes the residual: W = Wres + BA. Training then adjusts the most important directions of the weight directly, which tends to converge faster and score better than LoRA at the same rank, at the cost of an SVD per adapted matrix at setup.

9. Preference tuning without a reference model: SimPO

The third group revisits DPO (lm6). DPO's implicit reward for a response is β times the log-probability ratio between the policy and a frozen reference model, summed over the response's tokens. That has two costs: the reference model's forward passes (and, for full fine-tuning, a second copy of the weights in memory), and a mismatch between training and generation. Summed log-probabilities favour long responses in subtle ways, and the quantity DPO increases is not the quantity that decides what the model generates.

SimPO (Meng, Xia and Chen, 2024) replaces the reward with the policy's average log-probability per token, scaled by β, with no reference model, and asks the chosen response to win by a target margin γ:

r(y)   = (β / |y|) · log π(y | x)
L      = −log σ( r(y_chosen) − r(y_rejected) − γ )

A worked example with illustrative values β = 2 and γ = 1: the chosen response has 100 tokens with total log-probability −80 (average −0.80 per token); the rejected one has 50 tokens with total −45 (average −0.90). Summed log-probabilities would rank the rejected answer higher (−45 beats −80) simply because it is shorter. Averaged, the chosen one is ahead: rchosen = 2 × (−0.80) = −1.60 and rrejected = −1.80, a gap of 0.20. The margin asks for more than that: 0.20 − 1 = −0.80, and the loss is −log σ(−0.80) = ln(1 + e0.80) = ln 3.23 = 1.17. Training keeps pushing until the per-token gap exceeds the margin.

SimPO reported beating DPO by up to 6.4 points on AlpacaEval 2 and 7.5 on Arena-Hard, and its best model, built on Gemma-2-9B-it, reached a 72.4% length-controlled win rate on AlpacaEval 2. The price of dropping the reference is losing DPO's built-in leash on drift from the starting model; SimPO relies on the margin, length normalisation and a low learning rate instead, and it is sensitive to β, γ and the learning rate.

10. ORPO: preference tuning inside SFT

The standard pipeline runs SFT first, then DPO from the SFT model. ORPO (Odds Ratio Preference Optimisation, Hong et al., 2024) does both in one stage, with no reference model. It defines a response's likelihood P as the exponential of its average token log-probability, and its odds as P / (1 − P). The loss is the ordinary SFT loss on the chosen response plus a penalty when the chosen response's odds do not exceed the rejected one's:

L = L_SFT(chosen) + λ · ( −log σ( log [ odds(chosen) / odds(rejected) ] ) )

With the numbers from the SimPO example: Pchosen = e−0.80 = 0.449 and Prejected = e−0.90 = 0.407. The odds are 0.449 / 0.551 = 0.816 and 0.407 / 0.593 = 0.685, the log of their ratio is ln 1.19 = 0.17, and the odds-ratio term is −log σ(0.17) = 0.61. The SFT term is the chosen response's average negative log-probability, 0.80, so with an illustrative λ = 0.1 the total is 0.80 + 0.061 = 0.86. The SFT term teaches the model to produce the chosen kind of answer; the small odds-ratio term pushes it away from the rejected kind at the same time. The appeal is a simpler pipeline: one training run on preference data instead of two runs and a reference model.

11. KTO: learning from thumbs up and thumbs down

Both DPO and SimPO need pairs: two responses to the same prompt with one marked better. Real products rarely collect that; they collect single responses with a thumbs up or down. KTO (Kahneman-Tversky Optimisation, Ethayarajh et al., 2024) trains directly on such data. Each example is (prompt, response, desirable or undesirable). KTO measures how much the policy has raised the response's log-probability relative to the reference model, r = log π(y|x) − log πref(y|x), and compares it with a reference point z₀: an estimate of how far the policy has drifted from the reference overall, computed on the batch and not trained through. Desirable responses are rewarded for rising above the reference point, undesirable ones for falling below it, each through a sigmoid that saturates, so already-learned examples stop dominating:

desirable:    value = λ_D · σ( β (r − z₀) )
undesirable:  value = λ_U · σ( β (z₀ − r) )
loss = weight − value

The names come from prospect theory, Kahneman and Tversky's model of how people value gains and losses relative to a reference point. In practice, the weights λD and λU also let you balance imbalanced data, say 80% thumbs up. The authors reported that KTO matched or exceeded pair-based methods at scales from 1B to 30B parameters. It still uses a reference model, so it saves the pairing work, not the memory.

12. Continued pretraining: moving the base itself

The last group is the biggest walk on the map. Fine-tuning teaches behaviour from thousands of examples; it does not teach a model a domain it barely saw in pretraining (lm11's first myth). Continued pretraining (CPT) does: keep training the base model with the ordinary next-token objective on billions of tokens of raw domain text. Gururangan et al. (2020) showed the effect with RoBERTa: domain-adaptive pretraining on biomedical papers, computer-science papers, news and reviews improved downstream tasks in each domain, and a further pass on the task's own unlabelled text helped more. At LLM scale, Code Llama was Llama 2 continued on 500B tokens of code.

The danger is forgetting: a long walk towards law moves the model away from everything else. Ibrahim et al. (2024) studied how to continue pretraining cheaply and found three ingredients. Re-warming: the original run ended with the learning rate decayed to nearly zero, which is too small to learn new data, so raise it again with a warm-up. Re-decaying: then decay it again to the end of the new run. Replay: mix a share of general, pretraining-like data into the new data, which greatly reduces forgetting. With these, continued pretraining matched the quality of retraining from scratch on all the data combined, for both a weak shift (between two English datasets) and a strong one (English to German), at a fraction of the compute.

13. A worked continued-pretraining plan

The legal team from the opening has 20B tokens of cleaned, deduplicated legal text and an 8B base model. Add 5% general text as replay: about 1.05B tokens, for a total of about 21B. Training compute is about 6 × N × D (lm7) = 6 × 8×10⁹ × 21×10⁹ ≈ 1.0×10²¹ floating-point operations. An H100 delivers about 989 TFLOP/s of dense bf16 at peak; at a realistic 40% utilisation, about 400 TFLOP/s, that is 1.0×10²¹ / 4×10¹⁴ ≈ 2.5 million GPU-seconds, about 700 GPU-hours, or roughly $2,100 at $3 per GPU-hour. On 64 GPUs, about 11 hours. Cheap relative to the original pretraining, which used trillions of tokens, but not something you rerun casually.

The settings follow the recipe: full fine-tuning (Biderman et al. found LoRA substantially underperforms full fine-tuning for continued pretraining, lm11), a learning rate re-warmed to a peak below the original run's and decayed again, and evaluation every few billion tokens on three things: legal perplexity (should fall), general benchmarks and general-text perplexity (should hold), and legal task evaluations (should rise). If general scores fall, increase replay or lower the peak learning rate.

For a new language, one more decision appears: the tokenizer. If the base tokenizer splits Hindi into many short pieces, text costs several times more tokens and context. Teams sometimes add new tokens to the vocabulary, initialise their embeddings (for example as the average of the embeddings of the pieces each new token replaces) and train the embeddings briefly before the full run.

14. Restoring chat after continued pretraining

The result of continued pretraining is a base model: it knows law but continues documents instead of following instructions. You have three ways back to an assistant. Re-run SFT and preference tuning on the new base: most reliable, needs the data. Or use a chat vector (Huang et al., 2023), task arithmetic again: take the original model's chat version minus its base, θchat − θbase, and add it to the continued-pretrained model. Because the continued-pretrained model is still recognisably the same network, the chat vector often transfers much of the instruction-following and safety behaviour for free. Or merge the domain base with the original chat model (SLERP or TIES) and tune the blend. A common practical sequence is chat vector first, as a cheap baseline, then light SFT to fix what it missed.

15. Choosing the tool

The data you have usually decides. Billions of tokens of raw domain text: continued pretraining, then restoration. Several existing fine-tunes of the same base and a need for one model: merging, evaluated per task, with multitask fine-tuning as the fallback. A LoRA fine-tune that trails full fine-tuning: try DoRA, rsLoRA with higher rank, or LoRA+ before paying for full fine-tuning. Preference pairs and memory pressure: SimPO, or ORPO to fold SFT in; thumbs-up/down logs: KTO. And in every case, the evaluation suite from lm14 is what tells you whether the new tool helped or just moved the problem.

Why fine-tune deltas are so compressible

DARE's finding that 90-99% of delta values can be dropped, TIES's finding that trimming to the top 20% costs little, and LoRA's finding that low-rank updates suffice are three views of one fact: fine-tuning from a strong pretrained model makes a small, low-dimensional change. Most of the work is already done by pretrained features, and fine-tuning mostly re-weights them. This is also why merging degrades as models move further from the base: continued pretraining or very long fine-tuning produces large, high-rank, dense deltas that interfere everywhere, so merges of such models need small drop rates and careful weighting, and often simply fail.

Interference is also about scale, not only sign

TIES handles sign conflicts, but task vectors can also differ in overall magnitude by an order of magnitude: a long fine-tune on a large dataset dominates a short one even when they agree in sign. That is why mergekit exposes per-model weights and a normalisation option, and why the per-task validation sweep over weights matters more than the choice between TIES and DARE in many real merges. Some later methods normalise each task vector before merging, or merge layer by layer with different weights, since early layers tend to change less than later ones.

Merging as an ensemble you do not pay for at inference

A model soup approximates an ensemble of its ingredients: for small differences, the output of the averaged weights is close to the average of the outputs, by a first-order Taylor expansion around the shared base. Ensembles improve accuracy and calibration because their members make different errors; soups get part of that gain at the cost of one model. The approximation breaks down as models move further apart, which is the same condition under which linear mode connectivity fails, so soups and merges are most reliable for small, nearby fine-tunes.

What the reference model was doing in DPO

DPO's objective comes from the RLHF objective with a KL penalty to the reference policy (lm6). The reference is not bookkeeping: it defines the implicit reward, and its presence means the policy is rewarded for changes relative to where it started, so tokens the reference already found likely are not pushed further. Reference-free methods replace this with a property of the policy alone (average log-probability for SimPO, odds for ORPO). They save compute, but they change what is optimised: nothing stops the policy's overall distribution from sharpening or drifting as long as the preference margin grows. In practice this shows up as sensitivity to learning rate and β, and sometimes as degraded general abilities, which is why reference-free results are best trusted after independent replication on your data.

Length bias, and why normalisation is not a full fix

Human and model judges tend to prefer longer answers, so preference data correlates "chosen" with "longer". DPO's summed log-ratios let the policy exploit that by making responses longer. SimPO's per-token average removes the direct incentive from the reward, and the length-controlled AlpacaEval metric exists to correct the judge side. But length normalisation also changes what is optimised: a long answer with one terrible sentence averages out well. The robust fix is in the data: balance lengths between chosen and rejected responses, or include pairs where the shorter answer wins.

Why LoRA underperforms for continued pretraining

Continued pretraining asks the model to absorb new knowledge across billions of tokens, a change spread over many directions in weight space. Biderman et al. measured that full fine-tuning on code and maths learns weight perturbations of much higher rank than typical LoRA ranks (10-100× higher), so a rank-16 adapter cannot represent the update. The same study found that LoRA forgot less, which is the flip side: the constraint that limits learning also limits damage. For continued pretraining, where learning is the goal, full fine-tuning plus replay is the standard choice, with rsLoRA at high rank as a cheaper compromise.

Re-warming has a cost on the old distribution

Ibrahim et al. observed that simply re-warming the learning rate increases loss on the original data even when no new data is introduced: the bigger steps knock the model out of the sharp minimum the decayed schedule had settled into. Replay counteracts this as well as distribution shift. It also explains why some labs prefer schedules that do not decay to zero (for example, constant learning rate with a short cool-down) when they expect to continue training later: they leave the model in a state from which continuing is cheaper.

The public cases

Model merging became a mainstream technique through the open-model community: by 2024, merges built with mergekit regularly topped the Hugging Face Open LLM Leaderboard for their size, and DARE's authors reported that their merged 7B model ranked first among 7B models on it. The leaderboard experience is also the main warning: merges optimised against public benchmarks overfit to them, and some leaderboard-topping merges were weak in real use. Continued pretraining is the standard route to domain and language models: Code Llama (Llama 2 plus 500B tokens of code) and Llemma (Code Llama continued on mathematical text) are public examples.

What goes wrong

The merge lost a skill. The merged model scores well on average but one source task drops sharply. Cause: interference or scale imbalance. Fix: evaluate per task, sweep per-model weights and density, try DARE-TIES, or fall back to multi-adapter serving or multitask training.

Merging models that are not really siblings. Two models share an architecture but not a base (one was continued-pretrained, or different base versions), and the merge produces fluent nonsense. Check that both were fine-tuned from the same base checkpoint, and inspect the size of each task vector before merging.

Reference-free tuning drifts. After SimPO or ORPO, chat benchmarks rise but the model has become verbose or worse at maths and code. Track general benchmarks during training, keep the learning rate low, and compare with a DPO baseline.

KTO on biased feedback. Thumbs-up logs reflect what users click, which correlates with length, confidence and flattery. KTO will learn that faithfully. Audit samples of desirable and undesirable examples and rebalance before training.

Continued pretraining forgot too much. General benchmarks fall and instruction following disappears. Increase replay, lower the peak learning rate, check that evaluation includes general tasks from the first checkpoint, and plan the chat-restoration step.

Contamination through domain data. A domain corpus scraped from the web may contain the benchmark you report on. Decontaminate against every evaluation set before training (ev11), or the domain gains are partly fake.

What to track

  • For merges: every source task's metric, a general benchmark suite, and the norm and sparsity of each task vector.
  • For PEFT variants: validation loss and task metrics against a LoRA baseline at matched rank, plus training time and memory, since DoRA costs both.
  • For preference tuning: chosen and rejected rewards and their margin, response length, win rate on held-out prompts, general benchmarks and safety evaluations.
  • For continued pretraining: in-domain and general perplexity, general and domain benchmarks every few billion tokens, and the learning-rate schedule actually applied.

Operational notes

Keep every fine-tune's base checkpoint identifier with its weights; merging, chat vectors and adapter swapping all depend on it. Store task vectors or adapters rather than only merged models, so you can re-merge when one component is retrained. Treat merge configurations like code: version them, review them, and attach the evaluation results that justified each one. For continued pretraining, save intermediate checkpoints every few billion tokens: if a later evaluation shows forgetting, an earlier checkpoint is often the better starting point for restoration.

Goal: reproduce the lesson's TIES, DARE, DoRA and preference-loss numbers in NumPy, then see interference shrink as task vectors get sparser. A few seconds.

import numpy as np
sig = lambda x: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-x))

# 1. TIES on the lesson's three task vectors
T = np.array([[ 0.40, -0.02,  0.30,  0.01, -0.25,  0.03],
              [ 0.35,  0.03, -0.20,  0.02, -0.30, -0.01],
              [-0.10,  0.01,  0.25, -0.02,  0.05,  0.40]])
k = 3
trimmed = np.where(np.abs(T) >= np.sort(np.abs(T), 1)[:, [-k]], T, 0)
sign = np.sign(trimmed.sum(0))
agree = (np.sign(trimmed) == sign) & (trimmed != 0)
ties = np.where(agree.any(0), (trimmed * agree).sum(0) / np.maximum(agree.sum(0), 1), 0)
print("TIES   ", ties.round(3)); print("average", T.mean(0).round(3))

# 2. DARE keeps the expected value
rng = np.random.default_rng(0); p = 0.9
d = np.full(1_000_000, 0.002)
dare = np.where(rng.random(d.size) > p, d / (1 - p), 0)
print("DARE mean", dare.mean().round(5), "nonzero share", (dare != 0).mean())

# 3. Overlap of two random sparse task vectors
for keep in (1.0, 0.2, 0.1):
    a, b = rng.random(100_000) < keep, rng.random(100_000) < keep
    print(f"keep {keep:.0%}: overlap {(a & b).mean():.1%}")

# 4. DoRA on one column
W0, upd = np.array([3.0, 4.0]), np.array([0.3, -0.4])
V = W0 + upd; m = np.linalg.norm(W0)
print("LoRA", V, np.linalg.norm(V).round(2), " DoRA", (m * V / np.linalg.norm(V)).round(2))

# 5. SimPO and ORPO losses from the lesson
rw, rl = 2 * -0.80, 2 * -0.90
print("SimPO", (-np.log(sig(rw - rl - 1.0))).round(2))
Pw, Pl = np.exp(-0.80), np.exp(-0.90)
lor = -np.log(sig(np.log((Pw / (1 - Pw)) / (Pl / (1 - Pl)))))
print("ORPO ", (0.80 + 0.1 * lor).round(3))

What to look for:

  1. Step 1 prints TIES [0.375, 0, 0.275, 0, −0.275, 0.4] against the diluted average: each task's important change survives at full strength.
  2. Step 2: the mean stays at 0.002 although only about 10% of values are non-zero, which is why DARE preserves behaviour.
  3. Step 3: overlap falls from 100% to about 4% and 1% as density drops: sparse task vectors rarely collide.
  4. Steps 4 and 5 reproduce [3.38, 3.69] with length 5 for DoRA, a SimPO loss of 1.17 and an ORPO loss of about 0.86.

Extend it: in step 1, raise k to 6 (no trimming) and watch weight 3's merged value change, showing why trimming matters.

Design — one model from four fine-tunes

You have four LoRA fine-tunes of the same 8B base: SQL generation, invoice JSON extraction, support tone and a DPO adapter that reduces over-refusal. Product wants a single model behind one endpoint within two weeks, with no task losing more than 2 points against its specialist. Plan it.

Answer

Baseline: build a per-task evaluation (SQL execution accuracy, JSON field accuracy, a tone judge, an over-refusal and safety set) plus a general benchmark, and score each specialist and the base.

Merge first, because it takes minutes: merge each adapter into the base to get task vectors; try task arithmetic, TIES (density 0.2-0.5) and DARE-TIES, sweeping per-model weights on validation data. Check that the safety adapter's effect survives, since small-magnitude behaviour changes can be diluted.

If a task loses more than 2 points: either keep one LoRA adapter merged and serve the weakest task's adapter separately with a router, or train a single multitask adapter on the union of the four datasets (possibly with DoRA or rsLoRA at a higher rank), which usually beats merging when the data exists.

Ship: version the merge config and evaluation results together, keep the original adapters so you can re-merge when one is retrained, and monitor per-task metrics in production.

Estimate and plan — a Hindi assistant

You have an English-centric 8B chat model and 30B tokens of cleaned Hindi web text. The model writes poor Hindi, and its tokenizer spends about three times as many tokens on Hindi as on English. Plan the adaptation and estimate compute.

Answer

Tokenizer: consider adding a few thousand frequent Hindi tokens, initialising each new embedding as the average of the pieces it replaces, and training embeddings briefly first; this cuts the token count, cost and context use for Hindi.

Continued pretraining: start from the base model, not the chat model; 30B Hindi tokens plus about 10% English replay (a strong language shift needs more replay than a weak one) ≈ 33B tokens. Compute ≈ 6 × 8×10⁹ × 33×10⁹ ≈ 1.6×10²¹ FLOPs; at 400 TFLOP/s effective per GPU, about 1,100 GPU-hours. Use full fine-tuning, re-warm and re-decay the learning rate, and track Hindi and English perplexity plus English benchmarks.

Restore chat: add the chat vector (original chat minus original base), and note that it may transfer less cleanly if the vocabulary changed, since new token rows have no chat-vector entries; then do SFT and preference tuning on Hindi instruction data, and evaluate safety in Hindi specifically, since English-only safety data may not transfer.
