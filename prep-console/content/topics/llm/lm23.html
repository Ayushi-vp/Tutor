A team fine-tunes an open model to be their company's support assistant. Supervised fine-tuning on a few thousand good answers gets them a model that sounds right. Then they collect what users actually think: for 20,000 prompts, two candidate answers and a human choice between them. Now they face the question that every senior LLM interview asks in some form: "DPO or RLHF, and why?"

The quick answer, "DPO, because it's simpler", is true and not enough. In the first week, their DPO run looks excellent: the training loss falls, the win rate against the old model, judged by a strong LLM, rises by twelve points. In the second week, support agents notice answers have become noticeably longer, a few now ramble past the point where they should stop, and on a closer look the model's probability of producing the exact answers humans preferred has actually gone down during training. None of this is a bug in their code. It is how the DPO loss behaves when you do not understand what it optimises.

The same pattern repeats across the industry. Open-model leaderboards in 2023 and 2024 were full of DPO fine-tunes whose scores came partly from longer answers that LLM judges liked. Teams that switched from PPO to DPO for simplicity later found that their models stopped improving after a round or two, because the fixed dataset no longer described the model's own mistakes. And teams with product feedback (thumbs up and down, flagged answers) found they could not use DPO at all, because it needs pairs. Each of those experiences led to one of the methods in this lesson.

Preference optimisation is where much of a chat model's behaviour is set: tone, helpfulness, refusals, verbosity, how it handles ambiguity. lm6 introduced the pipeline and DPO's central trick; lm20 met three reference-free variants in passing. This lesson goes all the way down. It derives the DPO loss from the KL-constrained reward objective, one algebraic step at a time, so you can reproduce it on a whiteboard. It reads the gradient to see which pairs drive learning and what β really does, and works a three-pair batch by hand. Then it takes the main successors in turn, IPO, KTO, ORPO and SimPO, and for each one names the specific problem it fixes. It covers the questions that decide results in practice: offline versus online and iterative training, which reference model to use, length bias and likelihood displacement, and the quality of the preference data itself. It ends with when you would still choose PPO or GRPO, with Llama 3's post-training as the public case.

What preference data is

A preference dataset is a list of triples: a prompt x, a chosen response y_w (w for "win") and a rejected response y_l (l for "lose"). Someone, a human labeller or a judge model, read both and said which was better. It says nothing about how much better, or why. The job of preference optimisation is to change the model so that, for prompts like these, it puts more probability on responses like the chosen ones and less on responses like the rejected ones, without forgetting everything else it knows.

An analogy: a coach with a leash

Think of a capable junior writer (the fine-tuned model) and a coach who cannot write but can compare two drafts and say which is better. Classic RLHF hires the coach full-time: the writer drafts, the coach scores, the writer adjusts, over and over. The coach's judgement is first distilled into a scoring rubric (the reward model), and the writer optimises against the rubric. There is a leash: the writer may not drift too far from their original style (the KL penalty to the reference model), because a writer who optimises the rubric too hard learns to game it (reward hacking, rs5).

DPO's insight is that you do not need the coach in the room. If you know the leash length and have a pile of the coach's past comparisons, there is a direct formula for how the writer should change, and it can be applied to the pile like ordinary supervised training. The analogy also shows the catch: the pile contains comparisons of drafts the writer did not write, possibly from a different period of their career. Learning only from old comparisons is cheaper but blind to the new mistakes the writer starts making; this is the offline versus online question.

The three ways to use preferences

The map of this lesson

Everything follows from one objective: maximise reward, minus β times the KL divergence from the reference. Solve it exactly and you get a formula for the best policy in terms of the reward. Turn that formula around and the reward can be written in terms of the policy, which is DPO. Each later method changes one ingredient: IPO changes the loss shape so it stops at a finite margin, KTO changes the data to single labelled responses, ORPO and SimPO remove the reference model, and online methods change where the responses come from. Keep that map in mind and the alphabet soup becomes a set of deliberate choices.

What one training step looks like

Concretely, a DPO step takes a batch of, say, 64 triples. For each triple it runs the policy on the prompt plus the chosen response and on the prompt plus the rejected response, and adds up the log-probabilities of the response tokens; it does the same with the frozen reference (or reads precomputed values). That gives four numbers per triple. It turns them into one margin, applies a log-sigmoid, averages over the batch and backpropagates through the policy only. There is no generation, no reward model and no value function. Memory is roughly that of supervised fine-tuning on twice the sequences, plus the reference. This is why DPO spread so quickly: anyone who can run SFT can run it.

A reader who stops here should know three things. DPO is not an approximation: it targets the same optimum as RLHF from a fixed dataset. The reference model and β are the leash that keeps the policy sensible. And the practical problems (length, displacement, noisy labels, stale data) come from what the loss does and does not constrain, not from the maths being wrong.

1. The objective everything starts from

Let π(y|x) be the policy being trained (the probability it assigns to the whole response y given prompt x), π_ref the frozen reference, usually the SFT model, and r(x, y) a reward measuring how good a response is. RLHF's objective, per prompt, is

maximise over π:   E_{y∼π(·|x)} [ r(x, y) ]  −  β · KL( π(·|x) ‖ π_ref(·|x) )

The first term wants high-reward responses. The second, with KL(π‖π_ref) = Σ_y π(y) log(π(y)/π_ref(y)) (math12), charges for moving away from the reference. β sets the price. Without the KL term, the best policy would put all its probability on the single highest-reward response, and since r is a learned, imperfect model of human judgement, that response is often an exploit (rs5). With it, the policy improves while staying close to fluent, reasonable behaviour.

2. Solving the objective exactly

This objective has a closed-form maximiser, and every step is short.

Step 1: write it as one expectation. Since KL is itself an expectation under π, the objective is E_{y∼π}[ r(x,y) − β log(π(y|x)/π_ref(y|x)) ]. Divide by −β, which turns maximising into minimising:

minimise   E_{y∼π} [ log( π(y|x) / π_ref(y|x) ) − r(x,y)/β ]

Step 2: fold the reward into the denominator. −r/β = −log exp(r/β), so the bracket is log( π(y|x) / (π_ref(y|x)·exp(r(x,y)/β)) ).

Step 3: normalise. π_ref·exp(r/β) is not a probability distribution; its total is Z(x) = Σ_y π_ref(y|x) exp(r(x,y)/β), called the partition function. Define π*(y|x) = π_ref(y|x)·exp(r(x,y)/β) / Z(x), which is a proper distribution. Then the bracket equals log(π/π*) − log Z(x), and the objective is

minimise   KL( π(·|x) ‖ π*(·|x) )  −  log Z(x)

Step 4: read off the answer. Z(x) does not depend on π, and a KL divergence is smallest, zero, exactly when the two distributions are equal. So the optimal policy is

π*(y|x) = π_ref(y|x) · exp( r(x,y) / β ) / Z(x)

This is a satisfying formula: take the reference and re-weight each response by the exponential of its reward, with β as the temperature. Small β makes the re-weighting extreme; large β leaves π* close to the reference. RLHF with PPO is a way of approaching this distribution by sampling, because Z(x) sums over every possible response and cannot be computed.

3. From the optimum to the DPO loss

Step 5: invert. Take logs of the optimum and solve for the reward:

r(x,y) = β · log( π*(y|x) / π_ref(y|x) )  +  β · log Z(x)

Any reward can be written in terms of its own optimal policy, plus a term that depends only on the prompt.

Step 6: plug into the preference model. The Bradley–Terry model (lm6) says the probability a labeller prefers y_w over y_l is σ(r(x,y_w) − r(x,y_l)), where σ(z) = 1/(1 + e^−z). It uses only the difference of two rewards for the same prompt, so the β·log Z(x) terms cancel exactly:

P(y_w ≻ y_l | x) = σ( β·log(π*(y_w|x)/π_ref(y_w|x))  −  β·log(π*(y_l|x)/π_ref(y_l|x)) )

Step 7: fit by maximum likelihood. Replace the unknown π* with the policy we are training, π_θ, and choose θ to make the observed preferences as likely as possible: minimise the negative log-likelihood over the dataset D,

L_DPO(θ) = − E_{(x, y_w, y_l) ∈ D} [ log σ( β · ( Δ_θ(x,y_w) − Δ_θ(x,y_l) ) ) ],   Δ_θ(x,y) = log π_θ(y|x) − log π_ref(y|x)

That is the whole derivation. Δ is the log-ratio: how much more likely the policy has made a response than the reference made it. β·Δ is the implicit reward: the reward model "hiding in" the policy, as the DPO paper's title puts it. Fitting this loss fits a Bradley–Terry reward model whose optimal policy is the one being trained, so if the model class can represent it, minimising L_DPO gives the same policy as RLHF would on the same data.

Computing it needs, for each pair, four numbers: the log-probabilities of y_w and y_l under π_θ and under π_ref, each a sum of per-token log-probabilities over the response tokens (prompt tokens are excluded). The reference values can be precomputed once, so training costs about two forward and backward passes per pair, plus two forward passes of the frozen model.

4. What the gradient does

Let h = β(Δ(y_w) − Δ(y_l)) be the margin. Differentiating −log σ(h) gives

∇_θ L = − β · σ(−h) · [ ∇_θ log π_θ(y_w|x)  −  ∇_θ log π_θ(y_l|x) ]

Read it in three parts. The bracket says: increase the log-probability of the chosen response and decrease that of the rejected one, as in supervised learning with one positive and one negative example. The weight σ(−h) says how much this pair matters: it is close to 1 when the implicit reward ranks the pair the wrong way (h very negative), 0.5 when it has no opinion (h = 0, as at the start of training), and close to 0 once the pair is ranked correctly with a comfortable margin. Training therefore concentrates on pairs the model still gets wrong, which is exactly what the DPO paper argues keeps it from degenerating; an unweighted version ("push chosen up, rejected down" with equal force on every pair) collapsed in their experiments. And β scales both the margin inside σ and the overall gradient.

5. Three pairs by hand

Take β = 0.1 and three pairs, with whole-response log-probabilities (sums over tokens):

Pairlog π(y_w)log π(y_l)log π_ref(y_w)log π_ref(y_l)Δ_wΔ_lmargin
A−40−45−42−43+2−2+4
B−60−58−60−600+2−2
C−30−50−31−49+1−1+2

Pair A: h = 0.1 × 4 = 0.4; loss = log(1 + e^−0.4) = 0.513; gradient weight σ(−0.4) = 0.401. Pair B: the policy has made the rejected response more likely than the reference did, so h = −0.2, loss = log(1 + e^0.2) = 0.798 and weight 0.550, the largest of the three. Pair C: h = 0.2, loss 0.598, weight 0.450 (this is lm6's worked pair). Mean loss: (0.513 + 0.798 + 0.598)/3 = 0.636, below the starting value ln 2 = 0.693 that every batch has when π_θ = π_ref.

Two lessons hide in the table. First, look at pair C's raw numbers: the chosen response has log-probability −30 and the rejected −50, a huge gap, yet its margin is only 2. The reference already preferred the chosen response by 18 nats (it is probably shorter or more typical). DPO cares only about the change relative to the reference, which removes "this response is just more likely" from the reward. Second, the implicit rewards are β·Δ: for pair A, +0.2 for the chosen and −0.2 for the rejected. They are small numbers, and they are what a DPO model "believes" about quality; you can read them off any DPO-trained model as a reward model.

Now repeat with β = 0.5: the margins become 2.0, −1.0 and 1.0, losses 0.127, 1.313 and 0.313, weights 0.119, 0.731 and 0.269. The correctly ranked pairs are nearly "done", while the misranked pair B dominates the batch. That is β's second role: it sets the margin at which a pair counts as learned.

6. Choosing β

Because β is both the KL price and the margin scale, it trades two risks. Too small (0.01) and the policy can move far from the reference to separate pairs, which is when length exploitation, displacement and degeneration appear. Too large (1.0) and the policy barely moves; the result looks like the SFT model. Most published recipes land near 0.1: the DPO paper's main experiments, Llama 3 (with a learning rate of 10⁻⁵ for its large models), and many open recipes. Tuning should watch three things together: the held-out preference accuracy (how often the implicit reward ranks unseen pairs correctly), the KL from the reference (or the average log-ratio of the policy's own samples), and the real target metric. Learning rate interacts with β; DPO commonly uses learning rates of about 10⁻⁷ to 10⁻⁶ for 7B-scale models with one to three epochs, much lower than SFT.

7. IPO: when the leash goes slack

Azar et al. (2023) pointed out a flaw hidden in step 6. Suppose a pair is always labelled the same way, P(y_w ≻ y_l) = 1, which is also what a dataset with one label per pair looks like to maximum likelihood. Bradley–Terry can only produce probability 1 with an infinite reward gap, so the DPO loss keeps decreasing as Δ(y_w) − Δ(y_l) → ∞, which it can achieve by driving π(y_l) towards zero. The KL term was supposed to stop this, but in DPO the reference enters only through the log-ratios, and an infinite margin is optimal regardless of β. The paper frames this as the regularisation becoming "weaker and weaker the more deterministic the preferences", and in the finite-data regime even pairs with true preference 0.8 can look deterministic.

Their fix, from a general family they call ΨPO, takes Ψ as the identity, which yields IPO:

L_IPO = E [ ( (Δ(y_w) − Δ(y_l)) − 1/(2τ) )² ]

A squared loss with a finite target margin: once a pair's log-ratio margin reaches 1/(2τ), it stops pulling, and overshooting is penalised. With τ = 0.1 the target is 5 nats. For our pairs: A (margin 4) costs (4 − 5)² = 1, B (−2) costs 49, C (2) costs 9. Unlike DPO's log-sigmoid, which flattens as the margin grows but never stops rewarding more, IPO's squared loss turns back up past 5. It is a direct guard against overfitting small or one-sided preference datasets.

8. KTO: learning from thumbs up and thumbs down

Pairs are expensive, and many products produce a different signal: a single response with a thumbs up or down. KTO (Ethayarajh et al., 2024) is built for that. Each example is (x, y, label). The model's implicit reward is again r_θ = log(π_θ(y|x)/π_ref(y|x)). KTO compares it with a reference point z₀, an estimate of the KL between policy and reference computed from mismatched prompt-response pairs in the batch (and clipped at zero), and applies a value function inspired by Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory:

desirable y:    v = λ_D · σ( β · (r_θ − z₀) )        undesirable y:   v = λ_U · σ( β · (z₀ − r_θ) )
L_KTO = E [ λ_label − v ]

A desirable response is rewarded for being raised above the reference point and an undesirable one for being pushed below it, with saturation (the sigmoid) so outliers do not dominate, and separate weights λ_D and λ_U for gains and losses (prospect theory's loss aversion). The paper reports KTO matching or exceeding DPO on models from 1B to 30B parameters, even when pairs are split into separate single examples, and handling heavy imbalance: it matched DPO while using up to 90% fewer desirable examples. It recommends setting the weights so that λ_D·n_D / (λ_U·n_U) is between 1 and 1.5, where n_D and n_U count desirable and undesirable examples. Use KTO when your data is unpaired, imbalanced or comes from product feedback.

9. ORPO and SimPO: dropping the reference

The reference model costs a second copy of the weights in memory (or a precomputation pass) and, as pair C showed, ties the reward to change rather than to absolute quality. Two popular methods remove it.

ORPO (Hong, Lee and Thorne, 2024) folds preference learning into supervised fine-tuning, so there is one stage instead of two. Define P(y|x) as the exponential of the mean per-token log-probability, and the odds of a response as P/(1 − P). The loss is the usual SFT negative log-likelihood on the chosen response plus λ times an odds-ratio penalty:

L_ORPO = L_SFT(y_w)  +  λ · ( − log σ( log( odds(y_w) / odds(y_l) ) ) )

For pair C, with 20 tokens in y_w and 50 in y_l, the mean log-probabilities are −1.5 and −1.0, so P_w = e^−1.5 = 0.223 (odds 0.287) and P_l = e^−1.0 = 0.368 (odds 0.582). The log odds ratio is ln(0.287/0.582) = −0.71 and the penalty −log σ(−0.71) = 1.11, which ORPO adds (times λ, 0.1 to 0.2 in the paper) to the SFT loss. The SFT term keeps the model generating chosen-style responses; the odds term pushes the rejected style down. The paper reported strong AlpacaEval and IFEval results fine-tuning Phi-2, Llama 2 7B and Mistral 7B on UltraFeedback alone.

SimPO (Meng, Xia and Chen, 2024) keeps DPO's Bradley–Terry form but changes the reward to the length-normalised log-probability under the policy alone, and adds a target margin γ:

L_SimPO = − log σ( (β/|y_w|)·log π_θ(y_w|x)  −  (β/|y_l|)·log π_θ(y_l|x)  −  γ )

Two problems are fixed at once. DPO's reward (a log-ratio summed over tokens) does not match how the model generates, by average per-token likelihood, so a DPO model can rank a response highly yet be unlikely to produce it; SimPO's reward is exactly the quantity generation follows. And because it divides by length, adding tokens does not inflate the reward, which directly attacks length bias. For pair C with β = 2 and γ = 1: (2/20)(−30) − (2/50)(−50) − 1 = −3 + 2 − 1 = −2, loss log(1 + e²) = 2.13. Under DPO this pair looked nearly fine; under SimPO it is badly misranked, because per token the rejected response is the more likely one. The paper reports SimPO beating DPO by up to 6.4 points on AlpacaEval 2 and 7.5 on Arena-Hard without substantially longer responses, with β around 2 to 2.5 and γ between 0.3 and 1.6 depending on the setting.

11. Offline, online and iterative

Offline DPO learns from a fixed dataset, often generated by other models (UltraFeedback-style datasets collect responses from many models and rate them with GPT-4). Two things go wrong with that. The responses are off-policy: the model being trained would not produce them, so learning "prefer A over B" may say little about the responses it actually generates. And the data never updates: as the policy changes, its new failure modes are never compared or corrected.

Online methods sample from the current policy during training and get preferences on those samples, from a reward model or an LLM judge, and controlled studies favour them. Tang et al. (Google DeepMind, 2024) found a clear advantage for online over offline methods at matched budgets, tested hypotheses such as data coverage and data quality, and found that neither alone explained the gap; offline-trained policies were better at classifying pairs but worse at generating. Xu et al. (2024) found that PPO with the right implementation details beat DPO on every testbed they tried, from dialogue to competitive code.

Iterative DPO sits between: train with DPO, sample many responses per prompt from the new model, label new pairs (humans, a reward model, or best-versus-worst of k samples), train again, repeat. Each round's data is on-policy for the model at the start of that round. Llama 3's post-training is the prominent example: several rounds, each with reward-model training, rejection sampling, SFT and DPO on "the most recent batches of preference data collected using the best performing models from the previous alignment rounds", which, in their words, makes the data "conform better to the distribution of the policy model".

One iterative round, with illustrative numbers. Take 5,000 prompts drawn from real traffic. Sample 8 responses per prompt from the current model at temperature 0.8: 40,000 responses. Score each with a reward model or a rubric-driven judge. For each prompt, pair the highest-scoring response with the lowest-scoring one, and keep the pair only if the score gap exceeds a threshold, because near-ties are mostly label noise; suppose 3,600 of the 5,000 pairs survive. Have humans re-label a random 300 of them: if they agree with the automatic label 85% of the time, the noise is tolerable; if 65%, fix the labeller before training. Run one epoch of DPO from the current model, with the current model as the reference. Evaluate on held-out human preferences, length-controlled win rate and a capability suite. If the round helped, the new model becomes both the sampler and the reference for the next round. Three to five rounds is typical before returns diminish or the labeller's biases start to dominate.

12. Choosing the reference model

The reference does two jobs: it anchors the KL constraint, and it defines what "change" means in the log-ratio. It should therefore assign sensible probability to the responses in the dataset. The default is the SFT model the run starts from. If the pairs were generated by other models, the SFT model may find both responses unlikely, and log-ratios become noisy; the DPO paper's fix ("Preferred-FT", supervised fine-tuning on the preferred responses) is to first fine-tune on the chosen responses so the reference is in-distribution, then run DPO from there. In iterative training, the reference is usually reset to the latest starting checkpoint each round, so the KL leash is measured from the model that generated the round's data. Reference-free methods skip the question entirely, which is part of their appeal, but must then avoid drifting by other means: SimPO's margin and normalisation, ORPO's SFT term, few epochs and small learning rates.

Likelihood displacement, explained

DPO's gradient raises log π(y_w) and lowers log π(y_l) by equal force, scaled by σ(−h). But y_w and y_l for the same prompt usually share most of their tokens and structure, so their gradients overlap heavily; pushing y_l down also pushes y_w down. The loss is satisfied as long as y_l falls faster, so both log-probabilities can fall throughout training. Probability mass that leaves them goes somewhere, often to responses in neither set, which can be longer, oddly formatted, or fail to emit the end-of-turn token. Monitoring log π(y_w) on held-out pairs reveals it. Llama 3 added a negative log-likelihood (NLL) term, the ordinary SFT loss, with coefficient 0.2 on chosen responses and masked formatting tokens (headers and terminators) out of the DPO loss; they report that letting those tokens contribute caused "tail repetition or abruptly generating termination tokens". Both fixes target displacement directly.

Why the same algorithm is both a reward model and a policy

Because β·Δ_θ is an implicit reward, a DPO-trained model can be used to score responses: compute log-ratios against the reference and compare. Its held-out preference accuracy is the pairwise accuracy of that reward. Tang et al.'s observation that offline policies are good classifiers but worse generators fits this view: DPO optimises the reward on the given pairs, which shapes probabilities for those sequences, but says little about the probability of sequences the model would actually sample. Online methods spend their training signal on exactly those sequences.

The length problem, mechanically

Three effects compound. Raters prefer longer answers somewhat independently of quality, so chosen responses are longer on average. The DPO log-ratio sums over tokens, so a systematic per-token shift in favour of chosen-style text accumulates more in longer responses, making length an easy feature to separate pairs on. And many evaluations use LLM judges with the same bias, so a longer model wins more and the problem is invisible. Park et al. (2024) showed significant length exploitation by DPO and linked it to out-of-distribution bootstrapping; their R-DPO subtracts α(|y_w| − |y_l|) inside the sigmoid, so the model gets no credit for the length difference itself. SimPO and length-normalised DPO divide by length instead; Tülu 3 used length-normalised DPO throughout. On the evaluation side, use length-controlled win rates (AlpacaEval 2's length-controlled, or LC, metric) and track average length as a first-class metric.

Preference-data quality is the ceiling

InstructGPT reported that its trained labellers agreed with each other 72.6 ± 1.5% of the time (held-out labellers, 77.3 ± 1.3%). If a quarter of the pairs could flip with a different labeller, a perfect model of "the" human preference is not a well-defined target, and DPO's log-sigmoid keeps pushing on misranked pairs, including the mislabelled ones, with the most weight. Practical defences: collect two or three labels per pair on a sample and estimate noise; drop pairs whose responses are nearly identical or whose labels disagree; use conservative or label-smoothed variants that assume a fraction ε of labels are flipped; prefer on-policy pairs (differences in the model's own outputs are what matter); and keep a clean expert-labelled set for evaluation. With AI judges, measure agreement with humans before trusting them, and check the judge for position and length bias.

Online preference learning in practice

Online and iterative pipelines need a labeller that can keep up: humans are too slow for per-step labelling, so the usual choices are a reward model, an LLM judge with a rubric, or a programmatic check. Each brings its own failure: reward models get hacked when optimised too hard; judges carry their biases into the policy; checkers only exist for some tasks. A common recipe samples k responses per prompt (Llama 3 sampled 10 to 30 for rejection sampling), scores them, and forms best-versus-worst pairs, which are on-policy and have large quality gaps, reducing label noise. "Self-rewarding" variants use the policy itself as the judge, which is cheap but risks reinforcing its own blind spots.

When PPO or GRPO is still the right tool

Online RL earns its complexity when you can score new samples reliably. Verifiable rewards (tests pass, the answer matches, the JSON validates) are the clearest case, and they are how reasoning models are trained (GRPO with RLVR, rs4): the policy explores, finds strategies no dataset contains, and is scored without bias. RL also suits multi-objective shaping (helpfulness plus a safety penalty plus a format bonus), multi-turn and agentic tasks where a single pair is a poor description of success, and organisations with mature RL infrastructure. Llama 3 chose DPO over PPO for its preference stage because "DPO required less compute for large-scale models and performed better, especially on instruction following benchmarks like IFEval"; Tülu 3 used length-normalised DPO for preferences and then RL with verifiable rewards for maths and instruction constraints. Many current pipelines use both: DPO-family methods for broad human preferences, RL for capabilities with checkable outcomes.

Generalising DPO's derivation

The derivation works for any objective of the form "reward minus a divergence from a reference" whose optimum has a closed form. Swapping the KL for other divergences, adding per-token rewards, or using a list-wise (Plackett–Luce) model of rankings instead of pairs gives a large family of "direct alignment" losses. The common structure is always the same: write the reward through the optimal policy, cancel the intractable normaliser, and fit by maximum likelihood. When an interviewer asks about a new variant, place it on that map: what preference model, what divergence, what data, and what reference.

The public case: Llama 3's post-training (Meta, 2024)

Llama 3's paper is the most detailed public account of preference optimisation at scale. Post-training ran in several rounds. Each round trained a reward model on all preference data (chosen, rejected and, for some prompts, a third "edited" response better than both), used it for rejection sampling (10 to 30 samples per prompt from the latest model) to build SFT data, fine-tuned, and then ran DPO on the most recent batches of preference data collected with the best models from the previous round, so the pairs were close to the policy's own distribution. The team "explored on-policy algorithms such as PPO" and chose DPO because it needed less compute at their scale and performed better, especially on IFEval. They used β = 0.1 and a learning rate of 10⁻⁵, masked header and termination tokens from the DPO loss because they caused tail repetition and premature termination, and added a 0.2-weighted NLL term on chosen responses to keep formatting and prevent the chosen log-probability from falling. They also averaged models from runs with different data or hyperparameters at each stage. Nearly every failure mode in this lesson appears in that list as a deliberate fix.

Failure modes in production

Verbosity creep. Responses get longer each round, judges reward it, metrics look good, users complain. Track response length per release, use length-controlled evaluation, and balance or normalise length in training.

Formatting and stopping failures. Missing end-of-turn tokens, repeated tails, broken markdown or tool-call syntax after DPO: typically displacement on structural tokens. Mask them from the preference loss, add an SFT/NLL term, and include structural checks in evaluation.

Over-refusal or sycophancy shifts. Preference data encodes labeller habits: if raters reward agreement or caution, the model learns flattery or refusals. Evaluate refusal rates on benign prompts and agreement-with-false-premise tests each round (sf9 and rs5 cover these).

Capability regressions. Preference tuning can erode skills not represented in the pairs (maths, code, long context). Llama 3 found short-context DPO did not hurt long context as long as SFT was strong; check your own capability suite before and after.

Judge drift. In AI-labelled pipelines, a judge model upgrade silently changes the target. Version the judge and re-measure its human agreement when it changes.

What to monitor during a run

Training: loss, held-out preference accuracy, mean implicit reward of chosen and rejected, the log-probabilities of chosen and rejected separately (watch for both falling), KL from the reference on the policy's own samples, and average generated length. After training: task benchmarks, length-controlled win rates, human spot checks, refusal and safety metrics, and capability regressions. The two most common early warnings are chosen log-probability falling steadily and average generation length rising faster than win rate.

A minimal production recipe

For a team adding preference tuning to a fine-tuned model for the first time, a sound default is: start from a good SFT model trained on in-distribution data; build pairs on-policy by sampling several responses per prompt from that model; label with humans for a seed set and a calibrated judge for scale, keeping only pairs with a clear gap; run DPO with β = 0.1, one epoch, a low learning rate, formatting tokens masked and a small NLL term; evaluate with length control and a capability suite; and repeat in rounds. Reach for KTO when the data is product thumbs, for SimPO or length-normalised DPO when length creep appears, for IPO when the dataset is small and one-sided, and for GRPO when a verifiable reward exists. Keep every round's data, configuration and judge version, so a regression can be traced to the round that caused it.

Goal: compute the DPO loss, gradient weights and implicit rewards for three preference pairs by hand and in code, and see how β, IPO and SimPO change the picture. About 10 minutes, standard library only.

  1. Save and run with Python 3.12 as lm23_lab.py:
import math
sig = lambda z: 1 / (1 + math.exp(-z))
# (logp_w, logp_l, ref_w, ref_l, len_w, len_l)
pairs = {"A": (-40, -45, -42, -43, 30, 32),
         "B": (-60, -58, -60, -60, 45, 40),
         "C": (-30, -50, -31, -49, 20, 50)}

for beta in (0.1, 0.5):
    losses = []
    print(f"beta = {beta}")
    for k, (pw, pl, rw, rl, nw, nl) in pairs.items():
        margin = (pw - rw) - (pl - rl)
        h = beta * margin
        loss = -math.log(sig(h)); losses.append(loss)
        print(f"  {k}: margin {margin:+d}  loss {loss:.3f}  weight {sig(-h):.3f}"
              f"  implicit rewards {beta*(pw-rw):+.2f} / {beta*(pl-rl):+.2f}")
    print(f"  mean loss {sum(losses)/3:.3f}   (start of training: {math.log(2):.3f})")

tau = 0.1
for k, (pw, pl, rw, rl, nw, nl) in pairs.items():
    margin = (pw - rw) - (pl - rl)
    ipo = (margin - 1 / (2 * tau)) ** 2
    simpo_h = 2.0 * pw / nw - 2.0 * pl / nl - 1.0        # beta=2, gamma=1
    print(f"{k}: IPO {ipo:5.1f}   SimPO loss {-math.log(sig(simpo_h)):.3f}")

What to look for. At β = 0.1 the losses should be 0.513, 0.798 and 0.598 (mean 0.636, below ln 2 = 0.693), with pair B, the misranked one, getting the largest weight (0.550). At β = 0.5 the correctly ranked pairs nearly vanish (weights 0.119 and 0.269) and B dominates (0.731). IPO gives 1, 49 and 9: pair A is almost at the target margin of 5. SimPO flips pair C into a large loss (2.127) because, per token, the rejected response is more likely; DPO saw C as nearly solved. Change the lengths in pair A to make the chosen response twice as long and see how SimPO reacts while DPO's numbers stay the same.

Diagnose and redesign a preference stage

Your team ran offline DPO (β = 0.05, 3 epochs, learning rate 5 × 10⁻⁶) on 60,000 pairs from a public dataset whose responses came from a mix of other models, labelled by an LLM judge. Results: judge win rate +15 points over the SFT model; average response length +70%; the chosen-response log-probability fell steadily through training; 2% of responses now fail to end. What went wrong, and what would you change?

Answer

Diagnosis. Several compounding causes. β = 0.05 with three epochs and a high learning rate gives the policy a long leash and many passes to separate pairs, so it exploits whatever separates them most easily; length is the obvious one, and the judge, which likely shares the length bias, rewards it. The falling chosen log-probability is likelihood displacement, and the non-terminating responses are displacement reaching the end-of-turn token. The data is off-policy: responses from other models, which the SFT reference may find unlikely, make log-ratios noisy.

Changes. Re-evaluate first with a length-controlled metric and human spot checks: the +15 may be mostly length. Then: raise β to about 0.1 and cut to one epoch with a learning rate around 5 × 10⁻⁷; mask special and termination tokens from the DPO loss and add an NLL term (around 0.2) on chosen responses; use a length-normalised objective (SimPO or length-normalised DPO) or balance lengths in the pairs; fine-tune on chosen responses before DPO so the reference is in-distribution, or better, regenerate pairs on-policy by sampling several responses per prompt from your SFT model and labelling best versus worst. Calibrate the judge against a few hundred human labels. Then run iteratively, a round at a time, monitoring chosen log-probability, length, KL and a capability suite.
