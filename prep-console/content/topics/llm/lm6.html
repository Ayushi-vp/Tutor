In 2020 OpenAI released GPT-3, a 175-billion-parameter model that had read a large slice of the internet. It could write poetry, translate and complete code. It was also frustrating to use. Ask it "Explain the moon landing to a six-year-old" and it might produce a list of similar prompts, as if continuing a web page of writing exercises. Ask it a question and it might answer, or ramble, or invent a confident falsehood, or produce something offensive it had picked up from its training data. It knew a great deal; it simply was not trying to help you, because nothing in its training had asked it to.

Two years later the same company published InstructGPT, and the result surprised people: human raters preferred the answers of a 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT model to those of the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3, a model more than 100 times larger. The difference was not more data or a better architecture. It was what happened after pretraining: a stage of imitating example answers written by people, and a stage of optimising against people's preferences between answers. ChatGPT, launched later that year, was built the same way, and every chat assistant since follows some version of this pipeline.

For an AI engineer this pipeline matters in three practical ways. It explains the behaviour of the models you use: why they are helpful and polite, why they sometimes flatter you or refuse harmless requests, and why no amount of tuning makes them stop hallucinating. It is the toolkit you reach for when you adapt a model to your own domain or style. And it is a standard interview topic, usually with a request to write down and explain a loss.

This lesson starts from what pretraining produces and why it is not an assistant, then covers supervised fine-tuning, preference data, the reward model (with the Bradley–Terry loss worked by hand), RLHF with its KL penalty and the reward-hacking problem it guards against, the closed-form optimum that makes DPO possible, and DPO itself with one pair's loss computed by hand. It ends with what each stage can and cannot fix. The lab builds a toy world of five candidate responses, trains a reward model on simulated comparisons, and shows that DPO lands on exactly the policy RLHF would.

Three stages, three teachers

Think of training an assistant the way you might train a new employee who has read every book in the library but has never had a job. Pretraining is the reading: the model sees trillions of tokens of text and learns to predict the next token, absorbing language, facts, styles and reasoning patterns. It comes out knowledgeable but aimless. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is shadowing: the employee studies thousands of examples of good answers to customer requests, written by experienced colleagues, and learns to imitate them, absorbing the format, the tone and the habit of actually answering. Preference tuning is feedback: the employee drafts several answers, a supervisor says which ones are better, and the employee adjusts towards what gets approved. The supervisor does not need to be able to write the perfect answer, only to recognise a better one.

The analogy shows what each stage can fix. Reading gives knowledge; shadowing gives format and manners; feedback polishes judgement on whatever the supervisor can assess. None of the later stages adds knowledge the reading did not provide, and feedback teaches whatever the supervisor rewards, including bad habits if the supervisor is easily impressed by long, confident answers. Where the analogy breaks: a model has no intentions to correct, and "adjusting towards approval" is a precise mathematical operation on probabilities, which the body of this lesson writes down.

The pipeline in one picture

Why not just pretrain on better data?

A natural question is why the assistant behaviour is not simply baked into pretraining by choosing better text. The answer is scale and specificity. Pretraining needs trillions of tokens, far more than anyone could write in an assistant's voice, so it uses whatever text exists, and most text on the internet is not an assistant helping a user. The post-training stages are tiny by comparison (thousands to hundreds of thousands of examples) but highly specific: every example is exactly the behaviour you want. This division of labour is the reason the pipeline exists. Pretraining buys general competence cheaply per token; post-training spends expensive human attention where it has the most effect, on the model's default behaviour. It also explains why companies can release many differently behaving products, a coding assistant, a customer-service bot, a cautious medical assistant, from the same base model: they share the expensive stage and differ only in the cheap ones.

What "preference" means in numbers

The preference stage works with comparisons: a prompt, two responses, and a label saying which one a rater preferred. From many such comparisons, the training either fits a reward model, a network that gives any response a score, and then optimises the chat model to earn high scores (RLHF), or skips the reward model and adjusts the chat model's probabilities directly so that preferred responses become more likely relative to rejected ones (DPO). Both keep the model close to its starting point, measured by a quantity called KL divergence, so it does not forget how to write well in pursuit of a score.

1. What pretraining produces

Pretraining trains a decoder-only transformer (lm3) to predict the next token on an enormous corpus, typically trillions of tokens of web pages, books, code and papers, filtered and deduplicated. The objective is plain cross-entropy: at every position, −log of the probability the model gave to the actual next token, averaged over everything. This is self-supervised (ml1): the labels come from the text itself, so the data scales without human labelling, which is why pretraining can use so much of it and why it consumes almost all of a model's training compute. Llama 3, for example, was pretrained on about 15 trillion tokens.

The result, a base model, is remarkably capable and remarkably unhelpful. To minimise next-token loss on the internet it has had to learn grammar, facts, the structure of arguments, code, arithmetic patterns and many styles of writing. But its behaviour is to continue a document in the way such documents tend to continue. A question might be followed by an answer, or by another question, or by a forum signature. It has no default persona, no notion of a user, no habit of stopping when the answer is complete, and no preference for truth over plausible fiction, because the internet contains both.

2. Prompting a base model, and why it is not enough

You can coax a base model into answering by writing the start of a document in which an answer naturally follows, for example a few question–answer pairs and then your question ("few-shot prompting"). GPT-3's paper was largely about this. But it is fragile: results depend on the exact examples, the model still drifts, and there is no way to express "be concise, be honest, refuse harmful requests" except by more examples. Engineers want a model that treats any instruction as an instruction. That requires changing the model's weights so that the assistant role becomes its default behaviour.

3. Supervised fine-tuning

SFT continues training the base model on a dataset of demonstrations: prompts paired with high-quality responses, written by people or curated from good existing answers, formatted as conversations with the model's chat template (lm1). The loss is the same next-token cross-entropy as pretraining, with one change: it is usually computed only on the tokens of the assistant's response. The prompt tokens are fed in so the model can condition on them, but their predictions are excluded from the loss, because the goal is to learn to produce answers, not to imitate user messages.

A worked view of the loss: suppose the response is "Paris." (two tokens, "Paris" and "."). If after the prompt the model gives "Paris" probability 0.6 and then "." probability 0.9, the SFT loss on this example is −(ln 0.6 + ln 0.9)/2 = (0.511 + 0.105)/2 = 0.308 per token. Gradient descent raises those probabilities. Over thousands of examples, the model learns the format (answer directly, use the template's end-of-turn token), the style (structured, polite, concise or detailed as demonstrated), and the general habit of doing what the instruction says. InstructGPT's SFT dataset had about 13,000 training prompts, written by labellers or taken from early API usage. The LIMA study (Zhou et al., 2023) fine-tuned a 65B LLaMA on only 1,000 carefully curated examples and found its responses equivalent to or preferred over GPT-4's in 43% of cases, which the authors took as evidence that almost all knowledge comes from pretraining and SFT mainly teaches the format to express it.

4. What SFT can and cannot do

SFT is efficient at teaching anything that can be demonstrated: formats, tone, tool-call syntax (lm16), domain conventions, refusing certain requests. Its limits follow from imitation. The model can only be as good as its demonstrations, and writing excellent demonstrations is slow and expensive. It learns to imitate the surface of answers it may not be able to back up: if a demonstration states a fact the model does not know, SFT teaches it to state facts of that kind confidently, which is one root of hallucination (lm15). And imitation gives no signal about which of two imperfect answers is better; every token in the demonstration is treated as equally correct.

5. Why comparisons

People are much better at judging than at producing. Most of us cannot write a perfect explanation of quantum tunnelling for a child, but we can usually say which of two attempts is clearer. Comparisons are also more consistent across raters than absolute scores ("is this a 6 or a 7?"), which vary with each rater's personal scale. So the preference stage collects, for many prompts, several responses sampled from the SFT model and asks raters which they prefer. InstructGPT showed labellers between 4 and 9 responses per prompt and asked for a ranking, which yields up to K(K − 1)/2 pairwise comparisons per prompt (36 for K = 9); its reward-model dataset had about 33,000 training prompts.

6. The reward model and the Bradley–Terry loss

A reward model takes a prompt and a response and outputs one number, its score. In practice it is an LLM (InstructGPT used a 6B model initialised from SFT) with its vocabulary output layer replaced by a single scalar output read at the last token. It is trained with the Bradley–Terry model of comparisons, which says the probability that response A is preferred to B is the logistic sigmoid of the difference of their scores:

P(y_w ≻ y_l | x) = σ( r(x, y_w) − r(x, y_l) )        σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e^(−z))
loss = −log σ( r(x, y_w) − r(x, y_l) )

where y_w is the chosen ("winning") response and y_l the rejected one. This is exactly logistic regression (ml6) on the score difference. By hand: if the reward model gives the chosen response 1.5 and the rejected 0.3, the difference is 1.2, the predicted probability that the chosen one wins is σ(1.2) = 1/(1 + e^(−1.2)) = 1/(1 + 0.301) = 0.769, and the loss is −ln 0.769 = 0.263. If the scores were reversed the loss would be −ln σ(−1.2) = −ln 0.231 = 1.463, a much larger penalty. Note that only differences matter: adding 10 to every score changes nothing, so reward values have no absolute meaning. In the lab, a reward model fitted to 4,000 simulated comparisons recovers the hidden true rewards [0, 1, 2, 1.2, −2] as [0, 1.09, 2.16, 1.14, −1.96].

7. RLHF: optimising against the reward with a leash

With a reward model, the chat model (now called the policy, in reinforcement-learning language) can be optimised to produce high-scoring responses. The objective is

maximise  E_{x ~ prompts, y ~ π(·|x)} [ r(x, y) ]  −  β · KL( π(·|x) ‖ π_ref(·|x) )

The first term rewards high scores. The second is the KL divergence between the policy π and the frozen reference π_ref (the SFT model): a measure of how differently the two assign probability to responses, zero when they are identical. For one response it is estimated by log π(y|x) − log π_ref(y|x), summed over the response's tokens, and InstructGPT subtracts β times this per-token quantity from the reward. The coefficient β sets the trade-off. Because responses are generated by sampling, with no gradient through the choice of tokens, the optimisation uses a policy-gradient RL algorithm, typically PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization): sample responses for a batch of prompts, score them, estimate which tokens contributed to above-average reward, and nudge their probabilities up, with a clipping rule that limits how far any single update can move the policy. PPO needs four models in memory at once (policy, reference, reward model and a value model that estimates expected reward), which makes it heavy to run.

8. Reward hacking and why the leash matters

The reward model is trained on responses from the SFT model. As the policy moves away, it produces responses unlike anything the reward model saw, and there the reward model's scores are unreliable. Optimising hard finds those errors: if raters slightly preferred longer answers, the policy learns to pad; if a phrasing happens to score well, it repeats it. This is reward hacking, an instance of Goodhart's law ("when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"). The lab shows it in miniature. A proxy reward over-rates a "verbose" response. As β falls (weaker leash), the proxy reward of the optimised policy rises from 0.81 to 2.15, but its true reward peaks at 1.28 and then falls to 1.20, while with an accurate reward model the true reward would have reached 2.00. The KL penalty keeps the policy in the region where the reward model is trustworthy; early stopping on independent evaluations does the same from the other side.

9. The optimum of the RLHF objective

For a fixed prompt, the KL-regularised objective has an exact solution. The best policy is the reference policy reweighted by the exponentiated reward:

π*(y | x) = π_ref(y | x) · exp( r(x, y) / β ) / Z(x)

where Z(x) is the normalising constant that makes the probabilities sum to 1. Read it as: start from what the SFT model would say, and multiply each response's probability by e^(reward/β). With a large β the multipliers are all near 1 and the policy barely changes; with a small β the highest-reward response dominates. In the lab's toy world, with β = 10 the policy is almost the reference (KL 0.007), at β = 1 the best response's probability rises from 0.10 to 0.35, at β = 0.3 it reaches 0.88 and at β = 0.1 the policy puts all its mass on it (KL 2.27). For a real LLM this formula cannot be used directly, because Z(x) sums over every possible response, which is why RLHF uses RL to approach it. But it is the key to DPO.

10. DPO: the reward model was hiding in the policy

Rafailov et al. (2023) noticed that the formula can be turned around. Take logs and rearrange: r(x, y) = β·log(π*(y|x)/π_ref(y|x)) + β·log Z(x). Any reward can be written in terms of its optimal policy, plus a term that depends only on the prompt. Now substitute this expression into the Bradley–Terry loss. The loss uses only the difference of the rewards of the chosen and rejected responses to the same prompt, so the troublesome β·log Z(x) term cancels. What remains is a loss on the policy itself:

L_DPO = −log σ( β · [ (log π(y_w|x) − log π_ref(y_w|x)) − (log π(y_l|x) − log π_ref(y_l|x)) ] )

Training the policy with this loss on preference pairs is equivalent to fitting a reward model and then solving the KL-regularised RL problem exactly, but there is no reward model, no sampling and no RL: just a forward pass of the policy and the frozen reference on both responses of each pair, and a classification-style loss. The quantity β·log(π/π_ref) is called the implicit reward: how much more likely the tuned model has made a response than the reference made it. The lab trains a tabular policy with this loss on the same 4,000 comparisons and lands on exactly the RLHF optimum, [0.109, 0.327, 0.348, 0.207, 0.009] in both cases.

11. One DPO loss by hand

Take β = 0.1 and one pair. Log-probabilities of whole responses are sums over their tokens. Suppose the policy gives the chosen response a log-probability of −12.0 and the rejected −15.0, while the frozen reference gave −13.0 and −14.0. The chosen log-ratio is −12.0 − (−13.0) = +1.0 (the policy made it e¹ ≈ 2.7 times more likely), the rejected log-ratio is −15.0 − (−14.0) = −1.0 (the policy made it 2.7 times less likely). The margin is β × (1.0 − (−1.0)) = 0.2. The loss is −log σ(0.2) = −log 0.550 = 0.598. At the start of training the policy equals the reference, both log-ratios are 0, the margin is 0 and the loss is −log 0.5 = ln 2 = 0.693. The gradient on this pair is proportional to σ(−0.2) = 0.450: pairs the model already ranks correctly with a large margin contribute little, so training concentrates on pairs it still gets wrong.

12. DPO versus RLHF in practice

DPO's appeal is operational: one trainable model plus a frozen copy, a loss as simple as supervised learning, no generation in the loop, and stable training. Rafailov et al. reported that on the Reddit TL;DR ("too long; didn't read") summarisation task, judged against reference summaries by GPT-4, DPO reached a win rate of about 61% at sampling temperature 0, against 57% for PPO at its best temperature, and was more robust to the sampling temperature; in a human evaluation DPO samples were preferred 58% of the time over PPO's. The limitations come from DPO being offline: it learns only from the fixed pairs it is given and cannot try new responses and see how they score. If the pairs were sampled from a different model, or are noisy, DPO can over-fit them, and it has been observed to lower the probability of both chosen and rejected responses while widening the gap between them. Online and iterative variants generate new pairs from the current policy and label them (with humans, a reward model or a judge model), recovering some of RL's exploration. Large labs use RL with reward models, DPO-style methods, or both in sequence.

13. Variants worth knowing

Rejection sampling (best-of-n) fine-tuning: sample n responses per prompt, keep the one the reward model scores highest, and do SFT on those; simple and stable, used in Llama 2's pipeline alongside PPO. RLAIF and Constitutional AI: replace some human labels with labels from a model prompted with a written set of principles, as in Anthropic's Constitutional AI, which made harmlessness training cheaper and more transparent. RL with verifiable rewards: for maths and code, the reward can be computed by checking the answer or running tests, which removes reward-model errors on those tasks; this is central to recent reasoning-focused training. All of them sit on the same foundation: start from a good SFT model, optimise towards a preference or reward signal, and stay close to the reference.

The practical rule follows: if a model lacks knowledge, give it the knowledge at inference time (retrieval, lm9) or continue pretraining; if it has the knowledge but uses the wrong format, use SFT; if it produces acceptable answers but not the best ones by criteria people can judge, use preference tuning.

Deriving the optimal policy

For one prompt, maximise Σ_y π(y)r(y) − β Σ_y π(y) log(π(y)/π_ref(y)) over distributions π. Rewrite it as −β Σ_y π(y) log(π(y) / (π_ref(y) e^{r(y)/β})). Define π*(y) = π_ref(y)e^{r(y)/β}/Z with Z = Σ_y π_ref(y)e^{r(y)/β}; the objective becomes −β·KL(π ‖ π*) + β log Z. KL is never negative and is zero only when π = π*, so π* is the unique maximiser. This two-line argument is worth being able to reproduce: it explains both why RLHF's solution is "reference times exponentiated reward" and why DPO's reparameterisation is exact rather than an approximation. The catch in practice is that the policy is a neural network that may not be able to represent π* exactly, and both methods optimise from finite data.

PPO in a little more detail

Policy-gradient methods estimate the gradient of expected reward as E[∇log π(y|x)·A], where A, the advantage, is how much better a response (or token) did than expected. PPO estimates expected reward with a learned value model and computes per-token advantages; it then maximises a clipped surrogate objective in which the ratio π_new/π_old for each token is clipped to a band such as [0.8, 1.2], so that no update moves the policy far from the one that generated the samples. This stabilises training but brings many hyperparameters (clip range, value-loss weight, KL coefficient, number of epochs per batch) and the cost of generating fresh samples every iteration. InstructGPT also mixed in pretraining gradients ("PPO-ptx") to reduce the "alignment tax", the drop on standard NLP benchmarks after RLHF, finding it worked better than simply increasing the KL coefficient.

Reward-model over-optimisation as a curve

Studies that train policies against a learned "proxy" reward while measuring a separate "gold" reward find a characteristic shape: as the policy moves away from the reference (measured by KL), the proxy reward keeps rising, while the gold reward rises, peaks and then declines. Larger reward models and more preference data push the peak further out but do not remove it. This is the quantitative version of reward hacking, and it is why practitioners treat KL (or DPO's β) as a budget and pick checkpoints by independent evaluation rather than by training reward. The lab's last table is a five-response version of this curve.

Failure modes of DPO

Because DPO's loss depends only on the difference of log-ratios, it can be satisfied by pushing both responses' probabilities down, the rejected one more. Observers have reported exactly this: the chosen response's likelihood falls during training, mass moves to responses outside the dataset, and generations can lengthen or degrade. Remedies include adding an SFT term on the chosen responses, using pairs sampled from the policy being trained (so the data is "on-policy"), filtering noisy pairs, and tuning β (higher β keeps the policy closer to the reference). Variants change the loss shape (IPO, identity preference optimisation, adds a regulariser that stops margins growing without bound) or drop the need for pairs (KTO, Kahneman–Tversky optimisation, uses single responses labelled good or bad). The common thread is controlling how far, and in which direction, the policy moves away from data it was trained on.

Why β means the same thing in RLHF and DPO

In RLHF, β multiplies the KL penalty: larger β, stronger leash. In DPO, β multiplies the log-ratios inside the sigmoid. These are the same parameter, because DPO's loss was derived from the RLHF objective with that β. Intuitively, a larger β means a given difference in log-ratios produces a larger margin, so the loss is satisfied with smaller moves away from the reference; a smaller β requires larger log-ratio changes to reach the same margin, letting the policy drift further. Typical values in DPO practice are around 0.1, and the DPO authors note they barely tuned it, which is one reason published DPO numbers may understate what careful tuning achieves.

Who the raters are matters

InstructGPT's own discussion stresses that the model was aligned to the preferences of a particular group: about 40 contractors following OpenAI's instructions, plus the researchers who wrote those instructions, not to "human values" in general. Rater demographics, instructions and incentives shape the reward. Raters under time pressure favour longer, well-formatted answers that look thorough; raters without domain expertise cannot detect subtle factual errors, so the model learns to be convincing rather than correct in those domains. This is why high-stakes domains use expert raters, why rubrics are explicit, and why preference data is audited for systematic biases such as length.

Where the knowledge comes from: superficial alignment

LIMA's authors proposed a "superficial alignment hypothesis": a model's knowledge and capabilities are learned almost entirely during pretraining, and alignment teaches which sub-distribution of formats and styles to use when interacting with users. The evidence is that small, high-quality SFT sets produce strong assistants, and that post-training rarely improves knowledge benchmarks. The hypothesis is not the whole story, since preference tuning measurably changes behaviours such as refusal, honesty and multi-step reasoning, and RL with verifiable rewards can substantially improve maths and coding. But it is the right default expectation for an engineer deciding whether fine-tuning will fix a problem.

The public case: InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., OpenAI, 2022)

InstructGPT is the paper that defined the modern pipeline, and its numbers are worth knowing. OpenAI hired a team of about 40 contractors to label data. Step 1, SFT: labellers wrote demonstrations for prompts, many taken from the OpenAI API, giving an SFT dataset of about 13,000 training prompts. Step 2, reward model: labellers ranked between 4 and 9 sampled responses per prompt; the reward-model dataset had about 33,000 training prompts, and the authors used 6B reward models because 175B reward-model training was unstable. Step 3, RL: the SFT model was optimised with PPO against the reward model on about 31,000 prompts, with a per-token KL penalty from the SFT model and, in the "PPO-ptx" variant, pretraining gradients mixed in. All models used the GPT-3 architecture at 1.3B, 6B and 175B parameters.

The headline results: labellers preferred outputs of the 1.3B InstructGPT model to those of the 175B GPT-3, despite its having over 100 times fewer parameters; the 175B InstructGPT was preferred to 175B GPT-3 85 ± 3% of the time and to few-shot-prompted GPT-3 71 ± 4% of the time. On TruthfulQA it generated truthful and informative answers about twice as often as GPT-3. When prompted to be respectful it generated about 25% fewer toxic outputs, but it showed no improvement on bias benchmarks. Labellers held out from training preferred it at about the same rate. The authors also reported an "alignment tax" on some public NLP datasets, mitigated by PPO-ptx, and they were explicit that the model was aligned to their labellers' and researchers' preferences, not to a universal standard. A year later DPO (Rafailov et al.) showed the same objective could be optimised without the reward model and RL loop, and open-model pipelines adopted it widely.

Failure modes in production

Length creep. After preference tuning, answers get longer and more padded. Cause: rater or reward-model preference for length. Detect with response-length tracking and length-controlled win rates; fix with length penalties, rubric changes or pairs that prefer concise answers.

Over-refusal. Safety-focused preference data teaches the model to refuse borderline but legitimate requests ("how do I kill a Python process?"). Track refusal rates on a benign-but-edgy evaluation set alongside harmful-request refusal.

Sycophancy. The model agrees with users' incorrect claims or changes correct answers when challenged. Measure with paired prompts that state a wrong view; counter with data that rewards respectful correction.

Capability regressions. Fine-tuning on a narrow domain degrades general skills, such as coding after a customer-service fine-tune. Keep a broad regression suite and mix in general data.

Template mismatch. SFT and preference data formatted with a different chat template from the one used at inference silently degrades the model (lm1). Validate the full serving path on held-out conversations.

What to track during a preference-tuning run

Training reward or DPO loss and accuracy (fraction of pairs ranked correctly), the KL to the reference or the chosen and rejected log-ratios, response length, held-out win rate against the SFT model judged by humans or a strong model with a fixed rubric, and task-specific checks such as factual accuracy and policy compliance. Pick the checkpoint by the held-out evaluations, not by the training reward.

Goal: compute the Bradley–Terry and DPO losses by hand, fit a reward model to simulated comparisons, see the RLHF optimum for different β, show that DPO trained directly on the comparisons reaches the same policy, and watch reward hacking happen. A toy world with one prompt and five candidate responses; NumPy only, a few seconds.

import numpy as np
np.set_printoptions(precision=3, suppress=True)
sig = lambda z: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))
softmax = lambda z: np.exp(z - z.max()) / np.exp(z - z.max()).sum()

# ---- 1. the two losses for one preference pair, by hand
r_w, r_l = 1.5, 0.3                                         # reward model scores: chosen, rejected
print(f"reward-model (Bradley-Terry) loss: -log sigmoid({r_w} - {r_l}) = {-np.log(sig(r_w - r_l)):.3f}  (P(chosen) = {sig(r_w - r_l):.3f})")
beta = 0.1
lp_w, lr_w, lp_l, lr_l = -12.0, -13.0, -15.0, -14.0         # log-probs of whole responses: policy / reference
margin = beta * ((lp_w - lr_w) - (lp_l - lr_l))
print(f"DPO: implicit reward margin = {beta} x ((({lp_w}) - ({lr_w})) - (({lp_l}) - ({lr_l}))) = {margin:.2f};"
      f" loss = -log sigmoid({margin:.2f}) = {-np.log(sig(margin)):.3f}; at the start (policy = reference) loss = ln 2 = {np.log(2):.3f}")
print(f"gradient weight sigmoid(-margin) = {sig(-margin):.3f}  (how 'wrong' the model still is on this pair)")

# ---- 2. a toy world: one prompt, 5 candidate responses, hidden true rewards; the SFT model is the reference
names = ["curt", "helpful", "great", "verbose", "rude"]
true_r = np.array([0.0, 1.0, 2.0, 1.2, -2.0])
ref = softmax(np.array([1.0, 1.0, 0.0, 0.5, 0.5]))           # SFT model's probability of each response
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
pairs = []
for _ in range(4000):                                        # labellers compare two sampled responses
    a, b = rng.choice(5, 2, replace=False)
    pairs.append((a, b) if rng.random() < sig(true_r[a] - true_r[b]) else (b, a))   # (winner, loser)
W, L = np.array(pairs).T
print("\nresponses:", names, "\nreference (SFT) policy:", ref)

# ---- 3. fit a reward model to the comparisons (one scalar per response, gradient descent on BT loss)
r = np.zeros(5)
for _ in range(2000):
    g = sig(-(r[W] - r[L]))                                  # d loss / d margin, per pair
    grad = np.zeros(5); np.add.at(grad, W, -g); np.add.at(grad, L, g)
    r -= 0.5 * grad / len(W)
print("learned rewards (shifted to curt = 0):", (r - r[0]).round(2), " true:", true_r)

# ---- 4. RLHF's optimum in closed form: pi*(y) proportional to ref(y) * exp(r(y) / beta)
def kl(p, q): return float((p * np.log(p / q)).sum())
print("\n beta   policy over responses                   E[true reward]  KL to ref")
for b in (10.0, 1.0, 0.3, 0.1):
    pi = ref * np.exp((r - r.max()) / b); pi /= pi.sum()
    print(f"{b:5.1f}  {pi}   {pi @ true_r:6.2f}        {kl(pi, ref):6.3f}")

# ---- 5. DPO: train the policy directly on the comparisons, no reward model, no sampling
beta = 1.0; logits = np.log(ref).copy(); lref = np.log(ref)
for step in range(3000):
    lp = logits - np.log(np.exp(logits).sum())               # log pi_theta
    m = beta * ((lp[W] - lref[W]) - (lp[L] - lref[L]))
    gm = -sig(-m) * beta                                     # d loss / d margin
    g_lp = np.zeros(5); np.add.at(g_lp, W, gm); np.add.at(g_lp, L, -gm)
    pi = np.exp(lp); g_logits = g_lp - pi * g_lp.sum()       # chain rule through log-softmax
    logits -= 2.0 * g_logits / len(W)
dpo = softmax(logits)
target = ref * np.exp(r / beta); target /= target.sum()
print(f"\nDPO policy (beta=1):        {dpo}\nRLHF optimum with learned r: {target}\nsame answer: {np.allclose(dpo, target, atol=0.01)}")

# ---- 6. reward hacking: a proxy reward that over-rates 'verbose'
proxy = r.copy(); proxy[3] += 1.5
print("\n beta  E[proxy reward]  E[true reward]  | E[true reward] if the reward model were right")
for b in (3.0, 1.0, 0.3, 0.1):
    pi = ref * np.exp((proxy - proxy.max()) / b); pi /= pi.sum()
    ok = ref * np.exp((r - r.max()) / b); ok /= ok.sum()
    print(f"{b:5.1f}  {pi @ proxy:12.2f}  {pi @ true_r:13.2f}    | {ok @ true_r:6.2f}")

What to look for.

  1. Section 1: reward-model loss 0.263 (P = 0.769); DPO margin 0.20 and loss 0.598, against ln 2 = 0.693 at the start; gradient weight 0.450.
  2. Section 3: the learned rewards [0, 1.09, 2.16, 1.14, −1.96] closely match the hidden true rewards [0, 1, 2, 1.2, −2], recovered from comparisons alone.
  3. Section 4: as β falls from 10 to 0.1 the policy moves from the reference to all mass on "great"; expected true reward rises from 0.49 to 2.00 while KL grows from 0.007 to 2.27.
  4. Section 5: the DPO policy equals the RLHF optimum, [0.109, 0.327, 0.348, 0.207, 0.009], and prints True.
  5. Section 6: with a proxy reward that over-rates "verbose", the proxy reward keeps rising as β falls (0.81 → 2.15), but the true reward stalls at about 1.2-1.3, while an accurate reward model would have reached 2.00: reward hacking.
Diagnose — the model got worse while the reward went up

Your team runs PPO on a support assistant. The reward-model score climbs steadily for 2,000 steps. Human reviewers say answers after step 1,200 are worse: longer, repetitive, and full of apologies. What happened, and what do you change?

Answer

Reward hacking. The policy has moved into regions the reward model was not trained on and found features it over-rates, here length and apologetic phrasing (likely because raters slightly preferred thorough, polite answers). The training reward is a proxy; past some KL distance from the reference, proxy and true quality diverge.

Immediate fix: choose the checkpoint by held-out human or independent-judge evaluation (around step 1,200), not by training reward.

Structural fixes: increase the KL coefficient β or cap KL; add a length penalty or evaluate length-controlled; collect new comparisons on the policy's current outputs, including pairs where the concise answer wins, and retrain the reward model; consider a reward-model ensemble and stop when members disagree. Track KL, length and held-out win rate in the run dashboard so the divergence is visible next time.

Choose — SFT, DPO or retrieval?

Three requests arrive: (a) the model should answer in your company's JSON format; (b) it should know your product's 2026 pricing; (c) it gives correct answers but users prefer a competitor's tone and structure. Which technique for each?

Answer

(a) SFT on a few hundred to a few thousand examples in the target format (or constrained decoding, lm16): format is exactly what demonstrations teach. (b) Retrieval (lm9): post-training does not reliably add knowledge, and pricing changes; put the current price list in the context at inference time. (c) Preference tuning (DPO with pairs where the preferred answer has the desired tone and structure), because raters can judge it easily even if writing ideal answers is slow; start from a model already SFT-tuned for your domain and track helpfulness and accuracy so tone gains do not cost correctness.
