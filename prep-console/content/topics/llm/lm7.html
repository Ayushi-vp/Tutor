Imagine you have been given a budget of a few tens of millions of dollars of GPU time to train a language model. That buys a fixed number of floating-point operations, perhaps 10²⁴. You must decide, before you start, how big the model should be and how many tokens of text to train it on. You get one shot: the run will take a couple of months on thousands of GPUs, and if you choose badly there is no budget for a second. Make the model too big and you will run out of compute before it has seen enough data; make it too small and it will saturate, learning nothing more from extra text. How do you choose?

In 2020 the answer seemed to be "as big as possible". Researchers at OpenAI had found that loss falls predictably as models grow, and their analysis said most of any extra compute should go into parameters. GPT-3 (175B parameters), Gopher (280B) and Megatron-Turing NLG (530B, from Microsoft and NVIDIA) were all trained on roughly 300 billion tokens. Then in 2022 DeepMind published a study of over 400 training runs that reached a different conclusion: model size and data should grow together, about 20 tokens for every parameter, which meant the giants of the day were badly undertrained. To prove it, they trained Chinchilla, a 70B model, on 1.4 trillion tokens, using the same compute as their own 280B Gopher. The smaller model won almost everywhere, and it was four times cheaper to run.

That result changed how every lab plans its training runs, and it is a favourite interview topic because it combines a clean estimate (C ≈ 6ND), a surprising empirical finding and a trade-off that keeps evolving: today's small open models are trained far past Chinchilla's ratio on purpose, because the cost of serving them matters more than the cost of training them.

This lesson builds up from what a power law is and why loss follows one, through the 6ND compute estimate, Kaplan's original laws, Chinchilla's three methods and its parametric loss model, a worked compute-optimal sizing for a 10²⁴-FLOP budget, the 2024 replication that corrected Chinchilla's fit, and the shift to inference-aware over-training. The lab evaluates the fitted laws, finds compute-optimal sizes by walking along an iso-FLOP curve, and shows how to recover an exponent from small runs.

Three knobs and one budget

Three quantities describe a pretraining run. N is the number of parameters (model size). D is the number of training tokens the model sees. C is the total compute in floating-point operations (FLOPs). They are tied together by a simple estimate: every parameter takes part in about 2 FLOPs per token in the forward pass and about 4 in the backward pass (lm3 and gpu3 derive this), so

C ≈ 6 · N · D

A compute budget therefore fixes the product N·D. You can have a large model that sees few tokens or a small model that sees many, but not both. The question scaling laws answer is where on that curve to sit.

An analogy: a student and a library

Think of N as the size of a student's brain and D as the number of books they read. Study time is limited (the budget), and a bigger brain takes longer per book. A huge brain that reads three books will not have learned much; a tiny brain that reads the whole library will forget most of it. The best outcome for a fixed amount of study time comes from matching the two. Chinchilla's finding is that for language models the match is roughly linear: double the brain, double the books. The analogy breaks in one respect that turns out to matter commercially: in the real world you pay not only for the student's studying but for every exam they sit afterwards, and a smaller student is cheaper to examine, which is why modern small models read far more than Chinchilla's ratio.

Why "laws"

The surprising empirical fact is that loss falls smoothly and predictably as N, D or C grow, following a power law: loss ∝ X^(−k) for a small exponent k. On a graph with logarithmic axes, a power law is a straight line, and that line has held across many orders of magnitude of compute. This is what makes scaling laws useful: you can train a series of small, cheap models, fit the line, and predict how a model 100 or 1,000 times larger will do before spending the money. A pretraining loss is measured in nats per token (the cross-entropy with natural logarithms, ml5); differences that look tiny, such as 1.99 versus 1.94, correspond to clearly different model quality.

What a small difference in loss means

Scaling-law plots show losses like 2.00 and 1.94 and it is easy to dismiss the gap. Convert to perplexity, e^loss, which is roughly "how many equally likely next tokens the model is choosing between": e^2.00 = 7.39 and e^1.94 = 6.96, about 6% fewer effective choices at every single token. Because the irreducible part of the loss (the genuinely unpredictable text) is large, the reducible part is a fraction of the total, and a 0.06 drop is a substantial share of what is left to learn. In practice such gaps show up as clear differences on benchmarks: the 0.06-nat gap between Gopher and Chinchilla corresponded to 7.5 points on MMLU.

1. Power laws, from scratch

A power law has the form y = a·x^k. Take logarithms of both sides: log y = log a + k·log x. So if you plot log y against log x you get a straight line with slope k. For language-model loss the exponent is small and negative, which means each tenfold increase in x multiplies the (reducible) loss by the same factor 10^k. With k = −0.05, every tenfold increase in compute cuts loss by about 11% (10^−0.05 = 0.891). That is a slow but extremely regular improvement, and its regularity is the useful part: three or four points on a straight line in log-log space let you extrapolate. The lab generates nine noisy "runs" from a known law with exponent −0.05 between 10¹⁸ and 10²² FLOPs, fits a straight line in log space and recovers −0.0499, predicting the loss at 10²⁴ FLOPs within 0.003 of the truth.

2. Why loss should follow a power law at all

There is no complete theory, but an intuition helps. Language contains patterns at every level of frequency: a few extremely common ones (grammar, frequent words), and a long tail of rarer ones (specific facts, unusual constructions), whose frequencies themselves follow roughly power-law distributions. A larger model has room to store more of the tail; more data shows it more examples of rare patterns. Each increment of scale picks off the next slice of the tail, which is rarer and therefore worth less loss than the last, giving a smooth, diminishing return. Whatever the explanation, the empirical regularity has held for model families across architectures and labs, and that is what engineers rely on.

3. The compute estimate C ≈ 6ND, worked

From lm3, each parameter takes part in one multiply-add per token in the forward pass: 2 FLOPs. The backward pass computes gradients with respect to both activations and weights, roughly twice the forward work: 4 FLOPs. Total: about 6 FLOPs per parameter per token. (Attention adds a term that grows with context length; for typical contexts it is a modest correction and is usually ignored in these estimates.) Some examples: GPT-3, 6 × 175 × 10⁹ × 300 × 10⁹ = 3.15 × 10²³ FLOPs. Gopher, 6 × 280 × 10⁹ × 300 × 10⁹ = 5.0 × 10²³ (the Chinchilla paper quotes Gopher's budget as 5.76 × 10²³, which includes details such as attention). Chinchilla, 6 × 70 × 10⁹ × 1.4 × 10¹² = 5.9 × 10²³. Llama 3 405B, 6 × 405 × 10⁹ × 15.6 × 10¹² = 3.8 × 10²⁵, the figure Meta reports.

Turning FLOPs into time and money uses the hardware's delivered throughput (gpu12). An H100 has a dense bf16 peak of about 989 TFLOP/s; a well-tuned large run achieves perhaps 40% of that as model FLOPs utilisation (MFU), about 4 × 10¹⁴ useful FLOP/s per GPU. A 10²⁴-FLOP run therefore needs 10²⁴ / (4 × 10¹⁴) = 2.5 × 10⁹ GPU-seconds, about 700,000 GPU-hours: roughly 29 days on 1,000 H100s.

4. Kaplan et al. (2020): the first laws

Kaplan and colleagues at OpenAI trained many transformer language models and fitted three separate laws, each holding when the other factors are not the bottleneck. Loss versus non-embedding parameters: L(N) = (N_c/N)^0.076. Loss versus data: L(D) = (D_c/D)^0.095. Loss versus compute, when compute is allocated optimally: L(C) ∝ C^−0.050. They also found that architecture details such as depth versus width mattered much less than total parameters within a wide range. Their most consequential conclusion was about allocation: as compute grows, the optimal model size should grow as about C^0.73, much faster than the data. Ten times more compute should buy a model about 5.4 times bigger but only about 1.8 times more data. Following that advice, the large models of 2020-2021 grew enormously in parameters while staying near 300 billion training tokens.

5. Hoffmann et al. (2022): the Chinchilla study

DeepMind revisited the question with more than 400 models from 70 million to over 16 billion parameters, trained on 5 to 500 billion tokens, and three independent methods for finding the compute-optimal allocation:

  1. Training curves: for each model size, train with several token budgets and record the lowest loss achieved at each compute level; read off which size wins at each compute.
  2. Iso-FLOP profiles: fix nine compute budgets; at each, train models of different sizes for exactly that budget (so smaller models see more tokens); the loss traces a valley, and its bottom is the optimal size for that budget.
  3. Parametric fit: fit a formula for loss as a function of both N and D to all the runs, then minimise it along C = 6ND.

All three said the same thing qualitatively: N and D should be scaled in roughly equal proportion. The exponents for N_opt ∝ C^a and D_opt ∝ C^b were a = 0.50, b = 0.50 (method 1), a = 0.49, b = 0.51 (method 2) and a = 0.46, b = 0.54 (method 3). Double the compute, and both the model and the data should grow by about √2. The paper's Table 3 turns this into a recipe: a 1B model should see about 20 billion tokens, a 67B model about 1.5 trillion, a 175B model 3.7 trillion. The ratio, about 20 tokens per parameter, is the famous rule of thumb.

6. Why Kaplan and Chinchilla disagreed

The main culprit is the learning-rate schedule. Training uses a schedule that warms the learning rate up, then decays it (often along a cosine curve) to a small value by the end of training; loss improves sharply during that final decay. Kaplan et al. used a schedule of fixed length for all runs and compared models at intermediate points, so runs with fewer tokens were measured before their learning rate had decayed, understating how good short-trained (and so relatively small-for-their-compute) models can get. Chinchilla set each run's schedule to match its token count. The lesson generalises: scaling-law conclusions depend on every run being trained properly for its own budget, which is harder than it sounds.

7. The parametric loss model

Chinchilla's third method fits

L(N, D) = E + A / N^α + B / D^β

Each term has a meaning. E is the irreducible loss, the entropy of natural text: even a perfect model cannot predict every next token. A/N^α is the penalty for finite model size: what an infinitely trained model of N parameters still gets wrong. B/D^β is the penalty for finite data: what a model of unlimited size still gets wrong after seeing only D tokens. The published values were E = 1.69, A = 406.4, B = 410.7, α = 0.34, β = 0.28. Minimising this along C = 6ND balances the two shrinking terms: spending a FLOP on a bigger model should reduce loss exactly as much as spending it on more tokens. The formula is valuable as a mental model, but section 9 shows why its published constants should not be used as-is.

8. Sizing a 10²⁴-FLOP run by hand

Combine C = 6ND with the rule D = 20N:

C = 6 · N · (20 N) = 120 N²      →      N = √(C / 120),   D = 20 N
C = 10²⁴:  N = √(8.33 × 10²¹) = 9.13 × 10¹⁰ ≈ 91B parameters
           D = 20 × 9.13 × 10¹⁰ = 1.83 × 10¹² ≈ 1.8T tokens
check:     6 × 9.13 × 10¹⁰ × 1.83 × 10¹² = 1.0 × 10²⁴ ✓

For Gopher's budget, 5.76 × 10²³, the same calculation gives N ≈ 69B and D ≈ 1.4T: almost exactly Chinchilla. The rule also explains how far off the 2021 models were. Gopher, at 280B parameters, should have seen about 5.6T tokens to be compute-optimal for its size (and would have needed about 9.4 × 10²⁴ FLOPs, 16 times its actual budget); trained on 300B tokens it had about 1 token per parameter. GPT-3 had 1.7. These models were not too small; they were too big for the data they were given.

The lab walks along Gopher's iso-FLOP curve using the corrected fit from section 9: 280B parameters on 0.34T tokens gives loss 1.993; 140B, 1.979; 70B on 1.37T, 1.975 (the valley floor); 35B, 1.980; 10B on 9.6T, 2.015. The valley is shallow near the bottom, which is good news in practice: being within a factor of two of the optimal size costs very little loss, so the exact ratio matters less than avoiding the 15-fold mistakes of 2021.

9. The replication that corrected the fit

In 2024 Besiroglu and colleagues at Epoch AI reconstructed Chinchilla's data points from the paper's figures and re-ran the third method. They found the published parametric estimates were inconsistent with the paper's own first two methods, fitted the extracted data poorly, and came with confidence intervals so narrow they would have required over 600,000 experiments where likely fewer than 500 were run. They traced the problems to the optimiser stopping before convergence because of a poor choice of loss scale, and to the rounding of the reported parameters, which substantially biases predictions. Their re-fit is L = 1.8172 + 482.01/N^0.3478 + 2085.43/D^0.3658, and it is compatible with the first two methods and with roughly 20 tokens per parameter.

You can see the difference in the lab. Minimised along iso-FLOP curves, the published constants put the optimum at 50 to 120 tokens per parameter (for Gopher's budget, a 32B model on 3T tokens), contradicting the Chinchilla model the paper itself trained. The re-fit puts it at 17 to 22 tokens per parameter across budgets from 10²¹ to 10²⁵ FLOPs (for Gopher's budget, 72B on 1.33T tokens), matching Chinchilla. The broader lesson is that fitted constants in scaling-law papers are fragile, and the qualitative conclusion (scale N and D together) is far more robust than any particular number.

10. Compute-optimal is not cost-optimal

Chinchilla optimises one thing: the loss reached for a fixed training budget. But a deployed model's lifetime cost includes inference, about 2N FLOPs per generated token (lm3), multiplied by every token it ever serves. A model used by millions of people can spend far more compute in inference than it did in training. Then it pays to train a smaller model for longer: its loss is a little higher than the compute-optimal model's at the same training cost, but every served token is cheaper. In the lab, for a 10²⁴-FLOP budget, the optimal 96B model reaches loss 1.960; a 48B model trained on 3.5T tokens reaches 1.964 with half the inference cost per token, and a 24B model on 7T tokens reaches 1.977 at a quarter. Sardana et al. made this precise and found that for expected demand of about a billion requests or more, models should be trained smaller and longer than Chinchilla-optimal, with quality still improving at ratios up to 10,000 tokens per parameter in their experiments. Meta's Llama 3 report says exactly this: the 405B flagship is approximately compute-optimal for its 3.8 × 10²⁵-FLOP budget, while the smaller models were trained "for much longer than is compute-optimal" and perform better than compute-optimal models at the same inference budget.

The same logic explains a pattern you will see across open model releases: a family of several sizes all trained on the same, very large token count. The largest member is near compute-optimal; the smaller ones are heavily over-trained, because they are the ones most people will run on their own hardware, where inference cost per token and memory are what users feel. When you compare models for deployment, tokens seen in training is as informative as parameter count: a well-trained 8B model can beat an under-trained model several times its size.

11. How scaling laws are used in practice

Teams training large models use scaling laws as planning tools. Before a big run they train a sweep of small models on the exact data mix, tokeniser and recipe, fit loss against compute and optimal size against compute, and choose N and D for the target budget. Llama 3's team did this with budgets from 6 × 10¹⁸ to 10²² FLOPs and then predicted downstream benchmark accuracy for the flagship by chaining two fits: compute to the model's loss on the benchmark's answers, and that loss to accuracy. During the run, the loss curve is compared against the prediction, and a run falling behind is investigated early. Scaling laws are also the standard way to compare data mixes or architectures: the better choice shifts the line down or makes it steeper, and you can see it with small runs.

12. A worked cost estimate for Chinchilla itself

It is worth doing the full chain once for a real model. Chinchilla: 6 × 70 × 10⁹ × 1.4 × 10¹² = 5.9 × 10²³ FLOPs. On modern H100s at 40% MFU (about 4 × 10¹⁴ useful FLOP/s each), that is 5.9 × 10²³ / 4 × 10¹⁴ = 1.5 × 10⁹ GPU-seconds, about 410,000 GPU-hours, or about 17 days on 1,000 GPUs. At a few dollars per GPU-hour that is on the order of one to two million dollars of compute for the final run, before the experiments, failed runs and evaluations that typically multiply it. Gopher's run cost about the same, which is the point: the same money bought a much better model once it was spent in the right shape. Serving tells the other half of the story: Chinchilla needs 140 GFLOP per generated token against Gopher's 560, and 140 GB of bf16 weights against 560 GB, so every deployment of the smaller model is about four times cheaper.

13. What scaling laws do not tell you

They predict pretraining loss on the training distribution. They do not directly predict whether a model can do a particular task, how it behaves after fine-tuning, or how it handles data unlike its training set. They assume the data is effectively unlimited and of constant quality; when high-quality unique data runs out and must be repeated, the effective value of extra tokens falls. They depend on the tokeniser (loss per token is not comparable across tokenisers, lm1), so fitted constants from one lab do not transfer to another. And they say nothing about inference-time compute such as generating long reasoning chains, which follows its own scaling behaviour. Treat them as a precise tool for one question, how to spend pretraining compute, and as a rough guide for everything else.

Deriving the compute-optimal exponents

Minimise L = E + A·N^(−α) + B·D^(−β) subject to 6ND = C. Substitute D = C/(6N) and set dL/dN = 0: αA·N^(−α−1) = βB·(C/6)^(−β)·N^(β−1). Solving gives N_opt ∝ C^(β/(α+β)) and D_opt ∝ C^(α/(α+β)). With the published α = 0.34 and β = 0.28, the exponents are 0.45 and 0.55, close to method 3's reported 0.46 and 0.54; with the re-fit's α = 0.348 and β = 0.366, they are 0.51 and 0.49, matching methods 1 and 2. The algebra shows why the split depends only on the ratio of the exponents: whichever resource the loss is more sensitive to (larger exponent) gets a smaller share of the growth, because it gives diminishing returns faster. The tokens-per-parameter ratio itself depends on A and B too, which is why it shifts with data quality and tokeniser.

Hyperparameters must scale too

A scaling law is only as good as the runs behind it, and runs at different sizes need different hyperparameters. The optimal learning rate generally falls as models grow, and the critical batch size, beyond which larger batches stop helping, grows as loss falls (Kaplan et al. fitted this too). A sweep that uses the same learning rate for all sizes will under-train some of them and bend the fitted line. Modern practice uses parameterisations designed so that hyperparameters tuned on small models transfer to large ones (for example maximal-update parameterisation), plus explicit scaling rules for learning rate and batch size, so that the small runs used for fitting are each near their own optimum.

Data-constrained scaling

Chinchilla assumes every token is fresh. Frontier runs now approach the amount of high-quality text that exists, so data gets repeated across epochs. Studies of data-constrained scaling find that a few epochs over the same data are nearly as valuable as new data, while returns decline steeply after many repetitions, and propose modifying D to an "effective data" term that saturates with repetition. Practical consequences: deduplication matters (accidental repetition wastes budget), data quality filtering effectively raises the value of each token, and synthetic and multimodal data become ways to keep D growing. These considerations are why data teams are as central to frontier training as infrastructure teams.

Loss is per token, and tokenisers differ

Scaling-law losses are cross-entropies per token. A tokeniser that packs more text into each token (lm1) raises per-token loss even if the model is equally good per character, because each token carries more information. So losses, and the constants of fitted laws, cannot be compared across models with different tokenisers; comparisons should use bits per byte or per character of a fixed evaluation set. The same caution applies to token counts in the tokens-per-parameter ratio: 20 tokens per parameter with a 32K vocabulary is less text than with a 128K vocabulary.

Inference-time scaling

A newer axis of scaling spends compute at inference rather than in training: sampling several answers and choosing among them, or letting a model generate long chains of reasoning before answering. Both show their own smooth relationships between compute and accuracy on tasks such as mathematics, and they change the economics: a smaller model that "thinks longer" can match a larger one on some tasks, at a per-query cost that depends on difficulty. This complements the over-training argument: both push the frontier towards models that are cheap per token, with quality bought by more training tokens or more inference tokens rather than by more parameters.

Why the valley is shallow

Near the compute-optimal point, loss as a function of model size along the iso-FLOP curve is flat to first order (that is what being at a minimum means), so small deviations cost only second-order amounts. In log terms: if the optimum is N*, choosing N = 2N* or N*/2 shifts both the parameter and data terms by factors of 2^α and 2^β in opposite directions, and the sum changes only slightly. The lab shows it: along Gopher's budget, 35B and 140B are within 0.005 nats of the 70B optimum, while 280B (a factor of four) is 0.019 worse and 10B (a factor of seven) 0.040 worse. This is why practitioners can deviate from the optimum for good reasons, such as inference cost or hardware fit, with little training-efficiency penalty, as long as they avoid order-of-magnitude mistakes.

Mixture-of-experts and other architectures

Scaling laws are architecture-specific. Mixture-of-experts models (lm17) decouple total parameters from per-token compute, so the C ≈ 6ND estimate uses active parameters, while memory depends on total parameters, and their laws involve both. Changes such as better attention variants or optimisers typically shift the curve by a constant factor in effective compute ("this change is worth 1.5× compute") rather than changing its slope, and that is the standard way such improvements are reported.

The public case: Chinchilla versus Gopher (Hoffmann et al., DeepMind, 2022)

DeepMind's paper states the problem in its first table: GPT-3 (175B), Jurassic-1 (178B), Gopher (280B) and Megatron-Turing NLG (530B) had all been trained on about 300 billion tokens. Fitting their 400-plus runs with three methods, the authors concluded that for compute-optimal training, "for every doubling of model size the number of training tokens should also be doubled", and that current large models were "considerably over-sized" for their budgets. Their Table 3 estimates, for example, that a 175B model should be trained on about 3.7T tokens and that a 280B model like Gopher would be optimal at a budget of about 10²⁵ FLOPs with about 5.9T tokens.

They then tested the prediction directly. For Gopher's compute budget (5.76 × 10²³ FLOPs), their analysis gave an optimal size around 70B; they trained Chinchilla, 70B parameters on 1.4 trillion tokens. Chinchilla "uniformly and significantly" outperformed Gopher (280B), GPT-3 (175B), Jurassic-1 (178B) and Megatron-Turing NLG (530B) on a large range of downstream tasks. On MMLU it reached 67.5% average accuracy against Gopher's 60.0%, more than 7 percentage points better; on BIG-bench (a collection of 62 diverse tasks in this evaluation) 65.1% against 54.4%; on TruthfulQA it reached 43.6% zero-shot against Gopher's 29.5%. And because it is four times smaller, it is about four times cheaper for fine-tuning and inference. Two years later Epoch AI's replication corrected the paper's parametric fit while confirming its central conclusion, and subsequent open models adopted, then deliberately exceeded, its ratio.

How this goes wrong in practice

Mis-sized runs. A team picks a large model because "bigger is better", then cannot afford enough tokens; the result underperforms a smaller model that would have cost the same. Size with C = 6ND and the tokens-per-parameter target before committing.

Extrapolating from bad small runs. Small runs with untuned learning rates, mismatched schedules or a different data mix give a scaling law that does not describe the large run. Make sure each fitting run is trained properly for its own budget, on the same data and tokeniser.

Ignoring inference. A compute-optimal model chosen for training efficiency turns out too expensive to serve at the product's traffic. Include expected inference volume in the decision; for high-traffic products, train smaller and longer.

Running out of data. Planning 20 tokens per parameter for a large model may require more high-quality unique tokens than exist in the target domain or language. Check data availability and plan for repetition, filtering or synthetic data.

A modern decision: Llama 3's sizing

Meta's Llama 3 report is the clearest public example of scaling laws used for a real decision. The team ran iso-FLOP sweeps between 6 × 10¹⁸ and 10²² FLOPs, fitted the compute-optimal number of training tokens against compute, and extrapolated to their flagship budget of 3.8 × 10²⁵ FLOPs, which suggested a 402B-parameter model on 16.55T tokens; they trained 405B on 15.6T, about 39 tokens per parameter, a reminder that the optimal ratio depends on the data and recipe (their fit gave more tokens per parameter than Chinchilla's 20). They also predicted the flagship's downstream benchmark scores from the small runs before training it. For the 8B and 70B models, they ignored compute-optimality on purpose and trained on about the same 15T tokens, because those models would be deployed widely and a smaller, longer-trained model is cheaper to serve at a given quality.

What to measure

For a scaling study: final loss of each small run at its own schedule end, the iso-FLOP valleys, the fitted exponents with honest uncertainty (bootstrap over runs), and a held-out prediction (fit on the smaller runs, predict the largest one before looking). For a production run: the loss curve against the scaling-law prediction, MFU (which converts FLOPs into calendar time and cost), and downstream evaluations at checkpoints.

Goal: apply C ≈ 6ND to real models, find compute-optimal sizes for several budgets with the rule of thumb and with both the published and corrected Chinchilla fits, walk along Gopher's iso-FLOP curve, recover a power-law exponent from small runs, and quantify the trade-off of over-training. rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
Cs = np.logspace(18, 22, 9)
L_runs = 3.1 * (Cs / 1e18) ** -0.05 * np.exp(rng.normal(0, 0.005, Cs.size))
slope, intercept = np.polyfit(np.log(Cs), np.log(L_runs), 1)
print(f"\nfitted exponent from 9 small runs: {slope:.4f} (true -0.05); "
      f"extrapolated loss at 1e24: {np.exp(intercept) * 1e24 ** slope:.3f} vs true {3.1 * 1e6 ** -0.05:.3f}")

# ---- 5. over-training for cheaper inference: smaller models at the same training compute
C = 1e24; n_opt, d_opt = fitted_optimum(C, loss2)
print(f"\nat C=1e24 (re-fit): optimum {n_opt/1e9:.0f}B on {d_opt/1e12:.1f}T tokens -> loss {loss2(n_opt, d_opt):.4f}")
for N in (n_opt / 2, n_opt / 4):
    print(f"  {N/1e9:4.0f}B on {C/(6*N)/1e12:4.1f}T tokens: loss {loss2(N, C/(6*N)):.4f};"
          f" inference {2*N/1e9:.0f} vs {2*n_opt/1e9:.0f} GFLOP per token")

What to look for.

  1. Section 1: training FLOPs of 3.15 × 10²³ (GPT-3), 5.0 × 10²³ (Gopher), 5.9 × 10²³ (Chinchilla), 7.2 × 10²³ (Llama 3 8B) and 3.79 × 10²⁵ (Llama 3 405B), with tokens per parameter of 2, 1, 20, about 1,870 and 39. (The predicted losses use Chinchilla's data and tokeniser, so only compare them with each other.)
  2. Section 2: the rule of thumb gives 91.3B on 1.83T tokens at 10²⁴ FLOPs. The published fit puts the optimum at 50-122 tokens per parameter, contradicting Chinchilla itself; the corrected re-fit gives 17-22, matching the rule.
  3. Section 3: the loss valley along Gopher's budget, lowest near 70B (1.9745), with 280B at 1.9933: Gopher's shape was wrong, and the valley is shallow near its floor.
  4. Section 4: the fitted exponent −0.0499 from nine noisy runs, extrapolating to within 0.003 at 100× the largest run.
  5. Section 5: at 10²⁴ FLOPs, halving the model from 96B to 48B costs 0.004 in loss but halves inference cost per token.
Estimate — size a training run

You have 2,048 H100 GPUs for 30 days and expect 40% MFU. How much compute is that, what is the compute-optimal model, and what would you actually train if the model will power a high-traffic product?

Answer

Compute: 2,048 GPUs × 30 × 86,400 s × 989 × 10¹² FLOP/s × 0.40 ≈ 2,048 × 2.592 × 10⁶ × 3.96 × 10¹⁴ ≈ 2.1 × 10²⁴ FLOPs.

Compute-optimal: N = √(C/120) = √(1.75 × 10²²) ≈ 132B parameters on D = 20N ≈ 2.6T tokens.

What to actually train: for heavy inference, a smaller model trained longer, for example about 30-40B parameters on 9-12T tokens (C/6N), if that much good data exists. Its loss is slightly higher than the 132B model's, but every served token costs about a quarter as much, and it fits on fewer GPUs at inference. Before committing, run a small-scale sweep on your data to confirm the curves, check that you have enough unique high-quality tokens (repetition beyond a few epochs loses value), and leave budget for failures, evaluation and the long-context and post-training stages.

Explain — the undertrained giant

A manager asks why a 280B model trained in 2021 was beaten by a 70B model trained with the same compute a year later. Explain in three sentences, with numbers.

Answer

A training budget fixes parameters × tokens (C ≈ 6ND), and the 280B Gopher spent it on size, seeing only 300B tokens, about one token per parameter. DeepMind's scaling study showed loss is minimised when size and data grow together, about 20 tokens per parameter, which for Gopher's budget means about 70B parameters on 1.4T tokens. Chinchilla, built exactly that way, scored 67.5% on MMLU against Gopher's 60.0% and costs a quarter as much to run.
