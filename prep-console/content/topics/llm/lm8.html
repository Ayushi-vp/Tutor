Two teams build products on the same open model. The first team's chatbot gives lively, varied answers but occasionally wanders into nonsense halfway through a paragraph. The second team's summariser is reliable but painfully repetitive: on long documents it repeats the same sentence three times and then stops. A third engineer, asked to extract invoice fields as JSON, gets valid JSON nineteen times out of twenty and a creatively reworded key on the twentieth. None of them changed the model. All three problems live in the few lines of code that turn the model's output into words: the decoding strategy.

A language model does not produce text. At each step it produces a probability for every token in its vocabulary, often 100,000 or more numbers. Something has to choose one token, append it, and run the model again. The obvious choice, always take the most likely token, turns out to be one of the worst for open-ended writing, and the other obvious choice, pick randomly in proportion to the probabilities, has its own failure. Between them lies a small family of techniques with names you will see in every API: temperature, top-k, top-p, min-p, repetition penalties, beam width, seeds.

These settings matter for an AI engineer for three reasons. They change product behaviour as much as switching models does. They decide whether evaluations are reproducible and comparable. And interviewers like them because they have exact answers: given a distribution, you can compute what top-p keeps.

This lesson starts with the generation loop and the distribution the model hands you. It covers greedy decoding and beam search and why they degenerate, pure sampling and its unreliable tail, then temperature, top-k, top-p and min-p, each worked by hand on the same five-token distribution. It continues with repetition penalties, determinism, and constrained decoding, and ends with how to choose settings per task. The public case is Holtzman et al.'s study of neural text degeneration, which introduced nucleus (top-p) sampling. The lab implements every sampler in NumPy and runs them on a small bigram model so you can watch greedy decoding loop.

The generation loop

Generation is a loop. Feed the prompt to the model; it returns, for the next position, one logit per vocabulary entry; a softmax turns these into probabilities that sum to 1 (lm3). A decoding strategy picks one token from that distribution. The token is appended, the model runs again (reusing the KV cache, lm5), and so on until an end-of-sequence token or a length limit. Everything in this lesson happens in the "pick one token" box. The model's weights never change; only the rule for using its probabilities does.

Two families

Maximisation strategies try to find a high-probability output: greedy decoding takes the top token every step; beam search keeps several candidate sequences and returns the most probable one it finds. Sampling strategies draw randomly according to the probabilities, usually after reshaping them with a temperature and cutting off the unlikely tail. Maximisation is predictable and suits tasks with one right answer. Sampling gives variety and, perhaps surprisingly, more human-like text.

An analogy: ordering at a restaurant

Imagine choosing dinner by a friend's recommendations, each with a confidence. Greedy decoding always orders the top recommendation; after a week you have eaten the same dish seven times. Pure sampling orders in proportion to confidence, including the 1% recommendations, so occasionally you get something absurd. Temperature turns up or down how much you trust the ranking. Top-k says "only consider the top five", top-p says "only consider dishes that together make up 90% of my friend's confidence", and min-p says "ignore anything much less recommended than the favourite". The analogy breaks in one important way: dinners are independent, but tokens are not. One absurd token changes the context for every token after it, which is why an occasional bad pick can derail an entire paragraph and why truncation matters so much.

Why the choice compounds

Decoding is not a one-off choice but hundreds or thousands of them in a row, and each one becomes part of the input to all the others. That is why small differences in strategy produce large differences in output. Suppose a sampler has a 2% chance per token of picking something the model itself would consider a mistake. Over a 500-token answer, the chance of at least one such pick is 1 − 0.98⁵⁰⁰, more than 99.99%. Whether the text recovers from that pick depends on how far off it was: a slightly odd word is harmless, a token from the far tail can send the rest of the paragraph off course. Conversely, a strategy that always takes the safest token never makes that kind of mistake but slides into its own failure, repetition, because safe choices reinforce themselves. Good decoding is about controlling both risks at once: cut off the far tail, but leave enough randomness to keep the text moving.

Where you meet these settings

Every LLM API (application programming interface, the web endpoint you call) exposes some of these knobs as request parameters: temperature, top_p, sometimes top_k, presence and frequency penalties, a seed, and a maximum number of tokens. Open-source inference libraries expose more, including min-p, repetition penalty and beam width. The defaults vary by provider and by library, and a surprising number of production incidents are caused by a default that changed or a parameter that was silently ignored. Knowing what each parameter does to the distribution lets you read an API reference and predict the effect before you run anything.

The example used throughout

After "The capital of France is", suppose the model gives five candidates (a real model has a whole vocabulary, but five keeps the arithmetic visible): "Paris" 0.50, "the" 0.25, "a" 0.12, "London" 0.08, "banana" 0.05. Every method below is worked on these numbers.

1. What the model hands you

At each step the LM head outputs a vector of logits z, one per vocabulary entry, and the softmax p_i = exp(z_i)/Σ_j exp(z_j) turns them into probabilities. For a vocabulary of 128,000 tokens, that is 128,000 probabilities summing to 1. In a typical position the distribution has a handful of tokens with most of the mass and a very long tail of tokens with tiny probabilities each. The shape varies wildly between positions: after "The capital of France is" nearly all mass may sit on " Paris"; after "My favourite colour is" it is spread across dozens of colour words. A good decoding strategy must behave sensibly at both kinds of position.

A useful single number for the shape is the entropy, H = −Σ p log p: zero when one token has probability 1, larger when probability is spread out. Our five-token example has H = 1.30 nats. The lab prints the entropy after each transformation, which makes it easy to see which ones sharpen and which flatten.

2. Greedy decoding

Greedy decoding picks argmax p at each step: "Paris" in the example. It is deterministic, cheap and often correct for short factual answers, classification ("positive"/"negative") and extraction, where the most probable token usually is the right one. For longer open-ended text it has two well-known problems. First, it is short-sighted: the best first token does not necessarily start the best sequence. Second, and worse, it loops. Language models assign higher probability to continuing a pattern that is already present in the context, so once a phrase has repeated, repeating it again becomes the most probable continuation. Holtzman et al. showed the probability of a repeated phrase increases with each repetition, a positive feedback loop. Greedy decoding always follows the most probable path, so it walks straight into the loop and stays there. The lab's small bigram model shows it: greedy text from the word "the" has 49% repeated three-word sequences within 40 words, while sampled text has about 1%.

3. Beam search

Beam search tries to fix short-sightedness. It keeps the B best partial sequences (the beam), scored by total log-probability. At each step it extends every partial sequence by every possible next token, scores all the extensions, and keeps the top B overall. At the end it returns the best complete sequence. With B = 1 it is greedy; larger B explores more. Worked in miniature with B = 2: suppose step one gives "Paris" (log-prob −0.69) and "the" (−1.39). Extending "Paris" gives "Paris ." at −0.69 + (−0.22) = −0.91 and "Paris ," at −0.69 + (−1.90) = −2.59; extending "the" gives "the city" at −1.39 + (−0.36) = −1.75. The beam keeps "Paris ." (−0.91) and "the city" (−1.75). It found a sequence greedy might miss if, say, "the city of light" turned out more probable in total.

Two practical details. Because log-probabilities are negative and add up, longer sequences always score lower, so plain beam search favours short outputs; implementations divide by a length term (length normalisation). And beam search is the standard choice in machine translation and speech recognition, where the output is tightly constrained by the input and the most probable output is usually good. For open-ended text it amplifies greedy's problems: it finds even more probable, even more generic and repetitive text. Holtzman et al. measured 28.94% repetition with beam width 16 on GPT-2 Large, and noted that with large beams (64 and above) GPT-2 degenerated into repetition, even with substantial human context.

4. Why maximum probability is the wrong target for open-ended text

The intuition that "the most probable text is the best text" fails because human writing is not maximally probable. Holtzman et al. plotted the probability GPT-2 assigned to each token of real human text and found it fluctuates constantly, with frequent low-probability words: people make surprising, specific choices. Beam-search text, by contrast, sits at uniformly high probability and reads as bland and repetitive. Their Table 1 quantifies it with perplexity (the exponential of the average negative log-probability per token, lower meaning more predictable to the model): human text scored 12.38, greedy 1.50 and beam search 1.48. Maximisation produces text eight times "more predictable" than people write. Good open-ended decoding should therefore aim to produce text that is about as surprising as human text, not as unsurprising as possible. That reframing is the core of the paper.

5. Pure sampling and the unreliable tail

The opposite approach is to sample the next token at random with exactly the model's probabilities. On our example, "Paris" half the time, "banana" one time in twenty. Sampling produces varied text with no loops, and in principle it reproduces the model's distribution faithfully. The problem is the tail. In a real vocabulary there may be tens of thousands of tokens each with tiny probability, but together they can hold 10-30% of the mass, so sampling regularly picks one. The model's probabilities for those tail tokens are poorly calibrated: each individually is "unlikely but not impossible", yet most of them are nonsense in context. One nonsense token then becomes part of the context, the model tries to continue it, and the text derails. Holtzman et al.'s pure sampling gave perplexity 22.73, far less predictable than human text, and incoherent passages.

6. Temperature

Temperature reshapes the distribution before sampling: divide every logit by T, then apply the softmax.

p_i(T) = exp(z_i / T) / Σ_j exp(z_j / T)        equivalently   p_i(T) ∝ p_i^(1/T)

Worked on the example. At T = 0.5, each probability is raised to the power 2 and renormalised: 0.25, 0.0625, 0.0144, 0.0064, 0.0025, which sum to 0.3358; dividing gives [0.744, 0.186, 0.043, 0.019, 0.007]. "Paris" rose from 0.50 to 0.74 and "banana" fell from 0.05 to 0.007; entropy fell from 1.30 to 0.78 nats. At T = 2, each probability is square-rooted: 0.707, 0.500, 0.346, 0.283, 0.224, sum 2.060, giving [0.343, 0.243, 0.168, 0.137, 0.109]; the distribution flattened and "banana" doubled to 0.11. As T approaches 0 the top token's share approaches 1 (greedy); as T grows the distribution approaches uniform. Temperature never changes the ranking, only the gaps. It is the same operation as temperature scaling for calibration (ml15), used here not to fix calibration but to trade predictability for variety. Lowering temperature reduces tail sampling, but on its own it pushes towards the repetitive regime: Holtzman et al.'s top-k 40 with T = 0.7 had 8.86% repetition.

7. Top-k sampling

Top-k removes the tail by count: keep the k most probable tokens, set the rest to zero, renormalise, sample. With k = 2 on the example: keep 0.50 and 0.25, total 0.75, renormalised to [0.667, 0.333, 0, 0, 0]. Top-k was used in early GPT-2 demonstrations with k = 40. Its flaw is that k is fixed while the distribution's shape is not. When the model is confident (one token at 0.95), k = 40 still lets 39 unlikely tokens in; when it is uncertain (probability spread over 200 reasonable words), k = 40 throws away good options and makes text narrower than it should be. Holtzman et al. found top-k = 40 too predictable (perplexity 6.88) and top-k = 640 close to human perplexity but with poor quality scores.

8. Top-p (nucleus) sampling

Holtzman et al.'s proposal sizes the candidate set by probability mass instead of count. Sort tokens by probability, accumulate, and keep the smallest prefix whose cumulative probability reaches p (the nucleus), then renormalise and sample. On the example with p = 0.9: cumulative sums are 0.50 (Paris), 0.75 (+ the), 0.87 (+ a), 0.95 (+ London). The sum first reaches 0.9 at "London", so the nucleus is the first four tokens, total 0.95. Renormalising by 0.95 gives [0.526, 0.263, 0.126, 0.084, 0]: "banana" is gone, everything else keeps its relative odds. If instead the model put 0.93 on "Paris", the nucleus would contain "Paris" alone and top-p would act like greedy; if probability were spread over 200 plausible words, the nucleus would contain most of them. The candidate set adapts to the model's confidence, which is exactly what top-k lacks.

In their experiments with GPT-2 Large, nucleus sampling with p = 0.95 produced text with perplexity 13.13 (human 12.38), a vocabulary distribution and diversity close to human text, 0.36% repetition (human 0.28%), and the highest HUSE score of all methods, 0.97. Top-p became the default sampling method in most LLM APIs.

9. Min-p and other adaptive truncation

Top-p has a weakness at high temperature: flattening the distribution moves mass into the tail, so the nucleus can admit many poor tokens. Min-p (Nguyen et al., 2024) uses a threshold relative to the top token: keep every token whose probability is at least min_p × p_max. With min-p = 0.2 on the example, the threshold is 0.2 × 0.50 = 0.10, so "Paris", "the" and "a" survive and "London" (0.08) and "banana" (0.05) are cut; renormalised over 0.87 they become [0.575, 0.287, 0.138]. When the model is confident (p_max = 0.95) the threshold is 0.19 and almost everything else is cut; when it is uncertain (p_max = 0.05) the threshold is 0.01 and many options survive. Its authors argue this keeps text coherent at higher temperatures, enabling more creative output. Other adaptive methods exist (typical sampling, which keeps tokens whose surprise is close to the distribution's average; mirostat, which steers the sampled surprise towards a target), but top-p and min-p are the ones you will see in practice.

10. Order of operations and common defaults

Libraries apply these steps in a pipeline: penalties on the logits, then temperature, then truncation filters, then sampling. The order matters (truncating before or after temperature gives different sets), and it differs between libraries, so check it when you port settings from one stack to another. Typical API defaults are temperature 1 with top-p 1 (pure sampling from the model's distribution, relying on the model being well-tuned after post-training), and many applications lower temperature to 0.2-0.7 or set top-p to about 0.9. A setting of temperature 0 is usually implemented as greedy decoding. Because the settings interact, a sensible practice is to tune one knob (often temperature) and leave the others at neutral values unless there is a measured reason.

11. Repetition, frequency and presence penalties

Penalties modify the logits of tokens that have already appeared. The repetition penalty from the CTRL paper (Keskar et al., 2019, describing Salesforce's 1.6-billion-parameter conditional language model; the penalty was their remedy for greedy decoding's loops) divides a previously generated token's logit by θ (if positive), with θ = 1.2 found to balance truthful generation and lack of repetition with greedy decoding. API-style presence and frequency penalties subtract a constant once a token has appeared, or a constant times its count. Worked: with a frequency penalty of 0.5, a token that has appeared three times has 1.5 subtracted from its logit, dividing its probability by about e^1.5 ≈ 4.5 before renormalisation. Penalties are blunt. They cannot tell a pathological loop from legitimate repetition, so they damage code (a variable name must repeat), structured output (JSON keys repeat by design), names and technical terms. Use them lightly, mainly with greedy or low-temperature decoding where loops are likely.

12. Determinism, seeds and reproducibility

Sampling uses a random number generator, so setting a seed makes it repeatable on identical hardware with identical batching. Greedy decoding has no randomness at all. Yet even greedy outputs from hosted LLMs can differ between calls, because the logits themselves vary slightly: GPU kernels add numbers in an order that depends on batch size and contents, floating-point addition is not associative, and when two tokens are nearly tied a tiny difference flips the argmax, after which the whole continuation differs. For products that need consistency, use temperature 0, constrain the format, and cache answers; for evaluation, run multiple samples and report variance rather than trusting a single run.

13. Constrained decoding

When output must follow a strict format, such as valid JSON matching a schema, a function call, or one of a fixed set of labels, decoding can enforce it directly. At each step the decoder computes which tokens would keep the output valid under a grammar and sets all other tokens' logits to −∞ before picking. The model still chooses among valid tokens, so content comes from the model while validity is guaranteed. This beats both "please output JSON" prompting (which fails occasionally) and post-hoc repair. Many serving engines and APIs provide structured output modes built on this idea; lm16 uses it for tool calls. Constrained decoding composes with the methods above: you can sample with temperature and top-p among the valid tokens.

14. Stopping: end tokens, stop sequences and length limits

Decoding also decides when to stop. A chat model normally emits a special end-of-turn token (lm1) when its answer is complete; the decoder stops there. Applications can add stop sequences, strings that end generation when produced (for example a closing tag or "\nUser:"), and a maximum token limit as a safety net. Each generation reports a finish reason: the end token, a stop sequence, or the length limit. A rising share of length-limit finishes is one of the best signals that something is wrong, since it usually means repetition loops, a broken chat template that hides the end token, or a prompt that invites unbounded output. The limit also bounds cost and latency, because every generated token is a decode step (lm5).

The positive feedback behind repetition

Why do models make repeated phrases more likely? Training text contains many legitimate repetitions (lists, refrains, code), and attention makes copying earlier context easy: induction heads (lm2) predict "whatever followed this token last time". Once a sentence appears twice, the model's in-context evidence that "this document repeats" is strong, and the probability of a third repetition rises. Holtzman et al.'s figure 4 shows the probability of a repeated phrase climbing with each repetition. Maximisation decoding converts that rising probability into certainty. Sampling breaks the loop because it occasionally takes a different path; training-side remedies (penalising repeated tokens during training) also exist but are less common than fixing decoding.

The beam search curse

In machine translation it was observed that increasing beam width beyond a small value often makes translations worse, sometimes catastrophically: very wide beams find outputs with higher model probability that are shorter or even empty. This shows the model's probability is not a perfect measure of quality: the search finds the model's errors when it searches hard enough. The same principle reappears in RLHF as reward hacking (lm6): optimising hard against an imperfect score finds its flaws. Small beams with length normalisation are the practical compromise for constrained tasks.

Temperature, calibration and the tail

If a model's probabilities were perfectly calibrated, pure sampling at T = 1 would reproduce the training distribution exactly, including its variety. In practice probabilities in the tail are over-estimated relative to how often those tokens are good: models trained with cross-entropy are pushed to assign some probability to every possibility (any zero probability on a real token is an infinite loss), so they spread small amounts of mass widely. Post-training (lm6) also changes calibration, typically making chat models more confident. Truncation can be seen as a correction for tail miscalibration, which is why it helps even for models that are well calibrated on their top choices.

Sampling many and choosing: self-consistency and best-of-n

Decoding need not produce a single sample. Self-consistency samples several reasoning chains at moderate temperature and takes the majority final answer, which improves accuracy on maths and reasoning because errors are diverse while correct answers agree. Best-of-n samples n responses and picks the one a scorer (a reward model, a test suite, a judge model) rates highest. Both trade inference compute for quality, the "inference-time scaling" mentioned in lm7, and both require diversity: with greedy decoding all n samples are identical. Their cost is n times the generation, partly offset because the prompt's KV cache can be shared across samples (lm13).

Diversity across samples versus quality within one

Two different things are often both called "diversity". Within one long output, you want variety: no loops, natural word choice. Across many outputs for the same prompt, you may or may not want variety: a brainstorming tool wants ten different ideas, while a customer-support bot should give the same correct answer every time. Temperature and truncation affect both at once, which is why the right setting depends on the product. When you need consistency across calls but natural text within one, use low temperature with top-p rather than pure greedy decoding, and cache answers for repeated questions.

Where the logits are modified matters for serving

Logit processors run on every decode step for every sequence in a batch, on vectors as long as the vocabulary. Sorting 128,000 probabilities for top-p at each step is non-trivial at high throughput, so serving engines implement sampling in fused GPU kernels, sometimes using approximate or partial sorts. Constrained decoding adds a grammar state per sequence and a mask computation per step; naive implementations can become the bottleneck, which is why efficient grammar engines precompute token masks. Speculative decoding (lm13) must also reproduce the target sampling distribution exactly, which constrains how draft tokens are accepted under sampling.

Contrastive and guided decoding

Several methods change the scores rather than just truncating them. Contrastive decoding scores each token by the difference between a large model's log-probability and a small "amateur" model's, favouring continuations the strong model likes but the weak one does not, which suppresses generic, repetitive text the weak model also predicts. Classifier-free guidance, borrowed from image diffusion models, contrasts the model's predictions with and without part of the prompt to strengthen the prompt's influence. These methods cost an extra forward pass per step and are less common in production than temperature and top-p, but they illustrate a general point: decoding is a place where you can inject extra information or preferences at inference time without retraining.

Decoding and evaluation metrics

Many benchmark numbers are sensitive to decoding: pass@k for code depends on sampling temperature; multiple-choice benchmarks may be scored by comparing log-probabilities of options rather than generating at all; open-ended judge-based evaluations can change with temperature. Two models compared at different settings are not comparable. Good practice is to evaluate each model at its recommended settings (or sweep a few), fix seeds where possible, run multiple samples per item, and report the settings alongside the scores. lm14 returns to this.

The public case: "The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration" (Holtzman et al., 2019)

In 2019, GPT-2 was the most capable public language model, and its generations were famously uneven. Holtzman, Buys, Du, Forbes and Choi asked whether the problem was the model or the decoding. They generated 5,000 passages with GPT-2 Large (762M parameters) conditioned on WebText prefixes, under every common strategy, and compared them with the human continuations on several statistics: perplexity under the model, diversity between generations (Self-BLEU), how closely word frequencies matched human vocabulary use (Zipf coefficient), repetition, and HUSE, a combined human-and-statistical score.

Their Table 1 is the case in numbers. Human text: perplexity 12.38, Self-BLEU 0.31, repetition 0.28%. Greedy: perplexity 1.50 and 73.66% repetition. Beam search with width 16: 1.48 and 28.94%. Pure sampling: perplexity 22.73, too random. Sampling with temperature 0.9: 10.25. Top-k = 40: 6.88; top-k = 640: 13.82; top-k = 40 with temperature 0.7: 3.48 and 8.86% repetition. Nucleus sampling with p = 0.95: perplexity 13.13, Self-BLEU 0.32, repetition 0.36%, and the highest HUSE, 0.97. The conclusions: maximisation is an inappropriate objective for open-ended generation; pure sampling fails because of the unreliable tail; and truncating the tail adaptively, by cumulative probability, gives the most human-like text. Nucleus sampling was quickly adopted and top-p remains a standard parameter in essentially every LLM API.

Failure modes in production

Repetition loops in long outputs. Summaries or reports that repeat sentences, usually with greedy or very low temperature on long generations. Fix with sampling plus top-p, a mild penalty, or a maximum-length guard and loop detection.

Occasional derailment. A chat answer that turns into nonsense mid-paragraph, typically high temperature without truncation, or a provider default changed. Lower temperature or add top-p/min-p.

Broken structured output. Mostly-valid JSON with occasional invalid keys or truncation. Use constrained decoding or structured-output modes, temperature 0, and no repetition penalty.

Irreproducible evaluations. Scores that move between runs of the same model. Fix and log settings and seeds, run several samples per item, and report confidence intervals.

Settings lost in migration. Moving from one inference library to another changes the order of temperature and truncation or the meaning of a penalty, and output quality shifts. Re-validate settings after any stack change.

A decoding-related incident pattern

A common real-world sequence goes like this. A team tunes prompts with an API playground whose default temperature is 0.7, then ships code that calls the API without specifying temperature, so the provider's default of 1.0 applies. Outputs in production become noticeably more varied and occasionally off-topic; nobody changed the model or the prompt, so the investigation looks everywhere else first. The cure is cheap: set every decoding parameter explicitly in code, store the configuration with the prompt version, and include decoding settings in the evaluation harness so that a change in them shows up as a change in scores before it reaches users.

What to log

For every generation in production: model version, decoding parameters (temperature, top-p, top-k, min-p, penalties, max tokens, stop sequences), seed if used, finish reason (stop, length, content filter), and token counts. Monitor the rate of length-limit finishes (often loops), invalid structured outputs, and repetition statistics in a sample of outputs.

Goal: implement temperature, top-k, top-p and min-p, check them against the hand calculations, then generate from a tiny bigram language model to watch greedy decoding loop and sampling escape, and run a small beam search. NumPy and the standard library only, about a second.

import numpy as np, importlib, re, collections
np.set_printoptions(precision=3, suppress=True)

def softmax(z): z = z - z.max(); e = np.exp(z); return e / e.sum()
def process(logits, temperature=1.0, top_k=None, top_p=None, min_p=None):
    """Turn logits into the distribution actually sampled from (the usual order: temperature, then filters)."""
    p = softmax(logits / temperature)
    keep = np.ones_like(p, bool)
    if top_k is not None:
        keep &= p >= np.sort(p)[-top_k]
    if top_p is not None:
        order = np.argsort(-p); cum = np.cumsum(p[order])
        n = np.searchsorted(cum, top_p) + 1                  # smallest prefix whose mass reaches top_p
        mask = np.zeros_like(keep); mask[order[:n]] = True; keep &= mask
    if min_p is not None:
        keep &= p >= min_p * p.max()
    q = np.where(keep, p, 0.0); return q / q.sum()

# ---- 1. the lesson's 5-token example
tokens = ["Paris", "the", "a", "London", "banana"]
probs = np.array([0.50, 0.25, 0.12, 0.08, 0.05]); logits = np.log(probs)
print("tokens      ", tokens)
for label, kw in [("T=1", {}), ("T=0.5", {"temperature": 0.5}), ("T=2", {"temperature": 2.0}),
                  ("top-k=2", {"top_k": 2}), ("top-p=0.9", {"top_p": 0.9}), ("min-p=0.2", {"min_p": 0.2})]:
    q = process(logits, **kw); H = -(q[q > 0] * np.log(q[q > 0])).sum()
    print(f"{label:11s} {q}   entropy {H:.3f} nats")
print("cumulative mass in order:", np.cumsum(probs))

# ---- 2. a tiny word-level bigram 'language model' from standard-library docstrings
text = " ".join(importlib.import_module(m).__doc__ or "" for m in ["json", "csv", "argparse", "logging", "subprocess", "threading", "pathlib", "random", "heapq", "zipfile"])
words = re.findall(r"[a-z]+", text.lower())
vocab = sorted(set(words)); idx = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}
counts = collections.defaultdict(collections.Counter)
for a, b in zip(words, words[1:]): counts[a][b] += 1
def next_logits(w):
    l = np.full(len(vocab), -1e9)
    for b, c in counts[w].items(): l[idx[b]] = np.log(c)
    return l
def generate(start, n, rng=None, **kw):
    out = [start]
    for _ in range(n):
        l = next_logits(out[-1])
        if rng is None: nxt = vocab[int(np.argmax(l))]                      # greedy
        else: nxt = vocab[rng.choice(len(vocab), p=process(l, **kw))]
        out.append(nxt)
    return out
def repetition(seq, n=3):                                                     # share of repeated 3-grams
    grams = list(zip(*[seq[i:] for i in range(n)])); return 1 - len(set(grams)) / len(grams)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
print(f"\nbigram model over {len(vocab)} words")
g = generate("the", 40); print("greedy      :", " ".join(g[:16]), "...", f"| repeated 3-grams {repetition(g):.0%}")

# ---- 3. beam search on the same model: highest-probability sequences, not the most interesting
def beam(start, n, width):
    beams = [([start], 0.0)]
    for _ in range(n):
        cand = []
        for seq, lp in beams:
            l = next_logits(seq[-1]); lps = l - np.log(np.exp(l - l.max()).sum()) - l.max()
            for j in np.argsort(-lps)[:width]: cand.append((seq + [vocab[j]], lp + lps[j]))
        beams = sorted(cand, key=lambda c: -c[1])[:width]
    return beams[0]
seq, lp = beam("the", 12, 4)
print(f"\nbeam (width 4): {' '.join(seq)}  | log-prob {lp:.2f}, per word {lp/12:.2f}")

Compute — what does the sampler keep?

A model's next-token probabilities are 0.40, 0.20, 0.15, 0.10, 0.08, 0.04, 0.03. Which tokens survive top-k = 3, top-p = 0.8 and min-p = 0.25, and what are the renormalised probabilities for top-p?

Answer

Top-k = 3: 0.40, 0.20, 0.15 (sum 0.75) → 0.533, 0.267, 0.200.

Top-p = 0.8: cumulative 0.40, 0.60, 0.75, 0.85. The sum first reaches 0.8 at the fourth token, so keep four tokens with total 0.85: 0.40/0.85 = 0.471, 0.20/0.85 = 0.235, 0.15/0.85 = 0.176, 0.10/0.85 = 0.118.

Min-p = 0.25: threshold 0.25 × 0.40 = 0.10, so keep 0.40, 0.20, 0.15, 0.10 (the same four tokens here) with the same renormalised values as top-p.

Note that top-p and min-p agree on this distribution but would diverge if it were flatter or more peaked; top-k ignores the shape entirely.

Diagnose — the summariser that repeats

A document summariser built on an open model with greedy decoding sometimes outputs the same sentence three or four times before hitting the length limit. What is happening and what do you change?

Answer

A greedy repetition loop: once a sentence has appeared, the model assigns higher probability to repeating it, and greedy decoding always follows the most probable path, so it never leaves the loop until the token limit. The rise in "finish reason = length" is the telltale metric.

Options, in order: switch to sampling with a moderate temperature (around 0.3-0.7) plus top-p 0.9, which breaks loops while staying focused; or keep near-greedy decoding and add a mild repetition penalty (about 1.1-1.2) or n-gram blocking, checking that names and key terms can still repeat; add loop detection that stops generation when a sentence repeats; and check the prompt and chat template, since a malformed template can also cause run-on output. Evaluate the change with a summary-quality metric and the repetition rate on a held-out set of documents.
