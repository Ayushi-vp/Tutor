Your company has thousands of internal documents: product manuals, HR policies, support tickets, engineering runbooks. An employee asks the new AI assistant, "Can I carry over unused holiday into next year?" A general-purpose LLM has never seen your HR policy. It will still answer, fluently, probably with a typical policy from somewhere on the internet, and it may be wrong in a way that costs someone their holiday. Fine-tuning the model on your documents sounds like a fix, but lm6 showed post-training does not reliably add knowledge, the policy changes every January, and different employees are allowed to see different documents.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is the standard answer. Instead of hoping the model knows, the system first searches your documents for passages relevant to the question, puts the best few into the prompt, and asks the model to answer from them and cite them. Knowledge lives in the document store, where it can be updated in minutes, permission-checked per user, and shown to the user as evidence. The model contributes what it is good at: reading, reasoning over and summarising text it has been given.

The idea is simple, which is exactly why so many RAG systems disappoint. A demo over ten clean documents works; the production system over ten thousand messy PDFs answers wrongly a third of the time, and the team responds by rewording the prompt. Almost always the problem is elsewhere: the right passage was never retrieved, or it was split in half by chunking, or it was retrieved but buried in the middle of a long context, or the source was a scanned table that turned into gibberish during ingestion. Building RAG "properly" means treating it as a pipeline with measurable stages.

This lesson walks the whole pipeline: ingestion and chunking (with sizing arithmetic), keyword retrieval with BM25, dense retrieval with embeddings (covered in depth in lm10), hybrid fusion and reranking (worked by hand), query rewriting, context assembly under a token budget and the lost-in-the-middle effect, prompting for grounded, cited answers, and then the four places RAG breaks and how to evaluate each stage. The public case is the 2020 paper that named RAG. The lab builds a small retrieval system over Python's documentation in NumPy and measures it.

An open-book exam

A closed-book exam tests what a student memorised. An open-book exam lets them look things up, and a good open-book student does two things well: finds the right pages quickly, and answers from those pages rather than from vague memory. RAG turns an LLM's closed-book exam into an open-book one. The retriever is the student's skill at finding pages; the generator is their skill at reading them and writing an answer. Both must work. A student who finds the wrong chapter will write a confident, wrong answer; a student who finds the right page but ignores it will too.

The analogy also shows where RAG's strength comes from: the book can be updated without retraining the student, and different students can be given different books (access control). It breaks in one way that matters: a real student knows when the book does not contain the answer. An LLM given irrelevant passages often answers anyway, which is why RAG prompts instruct it to say "the sources do not say" and why evaluation checks that it does.

Two halves

A RAG system has an offline half that prepares documents and an online half that answers questions. Offline: ingest documents (parse PDFs, HTML, tickets into clean text plus metadata such as title, date and permissions), split them into chunks of a few hundred tokens, and build indexes: a keyword index for term matching and a vector index of embeddings, vectors that place texts with similar meaning close together (lm10 explains how they are made and searched). Online: take the question, retrieve the most relevant chunks, assemble them into a prompt, and generate an answer that cites them.

A question, followed through

Take the holiday question from the introduction. Offline, the HR handbook was parsed into sections, split into chunks of about 400 tokens, and each chunk indexed for keywords and embedded as a vector, with metadata saying it is the 2026 handbook, visible to all employees. Online, the question "Can I carry over unused holiday into next year?" is turned into a search. Keyword search finds chunks containing "holiday" and "carry"; vector search also finds the chunk titled "Annual leave: lapsing and carry-forward", which says "unused leave", not "holiday". The two result lists are merged, a reranker reads each candidate against the question and puts the carry-forward chunk first, and the top five chunks are placed in a prompt that says: answer only from these sources, cite them, and say if they do not contain the answer. The model replies "Up to five days can be carried forward and must be used by 31 March [1]", and the interface links [1] to the handbook section. Every step in that story is a place where quality is won or lost, and the body of this lesson takes them in turn.

What RAG is good for, and what it is not

RAG shines when answers live in specific documents: policies, manuals, contracts, tickets, research papers, code. It is weaker for questions that need reasoning across thousands of documents at once ("what are the most common complaints this year?"), which call for aggregation pipelines rather than top-k retrieval, and it cannot help if the answer is not written anywhere. Knowing which kind of question you face is the first design decision.

1. Ingestion: the unglamorous half of quality

Everything downstream depends on getting clean text out of source documents. PDFs are the classic trap: text extraction can scramble multi-column layouts, drop tables or turn them into runs of numbers, lose headings, and include headers and footers on every page. Scanned PDFs need optical character recognition. HTML brings navigation menus and cookie banners. Tickets and chats bring signatures, quoted replies and boilerplate. Good ingestion parses structure (headings, lists, tables), removes boilerplate, keeps metadata (source, title, section, date, author, version, permissions) and deduplicates near-identical copies. Barnett et al.'s study of three deployed systems lists "missing content" as the first failure point: the question cannot be answered because the answer is not in the documents, and the system should say so rather than guess. In practice, many "retrieval problems" are ingestion problems discovered late.

2. Chunking, with arithmetic

Documents are too long to embed or to put in a prompt whole, so they are split into chunks. A chunk must be small enough that it is about one thing (so its embedding and its keyword profile are specific) and that several fit in the context, but large enough to contain a self-contained answer. Common strategies are fixed-size windows of a few hundred tokens with an overlap of 10-20% so that a sentence on a boundary appears whole in at least one chunk, and structure-aware splitting on headings and paragraphs, which keeps related text together.

Sizing a corpus is simple arithmetic worth doing early. A 200-page manual at about 500 words per page is 100,000 words, about 130,000 tokens at 1.3 tokens per word (lm1). With 400-token chunks overlapping by 50 tokens, each new chunk advances 350 tokens, so there are about 130,000 / 350 ≈ 372 chunks. If each is embedded as a 1,024-dimensional vector of 4-byte floats, that is 4 KB per chunk and about 1.5 MB for the manual; ten thousand such manuals are 15 GB of vectors, which is when the index structures of lm10 start to matter. The lab chunks 28 Python module docstrings into 176 chunks of 60 words with 15 words of overlap.

3. Keyword retrieval with BM25

The classic way to find documents matching a query is keyword search, and its standard scoring function is BM25. For each query term, a chunk earns points if it contains the term, more if the term is rare in the collection, and more if the term appears several times, but with diminishing returns, and chunks longer than average are penalised slightly so long chunks do not win just by containing more words. Concretely, a term's weight is its inverse document frequency, idf = ln(1 + (N − n + 0.5)/(n + 0.5)) for N chunks of which n contain the term, multiplied by a saturating function of how often it appears in the chunk, f·(k₁ + 1)/(f + k₁·(1 − b + b·len/avg_len)), with typical k₁ = 1.2-2 and b = 0.75. For intuition: in the lab's 176 chunks, a term appearing in 2 chunks has idf ≈ 4.26, while one appearing in 90 has idf ≈ 0.67, so a rare term such as "sha256" counts more than six times as much as a word found in half the chunks, and words in almost every chunk count for almost nothing.

BM25 is fast (an inverted index lists the chunks containing each term), needs no model, explains itself, and is excellent at exact identifiers: product codes, error messages, function names, people's names. Its weakness is vocabulary mismatch: a question about "money back" will not match a policy that says "reimbursement".

4. Dense retrieval with embeddings

Dense retrieval represents the question and every chunk as an embedding vector produced by a neural encoder trained so that texts with related meaning get nearby vectors, and retrieves the chunks whose vectors are most similar to the question's, usually by cosine similarity (lm10 builds this up from scratch, including how to search millions of vectors fast). Because it compares meaning rather than words, it handles paraphrases ("money back" ~ "reimbursement"), synonyms and even cross-language matches. Its weaknesses mirror BM25's strengths: rare identifiers and exact strings are often blurred, and an embedding model trained on general web text may not understand your domain's jargon. The original RAG system used a dense retriever, DPR, over 21 million 100-word Wikipedia passages.

5. Hybrid retrieval and reciprocal rank fusion

Because keyword and dense retrieval fail on different queries, production systems usually run both and merge the results. Their raw scores are on incomparable scales (BM25 scores are unbounded, cosine similarities lie between −1 and 1), so a robust way to merge is reciprocal rank fusion (RRF), which uses only ranks: each chunk's fused score is the sum over retrievers of 1/(c + rank), with c usually 60. Worked: chunk A is ranked 1st by BM25 and 4th by dense retrieval, so it scores 1/61 + 1/64 = 0.01639 + 0.01563 = 0.03202. Chunk B is ranked 3rd and 1st: 1/63 + 1/61 = 0.01587 + 0.01639 = 0.03226. Chunk C is 2nd by BM25 and not in the dense top-50: 1/62 = 0.01613. Fused order: B, A, C. Agreement between retrievers is rewarded; a chunk found by only one retriever can still make it, just lower.

Hybrid is not automatically better; it is better on average across query types. In the lab, where the evaluation questions mostly share words with the documents and the "dense" retriever is a simple stand-in (TF-IDF vectors, which weight each word by its frequency in the chunk times its IDF, compressed with a singular value decomposition, SVD, into 64 dimensions; not a neural encoder), BM25 alone scores recall@1 of 0.87 and MRR 0.91, the stand-in 0.60 and 0.72, and the fusion 0.60 and 0.77 with recall@3 tied with BM25 at 0.93. With a strong neural embedder and paraphrased questions the picture changes. The lesson generalises: measure on your own query set before choosing.

6. Reranking

First-stage retrievers are built for speed: BM25 looks up terms, dense retrieval compares a query vector against precomputed chunk vectors. Neither lets the query and the chunk interact in detail. A reranker, usually a cross-encoder, is a transformer that reads the query and one candidate chunk together and outputs a relevance score; because attention runs across both texts (lm2), it can check that the chunk actually answers the question rather than just sharing topics. It is far too slow to run on millions of chunks, so it is applied to the top 20-100 candidates from the first stage, and the best 3-10 go to the prompt. Anthropic's contextual-retrieval experiments give a sense of the gains available from better retrieval: on their benchmarks, prepending a short description of each chunk's context before indexing, combining contextual embeddings with contextual BM25, and reranking reduced the top-20 retrieval failure rate from 5.7% to 1.9%, a 67% reduction.

7. Query rewriting

Users ask questions badly for retrieval: "what about the other one?" in the middle of a conversation, a question bundling three sub-questions, or wording that shares nothing with the documents. Query rewriting uses a (fast) LLM call before retrieval to turn the conversation into a standalone query, to split a multi-part question into several queries whose results are merged, or to expand it with synonyms and likely document phrasing. A related trick generates a hypothetical answer and retrieves with its embedding, on the grounds that an answer looks more like the documents than a question does. Rewriting adds latency and its own failure mode (a rewrite that changes the question's meaning), so it is worth measuring rather than assuming.

8. Context assembly under a token budget

The retrieved chunks must fit in the prompt with everything else. Budget it explicitly. With an 8,192-token window: system instructions 500 tokens, the question 100, and 1,000 reserved for the answer leaves 6,592 tokens for sources, about 16 chunks of 400 tokens. With a 128K window there is room for far more, but cost and time to first token grow with every input token (lm5), and more is not always better: irrelevant chunks dilute attention and give the model more to misread.

Order matters too. Liu et al.'s "Lost in the Middle" experiments placed the one relevant document at different positions among 20 retrieved ones and found a U-shaped curve: models used it best at the start or end of the context and worst in the middle. For GPT-3.5-Turbo, accuracy with the answer document in the middle fell below its accuracy with no documents at all (56.1% closed-book). Practical responses: send fewer, better chunks (reranking pays off here), and put the strongest at the edges. The lab's assembly step keeps chunks within a 300-token budget and orders them ranks 1, 3, 5 … 6, 4, 2 so the best two sit at the start and the end. Each chunk is labelled ([1], [2] …) with its source so the answer can cite it.

9. Prompting for grounded, cited answers

The generation prompt does three jobs. It tells the model to answer only from the provided sources, which reduces mixing in memorised (possibly outdated) knowledge. It asks for citations, the source labels supporting each claim, so users and automated checks can verify them. And it gives the model permission to say the sources do not contain the answer, which is the behaviour that prevents confident guessing when retrieval missed. A typical structure: instructions, then the labelled sources, then the question last (close to where generation starts). Use a low temperature (lm8) for factual answers. Some systems add a verification pass that checks each cited sentence against its source, with a cheaper model or a natural-language-inference classifier, and removes or flags unsupported claims before the answer is shown.

10. Worked retrieval metrics

Retrieval quality is measured on a labelled set of questions, each with the chunks that answer it. Recall@k is the fraction of questions with at least one relevant chunk in the top k; MRR (mean reciprocal rank) averages 1/(rank of the first relevant chunk). Worked on three questions: the first relevant chunk is at rank 1 for question 1, rank 3 for question 2, and missing from the top 10 for question 3. Recall@1 = 1/3 = 0.33, recall@3 = 2/3 = 0.67, recall@10 = 0.67; MRR = (1/1 + 1/3 + 0)/3 = 0.44. Recall@k at the k you actually put in the prompt is the number that bounds end-to-end accuracy: if the right chunk is not in the top 5 and you send 5, the model cannot use it.

11. The four places RAG breaks

Putting the stages together gives a diagnostic order. (1) Ingestion and content: the answer is missing from the corpus, stale, duplicated with conflicting versions, or garbled by parsing. (2) Retrieval: the right chunk exists but is not in the top-k, because of vocabulary mismatch, poor chunking, a weak embedding model or a missing metadata filter. (3) Assembly: it was retrieved but cut by the budget, buried in the middle, or split across chunks so no single chunk contains the answer. (4) Generation: it is in the context but the model ignores it, misreads it, merges it with memorised knowledge, extracts the wrong part, or formats the answer wrongly. Barnett et al. list seven failure points from their deployments, missing content, missed top-ranked documents, not in context, not extracted, wrong format, incorrect specificity and incomplete answers, which map onto these four stages. Debug in order: a prompt change cannot fix stage 1 or 2.

12. Evaluating generation

Given retrieved context, evaluate the answer on separate axes: correctness (does it answer the question correctly?), faithfulness (is every claim supported by the sources?), citation accuracy (do the cited sources support the cited sentences?), and appropriate refusal (does it decline when the sources lack the answer?). Human labels are the reference; LLM judges with a rubric scale them, provided their agreement with humans is checked (lm14 discusses evaluation design). Frameworks such as RAGAS package metrics like faithfulness and context precision. Keep a fixed evaluation set of real questions with labelled relevant chunks and reference answers, grow it with every production failure, and run it on every change to chunking, embeddings, prompts or models.

13. Operational concerns

A RAG system is a data system. Freshness: new and changed documents must be re-ingested, re-chunked and re-embedded, and deleted ones removed from every index; a pipeline with incremental updates and versioning prevents stale and conflicting answers. Access control: permission metadata on every chunk, enforced as a filter inside retrieval so a user never receives content they cannot see; never rely on the prompt to hide something that has been retrieved. Embedding model changes: a new embedding model means re-embedding the whole corpus, since vectors from different models are not comparable. Latency budget: retrieval and reranking add time before the first token; keyword and vector searches can run in parallel and the reranker can be small. Security: retrieved documents are untrusted input to the model; a document containing instructions ("ignore previous instructions and…") can hijack it, which lm18 covers as indirect prompt injection.

14. Beyond single-shot RAG

Some questions need more than one retrieval. Multi-hop questions ("which team does the author of this design doc now manage?") require retrieving one fact and using it to search for the next. Agentic RAG lets the model decide when and what to search, calling retrieval as a tool in a loop (lm16), at the cost of more latency and less predictable behaviour. Graph-based approaches extract entities and relations at ingestion to answer aggregation questions. These extend the same principles: every retrieval step still needs measurable recall, and every generated claim still needs a source.

Why first-stage retrieval decides the ceiling

Each stage after first-stage retrieval can only reorder or drop what it is given. If the relevant chunk is not among the 50 candidates, no reranker or prompt can recover it. That makes first-stage recall at a generous k (50-100) the ceiling on end-to-end accuracy, and precision at the final k the main lever on prompt quality. A practical consequence: when tuning, look at recall@50 first. If it is low, the problem is chunking, ingestion, the embedding model or vocabulary mismatch; if recall@50 is high but recall@5 is low, the reranker or fusion is the lever; if recall@5 is high and answers are still wrong, the problem is assembly or generation.

Bi-encoders versus cross-encoders

Dense retrievers are bi-encoders: query and chunk are encoded separately, so chunk vectors can be precomputed and compared with a single dot product per chunk, enabling search over millions. The price is that the whole meaning of a chunk must be squeezed into one vector before the query is known. A cross-encoder processes the concatenated query and chunk with full attention, so every query token can attend to every chunk token; it can recognise that "Can I carry holiday over?" is answered by a sentence about "unused leave lapses on 31 March". It cannot precompute anything, so its cost is one forward pass per candidate per query, which is why it is reserved for reranking a short list. Late-interaction models sit in between: they store one vector per chunk token and score with many small dot products, improving accuracy at the price of much larger indexes.

The original RAG formulation

Lewis et al. treated the retrieved document as a latent variable. Given input x, the retriever gives a distribution p(z|x) over passages (a softmax over inner products of DPR embeddings, approximated with the top k), and the generator gives p(y|x, z). RAG-Sequence uses one passage for the whole output and marginalises: p(y|x) = Σ_z p(z|x)·p(y|x, z). RAG-Token allows a different passage for each output token. Training maximises the marginal likelihood end to end, updating the query encoder and generator (the document encoder and index were kept fixed to avoid re-indexing). Modern systems rarely train jointly; they assemble pretrained components and put the passages in the prompt of an instruction-tuned LLM. The paper's lasting contributions are the framing (parametric memory in weights plus non-parametric memory in an index) and the demonstration that swapping the index updates the model's knowledge.

Contextual chunk enrichment

A chunk such as "Revenue grew 3% over the previous quarter" is unretrievable for "What was ACME's Q2 2023 revenue growth?" because the chunk never names the company or the quarter; that context lived in the document's title and earlier sections. Contextual retrieval asks an LLM, once per chunk at ingestion, to write a short sentence situating it in its document ("This chunk is from ACME's Q2 2023 SEC filing…"), and prepends it before computing both the embedding and the BM25 index. Simpler variants prepend the document title and section heading. The cost is an LLM call per chunk at ingestion, reduced by prompt caching of the shared document prefix; the benefit, in Anthropic's measurements, was a 35% reduction in top-20 retrieval failures from contextual embeddings alone and 49% with contextual BM25 added.

Long context versus retrieval, quantitatively

Consider a 500-page knowledge base, about 325,000 tokens. Sending it all on every question means 325,000 input tokens per request: at a few dollars per million input tokens that is about a dollar per question, prefill time of many seconds, and the lost-in-the-middle problem at full strength. Retrieval sends perhaps 3,000 tokens: about 1% of the cost and much faster. Prompt caching (lm13) can make a fixed long prefix cheaper to reuse, which helps for one large document used across many questions; but for collections larger than any context window, or with per-user permissions, retrieval remains necessary. The engineering answer is usually both: retrieve generously, rerank, and use long context to include more surrounding text per hit.

Metadata filters and structured retrieval

Many questions carry constraints that are better expressed as filters than as similarity: "the 2026 policy", "tickets about product X", "documents I am allowed to see". Storing metadata with each chunk and filtering on it before or during ranking is far more reliable than hoping the embedding captures the year or the product. Some systems let an LLM translate the question into a structured query (filters plus a search string) before retrieval. The design question is which facts belong in metadata, where exact matching is possible, and which belong in text, where fuzzy matching is needed.

Embedding drift and versioning

Embeddings from different models, or different versions of the same model, live in different vector spaces; a query embedded with model B compared against chunks embedded with model A gives meaningless similarities, often without any error. Indexes should record the embedding model and version, upgrades should rebuild the index in parallel and switch atomically, and evaluation should compare old and new on the same labelled set before switching. The same discipline applies to chunking changes, which alter which text each vector represents.

The public case: "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks" (Lewis et al., 2020)

Lewis and colleagues at Facebook AI, UCL and NYU combined a pretrained dense retriever (DPR, a BERT-based bi-encoder) with a pretrained sequence-to-sequence generator (BART-large, about 400M parameters). The non-parametric memory was Wikipedia (December 2018) split into 100-word chunks, 21 million passages, indexed for maximum inner-product search. They called the approach retrieval-augmented generation and trained the query encoder and generator end to end.

On open-domain question answering, RAG-Sequence reached 44.5 exact match on Natural Questions, against 34.5 for the closed-book T5-11B (a model with roughly 18 times more parameters answering from memory alone) and 41.5 for the DPR retrieve-and-extract system; on TriviaQA it scored 56.8 and 68.0 on the two test splits, and on WebQuestions 45.2. Human evaluators judged its generated Jeopardy questions more factual than BART's. The most influential experiment was index hot-swapping: they built a second index from a December 2016 Wikipedia dump and asked about 82 world leaders who had changed between 2016 and 2018. With matching indexes RAG answered 70% (2016 index, 2016 leaders) and 68% (2018 index, 2018 leaders) correctly; with mismatched indexes, 12% and 4%. Knowledge could be updated by replacing the index, with no retraining. That property, together with citations and access control, is why RAG became the default architecture for enterprise LLM applications.

What goes wrong in deployments

Garbled ingestion. Tables in PDFs become number soup and answers about prices or limits are wrong. Inspect parsed text for a sample of documents of each type; use layout-aware parsers or table extraction.

Conflicting versions. Old and new policies both indexed; the model mixes them. Track document versions and effective dates, retire superseded documents, and prefer recent ones at retrieval.

Silent recall collapse after a change. A new embedding model or chunking strategy deployed without re-running retrieval evaluation drops recall@5 for a class of queries. Gate index changes on the labelled set.

Permission leaks. Filtering applied after the LLM, or not at all, exposes restricted documents. Filter inside retrieval and test with different user roles.

Confident answers to unanswerable questions. When nothing relevant is retrieved, the model answers anyway. Include unanswerable questions in evaluation and reward "the sources don't say".

A cost and latency picture

For a typical deployment the money is in generation, not retrieval: keyword and vector search over millions of chunks take milliseconds and cost little; a small cross-encoder reranking 50 candidates takes tens to a hundred milliseconds on a GPU; the LLM call with a few thousand input tokens and a few hundred output tokens dominates both cost and latency. That makes context size the main cost lever (send five good chunks, not forty mediocre ones) and makes prompt caching of the fixed instructions worthwhile. Ingestion has its own cost profile: embedding the corpus (and, with contextual retrieval, one LLM call per chunk) is a one-off batch job that must be repeated whenever the embedding model changes.

What to monitor

Retrieval: recall@k and MRR on the evaluation set per release; in production, the distribution of top retrieval scores (a drop suggests drift or ingestion failure), the fraction of queries with no chunk above a threshold, and user feedback linked to the retrieved chunk IDs. Generation: faithfulness and citation accuracy on sampled traffic (LLM judge, periodically checked by humans), refusal rate, answer length. System: latency per stage, tokens per request and cost, and index freshness (time from document change to searchable).

Goal: build a small RAG retrieval stack over Python's own documentation: chunking with overlap, BM25, a dense stand-in (TF-IDF compressed by SVD, because no neural embedding model is downloaded), reciprocal rank fusion, evaluation with recall@k and MRR on 15 labelled questions, and prompt assembly under a token budget. NumPy and the standard library; about a second. There is no LLM call: the lab stops at the prompt you would send.

import numpy as np, importlib, re, collections

# ---- 1. corpus: standard-library module docstrings, chunked into ~60-word passages with 15 words of overlap
MODULES = ["configparser", "sys", "shelve", "csv", "statistics", "argparse", "json", "socket", "decimal", "timeit",
           "hashlib", "copy", "unittest", "heapq", "io", "ssl", "plistlib", "uuid", "sched", "struct", "doctest",
           "os", "subprocess", "gc", "time", "tempfile", "difflib", "random"]
def chunk(words, size=60, overlap=15):
    step = size - overlap
    return [" ".join(words[i:i + size]) for i in range(0, max(len(words) - overlap, 1), step)]
chunks, source = [], []
for m in MODULES:
    for c in chunk((importlib.import_module(m).__doc__ or "").split()):
        chunks.append(c); source.append(m)
print(f"{len(chunks)} chunks from {len(MODULES)} modules")
tok = lambda s: re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", s.lower())
docs = [tok(c) for c in chunks]

# ---- 2. BM25 keyword retrieval
vocab = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(sorted({w for d in docs for w in d}))}
tf = np.zeros((len(docs), len(vocab)))
for i, d in enumerate(docs):
    for w in d: tf[i, vocab[w]] += 1
df = (tf > 0).sum(0); N = len(docs); dl = tf.sum(1); avgdl = dl.mean()
idf = np.log(1 + (N - df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5))
def bm25(q, k1=1.5, b=0.75):
    s = np.zeros(N)
    for w in tok(q):
        if w in vocab:
            f = tf[:, vocab[w]]; s += idf[vocab[w]] * f * (k1 + 1) / (f + k1 * (1 - b + b * dl / avgdl))
    return s

# ---- 3. a stand-in 'dense' retriever: TF-IDF vectors compressed by SVD (latent semantic analysis), cosine similarity
X = np.log1p(tf) * idf; X /= np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1, keepdims=True) + 1e-9
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X, full_matrices=False); k = 64
emb = U[:, :k] * S[:k]; emb /= np.linalg.norm(emb, axis=1, keepdims=True)
def dense(q):
    v = np.zeros(len(vocab))
    for w in tok(q):
        if w in vocab: v[vocab[w]] += idf[vocab[w]]
    e = v @ Vt[:k].T; return emb @ (e / (np.linalg.norm(e) + 1e-9))

# ---- 4. hybrid: reciprocal rank fusion
def rrf(*score_lists, c=60):
    total = np.zeros(N)
    for s in score_lists:
        ranks = np.empty(N); ranks[np.argsort(-s)] = np.arange(1, N + 1); total += 1 / (c + ranks)
    return total

# ---- 5. evaluation set: question -> (module, phrase that marks a relevant chunk)
QA = [("how do I compute the median of my numbers", "statistics", "median"),
      ("turn a python object into a JSON formatted string", "json", "dumps"),
      ("generate a random unique identifier", "uuid", "uuid4"),
      ("measure how long a small piece of code takes to run", "timeit", "timeit"),
      ("priority queue implemented with a heap", "heapq", "heap"),
      ("parse options and arguments from the command line", "argparse", "command"),
      ("make a temporary file that is deleted automatically", "tempfile", "temporary"),
      ("compute a secure sha256 digest of some bytes", "hashlib", "sha256"),
      ("make an independent deep copy of a nested object", "copy", "deep"),
      ("start a child process and read its output through pipes", "subprocess", "pipes"),
      ("pack values into C structs stored as bytes", "struct", "struct"),
      ("a persistent dictionary stored on disk", "shelve", "persistent"),
      ("compute the deltas between two sequences of lines", "difflib", "deltas"),
      ("read settings from an ini configuration file", "configparser", "configuration"),
      ("schedule events to run at a later time", "sched", "event")]
gold = [{i for i, c in enumerate(chunks) if source[i] == m and p in c.lower()} for _, m, p in QA]
assert all(gold), "every question needs at least one relevant chunk"
def evaluate(fn, ks=(1, 3, 5)):
    rec = {k: 0 for k in ks}; mrr = 0
    for (q, _, _), g in zip(QA, gold):
        order = list(np.argsort(-fn(q)))
        for k in ks: rec[k] += bool(g & set(order[:k]))
        mrr += 1 / (1 + min(order.index(i) for i in g))
    return {f"recall@{k}": round(v / len(QA), 2) for k, v in rec.items()} | {"MRR": round(mrr / len(QA), 2)}
print("BM25   ", evaluate(bm25))
print("LSA    ", evaluate(dense))
print("hybrid ", evaluate(lambda q: rrf(bm25(q), dense(q))))

# ---- 6. assemble a prompt under a token budget, best chunks at the start and end ('lost in the middle')
q = QA[0][0]; order = [int(i) for i in np.argsort(-rrf(bm25(q), dense(q)))[:6]]
budget, used, picked = 300, 0, []
for i in order:
    t = int(len(chunks[i].split()) * 1.3) + 1                       # rough English tokens-per-word estimate
    if used + t <= budget: picked.append(i); used += t
arranged = picked[0::2] + picked[1::2][::-1]                          # ranks 1,3,5 ... then ... 6,4,2
prompt = "Answer using only the sources below. Cite them as [n]. If they do not contain the answer, say so.\n\n"
prompt += "\n".join(f"[{n+1}] ({source[i]}) {chunks[i][:70]}..." for n, i in enumerate(arranged))
print(f"\nquestion: {q}\npicked chunks {picked} (~{used} tokens), arranged {arranged}\n" + prompt + f"\n\nQuestion: {q}")

  1. 176 chunks from 28 modules.
  2. Retrieval quality: BM25 recall@1 0.87, recall@3 0.93, MRR 0.91; the SVD stand-in 0.60, 0.73, MRR 0.72 (but recall@5 1.0); hybrid 0.60, 0.93, MRR 0.77. With questions that share words with the documents and a weak dense model, keyword search wins and fusion does not help at rank 1: measure, do not assume.
  3. Assembly: for the median question, three statistics chunks fit the 300-token budget, ordered so the best two are first and last, each labelled for citation, with an instruction to answer only from the sources and to say when they do not contain the answer.

Diagnose — the RAG bot that is "wrong a third of the time"

A support bot built on your product documentation answers about a third of real questions incorrectly. The team has spent two weeks rewriting the prompt. You have one day. What do you do?

Answer

Build a small labelled set first. Take 50 failed questions from logs; for each, find the documentation passage that answers it (or mark it unanswerable).

Classify each failure by stage. (1) Is the answer in the indexed documentation at all, and is the parsed text correct? (2) Is a relevant chunk in the retriever's top-k (compute recall@k, and recall@50 to see the ceiling)? (3) If retrieved, did it make it into the prompt and where? (4) If in the prompt, did the model use it correctly?

Typical outcome: most failures land in stages 1-2 (missing or garbled content, vocabulary mismatch, chunks splitting the answer), which explains why prompt rewriting did not help. Fixes follow: correct ingestion for the broken document types; add BM25 alongside embeddings or vice versa, with RRF; add a reranker; adjust chunking to section boundaries; add query rewriting for conversational follow-ups. Then re-measure recall@k and answer accuracy on the same 50, and keep the set as a regression test.

Estimate — context budget and cost

You use a model with a 16K-token context, pricing $3 per million input tokens. System prompt 800 tokens, typical question 60 tokens, answers up to 700 tokens, chunks of 350 tokens. How many chunks fit, and what does retrieval save compared with sending a 120,000-token handbook in a long-context model at the same price?

Answer

Budget for sources: 16,384 − 800 − 60 − 700 = 14,824 tokens, about 42 chunks. You would rarely send that many: 5-10 well-reranked chunks (1,750-3,500 tokens) usually work better, given lost-in-the-middle effects. With 8 chunks, input is about 800 + 60 + 2,800 = 3,660 tokens, about $0.011 per question. Sending the handbook: about 120,900 input tokens, $0.36 per question, roughly 33 times more, plus much longer time to first token. At 100,000 questions a month that is about $1,100 against $36,000.
