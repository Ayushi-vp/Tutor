A friend calls you, shaken. Her routine screening test came back positive for a disease that affects 1 in 100 people her age. The leaflet says the test is 99% accurate: it catches 99% of people who have the disease and correctly clears 99% of people who do not. She asks: "So I almost certainly have it?"

Most people, and as you will see, most doctors, would say yes, about 99%. The real answer is 50%. Half of the people who test positive with this test are perfectly healthy. Nothing about the test is broken; the answer comes from how rare the disease is. Out of 10,000 people, 100 are sick and 99 of them test positive. But 9,900 are healthy, and 1% of them, another 99, also test positive. A positive result puts you in a crowd of 198 people, half of them healthy.

This is not a medical curiosity. It is the single most common way probability goes wrong in machine-learning work. A fraud model that is "99% accurate" on a balanced test set, deployed where 1 transaction in 1,000 is fraud, will produce roughly ten false alarms for every real one, and the operations team will stop trusting it within a week. A content classifier that looked excellent offline floods reviewers with innocent posts. A data scientist who reads a model output of 0.9 as "90% likely" when the model was trained on resampled 50/50 data will be wrong by an order of magnitude. Each of these is the same mistake: confusing the probability of the evidence given the answer with the probability of the answer given the evidence.

The tool that fixes it is two hundred years old and fits on one line: Bayes' theorem. To use it confidently you need the small set of ideas underneath it, and those same ideas are the foundation for everything else in this track and in machine learning: what an event is, what "probability" means, how to read joint and conditional probabilities off a table, what independence really means (and how it differs from "can't happen together"), and the law of total probability.

This lesson builds those ideas from scratch, assuming only school arithmetic. It starts with sample spaces and the three rules every probability obeys, reads probabilities from a table of real-looking counts, defines conditional probability as "shrinking the world", and derives Bayes' theorem from it. Then it works the disease test above by hand, three ways (frequency tree, formula, odds), applies the same arithmetic to a Naive Bayes spam filter and to correcting a classifier trained on rebalanced data, and ends with the traps that catch experts: Simpson's paradox, the prosecutor's fallacy, and evidence that is not as independent as it looks. The public case is Gerd Gigerenzer's work showing that most gynaecologists got the mammography version of this question wrong, and that a simple change in how the numbers were written fixed it.

The clearest way to think about probability, and the way this lesson will keep returning to, is to imagine a concrete crowd. Instead of "the probability of having the disease is 0.01", picture 10,000 people standing in a field. One hundred of them have the disease. That is all a probability of 0.01 means here: if you picked one person at random, every person equally likely, the chance of picking a sick one is 100 out of 10,000.

Gigerenzer calls these counts natural frequencies, and the reason they help is that every probability question becomes a counting question about the crowd. "What is the chance a random person tests positive?" becomes "how many people in the field are holding a positive result, out of 10,000?" "What is the chance a person who tests positive is sick?" becomes "walk over to the people holding positive results, and count how many of them are sick." That second question is the one people get wrong, and the crowd makes the error visible: you are no longer counting out of 10,000, you are counting out of the smaller group who tested positive.

That move, narrowing the crowd to the people who match what you already know, is called conditioning, and it is the most important idea in this lesson. The probability of A given B, written P(A | B) and read "probability of A given B", is the fraction of the B-crowd that also has A. The vertical bar means "inside the group where this is known to be true".

Here is the whole disease example as a tree of counts. Read it left to right: split the crowd by the truth (sick or healthy), then split each branch by the test result.

Everything in the tree is one of three kinds of number, and naming them now will make the formulas later feel familiar. The first split uses the base rate or prior: how common the thing is before you look at any evidence, here 1%. The second split uses the test's error rates, which are conditional probabilities going from the truth to the result: P(positive | sick) = 99% and P(positive | healthy) = 1%. These are called likelihoods. The answer the patient wants goes the other way, from the result to the truth: P(sick | positive). That is the posterior. Bayes' theorem is nothing more than the recipe for getting the posterior from the prior and the likelihoods, and the tree is that recipe done with counts.

The analogy has limits worth knowing. Real probabilities are rarely whole numbers of people, so the crowd sometimes contains "99.9 people"; that is fine, the arithmetic is still correct. And some probabilities are not about a crowd at all, such as "the probability that this particular model will beat the baseline in tomorrow's A/B test", which happens once. For those, probability is better read as a degree of belief, but it obeys exactly the same rules, so the crowd picture still gives the right numbers.

The big picture, then, in four moves. First, list what can happen (the sample space) and give each possibility a probability. Second, learn to read joint probabilities ("sick and positive") and marginal ones ("positive, regardless") from a table. Third, condition: shrink the world to what you know and renormalise. Fourth, use Bayes' theorem to reverse a conditional. The rest of this lesson takes each move slowly, with numbers, and then shows where the same four moves appear inside machine learning: in classifier precision, in Naive Bayes, and in fixing probabilities when the class balance changes. Later topics build on this directly: math2 turns outcomes into numbers (random variables), math5 uses Bayes' theorem to estimate model parameters (maximum a posteriori, or MAP, estimation), and math12 measures uncertainty itself (entropy).

1. Experiments, outcomes, sample spaces and events

Probability starts with an experiment: any process whose result you do not know in advance. Rolling a die, flipping a coin, picking a random user from your database, or running a model on the next incoming request are all experiments. Each possible result is an outcome, and the set of all outcomes is the sample space, written with the Greek capital letter Ω ("omega"). For one die, Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. For two dice rolled one after the other, Ω has 36 outcomes, the pairs (1,1), (1,2), … (6,6), because the first die has 6 possibilities and for each of them the second has 6.

An event is any set of outcomes, a question you can answer yes or no once the experiment is done. "The die shows an even number" is the event {2, 4, 6}. "The two dice sum to 7" is the event {(1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1)}, six outcomes. An event "happens" if the actual outcome is one of its members. The whole sample space Ω is an event that always happens, and the empty set ∅ is an event that never does.

Because events are sets, three set operations give you new events from old ones, and they map directly onto the words people use. The complement of A, written "not A" (or Aᶜ), is every outcome not in A: not-even is {1, 3, 5}. The intersection "A and B" (written A ∩ B) contains outcomes in both: even and greater than 3 is {4, 6}. The union "A or B" (A ∪ B) contains outcomes in either or both: even or greater than 3 is {2, 4, 5, 6}. In probability, "or" always means "one or the other or both" unless stated otherwise. Two events with no outcome in common, such as "shows 1" and "shows 6", are called mutually exclusive or disjoint: their intersection is empty.

Choosing the sample space carefully matters more than it looks. If you treat "the sum of two dice" as the outcome, the space is {2, 3, …, 12}, eleven outcomes, but they are not equally likely: a sum of 7 can happen six ways and a sum of 2 only one way. If you treat the ordered pair as the outcome, all 36 are equally likely for fair dice, and counting becomes easy. A good habit is to pick the sample space whose outcomes are equally likely whenever one exists.

2. The three rules every probability obeys

A probability is a function P that assigns each event a number. Every valid probability, whether it comes from counting, from long-run frequencies, or from someone's degree of belief, follows three rules, called the axioms (set down by Andrey Kolmogorov in 1933):

  1. Every event has a probability between 0 and 1: 0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1.
  2. Something certainly happens: P(Ω) = 1.
  3. For mutually exclusive events, probabilities add: if A and B cannot both happen, P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B). (The same holds for any list of events that pairwise cannot co-occur.)

When all outcomes are equally likely, these rules give the familiar counting formula: P(A) = (number of outcomes in A) / (number of outcomes in Ω). For a fair die, P(even) = 3/6 = 0.5. For two dice, P(sum is 7) = 6/36 = 1/6 ≈ 0.167, and P(sum is 2) = 1/36 ≈ 0.028.

Two useful rules follow directly. The complement rule: A and not-A are mutually exclusive and together make up Ω, so P(A) + P(not A) = 1, giving P(not A) = 1 − P(A). This is often the easy way in. "What is the probability of at least one six in four rolls?" is awkward to count directly, but its complement, "no six in four rolls", has probability (5/6)⁴ = 625/1296 ≈ 0.482 (each roll avoids six with probability 5/6, and the rolls do not affect each other, an idea made precise in section 5). So P(at least one six) ≈ 1 − 0.482 = 0.518.

The addition rule for events that can overlap: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B). The subtraction is needed because outcomes in both A and B were counted once in P(A) and again in P(B). With a 52-card deck, P(heart or king) = 13/52 + 4/52 − 1/52 = 16/52, because the king of hearts was counted twice. Forgetting the overlap is one of the most common arithmetic errors in probability, and it is the reason the axiom insists on "mutually exclusive" before letting you simply add.

A worked case of the complement rule that surprises almost everyone is the birthday problem: in a room of 23 people, what is the chance at least two share a birthday? Take the complement, all birthdays different, and build it one person at a time (ignoring leap years and assuming birthdays are spread evenly). The second person avoids the first person's birthday with probability 364/365, the third avoids both with 363/365, and so on down to 343/365 for the 23rd. Multiplying the 22 fractions gives about 0.4927, so P(some shared birthday) ≈ 0.5073, just over a half. The intuition fails because we think of matches with ourselves (22 chances) rather than all pairs: 23 people form 23·22/2 = 253 pairs. The same arithmetic tells you how often hash collisions happen in a table, or two randomly generated 32-bit IDs clash: with n random IDs out of N possible values, a collision becomes likely around n ≈ √N, so about 77,000 random 32-bit IDs already give a 50% chance of a duplicate.

3. Joint and marginal probabilities, read from a table

Most real probability questions involve two things at once: an email's label and whether it contains a word, a user's country and whether they convert, a patient's disease status and test result. The natural way to hold such data is a contingency table: counts for every combination, with totals on the edges. Here is one for 1,000 emails, classified by whether they are spam and whether they contain the word "free".

contains "free"no "free"row total
spam120180300
not spam35665700
column total1558451,000

A joint probability is the probability of a specific combination: divide one inner cell by the grand total. P(spam and "free") = 120/1000 = 0.12. P(not spam and no "free") = 665/1000 = 0.665. The four joint probabilities add to 1, because every email lands in exactly one cell.

A marginal probability concerns one variable while ignoring the other: divide a row or column total by the grand total. P(spam) = 300/1000 = 0.30 and P("free") = 155/1000 = 0.155. They are called marginal because they are literally written in the margins of the table, and computing one from the joint probabilities is called marginalising: P(spam) = P(spam and "free") + P(spam and no "free") = 0.12 + 0.18 = 0.30. You add up over all values of the variable you want to ignore. That operation will come back constantly: in math2 for distributions, and in every probabilistic model where some variable is unobserved.

4. Conditional probability: shrinking the world

Now ask: among emails that contain "free", what fraction are spam? You are no longer looking at all 1,000 emails, only at the 155 in the "free" column. Of those, 120 are spam, so the answer is 120/155 ≈ 0.774. That is the conditional probability P(spam | "free"): the probability of spam once you know the email contains "free".

In symbols, dividing top and bottom by 1,000 to turn counts into probabilities:

P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B), defined whenever P(B) > 0.

Read it as a two-step instruction. First shrink the world to B (the denominator). Then ask what share of that smaller world is also in A (the numerator). Here P(spam and "free") / P("free") = 0.12 / 0.155 ≈ 0.774, the same number as from the counts.

Now the reverse question: among spam emails, what fraction contain "free"? Shrink to the spam row, 300 emails, of which 120 contain "free": P("free" | spam) = 120/300 = 0.40. Compare: P(spam | "free") = 0.774, P("free" | spam) = 0.40. Same joint count of 120, different denominators, very different answers. These two numbers answer different questions, and swapping them is the root of the base-rate fallacy. A spam filter designer cares about the first ("if I see the word, how suspicious should I be?"); the statistics you can easily collect from a labelled spam folder give you the second.

A small die example builds intuition for how conditioning changes things. P(even) = 1/2. Given the roll is greater than 3, the world shrinks to {4, 5, 6}, of which {4, 6} are even, so P(even | >3) = 2/3. Learning "greater than 3" made "even" more likely. Given the roll is less than 3, the world is {1, 2}, and P(even | <3) = 1/2. Learning that changed nothing.

5. Independence, and what it is not

That last case has a name. Events A and B are independent when knowing one happened does not change the probability of the other: P(A | B) = P(A). Substituting the definition of conditional probability and multiplying through gives the more common form:

A and B independent ⇔ P(A and B) = P(A) · P(B)

Check it in the email table. If spam and "free" were independent, P(spam and "free") would equal 0.30 × 0.155 = 0.0465, i.e. about 46 or 47 emails. The table has 120, much more, so the word and the label are strongly dependent, which is exactly why the word is useful for filtering. A feature that is independent of the label is useless for predicting it. (With real, noisy counts, "is 120 far enough from 46.5 to rule out chance?" is a question for a statistical test: the chi-squared test of independence in math6.)

Independence is sometimes surprising. Roll two dice. Is "the sum is 7" independent of "the first die shows 3"? P(sum is 7) = 6/36 = 1/6. Given the first die is 3, the sum is 7 only if the second die is 4, probability 1/6. Equal, so yes, independent: whatever the first die shows, exactly one value of the second die makes 7. But "the sum is 8" is not independent of the first die: P(sum is 8) = 5/36 ≈ 0.139, while P(sum is 8 | first is 3) = 1/6 ≈ 0.167, and P(sum is 8 | first is 1) = 0, since no second die can make 8 from 1. Independence is a property of a specific pair of events and a specific probability, not something you can judge from whether the events "feel related".

Do not confuse independent with mutually exclusive. Mutually exclusive events cannot both happen: P(A and B) = 0. If A has happened, B certainly has not, so learning A changes P(B) from something positive to zero: that is the strongest possible dependence. "Shows 1" and "shows 6" are mutually exclusive and dependent. Interviewers ask this exact question because candidates often mix the two up.

The multiplication rule for independent events is what makes repeated trials tractable. Flip a fair coin three times; the flips do not influence each other, so P(three heads) = ½ · ½ · ½ = 1/8. In math2 this becomes the binomial distribution. But the rule is only valid when independence really holds, and assuming it falsely is behind some of the worst probability errors on record, as the deep section shows.

6. The chain rule and probability trees

Rearranging the definition of conditional probability gives the multiplication rule, which works whether or not events are independent:

P(A and B) = P(A) · P(B | A)

"The probability of both is the probability of the first, times the probability of the second given the first." Draw two cards without putting the first back. P(first is an ace) = 4/52. Given that, 3 aces remain among 51 cards, so P(second is an ace | first was) = 3/51. P(two aces) = 4/52 × 3/51 = 12/2652 = 1/221 ≈ 0.0045. The draws are dependent (removing an ace makes the next ace less likely), and the conditional takes care of that.

Applied repeatedly, this becomes the chain rule of probability: P(A and B and C) = P(A) · P(B | A) · P(C | A and B), and so on for any number of events. Each factor conditions on everything before it. This is the identity a language model is built on: the probability of a sentence is P(word₁) × P(word₂ | word₁) × P(word₃ | word₁, word₂) × …, and the model's job is to predict each factor, the next-token distribution given everything so far.

A probability tree is the chain rule drawn: each level is one step, each branch is labelled with a conditional probability, and the probability of a path is the product along it. The frequency tree in the primer is a probability tree with every branch multiplied by 10,000. The paths at the right-hand edge are mutually exclusive and cover everything, so their probabilities add to 1, a quick sanity check worth doing every time.

7. The law of total probability

How often does anyone test positive? There are two ways to be positive: be sick and test positive, or be healthy and test positive. Those two paths cannot both happen to one person, so their probabilities add. Each path's probability comes from the multiplication rule:

P(+) = P(+ | sick) · P(sick) + P(+ | healthy) · P(healthy)

With the numbers: 0.99 × 0.01 + 0.01 × 0.99 = 0.0099 + 0.0099 = 0.0198, or 198 per 10,000, matching the tree. In general, if cases A₁, A₂, …, Aₙ do not overlap and between them cover every possibility (a partition), then for any event B:

P(B) = Σᵢ P(B | Aᵢ) · P(Aᵢ)

The Σ (Greek capital sigma) means "add up over every i". In words: an overall rate is a weighted average of the rates within each case, weighted by how common each case is. This is the law of total probability. It explains a common production puzzle: a model's per-class behaviour can stay identical while its overall numbers move, simply because the mix of cases (the weights) changed. If your model's false positive rate on healthy users is fixed at 1%, the number of false alarms per day still doubles when traffic doubles, and the share of alerts that are real depends entirely on the case mix.

8. Bayes' theorem

We now have every piece. The multiplication rule can be written with either event first:

P(A and B) = P(A | B) · P(B) = P(B | A) · P(A)

Divide both sides by P(B):

P(A | B) = P(B | A) · P(A) / P(B)

That is Bayes' theorem, named after Thomas Bayes, whose essay was published in 1763 after his death. It reverses a conditional probability. Each term has a standard name when A is a hypothesis and B is evidence:

  • P(A) is the prior: how plausible the hypothesis was before the evidence (the base rate).
  • P(B | A) is the likelihood: how probable the evidence is if the hypothesis is true.
  • P(B) is the evidence or normaliser: how probable the evidence is overall, usually computed with the law of total probability.
  • P(A | B) is the posterior: how plausible the hypothesis is after the evidence.

Now the disease test by formula. Let D be "has the disease" and + be "tests positive". The test's sensitivity is P(+ | D) = 0.99: it catches 99% of real cases (ML calls this recall). Its specificity is P(− | not D) = 0.99: it clears 99% of healthy people, so its false positive rate P(+ | not D) is 0.01. The prior is P(D) = 0.01.

P(D | +) = (0.99 × 0.01) / (0.99 × 0.01 + 0.01 × 0.99) = 0.0099 / 0.0198 = 0.50

The numerator is the true-positive path; the denominator is all positive paths. Bayes' theorem is the frequency tree in algebra.

Watch what the prior does. Keep the same test and change only how common the disease is:

prevalence P(D)true positives per 10,000false positives per 10,000P(D | +)
10%9909091.7%
1%999950.0%
0.1%9.999.99.0%
0.01%0.9999.991.0%

The test never changed. At 0.1% prevalence, 91% of positives are false. The false positives are almost constant (they come from the healthy majority, which is roughly everyone) while the true positives shrink with the disease. This is the general law: when the thing you are looking for is rare, even a very accurate detector produces mostly false alarms, unless its false positive rate is much smaller than the prevalence.

9. Bayes in odds form, and chaining evidence

There is a second way to write Bayes' theorem that is easier to do in your head and easier to chain. The odds of an event are P(A) / P(not A). A probability of 0.5 is odds of 1 (one to one, "even odds"); 0.2 is 0.2/0.8 = 0.25 (one to four); 0.01 is 1/99 (one to ninety-nine). Write Bayes' theorem for A and for not-A and divide one by the other; P(B) cancels:

P(A | B) / P(not A | B) = [P(B | A) / P(B | not A)] × [P(A) / P(not A)]

In words: posterior odds = likelihood ratio × prior odds. The likelihood ratio (LR) measures how much more likely the evidence is under A than under not-A, and it is a property of the test alone. For our test, LR of a positive = 0.99 / 0.01 = 99. The prior odds at 1% prevalence are 1/99. Multiply: posterior odds = 99 × 1/99 = 1, which is probability 0.5. Same answer, one multiplication.

The odds form shines when evidence arrives in pieces. Suppose the patient takes a second, different test with the same accuracy, and it is also positive. Today's posterior is tomorrow's prior: odds 1 × 99 = 99, a probability of 99/100 = 99%. But there is a condition, and it matters: multiplying likelihood ratios is only valid if the two tests are conditionally independent given disease status, meaning that among sick people the second test's result does not depend on the first's, and likewise among healthy people. Two tests that detect the same antibody will tend to produce false positives on the same healthy people, and then the second positive is worth much less than a factor of 99. The lab at the end of this lesson simulates the independent case: about 98.8% after two positives, against 99% by formula (the gap is random noise from a finite crowd of only about 200 double-positives).

Converting back from odds to probability: p = odds / (1 + odds). Odds of 99 → 99/100. Odds of 0.25 → 0.25/1.25 = 0.2.

10. The public case: doctors and the mammography problem

Psychologist Gerd Gigerenzer spent years testing whether physicians can do this calculation. In a 2007 continuing-education session he gave 160 gynaecologists the standard facts about mammography screening in their region: the probability a woman has breast cancer is 1% (prevalence); if she has it, the probability she tests positive is 90% (sensitivity); if she does not, the probability she tests positive anyway is 9% (false positive rate). He asked for the best estimate of the chance that a woman with a positive mammogram has cancer, choosing among 1%, 10%, 81% and 90%. The majority, 60%, chose 81% or 90%. Only about one in five picked the correct answer.

The correct answer, by the frequency tree: of 1,000 women, 10 have cancer and 9 of them test positive; of the 990 without cancer, 9% means about 89 test positive. So about 98 positives, 9 of them with cancer: 9/98 ≈ 9.2%, best answered by "10%". By Bayes' theorem: 0.9 × 0.01 / (0.9 × 0.01 + 0.09 × 0.99) = 0.009 / 0.0981 ≈ 0.092. By odds: LR = 0.9/0.09 = 10, prior odds 1/99, posterior odds 10/99 ≈ 0.101, probability ≈ 0.092.

Gigerenzer's finding was not just that experts fail. When he re-taught the same doctors to translate the conditional probabilities into natural frequencies ("think of 1,000 women…"), 87% of them reached the right answer. The mathematics did not change, only the representation. That is the reason this lesson leads with the crowd picture: when a probability question confuses you, rewrite it as counts of a concrete group, and the conditioning step becomes obvious. It is also the best way to explain a model's precision to a non-technical stakeholder.

11. Bayes inside machine learning: three everyday uses

Precision is a posterior. When you evaluate a classifier (ml7 covers the metrics in full), precision is the fraction of predicted positives that are really positive: P(actual positive | predicted positive). Recall is P(predicted positive | actual positive), the sensitivity. Precision is therefore Bayes' theorem applied to the model, with the class prevalence as the prior. That is why precision measured on a balanced test set is meaningless for a rare-event deployment, and why ml8 (class imbalance) insists on measuring at the real class ratio. Recall and the false positive rate do not depend on prevalence; precision does.

Naive Bayes is Bayes' theorem as a classifier. Suppose an email contains both "free" and "meeting". From labelled data: P(spam) = 0.3, P("free" | spam) = 0.40, P("meeting" | spam) = 0.05; P("free" | not spam) = 0.05, P("meeting" | not spam) = 0.30. Assume the words are conditionally independent given the label (the "naive" assumption), so the likelihood of seeing both is the product. Unnormalised scores: spam 0.3 × 0.40 × 0.05 = 0.006; not spam 0.7 × 0.05 × 0.30 = 0.0105. Normalise by their sum, 0.0165: P(spam | both words) = 0.006/0.0165 ≈ 0.36. The word "free" pushed towards spam (LR 8), "meeting" pushed away (LR 1/6), and the prior tilted towards not spam. Real implementations add logs of probabilities instead of multiplying, because the product of thousands of small numbers underflows to zero in floating point, and add a small count to every word (Laplace smoothing) so a word never seen in spam does not make P(spam) exactly zero. The ML coding problem mc7 builds this classifier from scratch.

Correcting for a changed class balance. Fraud is often 0.1% of data, so teams train on a resampled 50/50 dataset to give the model enough positive examples. A model trained that way learns probabilities for a world where fraud is common. Suppose it outputs 0.9 for a transaction. Its odds, 0.9/0.1 = 9, equal the likelihood ratio times the training prior odds, which were 1 (50/50). In production the prior odds are 0.01/0.99 ≈ 0.0101 at 1% fraud. Keep the likelihood ratio, swap the prior: posterior odds = 9 × 0.0101 ≈ 0.091, probability 0.091/1.091 ≈ 0.083. The "90%" transaction is really about 8%. This correction changes no ranking (every score is scaled by the same factor in odds space), so AUC (the area under the ROC curve, a ranking-quality metric covered in ml7) is untouched, but any threshold, any expected-cost calculation, and any probability shown to a person must use the corrected number. Calibration (ml15) is the broader version of this repair.

12. Counting tools you will need next

Equally likely outcomes reduce probability to counting, and two counts come up constantly in the next topic. The number of ways to arrange n distinct items in order is n! ("n factorial") = n × (n−1) × … × 1; 4 items can be ordered in 4! = 24 ways. The number of ways to choose k items from n when order does not matter is the binomial coefficient, written C(n, k) or "n choose k":

C(n, k) = n! / (k! · (n − k)!)

Choosing 2 of 5 people: 5! / (2! · 3!) = 120 / (2 × 6) = 10, which you can verify by listing the pairs. Why the division? There are 5 × 4 = 20 ordered ways to pick two people, and each unordered pair appears twice (AB and BA). The probability of exactly 2 heads in 5 fair flips is then C(5, 2) × (1/2)⁵ = 10/32 = 0.3125: count the arrangements, times the probability of each. That formula, generalised to an unfair coin, is the binomial distribution, the first distribution in math2.

Simpson's paradox: when pooling reverses the answer

In a 1986 study of kidney-stone treatments (Charig et al., BMJ), open surgery succeeded in 78% of cases (273 of 350) and a less invasive keyhole procedure in 83% (289 of 350). Keyhole looks better. Split by stone size and the picture flips: for small stones, open surgery 93% (81/87) vs keyhole 87% (234/270); for large stones, open surgery 73% (192/263) vs keyhole 69% (55/80). Open surgery wins in both groups and loses overall. The law of total probability explains it: each overall rate is a weighted average of the two size-specific rates, and the weights differ. Keyhole was mostly used on small, easy stones (270 of its 350 cases), open surgery mostly on large, hard ones (263 of 350). The pooled comparison is mostly a comparison of case mixes. Which number to trust depends on causal structure, not on arithmetic: if stone size influences both which treatment a patient gets and how well it works, compare within sizes. ML teams meet the same paradox when an aggregate metric improves while every segment regresses, because the traffic mix shifted towards an easier segment. Always break headline metrics down by the major segments before believing them.

The prosecutor's fallacy

Courtrooms routinely present P(evidence | innocent), a small number, and juries hear it as P(innocent | evidence). Suppose a DNA profile matches 1 in a million people and police search a database of 10 million. About 10 innocent people match by chance, plus the guilty person if they are in it, so a suspect found by such a trawl has roughly a 1 in 11 chance of being the source, not a 1 in a million chance of being innocent. In the case of Sally Clark, convicted in England in 1999 of murdering her two infant sons, an expert told the court the chance of two cot deaths in such a family was 1 in 73 million. That figure was wrong twice: it squared a 1 in 8,543 single-death rate as if two deaths in one family were independent, ignoring shared genetic and environmental causes; and it was then implicitly read as the probability of innocence, ignoring that double murder is also extraordinarily rare. The Royal Statistical Society issued a public statement criticising the use of the statistic, and the conviction was overturned in 2003. For ML engineers the lesson transfers directly: "this pattern is very unlikely under normal behaviour" is a likelihood, and turning it into "this user is probably a fraudster" needs the prior.

Conditional independence is the assumption that quietly does the work

Two variables can be dependent overall but independent once you know a third, and vice versa. Two tests are strongly correlated across a population (both tend to be positive in sick people), yet can be conditionally independent given disease status. Conversely, two independent causes become dependent once you condition on a shared effect: if a party is fun when either the music or the company is good, then among fun parties, learning the music was bad makes good company more likely. That effect, called explaining away or collider bias, is how selection creates spurious correlations in datasets: if you only collect data on users who complained, two independent problems will look negatively correlated. Naive Bayes, multiplying likelihood ratios, ensembles of models and many fairness analyses all depend on conditional-independence assumptions, so the senior habit is to state the assumption and ask what would break it.

Monty Hall, and why conditioning must include how you learned something

A prize is behind one of three doors. You pick door 1. The host, who knows where the prize is and always opens a different door without the prize, opens door 3. Should you switch to door 2? Yes: switching wins with probability 2/3. With the crowd picture: in 300 games, the prize is behind door 1 in 100 (switching loses) and behind door 2 or 3 in 200; in all of those 200 the host is forced to open the empty one, leaving the prize behind the door you switch to. The general lesson is that the event you condition on is not just "door 3 is empty" but "the host chose to open door 3", and the host's rule changes the likelihood. If the host had opened a random door that happened to be empty, switching would win only 1/2 of the time. In data work the analogue is the data-generating process: a click is evidence about relevance only once you account for the position the result was shown in, because users click top results regardless. Conditioning on an observation without modelling how it was produced gives biased answers.

What a probability means: frequencies and beliefs

The frequentist reading says a probability is the long-run fraction of times an event occurs in repeated trials; it applies naturally to dice, A/B test traffic and sampling. The Bayesian reading says a probability is a degree of belief, which can be assigned to one-off events and fixed unknowns, such as "the conversion rate of variant B is above A's". Both obey the same axioms, so Bayes' theorem is uncontroversial. The disagreement is about what you may put a probability on. A frequentist confidence interval (math6) is a statement about a procedure across repeated samples; a Bayesian credible interval (math5) is a statement about the parameter given this data and a prior. Interviewers like asking for the difference because it reveals whether a candidate knows what their p-values claim.

Priors in modern models

A classifier's predicted probabilities embed the class balance of its training data, as section 11 showed, and the same is true of a language model's next-token probabilities, which embed the frequency of phrasings in its training corpus. When the deployment distribution of labels shifts but the class-conditional distribution of inputs does not (called label shift), the fix is exactly Bayes: multiply each class's predicted probability by π_new(y)/π_train(y), where π denotes the class prior, and renormalise. If you do not know the new priors, they can be estimated from the model's own predictions on unlabelled production data by an iterative procedure (Saerens et al., 2002, an expectation–maximisation method). The harder case, where the inputs themselves change meaning, is covered under drift in ml14 and ml16.

Numerical practice: logs and normalisation

Because probabilities of long sequences are products of many numbers below 1, software works with log-probabilities: log P(A and B) = log P(A) + log P(B | A). A product of 1,000 factors of 0.01 is 10⁻²⁰⁰⁰, which underflows to 0 in 64-bit floating point (the smallest positive double is about 10⁻³⁰⁸), while the sum of logs is a perfectly ordinary −4,605. Normalising a posterior from log-scores uses the log-sum-exp trick: subtract the largest score before exponentiating so the largest term becomes e⁰ = 1 and nothing overflows. The same trick is inside every softmax implementation (dl1, mc10).

Alert fatigue is the base-rate fallacy at scale

The most common production failure linked to this lesson is a detector that is accurate and still useless. Security tooling, fraud rules, anomaly detection on metrics and content moderation all look for rare events. A detector with a 1% false positive rate scanning 10 million events a day raises 100,000 false alerts, whatever its recall. Teams experience this as alert fatigue: reviewers learn that most alerts are noise and start dismissing them, including the real ones. The symptom in metrics is a falling action rate per alert and a growing queue. The fix is to design for precision at the true prevalence: measure the false positive rate on a uniform sample of real traffic, multiply by volume to see the review load, and only then pick thresholds. If the arithmetic says 95% of alerts will be false, use the detector to rank work for humans rather than to block automatically.

Offline precision that does not survive launch

Offline evaluation sets are often enriched with positives so there are enough examples to measure recall. Precision on such a set is computed with the wrong prior. A moderation model evaluated on a set that is 30% harmful might show 90% precision; at 0.5% real prevalence the same false positive rate gives a small fraction of that. The production habit is to report recall and false positive rate from the enriched set (these do not depend on prevalence), and then compute expected precision at the deployed prevalence with Bayes' theorem, before launch. After launch, sample the flagged items and a uniform random slice of unflagged items for human labelling every week; the first gives realised precision, the second catches missed positives and tells you the real prevalence.

Prevalence drift moves precision even when the model is unchanged

Because precision is a posterior, it moves with the prior. A fraud model's precision can double during an attack wave and halve after it, with no model change. Dashboards that show only precision will tempt someone to retrain a model that is fine. Show prevalence (estimated from the labelled uniform sample) next to precision and recall, and interpret precision changes against it.

Cascades and correlated evidence

Many systems chain detectors: a cheap rule flags candidates, a model scores them, a human confirms. The odds-form arithmetic says each stage multiplies the odds by its likelihood ratio, but only if stages err independently. In practice the stages often look at the same signals (the model was trained on rule-flagged data; the reviewer sees the model's score), so their errors are correlated and the combined precision is lower than the product predicts. Measure the cascade end to end on labelled samples rather than multiplying per-stage numbers, and show reviewers the evidence without the model's score where you want an independent check.

Resampled training data and uncorrected probabilities

A recurring bug: a team downsamples negatives 100:1 to make training fast, the model's scores are then used directly as probabilities in an expected-value calculation (for example, expected loss = P(fraud) × amount), and the system over-blocks by a factor of about 100. The fix is the prior correction from section 11, applied in odds space, or a calibration step fitted on data at the true class ratio. Code review for any pipeline that resamples should ask where the correction happens.

The public case, in production terms

Gigerenzer's mammography work is a production lesson as much as a psychology one: the people consuming a probability were experts and still misread it, and the fix was a change in presentation, not in the test. When you ship a model whose scores reach a human (an analyst, a moderator, a clinician), present what the score means in natural frequencies at the real prevalence ("of 100 transactions with this score, about 8 turn out to be fraud") rather than as a bare probability or an "accuracy". That single choice prevents more downstream mistakes than most model improvements.

Goal: check Bayes' theorem by brute force. Simulate a million people, a disease and a test, and confirm that the fraction of positives who are sick matches the formula; then see the second-test effect and how the posterior collapses as the disease gets rarer. NumPy only (the gpt_env interpreter); save as math1.py.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N = 1_000_000

def screen(prevalence, sensitivity, specificity, n=N):
    sick = rng.random(n) < prevalence                    # who really has it
    u = rng.random(n)
    positive = np.where(sick, u < sensitivity, u > specificity)
    return sick, positive

# 1. The base-rate problem: 1% prevalence, 99% sensitivity, 99% specificity
sick, pos = screen(0.01, 0.99, 0.99)
print("people who test positive :", pos.sum())
print("P(sick | positive), sim  :", round(sick[pos].mean(), 4))
bayes = 0.99 * 0.01 / (0.99 * 0.01 + 0.01 * 0.99)
print("P(sick | positive), Bayes:", round(bayes, 4))

# 2. A second, independent test for everyone who tested positive
u2 = rng.random(N)
pos2 = np.where(sick, u2 < 0.99, u2 > 0.99)
both = pos & pos2
print("P(sick | two positives)  :", round(sick[both].mean(), 4))

# 3. Same test, rarer disease: the posterior collapses
for prev in [0.1, 0.01, 0.001]:
    s, p = screen(prev, 0.99, 0.99)
    print(f"prevalence {prev:<6} -> P(sick | +) = {s[p].mean():.3f}")

# 4. Independence check on two dice: is 'sum is 7' independent of 'first die is 3'?
d1, d2 = rng.integers(1, 7, N), rng.integers(1, 7, N)
for target in (7, 8):
    a = (d1 + d2) == target
    b = d1 == 3
    print(f"sum={target}: P(A)={a.mean():.4f}  P(A|first=3)={a[b].mean():.4f}")

What to look for (seeded output from the test run):

  1. About 19,700 of a million people test positive, close to the 19,800 the law of total probability predicts (0.0198 × 1,000,000).
  2. P(sick | positive) comes out at 0.501 from the simulation and 0.5 from Bayes: the formula and the counting agree.
  3. After a second independent positive, about 0.988 of people are sick, close to the 0.99 from odds × 99. The gap is noise: only about 200 people are positive twice, so the estimate moves by about a percentage point between seeds.
  4. At prevalence 0.1 the posterior is about 0.917; at 0.001 it is about 0.09. Same test, same code, only the prior changed.
  5. For two dice, P(sum is 7) and P(sum is 7 | first die is 3) are both close to 0.167: independent. For a sum of 8 they differ (0.139 vs 0.167): dependent.

Try next. Make the second test correlated with the first: for healthy people, reuse the first test's random number with probability 0.8 (u2 = np.where(rng.random(N) < 0.8, u, u2)) and watch P(sick | two positives) fall well below 0.99. That is the conditional-independence assumption failing. Then rerun the mammography numbers (prevalence 0.01, sensitivity 0.9, specificity 0.91) and confirm about 0.092.

Estimate: a "99% accurate" fraud model

A payments company processes 2 million transactions a day, of which 0.2% are fraudulent. A new model catches 95% of fraud and wrongly flags 1% of legitimate transactions. Every flag is reviewed by an analyst who handles 400 flags a day. (a) How many flags per day, and what fraction are real fraud? (b) How many analysts are needed? (c) The team proposes a second model, also 95% recall and 1% false positives, applied only to flagged transactions. What does the cascade give, and what assumption is that number relying on?

Answer

(a) Fraud: 0.002 × 2,000,000 = 4,000 a day; caught: 0.95 × 4,000 = 3,800. Legitimate: 1,996,000; false flags: 0.01 × 1,996,000 = 19,960. Total flags ≈ 23,760, of which 3,800 are fraud: precision = 3,800 / 23,760 ≈ 16%. In odds form: prior odds 0.002/0.998 ≈ 0.002, LR = 0.95/0.01 = 95, posterior odds ≈ 0.19, probability ≈ 0.16. Five of every six flags are false.

(b) 23,760 / 400 ≈ 59.4, so about 60 analysts, mostly reviewing legitimate payments. That number is what makes the business case, and it comes straight from the false-positive volume on the large legitimate branch.

(c) If the second model errs independently of the first given the true label, it keeps 95% of 3,800 = 3,610 frauds and 1% of 19,960 ≈ 200 false flags: precision ≈ 3,610 / 3,810 ≈ 95%, about 10 analysts, with recall dropping to 0.95 × 0.95 ≈ 90%. In odds: 0.19 × 95 ≈ 18, probability ≈ 0.95. But that relies on conditional independence: if both models were trained on the same features, the legitimate transactions that look odd to one probably look odd to the other, so many more than 1% of the 19,960 will be flagged again. Measure the cascade's precision on a labelled sample before cutting the analyst team, and if possible make the second stage use different signals (device, network, merchant history) so its errors are less correlated.
