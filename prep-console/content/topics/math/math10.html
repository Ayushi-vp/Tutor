Training a neural network means adjusting millions or billions of numbers so that a loss gets smaller. For each number the training algorithm needs to know one thing: if I nudge this weight up a little, does the loss go up or down, and how fast? That quantity is a derivative, and the list of all of them is the gradient. Every optimiser in use, from plain stochastic gradient descent to Adam, is a rule for turning gradients into weight updates (ml4, dl5, math11). Without gradients there is no training.

The hard part is getting the derivative of the loss with respect to a weight buried deep inside a network, when the loss depends on that weight only through a long chain of later computations. The tool is the chain rule from school calculus, applied systematically. In 1986 David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams published a four-page letter in Nature, "Learning representations by back-propagating errors", that laid this out for networks with hidden layers: a forward pass to compute every unit's output, then a backward pass that applies the chain rule layer by layer from the output down. Their small experiments, such as a network with just two hidden units that learned to detect mirror symmetry in six-bit inputs, showed that hidden units could learn useful internal features by gradient descent. Backpropagation (dl1) has been the engine of deep learning ever since, and frameworks such as PyTorch and JAX implement it automatically.

Automatic differentiation does not remove the need to understand the calculus. Engineers still write custom layers and loss functions, debug NaN gradients, choose activations whose derivatives do not vanish, reason about why training is slow in some directions, set learning rates relative to curvature, and answer interview questions such as "derive the gradient of logistic regression" or "backprop through this two-layer net by hand". Each of those is the material of this lesson.

The lesson assumes only school algebra. It defines the derivative as a slope and as the best linear approximation, collects the rules you will use, then moves to functions of many variables: partial derivatives, the gradient and why it points uphill, and the gradient of squared error, which reproduces math8's normal equations. It then develops the chain rule for one and many variables, the Jacobian matrix for vector-valued functions, and works the chain rule through a 2-layer network number by number. It ends with numerical gradient checking, a quick look at how automatic differentiation organises all of this, and the 1986 paper.

Imagine driving along a hilly road with an altimeter. Your height depends on how far along the road you are. The steepness of the road at your current position, metres of climb per metre travelled, is the derivative of height with respect to distance. Positive means uphill, negative downhill, zero means flat (the top of a hill, the bottom of a dip, or a flat stretch). A large value means steep. The derivative is a local fact: it describes the road right where you are, not a kilometre ahead.

Now step off the road onto an open hillside, where your height depends on two coordinates, east and north. The steepness depends on which way you face. Facing east, the slope is one number (the partial derivative with respect to east); facing north, another. Combine the two into an arrow, (east slope, north slope): that arrow is the gradient. It points in the direction that climbs most steeply, its length is how steep that climb is, and it is perpendicular to the contour line through your feet. To descend as fast as possible, walk against it. That is gradient descent: the loss is the height, the weights are the coordinates, and the hillside has millions of dimensions instead of two.

The third idea is the chain rule, which handles chains of cause and effect. Picture three gears: turning the first by one degree turns the second by three degrees, and turning the second by one degree turns the third by two. Turning the first by one degree turns the third by 3 × 2 = 6. Rates along a chain multiply. A neural network is a long chain: a weight affects a pre-activation, which affects an activation, which affects the next layer, and so on to the loss. The derivative of the loss with respect to the weight is the product of the local rates along the chain. Backpropagation is simply computing those products efficiently, starting from the loss end so that shared parts of the chain are computed once.

Where do these analogies break? Real loss surfaces are not smooth hills everywhere: ReLU activations put creases in them, and the gradient at a crease has to be chosen by convention. And a gradient is only a local guide: following it in big steps can overshoot a valley, which is why learning rates exist (math11). With those caveats, the three pictures (slope, uphill arrow, multiplying gears) are all the intuition this lesson needs. Everything else is learning to compute them.

A first taste of each, which the body works in detail. The derivative of x² at x = 3 is 6: nudge x from 3 to 3.01 and x² moves from 9 to about 9.06. The gradient of L(w₁, w₂) = w₁² + 4w₂² at (2, 1) is (4, 8): moving in w₂ changes the loss twice as fast as moving in w₁ here. And for the chain y = (3x + 1)², the inner rate is 3 and the outer rate is 2(3x + 1), so at x = 1 the derivative is 2 × 4 × 3 = 24.

1. The derivative as a limit of slopes

Take f(x) = x² and the point x = 3, where f(3) = 9. Pick a second point a distance h away and draw the straight line through both: a secant. Its slope is the change in height over the change in x:

(f(3 + h) − f(3)) / h = ((3 + h)² − 9) / h = (6h + h²)/h = 6 + h

With h = 2 the slope is 8, with h = 1 it is 7, with h = 0.5 it is 6.5, with h = 0.01 it is 6.01. As h shrinks towards zero the secant pivots towards a single line that just touches the curve at (3, 9), the tangent line, and its slope approaches 6. The derivative is that limiting slope:

f′(x) = lim as h → 0 of (f(x + h) − f(x)) / h

The notation "lim as h → 0" (a limit) means "the value this expression gets arbitrarily close to as h gets arbitrarily close to 0". Other common notations for the derivative are df/dx and, for a quantity y, dy/dx: "the change in y per change in x" for infinitesimally small changes.

2. The derivative as the best linear approximation

Rearranging the definition says that for small h,

f(x + h) ≈ f(x) + f′(x)·h

Near any point, a smooth function looks like its tangent line. For x² near 3: f(3.1) ≈ 9 + 6 × 0.1 = 9.6, and the exact value is 9.61. For √x near 4, the derivative is 1/(2√x) = 0.25, so √4.1 ≈ 2 + 0.25 × 0.1 = 2.025, against the exact 2.0248. This view of the derivative, "the coefficient of the best linear approximation", is the one that generalises to many variables and to matrices, and it is exactly what gradient descent uses: it assumes the loss near the current weights is approximately linear, takes a small step in the direction that linear approximation says goes down, and repeats. The error of the approximation grows with h², at a rate set by the second derivative f″, the derivative of the derivative, which measures how fast the slope itself changes: curvature. Math11 uses curvature to explain why the learning rate must be small enough.

Three activation functions deserve their derivatives memorised. The sigmoid σ(z) = 1/(1 + e^(−z)) squashes any number into (0, 1). ReLU, max(0, z), has derivative 1 for z > 0 and 0 for z < 0; at exactly z = 0 there is a corner, and software uses 0 (any value between 0 and 1 is a valid subgradient, a slope that can stand in for the derivative at a kink). These small facts explain a lot of deep-learning history: the sigmoid's slopes of at most 0.25 shrink gradients when many are multiplied together, and ReLU's slope of exactly 1 for active units does not (dl2, dl3).

4. The chain rule for one variable

Most functions in ML are compositions: one function applied to the output of another. If y = f(u) and u = g(x), then a small change in x changes u at rate g′(x), and that change in u changes y at rate f′(u). The rates multiply:

dy/dx = f′(g(x)) · g′(x), or in the memorable form dy/dx = (dy/du)·(du/dx)

The one subtlety is that the outer derivative is evaluated at the inner value g(x), not at x. Worked: y = (3x + 1)². Inner u = 3x + 1 with du/dx = 3; outer y = u² with dy/du = 2u. So dy/dx = 2(3x + 1) × 3 = 6(3x + 1); at x = 1 that is 24. Check by expanding: (3x + 1)² = 9x² + 6x + 1, whose derivative is 18x + 6, also 24 at x = 1.

The rule extends to longer chains by multiplying more factors: if y = f(g(h(x))), then dy/dx = f′(g(h(x)))·g′(h(x))·h′(x). Every link contributes its local rate, evaluated at the value flowing through that link. That is all of backpropagation in one sentence; the rest is bookkeeping for many variables.

5. Two derivations every ML engineer should be able to do

The sigmoid. σ(z) = (1 + e^(−z))⁻¹. Outer function u⁻¹ with derivative −u⁻²; inner u = 1 + e^(−z) with derivative −e^(−z) (chain rule again for e^(−z)). Multiply: σ′(z) = −(1 + e^(−z))⁻² × (−e^(−z)) = e^(−z)/(1 + e^(−z))². Split it as [1/(1 + e^(−z))] × [e^(−z)/(1 + e^(−z))] = σ(z) × (1 − σ(z)), since e^(−z)/(1 + e^(−z)) = 1 − 1/(1 + e^(−z)).

Logistic regression's log loss. For one example with label y ∈ {0, 1}, the model computes z = wx + b, p = σ(z), and the loss is L = −[y ln p + (1 − y) ln(1 − p)] (math5). Chain rule: dL/dw = (dL/dp)(dp/dz)(dz/dw). The three factors are dL/dp = −y/p + (1 − y)/(1 − p) = (p − y)/(p(1 − p)); dp/dz = p(1 − p); dz/dw = x. Multiply and the p(1 − p) cancels: dL/dw = (p − y)·x, and dL/db = p − y. The gradient is "prediction minus target, times input", the same tidy form that appears for softmax with cross-entropy and for linear regression with squared error. It is not a coincidence: each pairs a loss with the noise model it is the likelihood of (math5), and that pairing makes the gradients clean.

6. Partial derivatives and the gradient

A loss depends on many weights. For a function of several variables, a partial derivative is the ordinary derivative with respect to one variable while all the others are held fixed, written with a curly ∂. For f(x, y) = x²y + 3y:

  • ∂f/∂x: treat y as a constant, so x²y differentiates to 2xy and 3y to 0: ∂f/∂x = 2xy.
  • ∂f/∂y: treat x as a constant: ∂f/∂y = x² + 3.

At (1, 2): ∂f/∂x = 4 and ∂f/∂y = 4. The gradient collects all partial derivatives into a vector, written ∇f ("nabla f" or "grad f"): here ∇f(1, 2) = (4, 4).

The gradient has three properties that make it the right tool for optimisation. First, the linear approximation extends to many variables: f(x + h) ≈ f(x) + ∇f(x)·h, with a dot product (math8) in place of a product. Second, the rate of change in any unit direction u, the directional derivative, is ∇f·u = |∇f| cos θ, largest when u points along ∇f. So the gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent, and its length is that steepest rate; −∇f is the direction of steepest descent. Third, moving along a contour line (a level set, where f is constant) changes nothing, so ∇f·u = 0 for directions along the contour: the gradient is perpendicular to the contours.

Worked on a loss surface L(w₁, w₂) = w₁² + 4w₂², a bowl four times steeper in w₂ than in w₁. ∇L = (2w₁, 8w₂). At (2, 1): ∇L = (4, 8), length √80 ≈ 8.94. A gradient-descent step with learning rate 0.1 moves to (2, 1) − 0.1 × (4, 8) = (1.6, 0.2), and the loss drops from 4 + 4 = 8 to 2.56 + 0.16 = 2.72. Notice the step mostly reduced w₂, the steep direction, and moved straight towards the w₁ axis rather than towards the minimum at the origin.

That figure contains the central difficulty of optimisation, which math11 takes up: in a stretched bowl the steepest-descent direction points across the valley, so gradient descent zig-zags, and the learning rate is limited by the steepest direction while progress is limited by the flattest.

7. The gradient of squared error, and the normal equations again

For a linear model with design matrix X (n examples, d features), weights w and targets y, the mean squared error is L(w) = (1/n)Σᵢ(xᵢ·w − yᵢ)². Differentiate with respect to one weight wⱼ: each term's inner function xᵢ·w − yᵢ has partial derivative xᵢⱼ (the j-th feature of example i), and the outer square contributes 2 × (xᵢ·w − yᵢ). So ∂L/∂wⱼ = (2/n)Σᵢ(xᵢ·w − yᵢ)xᵢⱼ. Stacking all j into a vector gives the compact matrix form

∇L(w) = (2/n) Xᵀ(Xw − y)

Read it as "the residuals Xw − y, sent back through Xᵀ": each weight's gradient is the correlation between its feature and the current errors. Setting the gradient to zero gives XᵀXw = Xᵀy, exactly math8's normal equations, now derived from calculus rather than geometry. The two views agree because minimising the squared length of the residual is the same as making the residual perpendicular to the columns. For the 5-row table, at w = (129, 13.5) the errors Xw − y are (6, −7, 10, −23, 14) (math8's residuals with the sign flipped); they sum to zero and have zero dot product with x, so the gradient is (0, 0): the bottom of the bowl. At w = (129, 13) the errors become (5, −9, 7, −27, 9), and the gradient's slope component is (2/5)(2 × 5 − 4 × 9 + 6 × 7 − 8 × 27 + 10 × 9) = (2/5)(−110) = −44. It is negative, so the update w₁ ← w₁ − η × (−44) increases w₁ back towards 13.5.

Useful general patterns, each derivable the same way: the gradient of a·w is a; of wᵀw = |w|² is 2w (so L2 regularisation, a penalty λ|w|² on the squared weights, adds 2λw to the gradient, the "weight decay" term); of wᵀAw is (A + Aᵀ)w, which is 2Aw for symmetric A. When in doubt, check shapes: the gradient of a scalar with respect to a d-vector must be a d-vector.

8. The multivariable chain rule

In a computation, one variable can influence the output along several paths. Suppose z = f(u, v), where u and v both depend on x. A small change in x changes u, which changes z, and also changes v, which changes z. The total effect is the sum of the effects along each path:

dz/dx = (∂z/∂u)(du/dx) + (∂z/∂v)(dv/dx)

Worked: z = u·v with u = x² and v = 3x. Paths: ∂z/∂u = v and du/dx = 2x; ∂z/∂v = u and dv/dx = 3. So dz/dx = 3x·2x + x²·3 = 9x². Check: z = 3x³, whose derivative is 9x². In a neural network, a unit's output feeds every unit in the next layer, so the gradient with respect to that output is a sum over all the units it feeds. That sum is equation (7) of the 1986 paper, ∂E/∂yᵢ = Σⱼ (∂E/∂xⱼ)·wⱼᵢ, and it is why autodiff frameworks add (accumulate) gradients whenever a value is used more than once.

9. Jacobians: derivatives of vector functions

A layer maps a vector to a vector. For a function y = f(x) from n inputs to m outputs, all the partial derivatives ∂yᵢ/∂xⱼ form an m × n matrix, the Jacobian J. It is the many-dimensional version of the derivative: the best linear approximation, f(x + h) ≈ f(x) + Jh. Three Jacobians cover most of a neural network:

  • Linear layer y = Wx + b: since yᵢ = Σⱼ Wᵢⱼxⱼ + bᵢ, ∂yᵢ/∂xⱼ = Wᵢⱼ. The Jacobian is W itself.
  • Element-wise activation h = φ(z) applied to each component: hᵢ depends only on zᵢ, so the Jacobian is diagonal with φ′(zᵢ) on the diagonal. For ReLU, a diagonal of 1s (active units) and 0s (inactive).
  • Softmax pᵢ = e^(zᵢ)/Σₖe^(zₖ): every output depends on every input, and the Jacobian is diag(p) − ppᵀ, so ∂pᵢ/∂zᵢ = pᵢ(1 − pᵢ) and ∂pᵢ/∂zⱼ = −pᵢpⱼ for i ≠ j. Raising one logit raises its own probability and lowers all the others.

The chain rule for compositions of vector functions is matrix multiplication of Jacobians: if y = f(g(x)), then J_y,x = J_f · J_g, with shapes (m × k)(k × n). The gradient of a scalar loss with respect to a layer's input is a row vector, the incoming gradient times the Jacobian. Backpropagation never forms the full Jacobian matrices (a softmax over a 100,000-token vocabulary would have 10¹⁰ entries); it computes these vector–Jacobian products directly. Through a linear layer the product with W is Wᵀ times the incoming gradient (as a column), and the gradient with respect to W is the outer product of the incoming gradient with the input. Through softmax followed by cross-entropy, the product collapses to p − y.

10. The chain rule through a 2-layer network, every number

A tiny network with input x = (1.0, 0.5), a hidden layer of two ReLU units, and one linear output, trained with squared error L = ½(ŷ − y)² against target y = 2. The weights are W₁ = [[1, −0.5], [0.5, 1]], b₁ = (0, 0), w₂ = (2, −1), b₂ = 0.5.

Forward pass (compute and keep every intermediate value):

  1. Pre-activations: pre = W₁x + b₁ = (1 × 1 − 0.5 × 0.5, 0.5 × 1 + 1 × 0.5) = (0.75, 1.0).
  2. Hidden activations: h = ReLU(pre) = (0.75, 1.0); both units are on.
  3. Output: ŷ = w₂·h + b₂ = 2 × 0.75 − 1 × 1.0 + 0.5 = 1.0.
  4. Loss: L = ½(1.0 − 2)² = 0.5.

Backward pass (start from the loss and multiply by one local derivative per step):

  1. dL/dŷ = ŷ − y = −1.0.
  2. Output weights: ŷ = w₂·h + b₂, so ∂ŷ/∂w₂ = h and ∂ŷ/∂b₂ = 1. Hence dL/dw₂ = −1.0 × (0.75, 1.0) = (−0.75, −1.0) and dL/db₂ = −1.0.
  3. Into the hidden layer: ∂ŷ/∂h = w₂, so dL/dh = −1.0 × (2, −1) = (−2.0, 1.0).
  4. Through ReLU: both pre-activations are positive, slope 1, so dL/dpre = (−2.0, 1.0). (A unit that was off would get 0 here and pass nothing further.)
  5. First-layer weights: preᵢ = Σⱼ W₁ᵢⱼxⱼ, so ∂preᵢ/∂W₁ᵢⱼ = xⱼ, and dL/dW₁ is the outer product of dL/dpre with x: [[−2 × 1, −2 × 0.5], [1 × 1, 1 × 0.5]] = [[−2, −1], [1, 0.5]]. And dL/db₁ = (−2, 1).

Sanity checks. The output was too low (1.0 versus 2), so the update −η·gradient should raise ŷ. It does: w₂ moves to (2.075, −0.9) with learning rate 0.1, increasing the weight on the first unit and reducing the negative weight on the second; W₁ moves to [[1.2, −0.4], [0.4, 0.95]], increasing the first unit's activation (it has a positive output weight) and decreasing the second's (negative output weight). After this one step the loss is about 0.077, down from 0.5. The lab verifies every entry of dL/dW₁ against central differences, with a largest relative error of about 7 × 10⁻¹². Dl1 develops this procedure in general and in matrix form, and mc11 implements it for a full network.

11. Numerical derivatives and gradient checking

The definition of the derivative suggests computing it numerically: pick a small h and evaluate the forward difference (f(x + h) − f(x))/h. Its error is roughly proportional to h (the curvature term from section 2). The central difference (f(x + h) − f(x − h))/(2h) cancels that term, so its error is proportional to h². For f(x) = x³ at x = 2 (true derivative 12), the lab measures: with h = 10⁻³ the forward difference is off by 6 × 10⁻³ and the central difference by 10⁻⁶; with h = 10⁻⁵, by 6 × 10⁻⁵ and 2 × 10⁻¹⁰. But making h even smaller backfires: at h = 10⁻¹¹ both are off by about 10⁻⁶, because f(x + h) and f(x − h) agree in almost all of their 16 significant digits and the subtraction keeps mostly rounding error (math3's catastrophic cancellation). The sweet spot in float64 is h around 10⁻⁵ for central differences.

Numerical derivatives are far too slow to train with, needing two function evaluations per parameter, but they are the standard way to test analytic gradients. A gradient check compares the two using relative error |a − n|/max(|a|, |n|), because absolute differences mean different things for gradients of size 1 and 10⁻⁵. The CS231n course notes suggest that a relative error above 10⁻² probably means a bug, between 10⁻² and 10⁻⁴ is uncomfortable, below 10⁻⁴ is acceptable for functions with kinks such as ReLU, and below 10⁻⁷ is what you should see for smooth functions. Practical rules: use float64, check a few random parameters on a small input, avoid inputs exactly at kinks, and switch off randomness such as dropout during the check. The lab plants a classic bug, forgetting the ReLU mask in the backward pass: with an input that switches the first hidden unit off, the numerical derivative of W₁[0, 0] is 0 and the buggy analytic one is 3.6.

12. Automatic differentiation, briefly

Frameworks compute gradients by automatic differentiation: they record the elementary operations a program performs (multiply, add, exp, matmul) as a computation graph, each with a known local derivative, and apply the chain rule mechanically. This is neither symbolic differentiation (which manipulates formulas and can blow up in size) nor numerical differentiation (which is approximate); it gives exact derivatives, to floating-point precision, of the program as executed. There are two orders in which to multiply the chain. Forward mode carries derivatives from inputs to outputs alongside the forward computation, giving the derivatives of all outputs with respect to one input per pass. Reverse mode runs the computation forward, stores intermediate values, then carries derivatives from the output back to all inputs in one backward pass. For a scalar loss and millions of parameters, reverse mode costs a small constant multiple of the forward pass (typically two to three times) for the entire gradient, while forward mode would need one pass per parameter. Reverse mode applied to neural networks is backpropagation; the price is memory, since every intermediate value must be kept for the backward pass, which is why activation memory dominates training memory (dl1 covers checkpointing and how PyTorch's autograd records the graph).

Taylor series and the Hessian

The linear approximation is the first two terms of a Taylor series: f(x + h) = f(x) + f′(x)h + ½f″(x)h² + …, each further term using a higher derivative and a higher power of h. In many variables the quadratic term is ½hᵀHh, where H is the Hessian, the d × d matrix of second partial derivatives ∂²f/∂wᵢ∂wⱼ. For L = w₁² + 4w₂², H = [[2, 0], [0, 8]]: curvature 2 along w₁ and 8 along w₂. The Hessian is symmetric (mixed partial derivatives agree for smooth functions), so math9's tools apply: its eigenvectors are the principal curvature directions and its eigenvalues the curvatures. At a minimum it is positive semi-definite; a saddle point has both positive and negative eigenvalues. Newton's method uses the quadratic approximation directly, stepping by −H⁻¹∇f, which jumps to the minimum of a quadratic in one step but needs the d × d Hessian, impossible for large models; math11 discusses the practical compromises.

Matrix calculus without tears

Gradients with respect to matrices follow from the same rules if you track shapes. For a linear layer Y = XWᵀ with a batch X (B × n) and weights W (m × n), and an incoming gradient G = ∂L/∂Y (B × m), the gradients are ∂L/∂W = GᵀX (m × n, the same shape as W) and ∂L/∂X = GW (B × n, the same shape as X). The shapes force the order: there is only one way to multiply G and X to get an m × n result. Two habits prevent most errors: the gradient of a scalar with respect to any tensor has the same shape as that tensor, and every gradient with respect to a weight is "incoming gradient" combined with "the input that weight multiplied". Identities such as ∇_w |Xw − y|² = 2Xᵀ(Xw − y) and ∇_w wᵀAw = (A + Aᵀ)w can all be checked by writing one component as a sum and differentiating.

Vanishing and exploding gradients as products of Jacobians

Backpropagating through L layers multiplies L Jacobians. The gradient's size is governed by how much each Jacobian stretches vectors, its singular values (math9). With sigmoid activations each diagonal activation Jacobian has entries at most 0.25, so over 10 layers the gradient can shrink by a factor of up to 0.25¹⁰ ≈ 10⁻⁶ from activations alone. With weight matrices whose largest singular value is above 1, products can grow exponentially instead. The remedies all aim to keep the product of Jacobians near norm 1: initialisation scaled to the layer width (Glorot, He), activations with slope 1 in their active region (ReLU and relatives), normalisation layers that re-centre and re-scale activations (dl4), residual connections whose Jacobian is the identity plus a small correction, and gated recurrent units that let gradients pass through time with a factor near 1 (dl7). Dl3 develops this in depth.

Jacobian–vector and vector–Jacobian products

Reverse mode computes vᵀJ (a vector–Jacobian product) per backward pass; forward mode computes Jv (a Jacobian–vector product) per forward pass. Combining them gives Hessian–vector products Hv at the cost of a couple of passes without forming H, which is how curvature is probed in large models: the largest Hessian eigenvalue by power iteration on Hv (math9), sharpness measures, and some second-order optimisers. Jacobian–vector products also give the sensitivity of a whole output vector to one input direction, used in adversarial-robustness analysis and in computing how much each input token influences the logits.

Differentiating through non-smooth and discrete operations

Many useful operations have zero or undefined derivatives: rounding (quantisation), argmax, sampling a token, sorting, thresholds. Their gradients are zero almost everywhere, which would stop learning. Common workarounds each replace the true derivative with a useful fiction. The straight-through estimator treats rounding as the identity in the backward pass, which is how quantisation-aware training works (lm12). Softmax with a temperature replaces argmax with a smooth approximation. The reparameterisation trick writes a sample as a deterministic function of parameters and fixed noise, so gradients flow through it (the variational autoencoder, VAE, dl10). Score-function (REINFORCE) estimators handle discrete samples by differentiating the log-probability instead, which is how policy-gradient methods for language-model reinforcement learning are built (rs3). Each comes with bias or variance trade-offs, and recognising which fiction a method uses explains many of its quirks.

Implicit differentiation

Sometimes the quantity you want to differentiate is defined as the solution of an equation, such as the fixed point of an iteration or the optimum of an inner optimisation, rather than by an explicit formula. If F(w, θ) = 0 defines w as a function of θ, differentiating the equation gives ∂F/∂w · dw/dθ + ∂F/∂θ = 0, so dw/dθ = −(∂F/∂w)⁻¹ ∂F/∂θ, without differentiating through every iteration of the solver. This is the basis of hyperparameter gradients, deep equilibrium models and differentiable optimisation layers, and it is the same step as the implicit function theorem from calculus.

NaN and infinite gradients

The most common gradient failure in production training is a NaN (not-a-number) that appears in one step and then contaminates every weight. Its causes are derivatives that blow up at specific points: log(0) in a hand-written loss (derivative 1/x at x = 0), √x at 0 (derivative 1/(2√x)), division by a quantity that can be zero (normalising a zero vector), exp of a large number overflowing, and the softmax computed without subtracting the maximum. Defences: use fused, numerically stable library functions (log_softmax, logsumexp, cross-entropy with logits), add small epsilons where a denominator or square root can reach zero, enable anomaly detection in debugging runs to find the first operation producing NaN, and log the global gradient norm every step so spikes are visible before the NaN.

Gradient norms, clipping and loss scaling

The global L2 norm of the gradient is one of the most informative training metrics. Sudden spikes often precede loss spikes and divergence; a steady decline to near zero with a high loss suggests vanishing gradients or dead units. Gradient clipping rescales the whole gradient whenever its norm exceeds a threshold, preserving its direction while limiting the step, and is standard for transformers and recurrent networks. In mixed-precision training, gradients of small magnitude can underflow to zero in float16, whose smallest normal value is about 6 × 10⁻⁵; loss scaling multiplies the loss by a large factor before the backward pass and divides the gradients afterwards, with automatic adjustment when overflow is detected. Bfloat16, with float32's exponent range, largely avoids the problem, which is one reason it became the default for large-model training.

Custom operations need gradient checks

When teams write custom GPU kernels (in CUDA, NVIDIA's GPU programming language), fused attention variants, custom losses or hand-derived backward functions for speed, a gradient check on small float64 inputs should be part of the unit tests: frameworks provide utilities for exactly this (for example, PyTorch's gradcheck). Bugs in backward passes are insidious because training often still "works" with a wrong gradient, just worse, and the symptom (a model that underperforms by a few points) is easy to attribute to something else. A useful companion test is to confirm that the loss decreases on a single batch when overfitting it deliberately.

Dead and saturated units

Units whose activation's derivative is zero for every input stop learning. ReLU units pushed into the negative region for all inputs by a large update ("dead ReLUs"), sigmoid or tanh units saturated at their extremes, and attention softmaxes saturated onto one token all have near-zero local derivatives, so no gradient flows through them. Monitoring the fraction of units that are active (or the distribution of pre-activations) per layer catches this; remedies include lower learning rates, leaky or smooth variants of ReLU (dl2), normalisation layers, and careful initialisation.

The public case, in production terms

The 1986 paper's experiments were tiny, a six-input symmetry detector and a network trained on 100 of 104 family-relationship triples, but their engineering lessons still hold. They trained with full-batch gradients accumulated over all examples, used a momentum term (α = 0.9) to speed progress, started from small random weights to break symmetry between hidden units, and, for interpretability, added a small weight decay. Each of those choices maps onto a modern default: mini-batch accumulation, momentum and Adam, careful initialisation, and weight decay. The paper also noted that the method "very rarely gets stuck in poor local minima" when the network has some spare capacity, an observation that large-model practice has borne out on a scale its authors could not have tested.

Goal: see why central differences beat forward differences and why h cannot be too small, reproduce the 2-layer backward pass from the lesson, verify it with a gradient check, take one gradient step, and catch a planted backward-pass bug. NumPy only (gpt_env); save as math10.py.

import numpy as np

# 1. Forward vs central differences for f(x) = x^3 at x = 2 (true derivative 12)
f = lambda x: x ** 3
for h in (1e-1, 1e-3, 1e-5, 1e-8, 1e-11):
    fwd = (f(2 + h) - f(2)) / h
    ctr = (f(2 + h) - f(2 - h)) / (2 * h)
    print(f"h={h:.0e}: forward error {abs(fwd - 12):.1e}   central error {abs(ctr - 12):.1e}")

# 2. The 2-layer network from the lesson, forward and backward by hand
x = np.array([1.0, 0.5]); y = 2.0
W1 = np.array([[1.0, -0.5], [0.5, 1.0]]); b1 = np.zeros(2)
w2 = np.array([2.0, -1.0]); b2 = 0.5

def loss(W1, b1, w2, b2):
    pre = W1 @ x + b1
    h = np.maximum(pre, 0)                     # ReLU
    yhat = w2 @ h + b2
    return 0.5 * (yhat - y) ** 2, pre, h, yhat

L, pre, h, yhat = loss(W1, b1, w2, b2)
print("pre", pre, " h", h, " yhat", yhat, " loss", L)
dyhat = yhat - y                               # dL/dyhat
dw2, db2 = dyhat * h, dyhat
dh = dyhat * w2                                # dL/dh
dpre = dh * (pre > 0)                          # through ReLU
dW1, db1 = np.outer(dpre, x), dpre
print("dL/dw2", dw2, " dL/db2", db2, "\ndL/dW1\n", dW1, "\ndL/db1", db1)

# 3. Gradient check: central differences on every entry of W1
eps = 1e-5
num = np.zeros_like(W1)
for i in range(2):
    for j in range(2):
        Wp, Wm = W1.copy(), W1.copy()
        Wp[i, j] += eps; Wm[i, j] -= eps
        num[i, j] = (loss(Wp, b1, w2, b2)[0] - loss(Wm, b1, w2, b2)[0]) / (2 * eps)
rel = np.abs(num - dW1) / np.maximum(np.abs(num), np.abs(dW1))
print("max relative error:", rel.max())

# 4. One gradient step with learning rate 0.1 lowers the loss
lr = 0.1
L_new = loss(W1 - lr * dW1, b1 - lr * db1, w2 - lr * dw2, b2 - lr * db2)[0]
print(f"loss before {L:.4f}, after one step {L_new:.4f}")

# 5. A planted bug: forgetting the ReLU mask is caught by the check when a unit is off
x = np.array([-1.0, 0.8])                      # makes the first hidden unit negative
L, pre, h, yhat = loss(W1, b1, w2, b2)
buggy = np.outer((yhat - y) * w2, x)           # no (pre > 0) mask
Wp, Wm = W1.copy(), W1.copy(); Wp[0, 0] += eps; Wm[0, 0] -= eps
print("pre", pre, "| numeric dW1[0,0]", round((loss(Wp, b1, w2, b2)[0] - loss(Wm, b1, w2, b2)[0]) / (2 * eps), 6),
      "| buggy analytic", buggy[0, 0])

  1. For x³ at 2: forward-difference error falls like h (0.61, 6 × 10⁻³, 6 × 10⁻⁵), central-difference error like h² (10⁻², 10⁻⁶, 2 × 10⁻¹⁰). At h = 10⁻⁸ and 10⁻¹¹ both get worse again because of rounding.
  2. Forward pass: pre = (0.75, 1.0), h = (0.75, 1.0), ŷ = 1.0, loss 0.5, exactly as worked by hand.

Derive: the gradient of a custom loss

A ranking team wants a pairwise loss: for a query, a relevant document gets score s⁺ = w·x⁺ and an irrelevant one s⁻ = w·x⁻, and the loss is L = ln(1 + e^(−(s⁺ − s⁻))), which is small when s⁺ is well above s⁻. (a) Derive ∇_w L. (b) Evaluate it for w = (1, 0), x⁺ = (1, 2), x⁻ = (2, 1). (c) Explain what the gradient does in words and name one numerical hazard and its fix.

Answer

(a) Let d = s⁺ − s⁻ = w·(x⁺ − x⁻). Then L = ln(1 + e^(−d)). Outer: dL/dd = −e^(−d)/(1 + e^(−d)) = −(1 − σ(d)) = −σ(−d), using section 5's identity. Inner: ∇_w d = x⁺ − x⁻ (the gradient of a dot product). Chain rule: ∇_w L = −σ(−d)·(x⁺ − x⁻).

(b) x⁺ − x⁻ = (−1, 1), so d = (1, 0)·(−1, 1) = −1: the irrelevant document currently scores higher. σ(−d) = σ(1) ≈ 0.731. ∇L = −0.731 × (−1, 1) = (0.731, −0.731). A gradient step w ← w − η∇L moves w towards (−1, 1) = x⁺ − x⁻, raising the relevant document's score relative to the irrelevant one. The loss is ln(1 + e¹) ≈ 1.313.

(c) The gradient pushes the score difference up along the direction that separates the two documents, with a weight σ(−d) that is near 1 when the pair is badly ordered and near 0 when it is already well ordered, so correctly ranked pairs stop contributing. The hazard is computing ln(1 + e^(−d)) directly: for d = −800, e^(800) overflows to infinity. Use a stable softplus, softplus(−d) = max(−d, 0) + ln(1 + e^(−|d|)), and compute σ from logits, as library functions do. This loss is the pairwise logistic loss behind RankNet-style learning to rank, and its gradient has the same "prediction minus target" shape as logistic regression.
