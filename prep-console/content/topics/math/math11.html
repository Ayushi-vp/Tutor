Nearly every model in machine learning is defined as the solution of an optimisation problem: find the weights that make a loss as small as possible. Linear regression minimises squared error, logistic regression minimises log loss, a neural network minimises cross-entropy over billions of examples, a support vector machine maximises a margin subject to constraints, and a recommendation model fits its factors under a penalty. Training is optimisation, and much of what goes wrong in training (a loss that explodes, a loss that crawls, a run that is fine at one learning rate and diverges at a slightly larger one) is an optimisation phenomenon with a mathematical explanation.

Three questions organise the subject. First, when is a problem easy? Some losses are shaped like a single bowl, and any downhill method finds the bottom; others have many dips, flat plateaus and saddle-shaped passes. The property that separates them is convexity. Second, why does gradient descent work at all, and how fast? It is not obvious that repeatedly stepping downhill should converge, or why a learning rate of 0.2 works while 0.26 explodes. The answer lies in curvature, and the ratio of the steepest to the flattest curvature, the condition number, determines how many steps you need. Third, what if the solution must satisfy constraints: probabilities that sum to one, weights below a size limit, a budget, a fairness condition? Lagrange multipliers turn a constrained problem into an unconstrained one and, as a bonus, tell you what each constraint costs.

The classical theory has a famous tension with deep learning, and it makes a good case study. Convex optimisation predicts that gradient descent is unstable once the sharpest curvature of the loss exceeds 2 divided by the learning rate. In 2021 Jeremy Cohen and colleagues measured that curvature throughout neural-network training and found that it climbs until it reaches exactly that threshold and then hovers just above it, where the classical theory says training should blow up, and yet the loss keeps decreasing. Knowing the classical picture is what lets you see why that is surprising, and what it implies for learning-rate choices.

This lesson builds on math10's gradients and math9's eigenvalues. It defines convex sets and functions and the tests for convexity, then explains gradient descent: the step-size limit on a quadratic, the best step and the convergence rate, the descent lemma that guarantees progress on any smooth function, why ill-conditioning makes training crawl, and how momentum and stochastic gradients change the picture. It surveys non-convex landscapes and saddle points, then turns to constraints: Lagrange multipliers worked by hand, the maximum-likelihood estimate of a categorical distribution as a constrained problem, inequality constraints and the KKT conditions, and regularisation as a constraint. Ml4 and dl5 cover the practical optimisers; this is the mathematics underneath them.

Picture a hiker in thick fog trying to reach the lowest point of a landscape. She cannot see the valley; she can only feel the slope under her feet. The obvious strategy is to take a step in the steepest downhill direction, feel again, and repeat. That is gradient descent: the landscape is the loss, her position is the weights, the slope she feels is the gradient (math10), and her stride length is the learning rate.

Whether the strategy works depends on the landscape. If it is a single bowl, every downhill step brings her closer to the bottom, and she cannot end up anywhere else. Such landscapes are convex, and they are the friendly case: linear and logistic regression live there. If the landscape has several valleys, she reaches the bottom of whichever valley she started in, which may not be the deepest. If it has a mountain pass (a saddle point, downhill in one direction and uphill in another), the slope at the exact centre is flat, and she might stand there until a gust (noise) pushes her off. Deep networks have landscapes like this, enormously high-dimensional, and a surprising amount of their success is that the hiker rarely ends up anywhere bad.

Stride length is the second issue. On a gentle bowl, a long stride is fine. In a narrow ravine, a long stride across the steep walls overshoots to the opposite wall, higher than where she started, and each stride makes things worse: the walk diverges. A short stride is safe but crawls along the ravine's gentle floor. The steepest wall sets the maximum safe stride, and the gentlest slope sets how long the walk takes; the ratio between them, the condition number, is the single number that best predicts how hard the problem is for gradient descent. Momentum is the hiker letting her body carry some speed from step to step: the sideways bounces across the ravine cancel, and the steady downhill progress along its floor builds up.

Constraints are fences. Suppose the hiker must stay on a straight path that crosses the landscape. She walks along the path while the ground keeps falling, and stops where the path just grazes a contour line, touching it without crossing. At that point the contour and the path are tangent, which means the uphill direction of the landscape is perpendicular to the path, parallel to the fence's own direction of "away from the path". That tangency condition, gradient of the landscape parallel to gradient of the constraint, is the method of Lagrange multipliers, and the factor between the two gradients tells you how much lower she could go if the fence moved one unit.

Where the analogy breaks: real training does not feel the exact slope (each mini-batch gives a noisy estimate), the landscape has millions of dimensions instead of two, and, as the edge-of-stability work showed, neural-network training often walks with strides that the ravine picture says should be unsafe and still gets down. The body makes each piece precise with numbers, starting with what "bowl-shaped" means.

1. Convex sets and convex functions

A set is convex if it contains the straight segment between any two of its points: a disc, a square, a half-plane, the set of weight vectors with |w|₂ ≤ 1. A crescent or a ring is not convex. Feasible sets in optimisation (the points that satisfy all constraints) are often convex, which is what makes many constrained problems tractable.

A function f is convex if, for any two points a and b and any t between 0 and 1,

f(t·a + (1 − t)·b) ≤ t·f(a) + (1 − t)·f(b)

In words: the function at any point between a and b is no higher than the straight chord joining (a, f(a)) and (b, f(b)). Graphically, every chord lies on or above the graph. x², |x|, eˣ, −ln x and every linear function are convex; x³, sin x and x⁴ − 3x² are not. Convex functions combine well: sums of convex functions are convex, a convex function of a linear function (such as |Xw − y|²) is convex, and the maximum of convex functions is convex. That is why the training objectives of linear regression, logistic regression, linear support vector machines and their L1- and L2-regularised versions are all convex.

The payoff is the key theorem of the subject: for a convex function, every local minimum is a global minimum, and any point where the gradient is zero is a global minimum. The proof is the chord itself: if some point b were lower than a local minimum a, the chord from a to b would sit below f(a) near a, and convexity says the function lies under that chord, so there would be lower points arbitrarily close to a, contradicting that a is a local minimum. For practice this means that on a convex problem, any method that drives the gradient to zero has found the answer, and different runs, initialisations or optimisers will agree (up to ties when the minimum is not unique).

2. Testing for convexity and for minima: second derivatives and the Hessian

For a function of one variable with a second derivative, convexity is simple to check: f is convex exactly when f″(x) ≥ 0 everywhere, meaning its slope never decreases. x² has f″ = 2; eˣ has f″ = eˣ > 0; x⁴ − 3x² has f″ = 12x² − 6, negative near zero, so it is not convex. The same second derivative classifies critical points (where f′ = 0): f″ > 0 means a local minimum, f″ < 0 a local maximum, and f″ = 0 is inconclusive.

For many variables the second derivatives form the Hessian H (math10), and the eigenvalues of this symmetric matrix (math9) are the curvatures along its principal directions. f is convex exactly when H is positive semi-definite everywhere (all eigenvalues ≥ 0). At a critical point, all eigenvalues positive means a local minimum; all negative, a local maximum; mixed signs, a saddle point. For L = w₁² + 4w₂², H = diag(2, 8): convex, with curvature 2 along w₁ and 8 along w₂. For logistic regression, H = (1/n)Xᵀdiag(pᵢ(1 − pᵢ))X, a weighted version of XᵀX, which is positive semi-definite because every weight pᵢ(1 − pᵢ) is positive; the lab computes its eigenvalues on random data (0.071, 0.157, 0.167), all positive. That is the proof that logistic regression's loss has no bad local minima.

A slightly stronger property makes optimisation fast. A function is strongly convex with constant μ > 0 if its curvature is at least μ everywhere (H's eigenvalues ≥ μ). It is β-smooth if its curvature is at most β everywhere, meaning the gradient cannot change faster than β per unit of movement. The bowl w₁² + 4w₂² is strongly convex with μ = 2 and smooth with β = 8. Adding an L2 penalty λ|w|² to any convex loss makes it strongly convex with μ at least 2λ, which is one more reason regularisation helps: it guarantees a unique solution and faster convergence.

3. Gradient descent on a quadratic, exactly

Gradient descent updates the weights by

w ← w − η·∇L(w)

with learning rate η. A quadratic bowl is the model case, because near any minimum a smooth loss looks like one (math10's Taylor series), so what happens here explains a great deal. On L = w₁² + 4w₂², ∇L = (2w₁, 8w₂), and the update acts on each coordinate separately: w₁ ← (1 − 2η)w₁ and w₂ ← (1 − 8η)w₂. Each coordinate is multiplied every step by (1 − ηλ), where λ is its curvature. The coordinate shrinks towards 0 only if |1 − ηλ| < 1, that is, 0 < η < 2/λ. Both must shrink, so the steepest direction sets the limit:

stable if and only if η < 2/λ_max

Here 2/8 = 0.25. The lab runs 30 steps from (2, 1) at several learning rates: η = 0.05 leaves the loss at 7.2 × 10⁻³; η = 0.1 reaches 6.1 × 10⁻⁶; η = 0.2 reaches 3.9 × 10⁻¹³; η = 0.24, just under the limit, only 2.7 × 10⁻², because the w₂ factor is 1 − 1.92 = −0.92, so w₂ flips sign every step and shrinks slowly (the zig-zag); and η = 0.26 gives 405 and growing, because the factor is −1.08.

Which step is best? The slowest-shrinking coordinate decides the overall speed, so choose η to make the worst factor as small as possible. The factors |1 − 2η| and |1 − 8η| are balanced when 1 − 2η = −(1 − 8η), giving η = 2/(2 + 8) = 0.2, and then both factors are 0.6. In general the best fixed step is η* = 2/(λ_min + λ_max), and the error shrinks per step by

(κ − 1)/(κ + 1), where κ = λ_max/λ_min is the condition number

For κ = 4 that is 0.6. For κ = 100 it is 0.98: each step removes only 2% of the remaining error. The lab measures the steps to drive the loss below 10⁻⁶: 16 for κ = 4 and 462 for κ = 100. The number of steps grows roughly in proportion to κ, which is why feature scaling, normalisation layers and adaptive optimisers, all of which reduce the effective condition number, matter so much in practice. Ml4 shows the same phenomenon with unscaled features.

4. Why gradient descent works on any smooth function: the descent lemma

Real losses are not exact quadratics, so we need a guarantee that does not depend on that. Suppose L is β-smooth: its curvature is at most β in every direction. Then the quadratic Taylor bound from math10 gives, for any step d,

L(w + d) ≤ L(w) + ∇L(w)·d + (β/2)|d|²

Plug in the gradient step d = −η∇L:

L(w − η∇L) ≤ L(w) − η(1 − βη/2)·|∇L|²

This is the descent lemma. Whenever 0 < η < 2/β, the bracket is positive, so the loss strictly decreases unless the gradient is zero. The best guaranteed decrease comes at η = 1/β and is at least |∇L|²/(2β). Two consequences follow. First, gradient descent cannot keep its gradient large forever: each step removes at least a fixed fraction of |∇L|², and the loss is bounded below, so the gradients must shrink towards zero, which means the iterates approach critical points. Second, with convexity, approaching a critical point means approaching the minimum, and the gap L(wₖ) − L* falls like 1/k; with strong convexity it falls geometrically, by a factor of at most (1 − μ/β) per step at η = 1/β, matching the quadratic picture with κ = β/μ.

The lemma also explains the familiar shape of training curves: rapid early progress while gradients are large, then slower progress as the gradient norm falls, because each step's guaranteed improvement is proportional to |∇L|².

5. Momentum

On an ill-conditioned bowl, plain gradient descent wastes its steps bouncing across the steep direction while crawling along the flat one. Momentum (the "heavy ball" method) keeps a velocity:

v ← β_m·v − η·∇L(w),   w ← w + v

with β_m between 0 and 1 (often 0.9; the 1986 backpropagation paper used α = 0.9). Along the steep direction, consecutive gradients alternate in sign, so they cancel inside the velocity and the oscillation is damped. Along the flat direction, consecutive gradients agree, so the velocity accumulates and the effective step grows towards η/(1 − β_m), ten times larger at β_m = 0.9. For quadratics there is an exact theory: with the best choices η = 4/(√β + √μ)² and β_m = ((√κ − 1)/(√κ + 1))², the error shrinks by about (√κ − 1)/(√κ + 1) per step, so the step count scales with √κ instead of κ. In the lab's κ = 100 bowl that means β_m ≈ 0.669 and 71 steps instead of 462. Nesterov's variant evaluates the gradient at the look-ahead point w + β_m·v, which corrects the velocity a step earlier. Adam (dl5) combines momentum with per-coordinate step sizes scaled by a running estimate of each gradient component's size, a cheap approximation to dividing by curvature.

6. Stochastic gradients

With millions of examples, computing the exact gradient every step is too expensive, so training uses a mini-batch: the average gradient over B random examples, an unbiased estimate of the full gradient whose noise shrinks like 1/√B (math4). This is stochastic gradient descent (SGD). The noise changes the theory in two ways. Near the minimum the gradient is small but the noise is not, so with a fixed learning rate SGD keeps bouncing in a region whose size grows with η; to converge exactly the learning rate must decay over time, which is one reason for learning-rate schedules (ml4). And the noise is not only a nuisance: it lets SGD escape saddle points and sharp, narrow valleys more easily, and there is evidence that it biases training towards flatter regions of the loss, which tend to generalise better (dl8). The descent lemma still applies on average: the expected decrease is η(1 − βη/2)|∇L|² minus a term proportional to η² times the gradient noise variance, which is the formal reason small batches need smaller learning rates.

7. Preconditioning and line search

Section 3 showed that the condition number, not the size of the problem, decides how slow gradient descent is. Preconditioning attacks it directly: multiply the gradient by a matrix P before stepping, w ← w − ηP∇L, chosen so that the problem looks rounder. With P = H⁻¹ you get Newton's method and every direction converges at once; with P diagonal, each coordinate gets its own step size. On w₁² + 4w₂², P = diag(1/2, 1/8) turns the bowl into a perfectly round one, and a single step of size 1 lands on the minimum. Feature standardisation is preconditioning done once on the data; normalisation layers do it inside the network; Adam and RMSProp do it adaptively, dividing each coordinate's step by a running estimate of that coordinate's gradient size, which tends to be large where curvature is large. None of these is exact for a real network, but each moves the effective condition number down, which is where most of their benefit comes from.

A second classical tool removes the need to know the curvature at all. A line search picks the step size afresh at each iteration: start with a trial η, and shrink it (for example, halve it) until the loss decreases by at least a fixed fraction of what the linear approximation predicts, L(w − η∇L) ≤ L(w) − c·η|∇L|² with a small c such as 10⁻⁴. This is the Armijo condition, and the descent lemma guarantees it is satisfied once η is below about 1/β, so the search always ends. Line searches make gradient descent and L-BFGS robust on deterministic problems such as logistic regression. They are rarely used in deep learning because each trial step needs a fresh loss evaluation on the same batch, and with noisy mini-batch losses the comparison itself is unreliable; learning-rate schedules play their role instead.

8. Non-convex landscapes: local minima, saddles and plateaus

Deep networks are not convex. Permuting the hidden units of a layer gives a different weight vector with the same loss, so there are always many separate minima, and the landscape between them has hills. What does gradient descent meet? Critical points of three kinds. Local minima, where all Hessian eigenvalues are positive. Saddle points, with eigenvalues of both signs. And flat regions where the gradient is tiny without being a critical point (plateaus). A heuristic argument suggests saddles dominate in high dimensions: for a critical point to be a minimum, all of perhaps millions of curvature directions must be positive, which is very unlikely unless the loss there is already low. Empirical studies of network landscapes support this picture: bad local minima are rare for large networks, saddle points are common, and noisy gradient methods escape them because the negative-curvature direction amplifies any small push. The slowdowns people see in practice are mostly plateaus and poor conditioning rather than true traps.

9. Constraints and Lagrange multipliers, worked by hand

Now add a constraint. Minimise f(x, y) = x² + 2y² subject to g(x, y) = x + y = 3. The unconstrained minimum (0, 0) violates the constraint, so the answer lies somewhere on the line. Walking along the line, f decreases until the line just touches one of f's elliptical contours. At that touching point the contour and the line are tangent. The gradient of f is perpendicular to its contour; the gradient of g, (1, 1), is perpendicular to the line. Tangency means those two perpendiculars are parallel:

∇f = λ·∇g, together with g(x, y) = c

The number λ is the Lagrange multiplier. Solve: ∇f = (2x, 4y) and ∇g = (1, 1), so 2x = λ and 4y = λ, giving x = λ/2 and y = λ/4. Substitute into the constraint: λ/2 + λ/4 = 3, so λ = 4. Then x = 2, y = 1 and f = 4 + 2 = 6. Check: at (2, 1), ∇f = (4, 4) = 4 × (1, 1).

A compact way to write the same conditions is to form the Lagrangian 𝓛(x, y, λ) = f(x, y) − λ(g(x, y) − c) and set all its partial derivatives to zero: ∂𝓛/∂x = 0 and ∂𝓛/∂y = 0 give ∇f = λ∇g, and ∂𝓛/∂λ = 0 gives back the constraint. A constrained problem in two variables has become an unconstrained stationarity problem in three, which the lab solves as a 3 × 3 linear system.

What λ means. Solve the problem for a general budget c: x = 2c/3, y = c/3, so the optimal value is f*(c) = 4c²/9 + 2c²/9 = 2c²/3. Its derivative is df*/dc = 4c/3, which equals 4 at c = 3: exactly λ. In general the multiplier is the sensitivity of the optimal value to the constraint level. Raising the budget from 3 to 3.001 raises the minimum by about 0.004 (the lab checks this numerically). Economists call λ a shadow price: what one more unit of the constrained resource is worth. In ML it is the exchange rate between a constraint and a penalty, as section 11 shows.

10. A Lagrange multiplier in ML: the maximum-likelihood estimate (MLE) of a categorical distribution

Math5 found the maximum-likelihood estimate of a coin's bias by calculus. With K categories (a die, word frequencies, class priors) there is a constraint: probabilities must sum to 1. Given counts n₁, …, n_K with total N, maximise the log-likelihood Σₖ nₖ ln pₖ subject to Σₖ pₖ = 1. The Lagrangian is Σₖ nₖ ln pₖ − λ(Σₖ pₖ − 1). Setting ∂/∂pₖ = 0 gives nₖ/pₖ − λ = 0, so pₖ = nₖ/λ. The constraint then forces Σₖ nₖ/λ = 1, so λ = N, and

pₖ = nₖ/N

the observed frequencies, as intuition suggests. Without the constraint the problem has no solution (raising every pₖ increases the log-likelihood forever); the multiplier is what turns "make every probability large" into "share a fixed total". The same calculation, with an entropy objective and constraints on expected feature values, derives the softmax and the whole exponential family (math12 and the deep section).

11. Inequality constraints, KKT, and regularisation as a constraint

Many constraints are inequalities: weights no larger than a bound, probabilities non-negative, a cost below a budget. For minimising f subject to h(x) ≤ 0, the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions generalise Lagrange's: at an optimum (under mild regularity conditions) there is a multiplier μ with

  • stationarity: ∇f + μ∇h = 0;
  • feasibility: h(x) ≤ 0;
  • dual feasibility: μ ≥ 0;
  • complementary slackness: μ·h(x) = 0.

The last condition carries the intuition. Either the constraint is slack (h < 0, the optimum is strictly inside, the fence does not matter) and then μ = 0; or it is tight (h = 0, the optimum is on the fence) and μ can be positive, measuring how hard the fence pushes. For convex problems the KKT conditions are not just necessary but sufficient: a point satisfying them is optimal.

The most important ML instance is regularisation. Minimising a loss subject to |w|² ≤ t (a constraint on the weights' size) has KKT stationarity condition ∇L + 2μw = 0, which is exactly the stationarity condition of minimising L + μ|w|² (an L2 penalty). So for each size limit t there is a penalty strength μ (the constraint's multiplier) that gives the same solution, and vice versa: penalised and constrained regularisation are two parameterisations of the same family of solutions. Penalties are easier to optimise; constraints are easier to interpret and are used directly in max-norm regularisation, trust-region methods and clipping. The L1 version explains sparsity geometrically: the constraint |w|₁ ≤ t is a diamond whose corners lie on the axes, and the loss's contours typically first touch it at a corner, where some weights are exactly zero (ml3).

When constraints are simple sets, projected gradient descent is often the easiest algorithm: take a gradient step, then project back onto the feasible set (math8's projection). On the worked problem, removing the component of the gradient that leaves the line x + y = 3 and stepping repeatedly converges to (2, 1), as the lab shows. Clipping weights to a box, renormalising embeddings to the unit sphere, and keeping probabilities on the simplex are all projections.

Duality, and why SVMs are solved in the dual

Every constrained problem has a companion dual problem. Minimising the Lagrangian over x for fixed multipliers gives a function of the multipliers alone, the dual function, which is always a lower bound on the original optimum; maximising it over non-negative multipliers gives the best such bound. For convex problems satisfying a mild condition (Slater's), the bound is tight: the dual optimum equals the primal optimum, and solving either solves both. Support vector machines (SVMs) are the textbook ML case. The primal problem (find the maximum-margin hyperplane) has one constraint per training example; the dual has one multiplier per example, and by complementary slackness only examples on or inside the margin get non-zero multipliers. Those are the support vectors. The dual also involves the data only through dot products xᵢ·xⱼ, which is what allows the kernel trick (math8's inner products) to fit non-linear boundaries.

Maximum entropy and the softmax

Which probability distribution over K outcomes is "least committal" while matching some known averages? Maximise entropy −Σ pₖ ln pₖ (math12) subject to Σ pₖ = 1 and Σ pₖ fₖ = F for a feature f. The Lagrangian conditions give −ln pₖ − 1 − λ₀ − λ₁fₖ = 0, so pₖ ∝ exp(−λ₁fₖ): an exponential of the features, normalised. With several features, pₖ ∝ exp(θ·fₖ), which is exactly the softmax with logits θ·fₖ. Logistic regression, softmax classifiers and the exponential family (math2's deep section) are therefore maximum-entropy models, and their weights are Lagrange multipliers of the moment constraints. This derivation is a good answer to "why the softmax?": it is the least-assuming distribution consistent with the features' expected values.

Second-order and quasi-Newton methods

Newton's method steps by −H⁻¹∇L, rescaling each eigen-direction by its curvature so that every direction converges at the same speed; on a quadratic it lands on the minimum in one step regardless of κ. Near a minimum of a smooth convex function it converges quadratically (the number of correct digits roughly doubles each step). Its costs are storing and solving with the d × d Hessian, impossible for large d, and the danger on non-convex losses that negative curvature turns the step towards maxima or saddles. Quasi-Newton methods such as L-BFGS (limited-memory Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno) build a low-memory approximation to H⁻¹ from recent gradient differences and are the method of choice for medium-sized smooth problems (logistic regression, calibration, many classical models). For deep networks, the practical compromise is diagonal or block-structured curvature estimates: Adam's per-coordinate scaling, and methods such as Shampoo and K-FAC (Kronecker-factored approximate curvature) that approximate the curvature of each layer with Kronecker products of small matrices.

The edge of stability, in more detail

Cohen and colleagues (ICLR 2021) trained networks with full-batch gradient descent, removing mini-batch noise to see the deterministic dynamics, and estimated the sharpness (the largest Hessian eigenvalue) throughout. Two behaviours were consistent across architectures and tasks. First, progressive sharpening: as long as the sharpness is below 2/η, it tends to keep rising. In one of their illustrations, a fully connected network with two hidden layers of width 200 trained on a 5,000-example subset of CIFAR-10 (a standard benchmark of small 10-class images) with a deliberately small step showed sharpness rising throughout training. Second, once sharpness reaches 2/η it does not keep growing, nor does training diverge: it hovers just above 2/η, the loss behaves non-monotonically over short timescales (oscillating along the sharpest direction) yet consistently decreases over long timescales. This contradicts the quadratic analysis of section 3, which predicts divergence above 2/η, and the descent lemma's monotone decrease. The working explanation is that the oscillation itself feeds back on the curvature, keeping it near the threshold (a kind of self-stabilisation). Practical implications: the learning rate effectively caps the sharpness of the region training ends up in, which links larger learning rates to flatter solutions; warm-up (starting with a small η) lets sharpness adjust before large steps; and loss spikes in large-model training are often episodes where sharpness overshoots and is pulled back.

Implicit bias: which minimum gradient descent picks

When a problem has many minima with the same training loss, the algorithm decides which one you get, and that choice can matter more than the loss. Two clean results show this. For under-determined least squares (more weights than examples, so infinitely many exact fits), gradient descent started from zero converges to the minimum-norm solution, the same answer as the pseudo-inverse in math9, because every update lies in the span of the data rows and so never adds a component the data cannot see. For logistic regression on separable data, the weights grow without bound, but their direction converges to the maximum-margin separator, the SVM solution, even with no explicit regulariser. These are examples of implicit regularisation: properties of the solution created by the optimiser rather than the loss. In deep networks the picture is less settled, but the same idea underlies the observations that learning rate, batch size and optimiser choice change generalisation even when the final training loss is identical, and it is part of why the edge-of-stability result matters: the learning rate selects the curvature of the minimum reached.

Convergence rates worth knowing

For reference, with β-smooth losses and well-chosen steps: on convex losses, gradient descent's loss gap falls like 1/k, and accelerated (Nesterov) methods like 1/k², which is optimal for first-order methods; on strongly convex losses, both converge geometrically, gradient descent with steps proportional to κ log(1/ε) and acceleration with √κ log(1/ε). SGD with decaying step sizes on strongly convex losses converges like 1/k in expectation, slower than full gradient descent per step but far cheaper per step, which is why it wins for large datasets. Variance-reduced methods (such as SVRG, stochastic variance-reduced gradient, and SAG, stochastic average gradient) recover geometric rates for finite sums by periodically computing full gradients. None of these guarantees apply to non-convex deep networks, where theory guarantees only that gradient norms become small, at rate 1/√k for smooth losses.

Choosing and finding learning rates

The 2/λ_max limit explains why a learning rate that works for one model diverges for a slightly different one: changing the architecture, initialisation, data scaling or batch size changes the curvature. Practical procedures are built around this. A learning-rate range test increases η exponentially over a short run and picks a value somewhat below where the loss starts to rise. Warm-up starts with a small η and ramps up over the first few hundred or thousand steps, giving curvature time to fall before large steps are taken, and is nearly universal for transformers. Decay schedules (cosine, linear, step) reduce η later so SGD noise shrinks and training settles (ml4). When a run diverges, halving the peak learning rate or lengthening warm-up is the first fix to try.

Loss spikes and instability in large-model training

Large language-model runs regularly show loss spikes: the loss jumps up sharply, sometimes recovering, sometimes not. Many are curvature events, where sharpness in some direction overshoots the stability threshold (often in attention logits or output layers), consistent with the edge-of-stability picture. Teams monitor gradient norms, per-layer update-to-weight ratios and, in some cases, estimates of top Hessian eigenvalues. Mitigations include gradient clipping, lower learning rates or longer warm-up, normalisation of queries and keys, z-loss penalties on output logits, restarting from a checkpoint before the spike with different data ordering, and skipping problematic batches.

Ill-conditioning in classical models

Logistic regression or linear models trained with gradient descent on unscaled features (raw counts next to small ratios) converge painfully slowly or stall at a poor solution because the condition number is enormous. Standardising features usually fixes it in one line. If a convex model still trains slowly, switching to L-BFGS or Newton-type solvers (the default in many libraries for logistic regression) often converges in tens of iterations instead of thousands. Adding a small L2 penalty both regularises and caps the condition number from below.

Constrained problems in ML systems

Production ML often contains explicit constrained optimisation outside the model: allocating an ads budget across campaigns, choosing thresholds per segment to meet a precision target, enforcing fairness constraints, scheduling GPU (graphics processor) capacity, or calibrating outputs to sum to one. These are frequently convex (linear or quadratic programs) and are solved reliably by dedicated solvers rather than by gradient descent. The Lagrange multipliers they return are useful outputs in their own right: the multiplier on a budget constraint says how much an extra dollar would earn, and the multiplier on a fairness constraint quantifies what that constraint costs in the objective, which is exactly the number a product discussion needs.

The public case, in production terms

The edge-of-stability finding changed how practitioners reason about learning rates. If training drives itself to the stability threshold, the peak learning rate implicitly chooses how sharp the final solution can be, warm-up length and peak learning rate interact, and a loss that oscillates over short windows is not necessarily a sign of a bug. The practical habits that follow are to tune learning rate and warm-up together, to judge progress over long windows rather than step to step, and, when instability appears, to check curvature-related signals (gradient-norm spikes, logit growth) before suspecting data or code.

Goal: see the 2/λ_max step limit and the zig-zag, measure how the condition number slows gradient descent and how momentum helps, confirm the logistic loss is convex, solve the Lagrange example and check the meaning of λ, and run projected gradient descent. NumPy only (gpt_env); save as math11.py.

import numpy as np

# 1. Gradient descent on L(w) = w1^2 + 4 w2^2 (curvatures 2 and 8) from (2, 1)
H = np.diag([2.0, 8.0])
def run(lr, steps=30, momentum=0.0):
    w, v = np.array([2.0, 1.0]), np.zeros(2)
    for _ in range(steps):
        v = momentum * v - lr * (H @ w)
        w = w + v
    return 0.5 * w @ H @ w            # = w1^2 + 4 w2^2

for lr in (0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.24, 0.26):
    print(f"lr={lr:.2f}: loss after 30 steps = {run(lr):.3e}")

# 2. Ill-conditioning: curvatures 2 and 200, best fixed step, steps to reach loss < 1e-6
for k in (4, 100):
    H = np.diag([2.0, 2.0 * k]); lr = 2 / (2 + 2 * k)
    w, n = np.array([2.0, 1.0]), 0
    while 0.5 * w @ H @ w > 1e-6:
        w = w - lr * (H @ w); n += 1
    print(f"condition number {k:3d}: {n} steps (rate (k-1)/(k+1) = {(k - 1) / (k + 1):.3f})")
# Heavy-ball momentum on the condition-number-100 bowl, with its textbook best settings
L_, mu = 200.0, 2.0
lr, beta = 4 / (np.sqrt(L_) + np.sqrt(mu)) ** 2, ((np.sqrt(L_ / mu) - 1) / (np.sqrt(L_ / mu) + 1)) ** 2
w, v, n = np.array([2.0, 1.0]), np.zeros(2), 0
while 0.5 * w @ H @ w > 1e-6:
    v = beta * v - lr * (H @ w); w = w + v; n += 1
print(f"condition number 100 with momentum {beta:.3f}: {n} steps")

# 3. Convexity check: Hessian of the logistic loss is X^T diag(p(1-p)) X / n, never negative
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X = rng.normal(size=(200, 3)); w = rng.normal(size=3)
p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-X @ w))
Hlog = X.T @ (X * (p * (1 - p))[:, None]) / len(X)
print("eigenvalues of logistic-loss Hessian:", np.linalg.eigvalsh(Hlog).round(4))

# 4. Lagrange multipliers: minimise x^2 + 2y^2 subject to x + y = 3
# Stationarity 2x = lam, 4y = lam, constraint x + y = 3  ->  linear system
A = np.array([[2, 0, -1], [0, 4, -1], [1, 1, 0]], float)
x, y, lam = np.linalg.solve(A, [0, 0, 3])
print(f"x={x:.3f} y={y:.3f} lambda={lam:.3f} f={x*x + 2*y*y:.3f}")
f_opt = lambda c: (2 / 3) * c ** 2                     # optimum as a function of the budget c
print("df*/dc numerically:", round((f_opt(3.001) - f_opt(2.999)) / 0.002, 4))

# 5. Projected gradient descent keeps w on the constraint x + y = 3
w = np.array([3.0, 0.0]); n = np.array([1.0, 1.0]) / np.sqrt(2)
for _ in range(200):
    g = np.array([2 * w[0], 4 * w[1]])
    g = g - (g @ n) * n                                 # remove the part that leaves the line
    w = w - 0.1 * g
print("projected GD solution:", w.round(4))

What to look for:

  1. After 30 steps on w₁² + 4w₂²: η = 0.05 → 7.2 × 10⁻³, 0.10 → 6.1 × 10⁻⁶, 0.20 → 3.9 × 10⁻¹³ (the best step), 0.24 → 2.7 × 10⁻² (zig-zag near the limit) and 0.26 → 405 (diverged, past 2/8 = 0.25).
  2. Steps to reach a loss below 10⁻⁶ with the best fixed step: 16 at condition number 4 (factor 0.6) and 462 at condition number 100 (factor 0.98). Heavy-ball momentum with its textbook settings (β ≈ 0.669) needs 71.
  3. The logistic-loss Hessian on random data has eigenvalues 0.071, 0.157 and 0.167, all positive: convex.
  4. The Lagrange system gives x = 2, y = 1, λ = 4, f = 6, and the numerical derivative of the optimum with respect to the budget is 4.0 = λ.
  5. Projected gradient descent starting from (3, 0) on the line x + y = 3 converges to (2, 1).

Diagnose: a model that trains at 1e-3 and explodes at 2e-3

A team's model trains stably with learning rate 1 × 10⁻³ but its loss explodes within a few hundred steps at 2 × 10⁻³. Someone estimates the largest Hessian eigenvalue early in training at about 1,200 and later at about 1,900. (a) Explain the observation with the stability limit. (b) Give three changes that would let them use a larger learning rate, with the mechanism of each. (c) What would you expect to see in sharpness and loss if they trained at exactly 1 × 10⁻³ for a long time?

Answer

(a) For plain gradient descent the stability limit is η < 2/λ_max. At 1 × 10⁻³ the limit allows sharpness up to 2,000; at 2 × 10⁻³ only up to 1,000. Early sharpness of 1,200 already exceeds 1,000, so the sharpest direction amplifies each step and the loss explodes. (With Adam the effective per-coordinate steps differ, but the same logic applies to the preconditioned curvature.)

(b) Learning-rate warm-up: start well below 1 × 10⁻³ and ramp up, giving the network time to move to flatter regions before large steps; sharpness early in training is often the binding constraint. Better conditioning: normalisation layers, careful initialisation scale, or query–key normalisation in attention reduce the largest curvature, raising 2/λ_max. Gradient clipping: it does not change the curvature but limits the damage from occasional large steps along sharp directions, which can turn a divergence into a recoverable spike. Reducing the condition number by standardising inputs and adding mild weight decay also helps in smaller models.

(c) By progressive sharpening, sharpness tends to rise while it is below 2/η = 2,000 (the measurements 1,200 then 1,900 already show it), then hover around 2,000 rather than exceeding it much: the edge of stability. The loss would oscillate over short windows while still trending down over longer ones. Seeing that pattern is not a reason to panic, but it confirms that the learning rate, not the data or code, is setting the sharpness of the solution.
