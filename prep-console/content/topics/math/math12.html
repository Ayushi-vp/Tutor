Every classifier and every language model you will train in this course minimises the same loss: cross-entropy. Every time a reinforcement-learning (RL) fine-tune keeps a model close to its starting point, it uses a KL divergence. Distillation, variational autoencoders, calibration, decision-tree splits and the perplexity numbers on language-model leaderboards all come from one small set of definitions in information theory. They are usually presented as formulas to memorise. They make far more sense as answers to one question: how surprised should you be?

The question was posed precisely by Claude Shannon in a 1948 paper at Bell Labs, "A Mathematical Theory of Communication". Shannon wanted to know how much a message could be compressed and how fast information could be sent over a noisy telephone line. To answer, he needed a number that measured information, and he defined it through probability: an unlikely message carries more information than a likely one, and the average information of a source, its entropy, is the minimum number of binary digits needed per symbol to encode it. He called the unit the bit, crediting the word to his colleague John Tukey, and used it to estimate that ordinary English is about 50% redundant: roughly half of what we write is determined by the structure of the language rather than chosen freely. A few years later he ran an experiment in which people guessed the next letter of a text, and concluded that with enough context English carries only about one bit per letter.

That experiment is, in hindsight, the first language-model evaluation. A modern language model is a machine for predicting the next token, and its training loss is the average surprise, in nats or bits, of the token that actually came next. Lower loss means the model has found more of the structure that makes language predictable. Understanding entropy, cross-entropy and KL divergence explains why that is the right loss, what the numbers on a training curve mean, why a model can improve in accuracy while its loss gets worse, and why the direction of a KL divergence changes what a model learns.

This lesson builds the ideas from one definition: the surprise of an outcome is −log p. Averaging it gives entropy, and Shannon's coding theorem says entropy is the shortest possible average code length, worked with a three-symbol code. Coding with the wrong distribution gives cross-entropy; the extra cost is the KL divergence, worked by hand for two three-outcome distributions in both directions, along with why it is never negative and why it is not symmetric. Then the machine-learning uses: cross-entropy as the classification and language-modelling loss, perplexity, forward versus reverse KL and the behaviours they produce, mutual information, label smoothing, distillation and the KL penalty in reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Ml5 compares losses from the modelling side; this is the information-theoretic view underneath.

Start with surprise. If someone tells you the sun rose this morning, you learn almost nothing: it was nearly certain. If they tell you it snowed in Dubai, you learn a lot: it was very unlikely. Information is the resolution of uncertainty, so the less likely an outcome, the more information its occurrence carries. Shannon made this exact with a logarithm: the surprise (or self-information) of an outcome with probability p is

surprise = −log₂ p bits

A certain event (p = 1) has surprise 0. A fair coin's heads (p = 1/2) has surprise 1 bit. One face of a fair die (1/6) has 2.585 bits. An event with probability 1/1,024 has 10 bits. Why a logarithm? Because surprises of independent events should add: the probability of two independent outcomes multiplies, and the logarithm turns that product into a sum. Two fair coin tosses together have probability 1/4 and surprise 2 bits, one per toss.

A useful way to feel what a bit is: the game of twenty questions. Each yes/no question, chosen well, halves the remaining possibilities, so identifying one of 1,024 equally likely objects takes 10 questions, log₂ 1,024. When outcomes are not equally likely, a clever questioner asks about the likely ones first, and the average number of questions falls. Entropy is that average under the best strategy: the expected surprise,

H(P) = −Σ p(x) log₂ p(x)

It is highest when all outcomes are equally likely (maximum uncertainty) and zero when one outcome is certain.

Entropy depends only on the probabilities, not on what the outcomes are called or how they are valued. A distribution over three film genres and a distribution over three server states with the same probabilities have the same entropy. That is both its strength (one number that applies to any random variable) and the reason it says nothing about meaning or importance, a point Shannon made explicitly: the engineering problem of communication does not care what the message means, only how unpredictable it is.

Now suppose you build your questioning strategy (or your compression code) for a distribution Q, but the outcomes actually come from P. You will ask the wrong questions in the wrong order and use more questions on average. That average, measured with Q's surprises but P's frequencies, is the cross-entropy H(P, Q). The extra cost compared with the best possible strategy, H(P, Q) − H(P), is the KL divergence KL(P‖Q): how many extra bits per outcome you waste by believing Q when the truth is P.

That last sentence is the whole connection to machine learning. A model is a belief Q about the data; the data come from an unknown P. Training with cross-entropy loss means making the model's average surprise at the real data as small as possible, which is the same as making it waste as few bits as possible relative to the truth. The analogy has one gap worth noting: real learning does not know P, only samples from it, so the loss is an average over a finite dataset and, like every sample average (math4), it is a noisy estimate of the true cross-entropy, which is why validation loss and training loss differ.

1. Surprise, and the units of information

The self-information or surprise of an outcome x with probability p(x) is −log p(x). The base of the logarithm sets the unit. Base 2 gives bits; the natural logarithm (base e ≈ 2.718) gives nats; base 10 gives "decimal digits" or hartleys. Converting is a constant factor: 1 nat = 1/ln 2 ≈ 1.443 bits, and 1 bit = ln 2 ≈ 0.693 nats. Machine-learning frameworks compute losses with natural logarithms, so training losses are in nats; information theory and compression usually use bits. A language model with a loss of 2.0 nats per token is at 2.0 × 1.443 ≈ 2.89 bits per token.

Shannon justified the logarithm by asking what properties a measure of information should have: it should depend only on the probability, be larger for less likely outcomes, be zero for certain ones, and add for independent events. The logarithm is essentially the only function with all four. In the 1948 paper he also gave the physical intuition for the unit: a device with two stable positions, such as a relay, stores one bit, and N such devices store N bits, because they have 2ᴺ states and log₂ 2ᴺ = N.

2. Entropy

The entropy of a discrete distribution P is the expected surprise:

H(P) = E[−log p(X)] = −Σₓ p(x) log p(x)

with the convention 0 × log 0 = 0 (an impossible outcome contributes nothing). Worked values, in bits:

  • A fair coin: −(½ log₂ ½ + ½ log₂ ½) = 1 bit.
  • A 90/10 coin: −(0.9 log₂ 0.9 + 0.1 log₂ 0.1) = 0.9 × 0.152 + 0.1 × 3.322 ≈ 0.469 bits. The coin is predictable, so each toss tells you little on average.
  • A fair six-sided die: log₂ 6 ≈ 2.585 bits.
  • P = (½, ¼, ¼): ½ × 1 + ¼ × 2 + ¼ × 2 = 1.5 bits.
  • P = (0.7, 0.2, 0.1): 0.7 × 0.515 + 0.2 × 2.322 + 0.1 × 3.322 ≈ 0.360 + 0.464 + 0.332 = 1.157 bits.

Three properties are worth knowing. Entropy is never negative and is zero only when one outcome is certain. Over K outcomes it is at most log₂ K, reached only by the uniform distribution, so any structure in a distribution lowers its entropy. And entropies of independent variables add: two fair dice have 2 × 2.585 = 5.17 bits. For continuous variables the analogous quantity, differential entropy −∫ f(x) log f(x) dx, behaves similarly but can be negative and depends on units; the normal distribution has the largest differential entropy for a given variance, one more reason it appears so often (math2).

In ML, entropy shows up directly as a measure of how uncertain a model's prediction is. A classifier's predicted distribution (0.98, 0.01, 0.01) has entropy 0.16 bits; (0.4, 0.3, 0.3) has 1.57 bits. Active learning picks examples with high predictive entropy to label next; RL policies add an entropy bonus to keep exploring; and the entropy of a language model's next-token distribution is a cheap signal of when it is unsure.

3. Entropy as the limit of compression

Shannon's source coding theorem gives entropy its operational meaning: no lossless code can use fewer than H bits per symbol on average, and by encoding long blocks of symbols together you can get as close to H as you like. Entropy is the compressibility of a source.

A worked code makes it concrete. A source emits A with probability ½ and B and C with probability ¼ each, entropy 1.5 bits. Use the code A → 0, B → 10, C → 11. It is a prefix code: no codeword is the beginning of another, so a stream such as 0100110 decodes unambiguously as 0 | 10 | 0 | 11 | 0 = A B A C A with no separators. The average length is ½ × 1 + ¼ × 2 + ¼ × 2 = 1.5 bits, exactly the entropy. That is no coincidence: the codeword length for each symbol equals its surprise, −log₂ p, which is possible here because every probability is a power of ½. In general, the best code assigns each symbol about −log₂ p bits, rounded, and Huffman's algorithm finds the best symbol-by-symbol code. A naive fixed-length code for three symbols would need 2 bits each; knowing the distribution saves 25%.

This is the bridge to everything that follows. A probability model is a compression scheme: give each outcome −log₂ q bits. Better models compress better. Modern lossless compressors built on language models compress text far below what classical tools achieve, precisely because the model's predicted probabilities for the next token are sharper.

4. Cross-entropy: coding with the wrong distribution

Suppose the data come from P but you designed your code, or your model, for Q: each outcome x costs −log q(x) bits. The average cost under the true frequencies is the cross-entropy:

H(P, Q) = −Σₓ p(x) log q(x)

Worked with P = (0.7, 0.2, 0.1) (the true frequencies of three outcomes A, B, C) and Q = (0.4, 0.4, 0.2) (a model). The model's surprises are −log₂ 0.4 ≈ 1.322 bits for A and B and −log₂ 0.2 ≈ 2.322 bits for C. Weight them by the true frequencies: H(P, Q) = 0.7 × 1.322 + 0.2 × 1.322 + 0.1 × 2.322 ≈ 0.925 + 0.264 + 0.232 = 1.422 bits. Compare H(P) = 1.157 bits. Using the wrong model costs 0.265 bits per outcome more than necessary: the model under-rates A, the most common outcome, so it is often more surprised than it should be.

Cross-entropy is always at least the entropy, H(P, Q) ≥ H(P), with equality only when Q = P (section 6 proves it). That is why it is a sensible loss: the best possible model, the true distribution, achieves the minimum.

5. Cross-entropy as the training loss

In classification, for each example the "true distribution" is the one-hot label: probability 1 on the correct class y. The cross-entropy between that and the model's predicted distribution q is −log q(y), the model's surprise at the right answer. Averaged over a dataset of n examples:

loss = −(1/n) Σᵢ log q(yᵢ | xᵢ)

which is exactly the negative log-likelihood of the labels under the model (math5). So three descriptions coincide: minimising cross-entropy, maximising likelihood, and (next section) minimising KL divergence from the data distribution to the model. Worked: five examples where the model gives the correct class probabilities 0.9, 0.6, 0.3, 0.95 and 0.05. The surprises are 0.152, 0.737, 1.737, 0.074 and 4.322 bits, averaging 1.404 bits per example (the lab computes this). The single confident mistake, 0.05 on the right answer, contributes more than the other four together, which is the characteristic behaviour of this loss: it punishes confident errors heavily and forgives uncertain ones.

A language model is the same thing at the level of tokens: at each position it predicts a distribution over the vocabulary, and its loss is the average of −log q(actual next token). Two consequences are worth stating. First, the minimum achievable loss is not zero but the entropy of the true next-token distribution: language is genuinely uncertain, and a model that drives its training loss to zero has memorised its training text. Second, the gradient of cross-entropy with respect to the logits of a softmax output is simply q − y, predicted probabilities minus the one-hot target (math10, dl1), which is part of why this pairing trains so well.

Perplexity is the exponentiated cross-entropy: 2 to the loss in bits, or e to the loss in nats. It reads as an effective number of choices. The five-example classifier above has perplexity 2^1.404 ≈ 2.65: on average it is as uncertain as a uniform choice among 2.65 options. A language model with a loss of 2.0 nats per token has perplexity e² ≈ 7.4. Because perplexity depends on what a token is, comparisons are only meaningful between models sharing a tokenizer and evaluation text (lm1, lm14).

6. KL divergence

The extra cost of using Q when the truth is P is the Kullback–Leibler divergence:

KL(P‖Q) = H(P, Q) − H(P) = Σₓ p(x) log (p(x) / q(x))

Read it as the expected log-ratio of the true probability to the model's, weighted by how often each outcome actually occurs. For P = (0.7, 0.2, 0.1) and Q = (0.4, 0.4, 0.2), term by term in bits: 0.7 × log₂(0.7/0.4) = 0.7 × 0.807 = 0.565; 0.2 × log₂(0.2/0.4) = −0.200; 0.1 × log₂(0.1/0.2) = −0.100. Total 0.265 bits, matching 1.422 − 1.157. Some terms are negative (where the model over-rates an outcome), but the total never is.

Why KL is never negative. Write KL(P‖Q) = Σ p·(−log(q/p)). The function −log is convex, so by Jensen's inequality (math3), the average of −log is at least −log of the average: Σ p·(−log(q/p)) ≥ −log(Σ p·(q/p)) = −log(Σ q) = −log 1 = 0. Equality requires q/p to be the same for every outcome with p > 0, which (since both sum to 1) means Q = P. This one-line argument, called Gibbs' inequality, is also the proof that cross-entropy is minimised by the true distribution.

Why KL is not symmetric. Swap the roles: KL(Q‖P) = H(Q, P) − H(Q) = 1.799 − 1.522 = 0.277 bits, close to 0.265 here but not equal. The asymmetry becomes dramatic when one distribution gives very small probability where the other does not. Take P = (0.5, 0.49, 0.01) and Q = (0.98, 0.01, 0.01): KL(P‖Q) ≈ 2.27 bits, because P often produces the second outcome, which Q considers nearly impossible; KL(Q‖P) ≈ 0.90 bits, because Q rarely produces it. And if Q gives exactly zero probability to something P can produce, KL(P‖Q) is infinite. The divergence asks "how badly does Q explain data from P?", and that question has a direction. That is why it is called a divergence rather than a distance: it is not symmetric and it does not satisfy the triangle inequality.

Since KL(P‖Q) = H(P, Q) − H(P) and H(P) does not depend on the model, minimising cross-entropy over a model's parameters is exactly minimising KL from the data distribution to the model. That is the precise sense in which maximum-likelihood training makes the model "as close as possible" to the data (math5's deep section).

7. Forward and reverse KL: what the direction does

When the model Q is too simple to match P exactly, which direction you minimise decides what kind of mistake the fit makes. Take a target P with two separate bumps, at −3 and +3, and fit a single Gaussian Q.

  • Forward KL, KL(P‖Q), weights log(p/q) by p. Wherever P has mass and Q has very little, the ratio and the penalty are huge. So Q must cover everything P does. The best single Gaussian is wide and centred between the bumps (the lab finds mean 0, standard deviation 3.2), putting substantial probability in the empty middle. This is mode-covering.
  • Reverse KL, KL(Q‖P), weights log(q/p) by q. Wherever Q puts mass and P has very little, the penalty is huge, but regions Q ignores cost nothing. So Q hides where P is dense. The best fit sits on one bump (mean −3, standard deviation 1.0) and ignores the other. This is mode-seeking.

Both behaviours appear in practice. Maximum-likelihood training (forward KL) makes language models mode-covering: they spread probability over every continuation seen in training, including mediocre ones, which is one reason sampling uses truncation such as top-p or temperature below 1 (lm8). Variational inference minimises reverse KL, so variational posteriors tend to underestimate uncertainty and pick one mode. The KL penalty in RLHF is a reverse KL (policy first), which lets the policy concentrate on high-reward regions the reference model supports. Neither direction is "correct"; each encodes a decision about whether missing real possibilities or inventing impossible ones is worse.

8. Conditional entropy and mutual information

For two variables, the conditional entropy H(Y | X) is the average remaining uncertainty about Y once X is known: the entropy of Y within each value of X, averaged over X. The reduction in uncertainty is the mutual information:

I(X; Y) = H(Y) − H(Y | X) = H(X) − H(X | Y) = KL(P(X, Y) ‖ P(X)·P(Y))

It is symmetric, never negative, and zero exactly when X and Y are independent; the last form shows it is the KL divergence between the real joint distribution and the one you would have if they were independent. Unlike correlation (math3), it detects any kind of dependence, including y = x², and it is measured in bits.

Worked on math1's spam table: 300 of 1,000 emails are spam, so H(spam) = −(0.3 log₂ 0.3 + 0.7 log₂ 0.7) ≈ 0.881 bits. Knowing whether the email contains "free" reduces that uncertainty by I ≈ 0.130 bits (the lab computes it from the four joint probabilities). The word is informative but leaves most of the uncertainty: a filter needs many such features. Decision trees choose splits by exactly this quantity, under the name information gain (ml10), and feature-selection methods rank features by their mutual information with the label. Contrastive objectives such as InfoNCE, used to train embedding models (lm10, mm2), maximise a lower bound on the mutual information between two views of the same item.

9. Joint entropy and the chain rule: why a language model's loss adds up

For two variables the joint entropy H(X, Y) is the entropy of the pair. It splits by the chain rule of probability from math1, P(x, y) = P(x)·P(y | x): taking −log and averaging gives

H(X, Y) = H(X) + H(Y | X)

The uncertainty of the pair is the uncertainty of the first plus the remaining uncertainty of the second once the first is known. Applied repeatedly to a sequence of tokens, the entropy of a whole text is the sum over positions of the entropy of each token given all the previous ones. A language model's loss on a sequence, the sum of −log q(tokenₜ | tokens before t), is the cross-entropy version of exactly this decomposition, which is why per-token losses can be averaged and why the "bits per token" of a model is a meaningful number: it estimates the average conditional entropy of the next token given the context. It also explains why longer context helps: conditioning can never increase entropy on average (H(Y | X) ≤ H(Y), with equality only for independent variables), so Shannon's English estimate fell from about 4.14 bits per letter with no context to about one bit with 100 letters of it.

Worked on the spam table: H(spam) ≈ 0.881 bits and I(spam; "free") ≈ 0.130 bits, so H(spam | "free") ≈ 0.751 bits. The word's presence ("free" or not) has entropy H(free) = −(0.155 log₂ 0.155 + 0.845 log₂ 0.845) ≈ 0.622 bits, so the joint entropy of label and word is 0.622 + 0.751 ≈ 1.373 bits, less than the 0.881 + 0.622 = 1.503 bits the two would have if independent; the 0.130-bit gap is again the mutual information.

10. Cross-entropy for continuous targets: squared error in disguise

Cross-entropy is not only for categories. For a regression model that predicts a mean μ(x) and assumes Gaussian noise with variance σ², the model's density for the observed target y is a normal density (math2), and the surprise −ln q(y) is (y − μ(x))²/(2σ²) plus a constant. Averaging over the data, the cross-entropy between the data and the model is a constant plus the mean squared error divided by 2σ². So minimising squared error is minimising cross-entropy under a Gaussian model, which is math5's maximum-likelihood story told in information units. The view pays off when the noise assumption is wrong: if errors are heavy-tailed, a Laplace or Student-t model gives a different surprise (absolute error, or a log of a squared term) that is less dominated by outliers, and if the noise level varies across inputs, letting the model predict σ(x) as well turns the loss into a heteroscedastic cross-entropy that can learn when it is uncertain. In every case the loss is "the model's average surprise at what actually happened", and the choice of loss is the choice of what the model is allowed to be surprised by.

11. The public case: Shannon's 1948 paper and the redundancy of English

Shannon's "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" appeared in two parts in the Bell System Technical Journal in 1948. It introduced the framework still used: a source producing symbols with probabilities, entropy as the average information per symbol, the coding theorem bounding compression, and channel capacity bounding reliable transmission over noise. It is also where the word "bit" entered print: Shannon wrote that base-2 units "may be called binary digits, or more briefly bits, a word suggested by J. W. Tukey".

The paper applied the theory to English. Treating text as a source over 26 letters (ignoring spaces and punctuation), the maximum entropy is log₂ 26 ≈ 4.7 bits per letter. Using real letter frequencies (E far more common than Z) gives about 4.14 bits; using statistics over longer stretches lowers it further, and Shannon estimated that when structure up to about eight letters is included, English has roughly 2.3 bits per letter. He defined redundancy as one minus the ratio of the actual entropy to the maximum, and concluded that English is roughly 50% redundant: "when we write English half of what we write is determined by the structure of the language and half is chosen freely". He even noted that 50% redundancy is about what makes large crossword puzzles possible.

In 1951 he published "Prediction and Entropy of Printed English", which measured predictability directly: subjects saw a passage and guessed the next letter, and the number of guesses they needed revealed how predictable the text was. With context of up to 100 letters, he estimated the entropy at something of the order of one bit per letter, a redundancy of roughly 75%. That experimental design, predict the next symbol and measure the average surprise, is exactly how language models are trained and evaluated today. A model's cross-entropy per character is an upper bound on the true entropy of the text (cross-entropy ≥ entropy), so each better language model is a tighter estimate of the quantity Shannon was measuring with people and pencils.

The paper also quantified noise with a famous example: a channel sending 1,000 bits per second in which 1% of the received symbols are wrong does not deliver 990 bits per second of information, as one might first think, but about 919, because the receiver does not know which symbols are wrong. The 81-bit loss is the conditional entropy of what was sent given what was received, an early use of the quantities in section 8.

Maximum entropy and where the softmax comes from

Among all distributions consistent with what you know, the one with maximum entropy assumes the least. Maximising entropy subject to constraints on expected feature values, using Lagrange multipliers (math11), gives distributions of the form p(x) ∝ exp(θ·f(x)): the exponential family. With no constraint except normalisation, the answer is uniform; with a fixed mean and variance on the real line, it is the normal; with class-indicator features, it is the softmax. So the softmax is the least-committal distribution matching given expected scores, and logistic regression is a maximum-entropy model. Softmax temperature controls this entropy directly: dividing logits by T > 1 flattens the distribution (higher entropy, more diverse samples), T < 1 sharpens it, and T → 0 approaches argmax.

Calibration, proper scoring rules and why cross-entropy rewards honesty

Cross-entropy (log loss) is a strictly proper scoring rule: if the true probability of an event is p, the expected log loss of a forecaster who reports q is minimised only at q = p. Reporting more confidence than warranted, or less, always costs in expectation; this is Gibbs' inequality again. That is why models trained with cross-entropy tend towards calibrated probabilities on their training distribution, and why calibration failures (ml15) usually arise from overfitting, distribution shift or post-training changes rather than from the loss itself. The Brier score (squared error on probabilities) is also proper; accuracy is not, since it ignores confidence entirely.

Distillation and soft targets

In knowledge distillation, a small student is trained to match a large teacher's output distribution rather than one-hot labels, minimising KL(teacher‖student), equivalently the cross-entropy to the teacher's probabilities. A teacher's distribution such as (0.7 cat, 0.25 dog, 0.05 car) carries information a label does not: that this cat looks somewhat like a dog and nothing like a car. Hinton and colleagues' formulation divides both models' logits by a temperature T > 1 to soften the distributions so that these small probabilities matter, and multiplies the soft loss by T² to keep gradient sizes comparable. Distillation of language models works the same way over vocabulary distributions, and sequence-level variants train on the teacher's sampled outputs. Because it minimises forward KL, a distilled student is mode-covering with respect to the teacher.

Label smoothing as an entropy floor

Label smoothing replaces the one-hot target with (1 − ε) on the correct class and ε spread over the others. The target now has positive entropy, so the minimum achievable loss is that entropy rather than zero, and the optimal prediction is the smoothed target itself rather than infinitely confident logits. With ε = 0.1 over three classes, a target of (0.9, 0.05, 0.05) scored against a confident prediction (0.98, 0.01, 0.01) gives a cross-entropy of about 0.69 bits per example, which the model reduces by becoming less extreme. The effect is better calibration and a regularised logit scale; the cost is that it blurs the similarity structure between classes, which can make smoothed models worse teachers for distillation.

KL in variational inference and the ELBO

Exact Bayesian posteriors are usually intractable. Variational inference picks a simple family of distributions q and minimises the reverse KL(q(z)‖p(z | x)) to the true posterior. That KL cannot be computed directly because it involves the unknown evidence p(x), but rearranging shows log p(x) = ELBO + KL(q‖p(z | x)), where the ELBO (evidence lower bound) is computable. Since KL ≥ 0, the ELBO is a lower bound on the log-likelihood, and maximising it both fits the model and tightens the approximation. Variational autoencoders (dl10) maximise exactly this, and their loss splits into a reconstruction term and a KL term pulling the encoder's distribution towards a prior, the information-theoretic cost of the code.

Other divergences, and why there are so many

KL is the divergence that arises from likelihood and coding, but it is not the only way to compare distributions, and its asymmetry and infinite values when supports differ are sometimes unwelcome. The Jensen–Shannon divergence averages the two KLs to the mixture M = ½(P + Q): JS = ½KL(P‖M) + ½KL(Q‖M). It is symmetric, always finite (at most 1 bit), and its square root is a true distance; the original generative adversarial network objective minimises it, which is part of why GAN training struggles when the generator's and the data's distributions barely overlap, since JS then saturates and gives little gradient. Total variation distance, half the sum of absolute differences, is the largest possible difference in the probability the two assign to any event, and is easy to interpret. The Wasserstein (earth-mover's) distance measures how far probability mass must move to turn one distribution into the other, so it stays informative even for distributions with no overlap, which motivated Wasserstein GANs. Choosing a divergence is choosing which differences matter, the same lesson as forward versus reverse KL.

Estimating information quantities from samples

Entropy, KL and mutual information are defined for known distributions; in practice they must be estimated from data, which is harder than it looks. Plug-in estimates (count frequencies, then compute) are biased: with few samples per category, empirical entropy is too low and empirical mutual information too high, so many unrelated features appear informative on small datasets. For continuous high-dimensional variables, direct estimation is hopeless, and methods instead optimise bounds (InfoNCE and others), which have their own biases, such as InfoNCE's bound being capped at the log of the batch size. In RLHF, the KL to the reference model is estimated from sampled tokens as the average log-probability ratio, which is unbiased but noisy; several lower-variance estimators are used in practice. Treat any reported information quantity as an estimate with error bars.

Reading loss curves in nats, bits and perplexity

Training dashboards report cross-entropy in nats (natural logarithms), papers sometimes in bits per token or bits per byte, and evaluations in perplexity. Mixing them up produces confused comparisons. Convert explicitly: bits = nats × 1.443; perplexity = e^(loss in nats). Bits per byte normalises by the raw text length rather than token count, which makes models with different tokenizers comparable, and is the fairer metric when tokenizers differ. A loss improvement that looks tiny in nats can be large in perplexity: going from 2.3 to 2.2 nats per token takes perplexity from about 10.0 to 9.0.

Validation loss rising while accuracy improves

A common and confusing pattern: validation accuracy keeps improving while validation cross-entropy starts rising. The model is getting more examples right but becoming overconfident on the ones it gets wrong, and each confident mistake costs many nats. It is a calibration problem, typically late in training. Responses: early stopping on validation loss when probabilities matter downstream, more regularisation or label smoothing, or temperature scaling after training, which fixes calibration without changing the argmax (ml15). If only the ranking matters, accuracy or AUC (area under the ROC curve, ml7) may be the right stopping criterion instead.

KL implementation pitfalls

KL losses are easy to get subtly wrong. Library functions have conventions about argument order and log space: PyTorch's KLDivLoss expects the input as log-probabilities and the target as probabilities by default, and computes KL(target‖input); passing probabilities instead of log-probabilities, or swapping arguments, silently computes something else. Reductions matter too: averaging over every element instead of over the batch divides the KL by the number of classes. And computing log of softmax in two steps underflows for very negative logits, producing infinite or NaN losses; use log_softmax. A quick test is to compute the KL between a distribution and itself (should be 0) and between two hand-worked distributions such as this lesson's P and Q (should be 0.184 nats in one direction and 0.192 in the other).

Monitoring entropy in production models

The entropy of a model's predictions is a cheap, label-free monitoring signal. A sudden rise in average predictive entropy on live traffic suggests inputs unlike the training data (distribution shift); a collapse towards zero in a generative model's next-token entropy can signal repetition loops or a degenerate decoding configuration; and in RL fine-tuning, a falling policy entropy and a rising KL to the reference model together are the classic early sign of reward hacking. Entropy also drives cost-saving routing: requests on which a small model is confident (low entropy) can be answered directly, and uncertain ones escalated to a larger model.

Entropy and compression as data-quality signals

Pretraining-data pipelines use information measures as cheap filters. Text that compresses extremely well (very low entropy per character) is often boilerplate, repeated navigation menus or spam; text that barely compresses can be encoded data, random strings or corrupted pages. Ratios from a standard compressor, or the perplexity of a small reference language model, are used to drop documents at both extremes, and perplexity under a model trained on high-quality text is a common quality score. The same ideas flag degenerate model outputs: a generation whose token entropy collapses has probably fallen into a repetition loop. These filters are heuristics, not truths (low perplexity also favours bland, common text), so their thresholds should be checked on samples by people before a large run.

The public case, in production terms

Shannon's framing, that a model's quality is how few bits it needs to describe real data, is the working definition of progress in language modelling and the reason compression benchmarks are used to compare models. It also sets expectations: the loss cannot go below the entropy of the data, so as models improve, each further gain in cross-entropy gets harder and is worth more, and a training loss that drops far below what validation loss supports is memorisation, not discovery. When someone reports a perplexity, the Shannon questions apply: in what units, over what text, with which tokenizer, and compared with what estimate of the entropy that remains?

import numpy as np

def H(p):                       # entropy in bits
    p = np.asarray(p, float); p = p[p > 0]
    return -(p * np.log2(p)).sum()
def CE(p, q):                   # cross-entropy H(p, q) in bits
    p, q = np.asarray(p, float), np.asarray(q, float)
    return -(p * np.log2(q)).sum()
def KL(p, q):
    return CE(p, q) - H(p)

# 1. Entropy of simple distributions
for name, p in [("fair coin", [0.5, 0.5]), ("90/10 coin", [0.9, 0.1]),
                ("fair die", [1/6] * 6), ("(1/2, 1/4, 1/4)", [0.5, 0.25, 0.25])]:
    print(f"H({name}) = {H(p):.3f} bits")

# 2. A prefix code that achieves the entropy: 0, 10, 11 for (1/2, 1/4, 1/4)
lengths = np.array([1, 2, 2]); p = np.array([0.5, 0.25, 0.25])
print("average code length:", (p * lengths).sum(), "bits")

# 3. Cross-entropy and KL in both directions for two 3-outcome distributions
P = np.array([0.7, 0.2, 0.1]); Q = np.array([0.4, 0.4, 0.2])
print(f"H(P)={H(P):.4f}  H(Q)={H(Q):.4f}  H(P,Q)={CE(P, Q):.4f}  H(Q,P)={CE(Q, P):.4f}")
print(f"KL(P||Q)={KL(P, Q):.4f}  KL(Q||P)={KL(Q, P):.4f} bits;"
      f"  in nats: {KL(P, Q) * np.log(2):.4f}, {KL(Q, P) * np.log(2):.4f}")

# 4. Cross-entropy loss of a classifier = average -log2 p(correct class); perplexity = 2^CE
probs_correct = np.array([0.9, 0.6, 0.3, 0.95, 0.05])
ce = -np.log2(probs_correct).mean()
print(f"mean cross-entropy {ce:.3f} bits/example, perplexity {2 ** ce:.2f}")

# 5. Forward vs reverse KL: fit one Gaussian to a two-bump target on a grid
x = np.linspace(-8, 8, 4001); dx = x[1] - x[0]
gauss = lambda m, s: np.exp(-0.5 * ((x - m) / s) ** 2) / (s * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))
p = 0.5 * gauss(-3, 1) + 0.5 * gauss(3, 1)
kl = lambda a, b: np.sum(a * (np.log(a + 1e-300) - np.log(b + 1e-300))) * dx
best_fwd = min(((m, s) for m in np.linspace(-4, 4, 81) for s in np.linspace(0.5, 5, 46)),
               key=lambda ms: kl(p, gauss(*ms)))
best_rev = min(((m, s) for m in np.linspace(-4, 4, 81) for s in np.linspace(0.5, 5, 46)),
               key=lambda ms: kl(gauss(*ms), p))
print("forward KL(p||q) best q: mean %.1f, sd %.1f  (covers both bumps)" % best_fwd)
print("reverse KL(q||p) best q: mean %.1f, sd %.1f  (locks onto one bump)" % best_rev)

# 6. Mutual information of the spam table (math1): I = H(label) - H(label | word)
joint = np.array([[120, 180], [35, 665]]) / 1000
pl, pw = joint.sum(1), joint.sum(0)
mi = sum(joint[i, j] * np.log2(joint[i, j] / (pl[i] * pw[j])) for i in range(2) for j in range(2))
print(f"H(spam) = {H(pl):.4f} bits, I(spam; 'free') = {mi:.4f} bits")

  1. Entropies: fair coin 1.000 bit, 90/10 coin 0.469, fair die 2.585, (½, ¼, ¼) 1.500.
  2. The code 0, 10, 11 averages exactly 1.5 bits: it reaches the entropy because every probability is a power of ½.
  3. For P = (0.7, 0.2, 0.1) and Q = (0.4, 0.4, 0.2): H(P) = 1.1568, H(Q) = 1.5219, H(P, Q) = 1.4219, H(Q, P) = 1.7990, KL(P‖Q) = 0.2651 and KL(Q‖P) = 0.2771 bits (0.1838 and 0.1920 nats).
  4. The five-example classifier: 1.404 bits per example and perplexity 2.65, with most of the loss from the single 0.05 prediction.
  5. Fitting one Gaussian to two bumps: forward KL gives mean 0.0 and standard deviation 3.2 (covers both); reverse KL gives mean −3.0 and standard deviation 1.0 (one bump).
  6. Spam table: H(spam) = 0.8813 bits and I(spam; "free") = 0.1304 bits.

Diagnose: the distilled model that lost its long tail

A team distils a large classifier with 1,000 product categories into a small one. They train the student on the teacher's argmax labels (hard labels) with cross-entropy. Overall accuracy is close to the teacher's, but on rare categories the student is much worse, and its predicted probabilities are far more confident than the teacher's. (a) Explain in information-theoretic terms what was lost. (b) Propose a better objective, with the role of temperature. (c) How would you check that the student's probabilities are trustworthy?

Answer

(a) Hard labels keep only the teacher's top choice: a one-hot target with zero entropy. The teacher's full distribution carried extra information on every example, such as that an item is 60% "running shoes" and 30% "trail shoes", which is exactly the similarity structure that helps rare categories. Training on one-hot targets also pushes the student towards infinitely confident predictions, since the minimum cross-entropy against a one-hot target is zero, hence the overconfidence. Rare classes suffer most because they appear as argmax labels rarely, while they appear as secondary probability mass in the teacher's distribution often.

(b) Minimise KL(teacher‖student), equivalently cross-entropy against the teacher's probabilities, computed after dividing both models' logits by a temperature T > 1 (for example 2 to 4). Softening raises the entropy of the targets so that small probabilities on related classes produce noticeable gradients; multiply this loss by T² to keep gradient magnitudes comparable, and optionally add a small hard-label cross-entropy term on true labels if they exist. Because forward KL is mode-covering, the student is pushed to keep probability on every class the teacher considers plausible, which is what the long tail needs.

(c) Measure calibration on a held-out labelled set: reliability diagrams and expected calibration error per frequency band of categories (ml15). Compare the student's and teacher's predictive entropy distributions on live traffic. Report validation cross-entropy and accuracy separately for head and tail categories, since aggregate metrics hide the tail. If calibration is still off, fit a temperature on validation data after training.
