In the summer of 1944, V-1 flying bombs were falling on London, and people on the ground noticed patterns. Some neighbourhoods seemed to be hit again and again while others were spared. The natural conclusion was that the Germans could aim the bombs at particular districts, which mattered enormously: if they could, defences and evacuations should be concentrated there. A 31-year-old actuary at the Prudential, R. D. Clarke, asked a different question. If the bombs were falling completely at random over south London, how clustered would they look anyway?

To answer it he needed to know what randomness looks like as a count: if 537 bombs land at random over 576 equal squares, how many squares should get no bomb, one bomb, two, three? That is a question about a probability distribution, specifically the Poisson distribution, and when Clarke wrote the predicted counts next to the observed ones they matched almost perfectly. The clusters were exactly as clustered as chance alone would make them. The apparent targeting was the human eye finding patterns in noise.

Engineers face Clarke's question every week. Seven pages last night against a usual two: incident or bad luck? A classifier scored 19 out of 20 on a test set: is it really 95% accurate? Requests arrive at 500 per second on average: how often does a 10-millisecond window see 15 of them, and how big should the queue be? Latency averages 120 ms: what fraction of users wait over a second? Each question has a quick, defensible answer once you know which distribution describes the quantity, and a misleading one if you guess, or assume everything is a bell curve.

Distributions also sit underneath machine learning itself. When you choose mean squared error you are assuming errors are normally distributed; when you choose log loss you are assuming each label is a biased coin flip; a language model's output is a probability distribution over tokens that you sample from. Knowing the handful of distributions behind these choices makes losses, sampling and evaluation make sense rather than being recipes.

This lesson starts by turning outcomes into numbers (random variables), then introduces the three ways to describe how probability is spread across those numbers: the probability mass function, the density and the cumulative distribution function. It then builds five distributions in order, each from the one before: Bernoulli (one yes/no trial), binomial (many trials), Poisson (many trials, rare successes), exponential (the waiting time between Poisson events) and normal (the bell curve). Each comes with a worked example by hand. It ends with how to choose a distribution, how to sample from one, which loss function each implies, and where the textbook shapes break in production.

In math1, outcomes were things like "heads", "spam" or "the pair (3, 4)". Most of the time you care about a number attached to the outcome: how many heads, how many emails flagged, how many milliseconds a request took. A random variable is exactly that attachment, a rule that turns each outcome into a number. Its name is a little misleading: it is not a variable in the algebra sense, and it is not random by itself; it is a function applied to a random outcome. We write random variables with capital letters (X, Y, T) and particular values they take with small letters (x, k, t), so P(X = 3) reads "the probability that the random variable X comes out equal to 3".

The distribution of a random variable is how its probability is spread across the possible values. A good mental picture is a pile of sand weighing exactly one kilogram, spread along a number line. Where the sand is piled high, values are likely; where there is little, they are rare. For a discrete variable, one that takes separate values like 0, 1, 2, the sand sits in little heaps on those values, and the weight of each heap is the probability of that value. For a continuous variable, one that can take any value in a range like a latency of 117.348 ms, the sand is spread smoothly, and only the amount over a stretch of the line is meaningful; the amount over a single point is zero.

The analogy breaks in one useful place. With real sand you can always ask "how high is the pile at this exact spot?", and for a continuous variable that height (the density) exists, but it is not a probability. It is probability per unit of x, the way kilograms per metre is not kilograms. That is why a density can be larger than 1 without anything being wrong, and why "the probability that latency is exactly 120 ms" is zero.

A small family of distributions covers a surprising share of real problems, and they are related to each other in a chain you can follow:

  1. Bernoulli: one yes/no trial with success probability p. Did this user click? Is this email spam?
  2. Binomial: the number of successes in n independent Bernoulli trials. How many of 1,000 users clicked? How many of 20 test cases did the model get right?
  3. Poisson: the binomial when n is huge and p tiny, so you just know the average count λ. Errors per hour, pages per night, bombs per square.
  4. Exponential: if events arrive Poisson-style in time, the gap between consecutive events. Time until the next request.
  5. Normal: the bell curve, which appears whenever many small independent effects add up, and which approximates the binomial and the Poisson when their counts are large.

Each distribution is really a family, and the member is picked by one or two numbers called parameters: p for Bernoulli; n and p for binomial; λ (lambda, the average count) for Poisson; λ (the rate) for exponential; μ (mu, the centre) and σ (sigma, the spread) for normal. Fitting a distribution to data means choosing parameter values; math5 shows how.

Two ideas from math3 are needed loosely here and defined properly there. The mean or expected value of a random variable is its long-run average: the balance point of the sand pile. The standard deviation is roughly the typical distance of values from the mean, a measure of spread in the same units as the variable. For now, read "mean λ" as "on average λ" and "standard deviation 15 ms" as "values typically land within about 15 ms of the centre".

A reader who stops here should hold this picture: a random variable is a number that depends on chance; its distribution is the shape of that chance; five shapes, connected to each other, cover most of what you will meet; and the right shape is decided by how the number is generated (one trial, many trials, rare events, waiting times, sums of many small effects), not by habit.

1. Random variables, worked on two dice

Roll two fair dice. The sample space has 36 equally likely ordered pairs (math1). Define the random variable S = the sum of the two faces. S turns each pair into a number: (1, 1) ↦ 2, (2, 5) ↦ 7, (6, 6) ↦ 12. Several outcomes map to the same value; S = 7 for six pairs. The probability that S takes a value is the total probability of all outcomes that map to it:

s23456789101112
P(S = s)1/362/363/364/365/366/365/364/363/362/361/36

The probabilities add to 36/36 = 1, as they must. The same experiment supports other random variables: M = the larger face, D = the absolute difference, or I = 1 if the faces match and 0 otherwise. That last kind, a variable that is 1 when an event happens and 0 when it does not, is an indicator variable. Indicators are simple but powerful: the number of spam emails in a batch is the sum of one indicator per email, and that one observation is how the binomial distribution will be built.

2. Describing a distribution: PMF and CDF

The table above is a probability mass function (PMF): for a discrete random variable X, the function p(k) = P(X = k) listing the probability of each possible value. Every PMF satisfies two conditions: each p(k) is between 0 and 1, and they add to 1. The set of values with positive probability is called the support.

The cumulative distribution function (CDF) is the running total: F(x) = P(X ≤ x). For the dice sum, F(4) = P(S ≤ 4) = 1/36 + 2/36 + 3/36 = 6/36. The CDF answers range questions with one subtraction: P(a < X ≤ b) = F(b) − F(a). P(5 ≤ S ≤ 9) = P(4 < S ≤ 9) = F(9) − F(4) = 30/36 − 6/36 = 24/36 = 2/3. A CDF always starts at 0 on the far left, ends at 1 on the far right and never decreases. It is the one description that works for every kind of random variable, which is why statistical libraries and tests are built around it.

3. Bernoulli: one yes/no trial

The simplest random variable has two values. A Bernoulli random variable with parameter p, written X ~ Bernoulli(p) (the tilde reads "is distributed as"), equals 1 with probability p and 0 with probability 1 − p. "1" is called success whatever it means: a click, a fraudulent transaction, a correct prediction. Its PMF fits on one line: P(X = 1) = p, P(X = 0) = 1 − p, or compactly P(X = x) = p^x (1 − p)^(1 − x) for x ∈ {0, 1}, a form that will matter later because its logarithm, x log p + (1 − x) log(1 − p), is exactly log loss (math5, math12).

Its mean is p: if 3% of visitors click, a single visitor's click indicator averages 0.03 over many visitors. Its spread depends on p: a coin with p = 0.5 is as uncertain as a yes/no trial can be, while p = 0.99 is nearly certain. Math3 shows the variance is p(1 − p), which is largest at p = 0.5 (0.25) and small near 0 or 1. Every binary classifier models the label as Bernoulli, with p predicted from the input.

4. Binomial: counting successes in n trials

Run n independent Bernoulli(p) trials and count the successes. That count, X, has a binomial distribution, X ~ Binomial(n, p). To find P(X = k), think about one particular arrangement first: k successes followed by n − k failures. Because the trials are independent, its probability is p multiplied k times and (1 − p) multiplied n − k times: p^k (1 − p)^(n − k). Every other arrangement with exactly k successes, in a different order, has the same probability, and there are C(n, k) such arrangements (choose which k of the n positions are successes, from math1). Adding them all:

P(X = k) = C(n, k) · p^k · (1 − p)^(n − k), for k = 0, 1, …, n

Worked example: spam in a batch. 30% of incoming emails are spam, independently. In a batch of 10, what is the chance of exactly 2 spam? C(10, 2) = 45; 0.3² = 0.09; 0.7⁸ ≈ 0.0576. Product: 45 × 0.09 × 0.0576 ≈ 0.233. About a quarter of batches have exactly two. The mean count is np = 3, and the most likely single value is also 3.

Worked example: how noisy is a small test set? This is the binomial question that matters most in ML. Suppose a classifier's true accuracy, on the whole population it will see, is 90%. You evaluate it on 20 examples. The number it gets right is Binomial(20, 0.9) if the examples are a random sample. The chance it gets all 20 right is 0.9²⁰ ≈ 0.122. The chance of exactly 19 is C(20, 19) × 0.9¹⁹ × 0.1 = 20 × 0.135 × 0.1 ≈ 0.270. So P(19 or 20 correct) ≈ 0.392: a 90%-accurate model reports "95% or better" on 20 examples about two times in five. Conversely, a model that truly scores 95% could easily show 17/20 (85%). Small evaluation sets give estimates so noisy that differences of several points between models mean nothing. Math4 shows the noise shrinks like 1/√n, and math6 turns it into a confidence interval.

The binomial has two assumptions worth saying aloud whenever you use it. The trials must be independent: 20 test examples that are near-duplicates of each other are not 20 independent trials, and the real uncertainty is larger. And p must be the same for every trial: conversion rates that differ by country make the overall count more spread out than a single binomial predicts.

A close cousin is worth a paragraph. The geometric distribution counts how many independent trials it takes to get the first success: P(K = k) = (1 − p)^(k − 1) · p, failure k − 1 times then success. If a flaky test passes 80% of the time and you rerun it until it fails (p = 0.2 for "fails"), the chance it first fails on the third run is 0.8² × 0.2 = 0.128, and on average it takes 1/p = 5 runs. Retry budgets are sized with this distribution.

5. Poisson: counts of rare events

Many counts have no natural number of trials. How many errors will the service log in the next hour? There are millions of requests, each with a tiny chance of erroring, and you rarely know n or p separately, only the average count. The Poisson distribution handles exactly this. Here is where it comes from. Chop the hour into n tiny slices, so small that each contains at most one error, with probability p = λ/n each, where λ (lambda) is the average count per hour. The count is then Binomial(n, λ/n). Let n grow without limit (the slices get infinitely thin). Two pieces of school-level limits do the work: C(n, k)/n^k approaches 1/k!, and (1 − λ/n)^n approaches e^(−λ), where e ≈ 2.718 is the base of natural logarithms. The result is:

P(X = k) = e^(−λ) · λ^k / k!, for k = 0, 1, 2, …

One parameter, λ, is both the mean count and (unusually) the variance. You can check the limit numerically: Binomial(1000, 0.002) gives P(0) = 0.1351, P(2) = 0.2709, while Poisson(2) gives 0.1353 and 0.2707.

Worked example: on-call pages. Your team averages λ = 2 pages per night, independently. The Poisson probabilities are:

k pages01234567+
P(X = k)0.1350.2710.2710.1800.0900.0360.0120.0045

To get P(0): e^(−2) ≈ 0.1353. P(1) = e^(−2) × 2 / 1 ≈ 0.2707. P(2) = e^(−2) × 4 / 2 ≈ 0.2707. P(3) = e^(−2) × 8 / 6 ≈ 0.1804. Each term is the previous times λ/k, which is the fastest way to compute them by hand. A quiet night with no pages happens about one night in seven. Five or more: 1 − (0.135 + 0.271 + 0.271 + 0.180 + 0.090) ≈ 0.053, so about once every 19 nights, by chance alone. Seven or more: about 0.0045, once in 220 nights. If you see seven, it is worth asking whether something changed; if you see five, it is probably just Tuesday.

The Poisson's assumptions are the binomial's in the limit: events independent of each other, and a constant rate across the window. Real error counts often violate both: one bad deploy causes 50 errors (not independent), and traffic doubles at noon (not constant). The tell-tale sign is a variance much larger than the mean, called overdispersion, covered in the deep section.

6. The public case: Clarke's flying bombs

Clarke chose an area of 144 square kilometres of south London, where the overall density of hits looked roughly even, and divided it into 576 squares of a quarter square kilometre each. In that area, 537 V-1s had landed. If they had been falling at random, independently and with no preference for any square, the number of hits in any one square would be approximately Poisson: many bombs (537 trials) each with a small chance (1/576) of landing in that particular square, giving λ = 537/576 ≈ 0.932 hits per square.

The expected number of squares with k hits is 576 × P(X = k). For k = 0: 576 × e^(−0.932) ≈ 576 × 0.3937 ≈ 226.7. For k = 1: multiply by λ/1, ≈ 211.4. For k = 2: multiply by λ/2, ≈ 98.5. For k = 3: multiply by λ/3, ≈ 30.6. For k = 4: ≈ 7.1. And the remaining 1.6 squares for 5 or more. Clarke's observed counts were 229, 211, 93, 35, 7 and 1.

If the bombs were aimed at particular districts, you would see too many empty squares (the spared areas) and too many with 4 or more hits (the targets): the distribution would be more spread out than Poisson. It was not. Clarke ran a chi-squared goodness-of-fit test (math6 explains it), which gave p ≈ 0.88: data like these are entirely typical of random scatter. His 1946 paper became a textbook example of the Poisson distribution, and the lesson generalises: random events cluster. Before you treat a cluster as a signal, compute how much clustering randomness alone produces. A 2019 re-analysis by Shaw and Shaw using the original bomb-damage maps found a similar fit for Clarke's area (p = 0.70) but noted that over a wider region the density was not uniform, with more hits in south London. Clarke's careful choice of a region with a roughly constant rate was what made the Poisson model appropriate, which is the constant-rate assumption in action.

7. Continuous random variables: densities and areas

Latency, temperature, a model's predicted score and the time until the next request can take any value in a range. For these, P(X = x) is zero for every single x: there are infinitely many possible values, and if each had positive probability the total would be infinite. Instead a continuous random variable has a probability density function (PDF) f(x), a curve with two properties: it is never negative, and the total area under it is 1. The probability that X lands between a and b is the area under f between a and b:

P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = area under f from a to b = F(b) − F(a)

(In calculus notation the area is the integral ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx; math10 covers the calculus, but nothing in this lesson requires computing an integral by hand, because every distribution here has a CDF with a simple formula or a table.)

The simplest example is the uniform distribution on [0, 1]: the density is 1 on that interval and 0 outside, so P(0.2 ≤ X ≤ 0.5) = 0.3, the width of the interval. Every random-number generator starts from this distribution, and section 11 shows how to turn uniform numbers into any other distribution. The uniform on [0, 0.5] has density 2: the same area of 1 squeezed into half the width. That is the clearest demonstration that a density's height is not a probability.

8. Exponential: the wait between random events

If events arrive independently at a constant rate λ per unit time (a Poisson process), how long do you wait for the next one? Call the waiting time T. Waiting more than t means zero events in a window of length t, and the number of events in that window is Poisson with mean λt. So P(T > t) = P(zero events) = e^(−λt). That one line defines the exponential distribution. Its CDF is F(t) = 1 − e^(−λt), its density is f(t) = λe^(−λt) for t ≥ 0, its mean is 1/λ, and its standard deviation is also 1/λ.

Worked example: gaps between requests. A service receives 5 requests per second on average, as a Poisson process. The mean gap is 1/5 = 0.2 s. The chance of a gap longer than half a second: e^(−5 × 0.5) = e^(−2.5) ≈ 0.082. The median gap, where P(T > t) = 0.5, is ln 2 / λ ≈ 0.693/5 ≈ 0.139 s, shorter than the mean: the exponential is right-skewed, with many short gaps and a few long ones pulling the average up. That skew is why autoscaling based on average traffic underestimates bursts: short gaps cluster.

The exponential has a famous and slightly strange property: it is memoryless. If you have already waited s seconds, the chance you wait at least t more is P(T > s + t | T > s) = e^(−λ(s+t)) / e^(−λs) = e^(−λt), the same as for a fresh wait. Having waited tells you nothing. For independent arrivals that is exactly right: requests do not "know" there has been a lull. For things that wear out it is exactly wrong. A five-year-old hard disk is more likely to fail tomorrow than a new one; a user who has stayed three years is less likely to churn this month than a new one. Applying the exponential to lifetimes gives confidently wrong answers, and lifetimes are usually modelled with the Weibull or lognormal instead.

9. The normal distribution

The normal or Gaussian distribution, the bell curve, is described by two parameters: its mean μ, where the peak sits, and its standard deviation σ, which sets the width. We write X ~ N(μ, σ²) (the second parameter is conventionally written as the variance σ², the square of the standard deviation; math3 defines it). The density is

f(x) = (1 / (σ√(2π))) · e^(−(x − μ)² / (2σ²))

You will almost never evaluate that by hand, but it is worth reading once. The exponent −(x − μ)²/(2σ²) is zero at the mean and falls off as the square of the distance from it, which is what makes the tails thin out so fast. The fraction in front just makes the total area equal 1.

The key practical fact is that every normal distribution is the same shape, stretched and shifted. Measure distance from the mean in units of standard deviations, the z-score z = (x − μ)/σ, and any normal becomes the standard normal Z ~ N(0, 1). One table of Z (or one function, usually written Φ, capital phi, for its CDF) answers every normal question. The proportions to memorise are the 68-95-99.7 rule: about 68.3% of values lie within 1σ of the mean, 95.4% within 2σ, and 99.7% within 3σ. Two more numbers appear everywhere in statistics: 95% lies within ±1.96σ, and 5% lies above +1.645σ.

Worked example: latency. Suppose (for now) inference latency is normal with μ = 120 ms and σ = 15 ms. What fraction of requests take over 150 ms? z = (150 − 120)/15 = 2. P(Z > 2) = (1 − 0.954)/2 ≈ 0.023, about 2.3% of requests. What latency do 99.9% of requests beat? The standard normal's 99.9th percentile is z ≈ 3.09, so 120 + 3.09 × 15 ≈ 166 ms. The prod section explains why, for real latencies, that second answer is badly wrong.

Why does the normal appear so often? Because of the central limit theorem (math4): when you add up many small independent random effects, the total is approximately normal whatever the shapes of the individual effects. Measurement error is a sum of many small disturbances; a person's height reflects many genes; an average over many users is a sum divided by n. That is also why the binomial and Poisson look normal when their counts are large: a binomial is a sum of n Bernoulli indicators. Binomial(1000, 0.03), the number of clicks among 1,000 visitors at a 3% rate, is close to a normal with mean 30 and standard deviation √(1000 × 0.03 × 0.97) ≈ 5.4, which is the approximation A/B testing relies on (math7).

Two more properties make the normal convenient. Shift and scale a normal and you get a normal (that is what made the z-score work). And the sum of independent normals is normal, with the means adding and the variances adding. Most other distributions do not have these closure properties.

10. Choosing a distribution, and the loss it implies

The right distribution follows from how the number is generated. Ask four questions. Is it a yes/no outcome? Bernoulli. A count of successes out of a known number of tries? Binomial. A count of events in a window with no fixed maximum? Poisson, if events are independent and the rate steady. A waiting time? Exponential, if arrivals are independent and the thing does not age. A sum or average of many small independent pieces? Normal. If the honest answer is "none of these", say so, and measure the empirical distribution.

The same choice is made, often unknowingly, every time you pick a loss function. Training a model by minimising a loss is usually equivalent to picking the parameters under which the observed labels are most probable, called maximum likelihood (math5). Under that view, squared error is the right loss when errors are normal with constant spread; log loss (binary cross-entropy) is the right loss when each label is a Bernoulli trial whose p the model predicts; categorical cross-entropy is the right loss when the label is one of K classes, a categorical distribution (the K-outcome version of the Bernoulli, which is what a softmax layer outputs); and Poisson loss is the right loss for counts. Ml5 compares these losses in detail, and math12 shows cross-entropy from the information-theory side. When a regression model trained with squared error predicts negative numbers of orders, the loss and the data's distribution disagree.

11. Sampling from a distribution

Computers generate uniform random numbers; every other distribution is built from them. The cleanest method is inverse CDF sampling: draw u uniformly from (0, 1) and find the x where F(x) = u. Values of x where F rises steeply (where the density is high) catch many u values, so they are sampled often. For the exponential, F(t) = 1 − e^(−λt) = u gives t = −ln(1 − u)/λ, and since 1 − u is also uniform, t = −ln(u)/λ works too. With u = 0.5 and λ = 5, t = 0.693/5 ≈ 0.139 s, the median, as it should be.

For a discrete distribution the same idea is a walk along the cumulative probabilities. To sample from probabilities (0.5, 0.3, 0.2) for tokens A, B, C, draw u; if u < 0.5 return A, else if u < 0.8 return B, else C. That is precisely how a language model picks its next token from the softmax output; temperature and top-p (lm8) only reshape the probabilities before this step. The normal has no simple inverse CDF formula, so libraries use special methods such as the Box–Muller transform, which turns two uniforms into two independent standard normals.

Poisson processes combine and split cleanly

Two independent Poisson processes added together form a Poisson process whose rate is the sum: if service A sends 3 requests per second and service B 2, the combined stream arriving at the database is Poisson with rate 5. This is called superposition. The reverse also holds: if each event of a rate-λ Poisson process is independently kept with probability q (say each request is a cache miss with probability 0.1), the kept events form a Poisson process with rate qλ. This is called thinning. Together they explain why Poisson arrivals are a reasonable first model for traffic aggregated from many independent users, and why the model degrades when users are not independent (a push notification sent to everyone at 9:00 produces a synchronised spike).

Overdispersion and the negative binomial

A Poisson forces the variance to equal the mean. Real counts often have variance several times larger, because the rate itself varies: some hours are busier, some users are heavier, some deploys are worse. If the rate λ is itself random, drawn from a gamma distribution (a flexible family of positive continuous distributions), the resulting counts follow a negative binomial distribution, with an extra parameter controlling how much bigger the variance is than the mean. For alerting and forecasting this matters a lot: with mean 4 errors per hour, a Poisson says 12 or more happens with probability about 0.001, while an overdispersed process with variance 20 makes it many times more common. Fitting the simpler model leads to alerts that fire constantly and forecast intervals that are too narrow. Generalised linear models (Poisson regression, negative binomial regression) let the mean depend on features while keeping the right noise model.

Heavy tails: when the mean is a bad summary

In a normal distribution, values beyond 5σ essentially never happen (about 6 in 10 million, counting both tails). In heavy-tailed distributions they are routine. File sizes, request latencies, the number of followers per account and the frequency of words follow distributions such as the lognormal (whose logarithm is normal) or the Pareto (a power law, where P(X > x) falls like x^(−α)). Two consequences matter for engineers. First, the mean is dominated by rare huge values, so it is unstable and a poor description of a typical case; report medians and high percentiles. Second, for a Pareto with α ≤ 2 the variance is infinite, so standard-deviation-based reasoning, including the normal approximation for averages, converges very slowly or not at all. Word frequencies following a power law (Zipf's law) is why a tokenizer's vocabulary has a long tail of rare tokens and why rare tokens get poor embeddings.

The normal approximation and its limits

Binomial(n, p) is close to normal when both np and n(1 − p) are reasonably large (a common rule of thumb is at least 10). With a 0.5% conversion rate and 1,000 users, np = 5, the distribution is skewed and the normal approximation puts noticeable probability on negative counts. That is one reason A/B tests on rare conversions need very large samples, and why exact binomial methods or the Poisson are used for rare events. When a discrete count is approximated by a continuous normal, a continuity correction (treating P(X ≤ 5) as P(Y ≤ 5.5) for the normal Y) improves accuracy, because the bar at 5 covers the interval from 4.5 to 5.5.

Mixtures: when one distribution is really several

Latency is often bimodal: cache hits around 5 ms, cache misses around 80 ms. No single named distribution fits; a mixture does. A mixture density is a weighted sum of component densities, f(x) = π₁f₁(x) + π₂f₂(x) with weights adding to 1, which is just the law of total probability from math1 applied to densities: the overall distribution is the cache-hit distribution with weight P(hit) plus the miss distribution with weight P(miss). Gaussian mixture models (ml12) fit such data, and mixture thinking explains surprises such as an average latency that describes no real request.

Distributions over distributions, and families with shared structure

Bernoulli, binomial, Poisson, exponential, normal and categorical all belong to the exponential family: their densities can be written as h(x)·exp(η·T(x) − A(η)) for some simple functions. (Despite the name this is broader than the exponential distribution.) Membership is why each has a matching loss with a clean gradient (prediction minus target, as dl1 shows for softmax with cross-entropy) and why each has a convenient prior for Bayesian updating: the Beta distribution for a Bernoulli's p, the gamma for a Poisson's λ, the normal for a normal's mean. Math5 uses the Beta–Bernoulli pair to do maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation by hand. A Beta distribution is itself a distribution over probabilities, which is the first example of a distribution whose values are parameters of another distribution.

Sampling at scale

Production sampling raises issues textbooks skip. Seeding matters for reproducibility, and NumPy recommends independent generator objects (np.random.default_rng(seed)) per worker rather than a shared global state, because parallel workers drawing from copies of the same seed produce identical "random" data. Sampling from a categorical over a 100,000-token vocabulary is done on the graphics processor (GPU) with a cumulative sum and a search, and numerical precision in that cumulative sum (float16 accumulates error) can make the last tokens unreachable or over-represented. For very large categories with fixed probabilities, the alias method samples in constant time after linear-time setup.

Latency is not normal, and percentiles are the fix

The most common distribution mistake in production is assuming latency is normal. Real latency distributions have a floor (the fastest possible path), a hump for typical requests, and a long right tail from garbage-collection pauses, cache misses, lock contention, retries and noisy neighbours. The mean and standard deviation of such a distribution are dominated by the tail, so "mean 120 ms, σ 15 ms" and a normal-based p99.9 of 166 ms can coexist with a real p99.9 of 900 ms. Production systems therefore record latency in histograms (Prometheus histograms, HDR, high-dynamic-range, histograms) and report percentiles directly: p50, p90, p99, p99.9. Percentiles also do not average: the mean of per-host p99s is not the fleet p99, so aggregate histograms, not percentiles. The tail matters more as systems fan out: if a page calls 100 backends in parallel and waits for all of them, each backend's p99 becomes the page's typical case, since 1 − 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 63% of page loads hit at least one slow call.

Alert thresholds on counts

Count-based alerts (errors per minute, failed logins per hour) are a direct application of the Poisson tail. If the baseline is 2 per interval, an alert at 5 fires by chance about 5% of intervals, which at one interval per minute is 75 false alerts a day. A threshold at 9 (P ≈ 0.0002 under Poisson(2)) fires by chance about once every three days. But real counts are overdispersed, so the realised false-alarm rate is higher than the Poisson calculation says; validate thresholds on a few weeks of history, and prefer alerting on ratios (errors / requests) so the threshold scales with traffic and the binomial, not a fixed Poisson, is the reference.

Traffic is a Poisson process only on a calm day

Capacity planning often starts from Poisson arrivals because queueing formulas assume them. Real traffic departs from it in predictable ways: diurnal and weekly cycles (the rate is not constant), synchronised clients (cron jobs, push notifications, cache expiries at the same second), and retries that turn one failure into several correlated requests. Each makes arrivals burstier than Poisson, so queues and tail latencies are worse than the model predicts. A practical check is to compare the variance-to-mean ratio of per-second request counts within a stable hour: near 1 is Poisson-like; much larger means plan for bursts and add jitter to anything that can synchronise.

Small evaluation sets in model work

The binomial calculation in section 4 is a frequent source of wasted effort in ML teams: a prompt change that moves a large language model (LLM) eval from 41/50 to 44/50 is often celebrated, but under a binomial with p around 0.85, the standard deviation of the count is about √(50 × 0.85 × 0.15) ≈ 2.5, so a 3-point swing is barely more than one standard deviation of noise. Teams that track many small evals without accounting for this chase noise, and lm14 covers how to size eval sets. The quick sanity check is to compute √(np(1 − p)) before reading a difference.

The public case, in production terms

Clarke's flying-bomb analysis is the template for every "is this cluster real?" question in operations: incidents clustered on one team, fraud clustered in one region, failures clustered on one rack. Compute what random scatter would produce first. His care in choosing a region with a constant rate is the other half of the lesson: the Shaw and Shaw re-analysis found the density was not uniform across a wider area, so a Poisson test over a region with varying rate would wrongly report clustering. In production terms, compare like with like: test for clustering within a period and segment where the baseline rate is steady, or model the varying rate explicitly.

Goal: check each distribution against its formula by simulation: binomial noise in a 20-example test set, the Poisson as a limit of the binomial, Clarke's expected counts, exponential gaps from uniform numbers, and the 68-95-99.7 rule. NumPy only (gpt_env); save as math2.py.

import numpy as np
from math import comb, exp, factorial

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N = 200_000

# 1. Binomial: a 90%-accurate model scored on 20 test examples
correct = rng.binomial(n=20, p=0.9, size=N)
print("P(>=19 correct) sim:", (correct >= 19).mean().round(4),
      " formula:", round(0.9**20 + 20 * 0.9**19 * 0.1, 4))

# 2. Poisson as the limit of a binomial with large n, small p (n*p = 2)
pois = lambda k, lam: exp(-lam) * lam**k / factorial(k)
for k in range(5):
    b = comb(1000, k) * 0.002**k * 0.998**(1000 - k)
    print(f"k={k}: binomial(1000, 0.002)={b:.4f}  poisson(2)={pois(k, 2):.4f}")

# 3. Clarke's flying bombs: expected squares under a Poisson with lambda = 537/576
lam = 537 / 576
observed = [229, 211, 93, 35, 7, 1]
expected = [576 * pois(k, lam) for k in range(5)]
expected.append(576 - sum(expected))                 # "5 and over"
for k, (o, e) in enumerate(zip(observed, expected)):
    print(f"{k if k < 5 else '5+'} hits: observed {o:3d}  expected {e:6.2f}")
# Scatter 537 bombs uniformly at random over 576 squares and count again
hits = np.bincount(rng.integers(0, 576, 537), minlength=576)
print("one random scatter :", np.bincount(np.minimum(hits, 5), minlength=6))

# 4. Exponential gaps via the inverse CDF, and memorylessness
u = rng.random(N)
gaps = -np.log(u) / 5.0                               # 5 requests per second
print("mean gap:", gaps.mean().round(4), " P(gap>0.5):", (gaps > 0.5).mean().round(4),
      " formula:", round(exp(-2.5), 4))
print("P(gap>0.7 | gap>0.2):", ((gaps > 0.7).sum() / (gaps > 0.2).sum()).round(4))

# 5. Normal: the 68-95-99.7 rule for latency ~ N(120 ms, 15 ms)
lat = rng.normal(120, 15, N)
for k in (1, 2, 3):
    print(f"within {k} sd: {(np.abs(lat - 120) < 15 * k).mean():.4f}")
print("P(latency > 150 ms):", (lat > 150).mean().round(4))

What to look for (seeded output from the test run):

  1. P(19 or 20 correct) for a 90% model: 0.3919 simulated, 0.3917 by formula. A small test set flatters or punishes a model by several points routinely.
  2. Binomial(1000, 0.002) and Poisson(2) agree to three decimals at every k from 0 to 4: the Poisson really is the binomial's limit.
  3. Clarke's expected counts reproduce: 226.74, 211.39, 98.54, 30.62, 7.14 and 1.57. Then one random scatter of 537 bombs over 576 squares gives counts like 218, 220, 101, 33, 4, 0: pure randomness produces Clarke's pattern, including squares hit three or four times.
  4. Exponential gaps made from uniform numbers with −ln(u)/5 have mean 0.200 s, and P(gap > 0.5) is 0.082, matching e^(−2.5). Memorylessness: P(gap > 0.7 | gap > 0.2) ≈ 0.081, the same as P(gap > 0.5).
  5. Normal latency: 0.683, 0.955 and 0.997 within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations, and 2.3% above 150 ms.

Try next. Replace the normal latency with a lognormal (rng.lognormal(np.log(110), 0.4, N)), compute its mean and standard deviation, then compare the real 99.9th percentile (np.percentile(lat, 99.9)) with mean + 3.09 × sd. The normal formula underestimates the tail. Then make the bomb scatter non-uniform (put half the bombs in the first 100 squares) and see the counts stop matching a Poisson.

Diagnose: is the new model really better?

Your team's support-ticket classifier is evaluated on a fixed set of 40 labelled tickets. The current model gets 34/40 (85%). A new prompt gets 37/40 (92.5%). Separately, the on-call dashboard shows the classifier's daily count of "uncertain, escalated to human" tickets: it averaged 6 per day for a month, and today it is 12. Two questions: (a) How much evidence is the 34 → 37 change? (b) Is today's 12 alarming?

Answer

(a) Treat the correct count as Binomial(40, p). If the true accuracy were 85% for both, the count has mean 34 and standard deviation √(40 × 0.85 × 0.15) ≈ 2.26. A score of 37 is about 1.3 standard deviations above 34, something a model with unchanged accuracy produces roughly one time in ten. Also the two scores are on the same 40 tickets, so the right comparison looks at the tickets where they disagree (a paired comparison, math6), but even before that it is clear 40 examples cannot distinguish 85% from 92%. Collect a few hundred examples or check the disagreements by hand before shipping on accuracy grounds.

(b) If escalations were Poisson with λ = 6, P(12 or more) is about 0.020: roughly once in 50 days. Unusual, so worth a look, but not proof of a regression. Two checks before acting: is today's traffic higher (12 escalations from double the tickets is no change in rate; compare the escalation rate, a binomial proportion), and over the past month was the daily variance close to 6 (if it was, say, 20, counts are overdispersed and 12 is much less surprising)? If the rate is up on normal traffic, look at what changed: a new ticket category or a model or prompt update.
