A team is choosing between two retrieval models for a support chatbot. Model A answers in 200 ms on average; model B in 180 ms. B looks faster. Then someone plots the full latency distribution: B is fast for most queries but takes over two seconds for one query in fifty, while A almost never exceeds 300 ms. The average hid the thing users would actually feel. The missing number was the spread.

The same team then looks at a feature that correlates 0.8 with customer satisfaction and wants to push it everywhere. Someone asks whether the feature causes satisfaction or whether happy customers simply use more features. Someone else plots the data and finds that the 0.8 comes almost entirely from a dozen enterprise accounts; without them the correlation is near zero. The single correlation number concealed both a causal question and an outlier.

Finally they average five models to make predictions more stable and are disappointed: the ensemble is barely better than the best single model. The five models were trained on the same data with the same features and made the same mistakes. How much averaging helps depends on how correlated the errors are, and that has an exact formula.

All three situations turn on four quantities: expected value (where a distribution is centred), variance (how spread out it is), covariance and correlation (how two quantities move together). They are the most-used numbers in data work. Every loss function is an expected value. Every confidence interval and A/B test rests on a variance. Principal component analysis (PCA, ml13) is built from a covariance matrix. Feature selection, ensembling, portfolio-style risk reasoning and anomaly detection all use them. And because they are summaries, they can mislead in characteristic ways that interviewers like to probe.

This lesson defines each quantity for random variables, then computes it from data, by hand, on small tables. It covers the rules that make them powerful (linearity of expectation, how variances add, why averages get less noisy like 1/√n), the covariance matrix used throughout ML, conditional expectation (what a regression model is actually predicting), and the classic traps: averages of ratios, correlation without causation, zero correlation without independence, and numerical instability in the textbook formula. The public case is Anscombe's 1973 quartet, four tiny datasets that share every summary statistic and look nothing alike.

Picture a distribution as weights placed along a ruler, as in math2: heavier weights where values are more likely. The expected value is the point where the ruler balances. Put a weight of 1/6 at each of the positions 1 to 6 (a fair die) and the ruler balances at 3.5. Move one weight far to the right, a rare huge value, and the balance point shifts towards it even though most of the weight has not moved. That is exactly why averages of skewed quantities (latency, revenue per user) are pulled upwards by a few extreme values.

The variance measures how far the weights sit from the balance point. Take each value's distance from the mean, square it, and average using the same probabilities. Squaring makes every distance positive and makes far-away values count much more than near ones: a value 10 units out contributes 100, one 1 unit out contributes 1. Because it is in squared units (ms², dollars²), we usually report its square root, the standard deviation, which is back in the original units and reads as "a typical distance from the mean".

Now take two quantities measured on the same things, such as the length of each prompt and the latency of its response. Draw a cross through the scatter plot at the two means. Points up-and-right of the centre (long prompt, slow response) and down-and-left (short, fast) are "agreeing"; points in the other two corners are "disagreeing". For each point multiply its two deviations from the means: agreeing points give a positive product (+ × + or − × −) and disagreeing points a negative one. The covariance is the average of these products. It is positive when agreeing points dominate, negative when disagreeing ones do, and near zero when they balance.

Covariance has an awkward feature: its size depends on units. Measure latency in seconds instead of milliseconds and the covariance shrinks a thousand-fold though nothing real changed. Correlation fixes this by dividing the covariance by both standard deviations, giving a unitless number between −1 and +1. +1 means the points lie exactly on a rising straight line, −1 exactly on a falling one, 0 means no straight-line tendency at all.

The analogy has one important limit. A balance point, a spread and a single co-movement number are summaries, and very different shapes can share them. A dataset that forms a perfect curve, one that forms a line with an outlier, and one that is a noisy cloud can all have the same means, variances and correlation; that is the point of Anscombe's quartet later in the lesson. The summaries are indispensable for computation and comparison, and dangerous as the only thing you look at.

The rules you will use most, which the body derives: expected values add, always (E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]); variances add only when the variables are uncorrelated, otherwise a covariance term appears; the average of n independent measurements has a standard deviation σ/√n, so noise falls slowly; and correlation measures only straight-line association, and never by itself tells you what causes what.

1. Expected value, defined and computed

For a discrete random variable X (math2) the expected value, also called the mean and written E[X] or μ (mu), is the sum of each value times its probability:

E[X] = Σ x · P(X = x), summed over every value x X can take.

For a fair die: E[X] = 1·(1/6) + 2·(1/6) + 3·(1/6) + 4·(1/6) + 5·(1/6) + 6·(1/6) = 21/6 = 3.5. For a continuous variable the sum becomes an area: E[X] is the integral of x·f(x) over all x, where f is the density. You will rarely compute those integrals by hand; the means of the standard distributions are known: Bernoulli(p) has mean p, Binomial(n, p) mean np, Poisson(λ) mean λ, Exponential(λ) mean 1/λ, Normal(μ, σ²) mean μ.

Why this definition? If you repeat the experiment many times, each value x turns up in about a fraction P(X = x) of the repetitions, so the average of all the results is about Σ x·P(X = x). The expected value is the long-run average. (Math4 makes "about" precise with the law of large numbers.)

The expected value is how decisions under uncertainty are made. Continue the fraud example from math1: a transaction has a corrected fraud probability of 0.083 and an amount of $500. If you let it through, the expected loss is 0.083 × $500 ≈ $41.50. If you block it, there is a 0.917 chance it was legitimate and you annoy a good customer, which the business values at $10 of lost goodwill, an expected cost of 0.917 × $10 ≈ $9.17. Blocking has the lower expected cost. Every threshold choice for a classifier is this comparison done for each score, and it only works if the probability is right (which is why calibration, ml15, matters).

The mean is one of three common ways to say where a distribution is centred, and the other two are worth naming because they disagree on skewed data. The median is the middle value: half the probability lies below it and half above. The mode is the single most likely value. Take five request latencies: 40, 45, 50, 55 and 810 ms. The median is 50 ms, a fair description of a typical request. The mean is 1,000/5 = 200 ms, a value no request actually had, dragged up by the one slow request. For a symmetric distribution such as the normal, mean, median and mode coincide; for right-skewed ones (latency, income, file size) the mean sits to the right of the median, often far to the right. Which one you want depends on the question. Capacity and cost scale with totals, so they need the mean (total CPU time is n times the mean); user experience is better described by the median and the tail percentiles. Reporting a mean when the reader will picture a typical case is one of the most common ways honest numbers mislead.

2. Expected values of functions, and why E[g(X)] ≠ g(E[X])

Often you want the average of some function of X: its square, its logarithm, a cost that depends on it. The rule is to apply the function to each value and weight by the same probabilities:

E[g(X)] = Σ g(x) · P(X = x)

For the die, E[X²] = (1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 + 36)/6 = 91/6 ≈ 15.17. Notice that this is not (E[X])² = 3.5² = 12.25. In general the average of a function is not the function of the average. This matters constantly. Suppose half your requests go to a server with 50 ms latency and half to one with 150 ms. The mean latency is 100 ms. The throughput of a single connection is 1/latency: 20 requests per second on the fast server and 6.67 on the slow one, an average of 13.3 per second, not the 1/0.1 s = 10 per second you get by inverting the mean latency. Averages of ratios, reciprocals, logarithms and percentages all behave this way. The general statement, Jensen's inequality, is in the deep section; the practical rule is to compute the function per item first and average afterwards, or to be explicit about which order you mean.

3. Linearity of expectation

The most useful property of expected values is that they pass through sums and scalings. For any constants a and b and any random variables X and Y:

E[aX + b] = a·E[X] + b and E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]

The first says that if you convert latency from seconds to milliseconds (a = 1,000) and add a fixed 5 ms network hop (b = 5), the mean converts the same way. The second is remarkable because it needs no independence: it holds however X and Y are related. (The proof is just rearranging a double sum: E[X + Y] = Σ (x + y)·P(x, y), which splits into Σ x·P(x, y) + Σ y·P(x, y), and each piece reduces to the marginal mean.)

Linearity lets you find the mean of a complicated count without ever finding its distribution, by writing the count as a sum of indicator variables (1 if something happens, 0 if not; the mean of an indicator is the probability of its event). The binomial mean in one line: X ~ Binomial(n, p) is the sum of n Bernoulli indicators, each with mean p, so E[X] = np. A harder one: n people throw their hats in a pile and each picks one at random; how many get their own hat back, on average? Let Iᵢ = 1 if person i gets their own hat. Each person is equally likely to get any of the n hats, so E[Iᵢ] = 1/n. The total number of matches is I₁ + … + Iₙ, so its mean is n × 1/n = 1, for any n. The indicators are dependent (if the first n − 1 people all match, the last must too), and it does not matter. In ML the same trick gives the expected number of duplicate hashes, of distinct items in a sample, or of tokens a model gets right in a sequence.

4. Variance and standard deviation

The variance of X is the expected squared distance from the mean:

Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²], which expands to Var(X) = E[X²] − μ²

The second form comes from expanding the square and using linearity: E[X² − 2μX + μ²] = E[X²] − 2μ·μ + μ² = E[X²] − μ². It is convenient by hand. For the die, Var(X) = 91/6 − 3.5² = 15.17 − 12.25 = 35/12 ≈ 2.92. The standard deviation is the square root, σ = √(35/12) ≈ 1.71: a typical roll lands about 1.7 away from 3.5. (The prod section explains why the second form is dangerous on a computer even though it is fine by hand.)

Variances of the distributions from math2 are worth knowing because they come up in every test and interval later:

This is why A/B tests on rare conversions need huge samples: the variance is small in absolute terms, but so is the effect you are trying to see.

5. How variance behaves under scaling and sums

Shifting a variable does not change its spread, and scaling it scales the spread:

Var(aX + b) = a² · Var(X), so the standard deviation scales by |a|.

Convert seconds to milliseconds and the variance multiplies by a million, the standard deviation by a thousand. A consequence used everywhere: the standardised variable Z = (X − μ)/σ has mean 0 and variance 1. That is the z-score from math2, and also what feature scaling does before training (ml4 explains why gradient descent needs it).

For sums, a new quantity appears:

Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2·Cov(X, Y)

where Cov is the covariance, defined in section 7. If X and Y are independent, their covariance is zero and variances simply add. That leads to one of the most important results in statistics. Take n independent measurements X₁, …, Xₙ, each with variance σ², and average them. The sum has variance nσ²; dividing by n divides the variance by n², so

Var(X̄) = σ²/n and the standard deviation of the average is σ/√n.

That standard deviation of an estimate is called its standard error. Averages get less noisy as you collect more data, but only like the square root: four times the data halves the noise, a hundred times cuts it to a tenth. For clicks at p = 0.03, one visitor's outcome has sd 0.17; the click rate over 10,000 visitors has standard error 0.17/100 = 0.0017, about ±0.17 percentage points. Every sample-size formula in math6 and math7 starts from this line.

6. From random variables to data: sample statistics on a 5-row table

So far E, Var and Cov were properties of a distribution, which you rarely know. With data you compute sample versions, which estimate them. The sample mean is x̄ = (x₁ + … + xₙ)/n. The sample variance is s² = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)²/(n − 1). Dividing by n − 1 rather than n is Bessel's correction: deviations from the sample's own mean are on average a little too small (the sample mean is fitted to these very points), and dividing by n − 1 exactly compensates. Math5 proves it as an example of an unbiased estimator; with large n the difference is negligible.

7. Covariance

The covariance of X and Y is the expected product of their deviations from their means:

Cov(X, Y) = E[(X − μ_X)(Y − μ_Y)] = E[XY] − μ_X μ_Y

and its sample version is Σ(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ)/(n − 1). In the table, the products sum to 540, so the sample covariance is 540/4 = 135 (hundreds of tokens × ms). It is positive because every row is either above both means or below both: longer prompts went with slower responses. The figure in the primer shows the geometry: each product is the signed area of a rectangle from the point to the centre cross, green when it adds and red when it subtracts.

Three properties are worth having at your fingertips. Cov(X, X) = Var(X): a variable's covariance with itself is its variance. Covariance is symmetric: Cov(X, Y) = Cov(Y, X). And it scales with both variables: Cov(aX, bY) = ab·Cov(X, Y), which is precisely why its size is hard to interpret. Measure latency in seconds and the 135 becomes 0.135, with nothing real changed.

If X and Y are independent, then E[XY] = E[X]E[Y] and the covariance is zero. The reverse is false, and section 8 shows a vivid counterexample.

8. Correlation

Dividing covariance by both standard deviations removes the units:

r = Cov(X, Y) / (σ_X · σ_Y)

This is the Pearson correlation coefficient. In the table, r = 135 / (3.162 × 45.28) ≈ 135/143.2 ≈ 0.943 (equivalently 540/√(40 × 8,200), since the n − 1s cancel). It always lies between −1 and +1, reaching ±1 only when the points lie exactly on a straight line, and it does not change if you rescale either variable or shift it. Here it says the relationship is strongly linear and positive.

Correlation and the best-fit straight line are tightly linked. The least-squares slope (the line minimising squared vertical errors) is Cov(X, Y)/Var(X) = 135/10 = 13.5 ms per hundred tokens, and the line passes through the means, so the intercept is 210 − 13.5 × 6 = 129 ms. The model predicts 129 ms of fixed overhead plus 13.5 ms per hundred tokens. And r² ≈ 0.889 is the share of the variance in latency that this line accounts for; the remaining 11% is scatter around it. Math10 and math11 derive the least-squares line properly, and mc1 builds it in code.

Correlation has two limits that interviewers test. First, it measures only linear association. Let x be spread evenly between −1 and 1 and y = x². Knowing x tells you y exactly, but the correlation is zero: on the left half y falls as x rises, on the right half it rises, and the products cancel. Zero correlation means "no straight-line tendency", not "no relationship". (Rank correlation, which correlates the orderings of the values, catches any consistently increasing relationship but would also miss this U shape; a scatter plot catches everything.) Second, correlation is sensitive to outliers: a single extreme point can create or destroy a strong correlation, as the case below shows.

And the third limit, the most important one in practice: correlation is not causation. Ice-cream sales and drowning deaths rise and fall together because hot weather drives both; hot weather is a confounder. In product analytics, users who adopt a feature retain better, but engaged users both adopt more features and retain better, so pushing the feature on everyone may change nothing. Correlations also arise from reverse causation (retention causes feature use, not the reverse), from selection (surveying only users who stayed), and from chance when you test many pairs of variables. Experiments (math7) are how you break these ties.

9. The covariance matrix

With many variables, say d features for each example, you have a variance for each feature and a covariance for each pair. They are stored in the covariance matrix Σ (capital sigma, here not a sum sign), a d × d table where the entry in row i, column j is Cov(Xᵢ, Xⱼ). The diagonal holds the variances (Cov(Xᵢ, Xᵢ) = Var(Xᵢ)), and the table is symmetric. For the 5-row table, with x first and y second:

Σ = [[10, 135], [135, 2,050]]

Dividing each entry by the two standard deviations gives the correlation matrix, with 1s on the diagonal and 0.943 off it. If you arrange the data as an n × d table with each column's mean subtracted, the covariance matrix is that table's transpose times itself divided by n − 1, a matrix product you will meet in math8. That one object underlies a large part of ML. PCA (ml13) finds the directions in which data varies most; they are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix (math9). The multivariate normal distribution is defined by a mean vector and a covariance matrix. Whitening rescales features so their covariance matrix becomes the identity. And the variance of any weighted combination of features, w₁X₁ + … + w_dX_d, is computed from the covariance matrix, which is how the ensemble formula in section 11 arises.

10. Conditional expectation, and what regression predicts

The conditional expectation E[Y | X = x] is the mean of Y among the cases where X equals x: math1's "shrink the world" step, followed by an average. Mean latency for prompts of 800 tokens; mean spend for users in Germany. Two results make it central to ML.

The law of total expectation says the overall mean is the probability-weighted average of the group means: E[Y] = Σ E[Y | X = x]·P(X = x). If 90% of requests hit a cache with mean latency 5 ms and 10% miss with mean 80 ms, the overall mean is 0.9 × 5 + 0.1 × 80 = 12.5 ms. It is the law of total probability from math1, one level up.

And when you train a model to predict y from x by minimising squared error, the best possible prediction at each x is exactly E[Y | X = x]: the conditional mean minimises expected squared error. (If you minimise absolute error instead, the best prediction is the conditional median, which is why ml5 recommends absolute or Huber loss when outliers should not drag predictions.) So a regression model is an estimate of a conditional expectation, and a classifier trained with log loss is an estimate of a conditional probability, which is a conditional expectation of an indicator.

11. Averages of correlated quantities: why ensembles disappoint

Combine section 5's sum rule with covariance. Suppose n models each have prediction error with variance σ², and every pair of models has error correlation ρ (rho), so each pair's covariance is ρσ². The variance of the average error is (1/n²) times the sum of all n variances and all n(n − 1) covariances:

Var(average) = (1/n²)[nσ² + n(n − 1)ρσ²] = ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ²/n

Read it as two parts. The second, (1 − ρ)σ²/n, is the independent part of the errors, and it shrinks as you add models. The first, ρσ², is the part the models share, and averaging does nothing to it. With σ² = 1 and ρ = 0.5, ten models give 0.5 + 0.05 = 0.55, a 45% reduction, and a thousand models give 0.5005. With ρ = 0.9, ten models give 0.91.

This formula is the reason random forests (ml10) deliberately decorrelate their trees, by training each on a bootstrap sample and letting each split consider only a random subset of features: a slightly worse but less correlated tree adds more to the ensemble. It is also why averaging several runs of the same LLM prompt helps less than combining genuinely different methods, and why diversified portfolios reduce risk only as far as their assets are uncorrelated. When you plan an ensemble, measure the correlation between members' errors on validation data; it tells you the ceiling before you build anything.

12. The public case: Anscombe's quartet

In 1973 the statistician Francis Anscombe published four small datasets, each of 11 (x, y) points, in a short paper titled "Graphs in Statistical Analysis". He wanted to counter the attitude, common at the time, that numerical calculations are exact while graphs are rough. For all four datasets the mean of x is 9 and its sample variance is 11, exactly; the mean of y is 7.50 and its variance about 4.12; the correlation is 0.816; and the least-squares line is y = 3.00 + 0.500x. A summary table of the four would be practically identical.

The plots tell four different stories. Dataset I is what the statistics suggest: a noisy linear relationship. Dataset II is a smooth curve, perfectly predictable but not linear, so the straight line and the correlation are the wrong tools. Dataset III is an almost perfect line, except for one outlier that drags the slope and pulls the correlation down from essentially 1 to 0.816. Dataset IV has ten points at the same x value, with y varying freely, and one point far to the right; that single point creates the entire correlation, and without it x tells you nothing about y because x does not vary.

In 2017 Justin Matejka and George Fitzmaurice of Autodesk Research pushed the idea further with the "Datasaurus Dozen": an algorithm that nudges points step by step into any target shape (a star, a circle, a dinosaur) while keeping the means, standard deviations and correlation identical to two decimal places. The lesson for ML work is direct. Before training on a feature, before reporting a correlation, before trusting an evaluation metric, look at the data: plot the scatter, check the distribution of residuals, and slice by segment. The summary numbers are where an analysis ends, not where it starts. The lab recomputes Anscombe's statistics so you can see the match yourself.

Jensen's inequality

Section 2 showed E[g(X)] ≠ g(E[X]) in general. Jensen's inequality says which way it goes. If g is convex, meaning its graph curves upward like x² or eˣ (any chord lies above the curve), then E[g(X)] ≥ g(E[X]). If g is concave, curving downward like log x or √x, the inequality flips. The intuition is the chord: averaging two points on an upward-curving graph lands above the curve. Three places it matters in ML. The die's E[X²] = 15.17 exceeds 3.5² = 12.25, which is just the statement that variance is non-negative. Because log is concave, the average log-likelihood is at most the log of the average likelihood, which is the inequality behind the evidence lower bound (ELBO) used to train variational autoencoders (dl10). And because a square root is concave, the square root of an unbiased variance estimate underestimates the standard deviation on average, a small bias that matters for tiny samples.

The law of total variance, and where bias and variance come from

The variance version of the law of total expectation is Var(Y) = E[Var(Y | X)] + Var(E[Y | X]): total spread equals the average spread within groups plus the spread between the group means. In the cache example, if hits have standard deviation 2 ms and misses 20 ms, the within-group part is 0.9 × 4 + 0.1 × 400 = 43.6 and the between-group part is 0.9 × (5 − 12.5)² + 0.1 × (80 − 12.5)² ≈ 50.6 + 455.6 = 506.3, so the total variance is about 550 and the standard deviation about 23 ms, far more than either group's spread. The decomposition is everywhere. R², the "variance explained" by a model, is the between-group part as a share of the total, with groups defined by the model's prediction. The bias–variance decomposition of a model's error (ml2) is the same kind of split, taken over random training sets. And variance-reduction techniques in experiments (CUPED, short for Controlled-experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data, in math7) work by conditioning on a pre-experiment variable that captures much of the between-user variance.

Numerical stability: why software does not use E[X²] − μ²

The formula Var(X) = E[X²] − μ² is perfect algebra and terrible floating-point arithmetic when the mean is large relative to the spread. With values around 10,000 and a standard deviation of 0.1, E[X²] is about 100,000,000.01 and μ² about 100,000,000.00. Float32 holds about seven significant decimal digits, so both round to the same neighbourhood and the subtraction returns noise, which can be negative. This is catastrophic cancellation. The lab produces a variance of −16 from data whose true variance is 0.01. Welford's algorithm avoids it by updating, for each new value v, the running mean m and the running sum of squared deviations M: d = v − m; m ← m + d/k; M ← M + d·(v − m), where k is the count so far. The variance is M/(k − 1). It is one pass, needs constant memory, and never subtracts two large nearly-equal numbers. A parallel form merges partial results from shards. Metrics libraries, batch normalisation's running statistics and streaming monitors all rely on stable updates like this.

Correlation estimates are noisy and fragile

A sample correlation has its own sampling error, roughly 1/√n near r = 0: with 30 points, correlations up to about ±0.36 arise between unrelated variables by chance about 5% of the time. Screen 1,000 features against a label on a small dataset and dozens will look correlated by luck alone, the multiple-comparisons problem treated in math7. Because Pearson correlation uses squared deviations, a single extreme point can dominate it, as Anscombe III and IV show. Two robust alternatives: Spearman's rank correlation, which computes Pearson's r on the ranks of the values and so measures any monotonic relationship while ignoring how extreme an outlier is; and simply winsorising (clipping the top and bottom few per cent) before computing. For feature screening in ML, mutual information (math12) captures non-linear dependence that neither correlation sees.

Covariance in high dimensions

A covariance matrix for d features has d(d + 1)/2 distinct entries: 500,500 for 1,000 features. Estimated from a sample with fewer rows than that, it is very noisy, and if n ≤ d it is singular (it cannot be inverted; math9 explains rank). The large eigenvalues come out too large and the small ones too small. Methods that need the inverse, such as the Mahalanobis distance used in anomaly detection or Gaussian discriminant analysis, then behave badly. The standard fix is shrinkage: blend the sample covariance with a simple target such as a scaled identity, Σ̂ = (1 − α)S + αλI, with α chosen from the data (the Ledoit–Wolf estimator does this automatically). It is the covariance analogue of regularisation (ml3): accept a little bias for a large cut in variance.

When the mean does not exist

Some distributions have no finite mean or variance. The Cauchy distribution, the ratio of two independent standard normals, has tails so heavy that the average of n Cauchy draws has exactly the same distribution as a single draw: averaging never settles. Pareto distributions with small tail exponents have a finite mean but infinite variance. These are not curiosities: ratios of noisy quantities (for instance a metric divided by a near-zero baseline) can behave like them, and so can sizes of files, cascades and losses. The symptom in practice is a running average that keeps jumping whenever a new extreme value arrives. When you see it, switch to medians, trimmed means or log scales, and treat any standard error computed from the sample variance with suspicion.

Dashboards of averages

The most common misuse of this lesson's ideas in production is the dashboard that shows only means. Mean latency, mean session length and mean revenue per user are all dominated by long right tails, so they move when a handful of extreme events happen and miss changes in the experience of typical users. Teams that alert on mean latency notice incidents late and chase false alarms caused by one huge request. The fix is standard: plot distributions or at least percentiles (p50, p90, p99), compute them from merged histograms rather than averaging per-host percentiles, and put a standard deviation or interval next to any mean so a reader can tell signal from noise.

Ratio metrics and the mean of ratios

Click-through rate, conversion rate, revenue per session and latency per token are ratios, and section 2's warning bites hard. "Mean of per-user CTR" and "total clicks / total impressions" can differ by orders of magnitude, and the choice changes which users dominate the metric. Teams have shipped changes that improved one definition while worsening the other. The production rule is to define each metric precisely (which numerator, which denominator, which unit is averaged), document it, and keep it fixed. In A/B tests, a ratio-of-sums metric whose randomisation unit is the user does not have the simple binomial variance; using that formula understates the noise and inflates false positives. The delta method (math7) gives the correct variance.

Correlation-driven decisions

Feature-importance reports, "users who did X retain Y% better" analyses and correlation heatmaps drive many product decisions. Three failures recur. Confounding, where engaged users do everything more, makes nearly every engagement feature look valuable. Leakage, where a feature correlates almost perfectly with the label because it is recorded after the outcome (a "refund issued" flag predicting churn), shows up as a suspiciously high correlation and an excellent offline model that fails in production (ml9, ml14). And chance, where screening many features on a small sample guarantees some high correlations. The habits that help: treat any correlation above about 0.95 with a label as a probable leak until proven otherwise; check whether a correlation survives within segments (Simpson's paradox, math1); and test causal claims with an experiment.

Ensembles and correlated failures

The ensemble formula applies far beyond model averaging. Running three replicas of a service on the same hardware model, the same kernel version and the same configuration does not give three independent chances to stay up: their failures are correlated, and the shared component (a bad config push, a kernel bug) takes them all down together. Reliability planning that multiplies independent failure probabilities overstates availability for the same reason ensembles disappoint. In ML, large language model (LLM) as-judge setups that use several prompts on the same model, or several models trained on similar data, give less independent evidence than their number suggests; measure agreement between judges on labelled data to see how much each adds.

Streaming statistics and numerical issues

Monitoring systems, feature stores and normalisation layers compute means and variances over streams. Implementations that use the textbook E[X²] − E[X]² formula in float32 produce negative variances and NaN standard deviations for features with large offsets (timestamps, prices in cents, IDs accidentally used as features), which then poison downstream z-scores. Use Welford-style updates in float64, or subtract a reference value first; and alert on any variance that comes out negative, since it is always a numerical bug, never a property of the data.

The public case, in production terms

Anscombe's quartet and the Datasaurus Dozen are the reason good teams pair every summary table with a plot. Two operational versions of the lesson: an evaluation metric that improved overall can hide a segment that got much worse (the quartet in miniature: same aggregate, different structure), so slice evaluations by the major segments before shipping; and a model's correlation with ground truth can be carried by a few extreme items, so look at a scatter of predictions against labels, not just r or R².

import numpy as np

# 1. Anscombe's quartet: four datasets, (almost) the same summary statistics
x123 = np.array([10, 8, 13, 9, 11, 14, 6, 4, 12, 7, 5], float)
quartet = {
    "I":   (x123, [8.04, 6.95, 7.58, 8.81, 8.33, 9.96, 7.24, 4.26, 10.84, 4.82, 5.68]),
    "II":  (x123, [9.14, 8.14, 8.74, 8.77, 9.26, 8.10, 6.13, 3.10, 9.13, 7.26, 4.74]),
    "III": (x123, [7.46, 6.77, 12.74, 7.11, 7.81, 8.84, 6.08, 5.39, 8.15, 6.42, 5.73]),
    "IV":  (np.array([8, 8, 8, 8, 8, 8, 8, 19, 8, 8, 8], float),
            [6.58, 5.76, 7.71, 8.84, 8.47, 7.04, 5.25, 12.50, 5.56, 7.91, 6.89]),
}
for name, (x, y) in quartet.items():
    y = np.array(y)
    cov = np.cov(x, y)                       # 2x2 sample covariance (divides by n-1)
    r = np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1]
    slope = cov[0, 1] / cov[0, 0]
    print(f"{name:>3}: mean x {x.mean():.2f}  var x {cov[0,0]:.2f}  mean y {y.mean():.2f}  "
          f"var y {cov[1,1]:.3f}  r {r:.3f}  line y = {y.mean()-slope*x.mean():.2f} + {slope:.3f}x")

# 2. Zero correlation does not mean independent
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.uniform(-1, 1, 100_000)
print("corr(x, x^2) =", np.corrcoef(x, x**2)[0, 1].round(4))

# 3. Averaging models: variance of the average with correlated errors
for rho in (0.0, 0.5, 0.9):
    cov = np.full((10, 10), rho) + (1 - rho) * np.eye(10)      # 10 models, error sd 1
    errs = rng.multivariate_normal(np.zeros(10), cov, size=100_000)
    print(f"rho={rho}: var of 10-model average = {errs.mean(axis=1).var():.3f}"
          f"  formula {rho + (1 - rho) / 10:.3f}")

# 4. Catastrophic cancellation: E[X^2] - E[X]^2 in float32 vs Welford
data = (10_000 + rng.normal(0, 0.1, 100_000)).astype(np.float32)
naive = np.mean(data * data, dtype=np.float32) - np.mean(data, dtype=np.float32) ** 2
mean = m2 = 0.0
for i, v in enumerate(data[:20_000].astype(np.float64), 1):    # Welford's streaming update
    d = v - mean
    mean += d / i
    m2 += d * (v - mean)
print("true variance ~0.01 | naive float32:", float(naive), "| Welford:", round(m2 / (i - 1), 5))

What to look for (seeded output from the test run):

  1. All four Anscombe datasets print mean x 9.00, variance x 11.00, mean y 7.50, variance y between 4.123 and 4.128, r 0.816 or 0.817, and the line y = 3.00 + 0.500x. The numbers are identical; the plots in the lesson are not.
  2. corr(x, x²) is about −0.006: zero to within sampling noise, though x² is a perfect function of x.
  3. The variance of a 10-model average is 0.100, 0.549 and 0.915 for error correlations 0, 0.5 and 0.9, matching ρ + (1 − ρ)/10 = 0.100, 0.550 and 0.910.
  4. With values near 10,000 and a true variance of 0.01, the float32 E[X²] − E[X]² formula returns −16.0, an impossible negative variance. Welford's one-pass update in float64 returns 0.0099.

Estimate: should we add more models to the ensemble?

Your ranking system averages the scores of 4 models. On validation data, each model's error (prediction minus label) has standard deviation 0.20, and the average pairwise correlation of errors is 0.6. A colleague proposes adding 4 more models of the same kind (same features, different random seeds). Another proposes adding 1 model built on a new data source, whose error standard deviation is 0.25 but whose errors correlate only 0.1 with the existing models. Which helps more? Assume equal weighting.

Answer

Current ensemble. σ² = 0.04, ρ = 0.6, n = 4: Var = 0.6 × 0.04 + 0.4 × 0.04 / 4 = 0.024 + 0.004 = 0.028, standard deviation ≈ 0.167.

Eight similar models. Var = 0.024 + 0.4 × 0.04 / 8 = 0.024 + 0.002 = 0.026, sd ≈ 0.161. Doubling the ensemble removes only 0.002 of variance, because the shared part, 0.024, does not average away.

Four old plus one new. Use Var(average of 5) = (1/25) × (sum of all variances + all covariances). Variances: 4 × 0.04 + 0.0625 = 0.2225. Covariances among the old four: 12 ordered pairs × 0.6 × 0.04 = 0.288. Between the new model and each old one: covariance = 0.1 × 0.20 × 0.25 = 0.005, times 8 ordered pairs = 0.04. Total = 0.2225 + 0.288 + 0.04 = 0.5505; divided by 25 gives 0.0220, sd ≈ 0.148.

The single, individually worse but decorrelated model cuts variance by about 21%; four more look-alike models cut it by about 7%. Optimising the weights instead of averaging equally would help further, because the new model deserves more weight than any one of the four correlated ones. The general lesson: when adding ensemble members, measure their error correlation with what you have, and prefer new information over more copies of the old.
