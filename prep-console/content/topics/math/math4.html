In the 1990s, analysts looking at school test results noticed that the highest-scoring schools were disproportionately small. The finding fitted a story everyone liked: in small schools children get more attention, teachers know every student, and nobody falls through the cracks. Major foundations, the Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation among them, put large sums into breaking big schools into smaller ones. Years later the statistician Howard Wainer pointed out what the analysis had missed. The lowest-scoring schools were also disproportionately small. Small schools were not better; they were more variable. An average over 50 students jumps around far more than an average over 1,000, so small schools crowd both ends of any ranking even when every school is identical.

Wainer called the formula behind this "the most dangerous equation": the standard deviation of an average is σ/√n, first worked out by Abraham de Moivre in the 1730s. The same mistake shows up everywhere data are averaged over groups of different sizes. The US counties with the lowest kidney-cancer rates are mostly small and rural, which suggests clean country living. The counties with the highest rates are also mostly small and rural. Small populations produce extreme rates in both directions.

ML engineers make this mistake weekly. A dashboard ranks markets by conversion and the top five are all tiny. A prompt variant tested on 30 examples tops the leaderboard. A model's accuracy on a 200-example slice swings five points between runs. A hyperparameter sweep crowns a winner that disappoints on fresh data. Each time the question is the same: how much does an average move by chance alone, and how does that shrink as the sample grows?

Two theorems answer it. The law of large numbers says averages settle down to the true value as you collect more data; it is why sampling works at all, why a mini-batch gradient points roughly the right way, and why Monte Carlo simulation gives answers. The central limit theorem says the leftover error is shaped like a bell curve, with width σ/√n, whatever the shape of the data: that is what lets you draw a confidence interval around an accuracy, size an A/B test, or say that a 2-point difference on a 1,000-example eval is real.

This lesson builds both from math3's fact that independent variances add. It starts with the sample mean as a random variable and its standard error, proves the law of large numbers with Chebyshev's inequality, shows the central limit theorem happening by averaging dice, and uses it to approximate binomial counts and build a first confidence interval. Then it covers when the theorems are slow or fail (skewed data, heavy tails, correlated observations), how they explain Monte Carlo and stochastic gradients, and the small-schools trap with the fixes: funnel plots, minimum sample sizes and shrinkage.

Think about a political poll. Nobody asks all 50 million voters; a pollster asks 1,000 and reports that 52% support a candidate, "plus or minus 3 points". Two separate claims hide in that sentence. The first is that 52% from a thousand people is close to the real figure for fifty million, which is surprising if you think about it: why should a tiny sample tell you about a huge population? The second is the "plus or minus 3", which says how close, and with what confidence. The law of large numbers is the first claim; the central limit theorem is the second.

The key mental shift is to see the sample mean as a random variable. If the pollster drew a different 1,000 people, they would get a slightly different percentage: 51.2%, 53.1%, 50.7%. Imagine repeating the poll thousands of times and plotting the results. That histogram is the sampling distribution of the mean, the distribution of the estimate itself. Its centre sits at the true value (the estimate is not systematically off), and its width, the standard error, is how far a typical poll lands from the truth. From math3 you know the width: σ/√n, where σ is the spread of individual answers and n the sample size. For a yes/no question near 50%, σ = √(0.5 × 0.5) = 0.5, so with n = 1,000 the standard error is 0.5/√1000 ≈ 0.016, about 1.6 points. Twice that is the "plus or minus 3".

The law of large numbers says this width shrinks to zero as n grows, so the estimate homes in on the truth. Notice what does not appear in σ/√n: the population size. A sample of 1,000 is as accurate for a country of 50 million as for a city of 500,000, provided it is a random sample. What matters is how many you asked, not what fraction.

The central limit theorem says something stronger and stranger: the shape of the sampling distribution is approximately a normal bell curve, regardless of the shape of the individual answers. The cleanest demonstration is dice. A single die is flat: each face 1 to 6 equally likely. The average of two dice is a triangle peaking at 3.5. The average of five is already a rounded hump, and of thirty a narrow bell.

Why does a bell appear from flat ingredients? Because there are many more ways to get an average near the middle than at the extremes. To average 6 over thirty dice, every single die must show 6, one combination out of 6³⁰; to average 3.5 there are astronomically many combinations of highs and lows that balance. Adding up many independent pieces piles probability towards the centre in a very specific shape, and that shape is always the normal curve, provided no single piece dominates.

The analogy's limits are the limits of the theorems, and they matter in practice. The sample must be random: a poll of people who answer landlines is precise about the wrong population, and no n fixes that. The observations must be roughly independent: asking the same household twice adds little. And the bell takes longer to appear for lopsided data than for dice; for some heavy-tailed quantities it never appears. The body works through each of these, with numbers.

1. Populations, samples and the sample mean as a random variable

Statistics distinguishes the population, everything you want to know about (all future requests, all users, all inputs the model will ever see), from the sample, the observations you actually have. The population has a true mean μ and standard deviation σ, usually unknown. From a sample X₁, …, Xₙ you compute the sample mean X̄ = (X₁ + … + Xₙ)/n. We assume, for now, that the observations are i.i.d.: independent (none affects another) and identically distributed (each drawn from the same population distribution).

Before you collect the data, X̄ is a random variable: its value depends on which observations you happen to draw. Its distribution across all possible samples is the sampling distribution. Using math3's rules: by linearity, E[X̄] = (1/n)(μ + … + μ) = μ, so the sample mean is centred on the truth; and because independent variances add, Var(X̄) = (1/n²)(nσ²) = σ²/n. Its standard deviation is the standard error:

SE(X̄) = σ / √n

For a fair die, μ = 3.5 and σ = √(35/12) ≈ 1.708. The average of 25 rolls has SE = 1.708/5 ≈ 0.342; of 100 rolls, 0.171; of 10,000 rolls, 0.017. Each hundred-fold increase in data buys a ten-fold gain in precision. That square root is the single most important scaling law in applied statistics, and the reason precise measurement is expensive.

In practice σ is unknown too, so you plug in the sample standard deviation s (math3) and use s/√n. For large n this makes little difference; for small n it adds extra uncertainty, which math6 handles with the t-distribution.

2. Chebyshev's inequality: a guarantee for any distribution

How likely is a random variable to land far from its mean? For a normal distribution you can read it from a table, but suppose you know nothing about the shape, only the mean and variance. Chebyshev's inequality still gives a guarantee:

P(|X − μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ 1/k², for any k > 0.

At most 1/4 of any distribution lies 2 or more standard deviations from its mean, at most 1/9 lies 3 or more away, at most 1/100 lies 10 or more away. The proof is short enough to give. Let A be the event that X lands at least kσ from μ. Whenever A happens, (X − μ)² is at least k²σ²; whenever it does not, (X − μ)² is still at least 0. So the average of (X − μ)², which is σ², is at least k²σ² × P(A). Divide both sides by k²σ²: P(A) ≤ 1/k².

Chebyshev is usually loose. For a normal, the true chance of being 2σ out is 4.6%, not 25%. Its value is that it holds for every distribution with a finite variance, including the skewed and lumpy ones engineers actually meet, and that it is all you need to prove the law of large numbers.

3. The law of large numbers

Apply Chebyshev to the sample mean, whose standard deviation is σ/√n. Choose any margin ε (epsilon, a small positive number such as 0.01). Writing ε = k·σ/√n:

P(|X̄ − μ| ≥ ε) ≤ σ² / (nε²)

As n grows, the right-hand side goes to zero, for any fixed ε however small. That is the weak law of large numbers: the probability that the sample mean misses the true mean by more than any given margin shrinks to zero as the sample grows. (A stronger version says the running average converges with probability 1, and needs only a finite mean, but the weak version carries the intuition.) Worked: for 100 dice, the chance their average is 0.5 or more from 3.5 is at most (35/12)/(100 × 0.25) ≈ 0.117 by Chebyshev; the true value, using the normal approximation from section 5, is about 0.003. The bound is loose, but it goes to zero, and that is the theorem.

The LLN is the formal reason so much of ML works. A training loss is an average over examples, an estimate of the expected loss on the population; the LLN says that with enough data the training estimate approaches the real thing (for a fixed model; the full story with model selection is ml2 and ml9). A mini-batch gradient is the average of per-example gradients over B examples, an estimate of the full-data gradient. An eval score is an average of per-example correctness. Each is trustworthy to the extent n is large.

The LLN is also widely misread. It does not say that deviations get corrected. If a fair coin has come up heads 8 times in a row, the next toss is still 50/50; the coin does not owe you tails. The proportion of heads still converges to 1/2, because 8 extra heads become negligible against a million later tosses: (500,008)/(1,000,008) is 0.500004. The absolute difference between heads and tails typically grows, like √n, while the proportion converges. Believing that a run must be balanced out is the gambler's fallacy, and the same error appears when someone expects a metric that ran hot this week to run cold next week "to even out".

4. Monte Carlo: the law of large numbers as a tool

If averages converge to expectations, you can estimate any expectation by simulating and averaging. This is Monte Carlo estimation, named after the casino. Want the probability that a sequence of five retries all fail if each fails with probability 0.3, under some complicated dependency? Simulate the process 100,000 times and count. Want the expected cost of a fraud policy under realistic transaction amounts? Simulate. Want π? Throw random points into a square of side 2 and count the fraction landing inside the inscribed circle of radius 1: the fraction approaches π/4, about 0.785.

The error of a Monte Carlo estimate is the standard error σ/√n of the average, so accuracy improves slowly: each extra decimal digit needs 100 times as many samples. But that rate does not depend on how many dimensions the problem has, unlike methods that lay down a grid (a grid with 10 points per axis in 20 dimensions needs 10²⁰ points). That is why Monte Carlo methods are the default for high-dimensional problems, from Bayesian posteriors (math5) to reinforcement-learning returns (rs3) to estimating a policy's value by rollouts. When you report a Monte Carlo result, report its standard error too.

5. The central limit theorem

The LLN says where the sample mean goes. The central limit theorem says what its error looks like on the way. In its classical form: if X₁, …, Xₙ are i.i.d. with mean μ and finite variance σ², then for large n

X̄ is approximately Normal(μ, σ²/n), equivalently Z = (X̄ − μ) / (σ/√n) is approximately standard normal.

The same holds for the sum X₁ + … + Xₙ, which is approximately Normal(nμ, nσ²). The theorem's power is in what it does not require: the Xᵢ can be flat (dice), lopsided (exponential waiting times), discrete (clicks) or continuous. Their individual shape washes out; only the mean and variance survive into the bell.

Worked: the sum of 100 dice. What is the chance that 100 dice sum to 380 or more? The sum has mean 100 × 3.5 = 350 and standard deviation √(100 × 35/12) ≈ 17.08. Because the sum is a whole number, P(sum ≥ 380) is better approximated as P(normal ≥ 379.5), the continuity correction: each integer value occupies the interval half a unit either side. z = (379.5 − 350)/17.08 ≈ 1.73, and P(Z > 1.73) ≈ 0.042. Computing the exact distribution by brute force (the lab's figure code does this by repeated convolution) gives 0.04203. The CLT answer is right to three decimal places from a calculation you can do on a napkin.

The figure in the primer showed the theorem happening: averages of 1, 2, 5 and 30 dice. The lab checks the numbers: the standard deviation of the average is 1.706, 1.204, 0.766 and 0.312 for n = 1, 2, 5, 30, against σ/√n = 1.708, 1.208, 0.764, 0.312, and by n = 30 the share of averages within ±1.96 standard errors is 0.953, against 0.95 for an exact normal.

Why should a bell appear? One way to see it: when you add independent variables, their distributions combine by a smoothing operation (convolution), and repeated smoothing wears away every feature of the original shape except its centre and spread. Another: the normal is the only shape that is stable under this process, since the sum of independent normals is normal, so it is where repeated summing must end up. A full proof needs tools beyond this track, but the dice make it concrete.

6. The normal approximation to the binomial and Poisson

A binomial count is a sum of n Bernoulli indicators (math2), so the CLT applies: Binomial(n, p) is approximately Normal(np, np(1 − p)) when n is large. This approximation is what makes A/B testing formulas simple. With 1,000 visitors and a 3% conversion rate, conversions have mean 30 and standard deviation √(1000 × 0.03 × 0.97) ≈ 5.39. The chance of 40 or more: z = (39.5 − 30)/5.39 ≈ 1.76, P ≈ 0.039. The exact binomial answer is 0.044, so the approximation is in the right neighbourhood but not exact, because with np = 30 the binomial is still slightly right-skewed. A common rule of thumb is to require np and n(1 − p) both at least 10; with rarer events, use exact binomial or Poisson calculations.

A Poisson(λ) count can be seen as the sum of many independent small counts, so it too is approximately Normal(λ, λ) for large λ. With an average of 400 errors an hour, an hour with 460 is (460 − 400)/√400 = 3 standard deviations high, worth an alert; with an average of 4, the normal approximation would be poor and you should use the Poisson tail directly, as in math2.

7. A first confidence interval

The CLT turns an estimate into a range. If X̄ is approximately normal around μ with standard error SE, then X̄ lands within 1.96·SE of μ about 95% of the time. Turned around: the interval X̄ ± 1.96·SE contains μ for about 95% of the samples you might draw. That is a 95% confidence interval.

Worked: an accuracy estimate. A model gets 870 of 1,000 test examples right. Each example is a Bernoulli trial, so the observed accuracy p̂ = 0.87 is a sample mean with standard error √(p̂(1 − p̂)/n) = √(0.87 × 0.13/1000) ≈ 0.0106. The 95% interval is 0.87 ± 1.96 × 0.0106, from about 0.849 to 0.891. If a competing model scored 0.88 on a different 1,000 examples, the evidence that it is better is weak: the intervals overlap heavily. (Comparing two models properly is a two-sample test, and comparing them on the same examples is a paired test; math6 does both.) To get ±1 point at this accuracy you need 1.96 × √(0.87 × 0.13/n) ≤ 0.01, so n ≈ 4,350, which is the arithmetic behind eval-set sizing in lm14.

Two cautions about the interpretation, which math6 develops. The 95% is a property of the procedure across repeated samples, not a probability that μ is in this particular interval. And the interval only covers sampling noise: if the test set is unrepresentative (all short inputs, all from one month), the interval is precise about the wrong thing.

8. How large is "large n"? Skew and the speed of convergence

The CLT is a statement about the limit; in practice you need to know how quickly the approximation becomes good. For symmetric, light-tailed data like dice it is excellent by n = 5 to 10. For skewed data it takes longer, because each observation carries some of the skew into the average and the skew fades only like 1/√n. The lab measures this for exponential data (mean 1, strongly right-skewed): the probability that the average exceeds the normal 95th-percentile cut-off should be 5%, and comes out 6.7% at n = 5, 5.8% at n = 30 and 5.4% at n = 300. The familiar "n = 30 is enough" is a rule of thumb for mildly skewed data, not a law. (The deep section names the result that quantifies this, the Berry–Esseen bound.)

For very skewed data, such as revenue per user where a few customers spend thousands of times the median, you may need tens of thousands of observations before the average is reliably normal, and even then a single extreme user can move it. The practical responses are to cap extreme values (winsorising), transform (analyse the logarithm), change the metric (did the user purchase at all?), or check the normal approximation with the bootstrap, described in the deep section.

9. When the CLT fails outright

Two assumptions can fail badly. The first is finite variance. The Cauchy distribution (the ratio of two independent standard normals) has tails so heavy that its mean and variance do not exist; the average of n Cauchy draws has exactly the same distribution as a single draw, so averaging gains nothing. The lab shows a running mean of a million Cauchy draws still lurching between values like 0.6, −1.1 and −3.2. Real data rarely are exactly Cauchy, but ratios with a noisy denominator near zero, and power-law quantities such as file sizes and follower counts, can behave similarly over practical sample sizes.

The second is independence. If observations are positively correlated, each new one carries less new information, and the standard error falls more slowly than σ/√n. For a time series where each value is correlated with the previous one with correlation ρ (a so-called AR(1) process), the effective sample size is roughly n(1 − ρ)/(1 + ρ): with ρ = 0.9, a thousand minutes of a metric are worth only about 53 independent observations. The same applies to many events per user, many tokens per document, or many examples generated from the same template. The CLT still often holds for the average, but with a much larger variance than the i.i.d. formula claims, so intervals computed as if observations were independent are far too narrow. Always ask what the independent unit is.

10. What a large n cannot fix: bias

Everything so far is about sampling error, the random scatter of an estimate around the true value, which shrinks like 1/√n. A second kind of error does not shrink at all: bias, a systematic gap between what your sample measures and what you wanted to know. If your evaluation set was drawn only from English tickets, a million of them will estimate English-ticket accuracy to a fraction of a point and tell you nothing reliable about Spanish ones. If an online survey reaches only users who opened the app this week, its huge sample is precise about engaged users. If you log latency only for requests that completed, the timeouts are missing from every average. The law of large numbers converges to the mean of the population you actually sampled; when that is not the population you care about, more data makes you more confidently wrong.

A historical illustration is the 1936 Literary Digest poll, which mailed about ten million ballots, received over two million back, and predicted a landslide defeat for Franklin Roosevelt, who then won 46 of 48 states. Its mailing lists over-represented wealthier households and its respondents were self-selected. A far smaller poll with a properly random sample would have done better. In ML terms: total error ≈ bias + sampling noise, and the √n law attacks only the second. Before increasing a sample, ask whether it is drawn from the right population: the same traffic mix, time period, user segments and input lengths as production. A confidence interval is a statement about sampling noise only; it says nothing about whether the sample was the right one.

11. The public case: the most dangerous equation

Wainer's 2007 article in American Scientist, "The Most Dangerous Equation", nominated de Moivre's σ/√n for the title because ignoring it has repeatedly led to expensive mistakes. Its central examples are the two in this lesson's opening. On a US map of age-adjusted kidney-cancer rates by county, the counties in the lowest tenth are mostly rural, sparsely populated ones, which invites an explanation about healthy rural living. The counties in the highest tenth are also mostly rural and sparsely populated, which invites the opposite story about poverty and poor access to care. Both are the same statistical effect: a county of a few thousand people has a very noisy cancer rate, so it is far more likely than a large county to land in either extreme decile by chance.

The small-schools story is the policy version. When the highest-performing schools were found to be disproportionately small, foundations including the Gates Foundation funded a large movement to create small schools. But small schools were over-represented among the lowest performers too, which is what the √n effect predicts and what a small-school advantage does not. The figure shows the same thing with simulated data: 300 schools of identical true quality, individual scores with mean 500 and standard deviation 100. A school of 50 students has a standard error of 100/√50 ≈ 14 points; a school of 1,000 has 100/√1000 ≈ 3.2. A small school two standard errors above average scores about 528, a result the large school essentially cannot reach.

In the lab's simulation of 1,000 identical schools with 20 to 2,000 students, the median school has about 1,031 students, but the median among the top 50 has about 309 and among the bottom 50 about 253. The lines in the figure are a funnel plot: ±2 standard errors around the overall mean, drawn as a function of size. Points inside the funnel are consistent with no real difference. Funnel plots are used to compare hospital mortality rates and surgeons' outcomes for exactly this reason, and they are the right default for any ranking of groups of different sizes, including ranking markets, customers, prompts or data slices by a metric.

How fast the CLT kicks in: Berry–Esseen

The Berry–Esseen theorem quantifies the convergence: the largest gap between the CDF of the standardised mean and the standard normal CDF is at most C·ρ₃/(σ³√n), where ρ₃ = E|X − μ|³ measures how heavy and lopsided the tails are and C is a constant below 0.5. Two practical lessons follow. The error falls only like 1/√n, so halving it needs four times the data. And the constant scales with a skewness-like ratio, so distributions with a long tail on one side (revenue, latency, counts of rare events) need far more data than symmetric ones for the same accuracy. For a Bernoulli with small p, ρ₃/σ³ is about 1/√p, so the normal approximation for a 1% conversion rate needs roughly a hundred times the sample of a 50% one to be equally good, matching the np ≥ 10 rule of thumb.

The bootstrap: a sampling distribution without the formula

For a mean, the CLT gives the sampling distribution directly. For a median, a 95th percentile, a ratio of sums, an AUC (area under the ROC curve, ml7) or the difference in F1 (the harmonic mean of precision and recall) between two models, no simple formula exists. The bootstrap (Efron, 1979) estimates it by treating the sample as a stand-in for the population: draw n observations from your n with replacement, recompute the statistic, repeat a few thousand times, and use the spread of the results as the standard error, or their 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles as a 95% interval. It works because, for large n, resampling from the data mimics sampling from the population. It fails when the statistic depends on extremes (the maximum, very high percentiles) and, like the CLT, when observations are dependent, unless you resample whole independent units (all of a user's sessions together, the "block" or "cluster" bootstrap). For ML evaluation, bootstrap intervals over test examples are the standard way to put error bars on metrics that are not simple means.

The delta method: standard errors of functions of means

Many metrics are functions of means: a click-through rate defined as total clicks over total impressions is a ratio of two means; a log-odds is a function of a proportion. If an estimate X̄ is approximately normal with standard error SE, then a smooth function g(X̄) is approximately normal with standard error |g′(μ)|·SE, where g′ is the derivative (math10). This is the delta method, the first-order Taylor expansion of g around μ. For a ratio of two correlated means the multivariate version uses the covariance matrix from math3. A/B testing platforms use it to compute correct standard errors for ratio metrics whose randomisation unit is the user, which is the issue raised in math3's prod section and developed in math7.

Regression to the mean and the winner's curse

Any measurement is part signal and part noise. Pick the units with the most extreme measurements and you have selected for both extreme signal and extreme noise; measure them again and the noise part does not repeat, so they look less extreme. This is regression to the mean, first described by Francis Galton for the heights of parents and children. It explains why the best-performing team this quarter usually does worse next quarter, why a sales region "fixed" by an intervention after a terrible month looks improved even if the intervention did nothing, and why the top configuration from a hyperparameter sweep underperforms on fresh data. That last case is the winner's curse: among k candidates of equal true quality, each estimated with standard error SE, the best observed one overstates its quality by about the expected maximum of k standard normals times SE, roughly 1.2 SE for k = 5, 1.9 SE for 20, 2.5 SE for 100. The remedies are a held-out confirmation run and shrinkage.

Shrinkage: borrowing strength across groups

For the small-schools problem the statistically principled fix is to estimate each group's mean as a weighted average of its own sample mean and the overall mean: θ̂ᵢ = wᵢ·x̄ᵢ + (1 − wᵢ)·x̄, with wᵢ = τ²/(τ² + σ²/nᵢ), where τ² is the true variance between groups and σ²/nᵢ the noise variance of group i. Large groups (small noise) keep almost all of their own estimate; small groups are pulled strongly towards the overall mean. This is the empirical Bayes or James–Stein idea, and James and Stein showed in 1961 that, for three or more groups, it beats using each group's raw mean in total squared error, a result that surprised statisticians. It is what recommendation systems do with item ratings that have few votes (a 5-star average from two reviews should not outrank a 4.8 from two thousand), and math5's maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation with a prior is the same mechanism for a single parameter.

Gradient noise, batch size and learning rate

A mini-batch gradient of size B is a sample mean of per-example gradients, so its noise has covariance roughly Σ/B, where Σ is the covariance of per-example gradients. The update moves by the learning rate η times that gradient, so the noise injected per step scales like η/√B, and the noise per unit of training progress like η/B. That is the reasoning behind the "linear scaling rule" for large-batch training (multiply the learning rate by k when you multiply the batch by k, with a warmup period), and behind the observation that beyond a certain "critical batch size" larger batches stop speeding up training, because the gradient estimate is already precise and the bottleneck is the curvature of the loss, not noise. Ml4 and dl5 cover the optimisation side; the statistical core is the CLT.

The multivariate CLT

The CLT extends to vectors: the mean of n i.i.d. random vectors with covariance matrix Σ is approximately multivariate normal with covariance Σ/n. This is what justifies joint confidence regions for several metrics at once, the normal approximation for estimated model coefficients (their covariance matrix gives standard errors and correlations between estimates), and the analysis of several A/B metrics together. It is also why errors in estimates of related quantities are correlated: precision and recall estimated on the same test set move together, so their intervals cannot be treated as independent.

Rankings of small groups

The small-schools effect appears in every ranking dashboard: top cities by conversion, worst-performing data slices for a model, best prompts on a leaderboard of small evals, suppliers with the highest defect rate. Small groups fill both ends. Teams then act on noise: they investigate a "failing" slice of 40 examples that is merely unlucky, or roll out a "winning" variant that regresses. Defences, in rough order of effort: show the count next to every rate and hide groups below a minimum size; draw funnel plots or confidence intervals instead of sorted bars; and for systems that act automatically on rankings (recommendations, fraud rules by merchant), use shrunken estimates. A useful habit when reviewing any ranked list is to check whether the bottom of the list has the same size profile as the top.

Eval sets that are too small

Large language model (LLM) and classifier evaluation sets of 50 to 200 examples are common because labelling is expensive, and differences of a few points on them are routinely reported as improvements. With 200 examples at 80% accuracy, the standard error is √(0.8 × 0.2/200) ≈ 2.8 points, so a 4-point difference between two prompts is within noise for independent test sets. Paired comparisons on the same examples are more sensitive (math6), and bootstrap intervals over examples should be standard in every eval report. When many variants are compared on the same small set, the winner's curse means the best one is overestimated; confirm on a held-out set before shipping.

Independence violations in experiment analysis

A frequent and expensive error in A/B analysis is computing standard errors per event (page view, query, message) when randomisation is per user. Events from one user are correlated, so the real standard error can be several times larger than the naive one, and the false positive rate far above the nominal 5%. The symptom is A/A tests (two identical arms) that come out significant much more than 5% of the time, which is why mature experimentation platforms (in10) run continuous A/A tests as a health check. The fixes are to analyse at the randomisation unit, or use the delta method or a cluster bootstrap.

Heavy-tailed metrics

Revenue, session length, tokens generated per request and GPU-seconds per job (time on a graphics processor) are heavy-tailed, so their averages converge slowly and are moved by single extreme units. Experiments on these metrics need far larger samples than conversion metrics, and a single bot or whale can flip a result. Standard practice: cap at a high percentile (for example the 99.9th) before analysis, monitor the contribution of the top units, and pair the mean with a robust metric such as the share of users above a threshold.

Monitoring windows

Alerting on a metric averaged over a short window is an LLN trade-off: short windows react fast but are noisy (high standard error), long windows are stable but slow. A 1-minute error rate over 600 requests at a 1% baseline has a standard error of about 0.4 points, so swings anywhere from 0.2% to 1.8% are routine; the same rate over an hour of 36,000 requests has a standard error of about 0.05 points. Choose window lengths and thresholds together, in units of standard errors, and prefer burn-rate style alerts that combine a short and a long window.

The public case, in production terms

Wainer's examples are policy mistakes, but the pattern is a product-analytics one: an apparent effect concentrated in small groups, a causal story that fits it, and money spent before anyone checks the other end of the distribution. The engineering defence is cultural as much as statistical: require sample sizes and intervals on every rate shown in a review, and when someone presents "the top performers share trait X", ask whether the bottom performers share it too.

for n in (1, 2, 5, 30):
    means = rng.integers(1, 7, size=(100_000, n)).mean(axis=1)
    inside = (np.abs(means - 3.5) < 1.96 * sigma / np.sqrt(n)).mean()
    print(f"n={n:2d}: sd of average {means.std():.3f} (sigma/sqrt(n) = {sigma/np.sqrt(n):.3f}),"
          f" within 1.96 SE: {inside:.3f}")

# 2. Skewed data needs bigger n: exponential (mean 1) averages, check the right tail
for n in (5, 30, 300):
    means = rng.exponential(1.0, size=(100_000, n)).mean(axis=1)
    upper = (means > 1 + 1.645 / np.sqrt(n)).mean()          # normal says 0.05
    print(f"exponential n={n:3d}: P(mean > 1 + 1.645 SE) = {upper:.3f}  (normal: 0.050)")

# 3. No finite variance, no convergence: running means of Cauchy draws
c = rng.standard_cauchy(1_000_000)
running = np.cumsum(c) / np.arange(1, c.size + 1)
print("Cauchy running mean at 1e3, 1e4, 1e5, 1e6:", np.round(running[[999, 9_999, 99_999, -1]], 3))

# 4. Small schools: identical true quality, sizes from 20 to 2,000 students
sizes = rng.integers(20, 2001, size=1_000)
school_means = np.array([rng.normal(500, 100, s).mean() for s in sizes])
order = np.argsort(school_means)
print("median size of all schools  :", int(np.median(sizes)))
print("median size of the top 50   :", int(np.median(sizes[order[-50:]])))
print("median size of the bottom 50:", int(np.median(sizes[order[:50]])))

What to look for (seeded output from the test run):

  1. The standard deviation of the average of n dice tracks σ/√n almost exactly: 1.706, 1.204, 0.766 and 0.312 for n = 1, 2, 5 and 30. The share within 1.96 SE is 1.000 for one die (a single die never lands more than 2.5 from 3.5, so the normal rule means nothing yet), then 0.945, 0.935 and 0.953: close to 0.95 once the bell has formed.
  2. Exponential averages exceed the normal 5% cut-off 6.7% of the time at n = 5, 5.8% at n = 30 and 5.4% at n = 300: skew fades slowly.
  3. The running mean of Cauchy draws reads about 0.61, −1.14, −0.12 and −3.25 at 10³, 10⁴, 10⁵ and 10⁶ draws: no convergence, because there is no finite mean to converge to.
  4. With 1,000 schools of identical quality, the median school size is about 1,031, the median size of the top 50 about 309 and of the bottom 50 about 253: small schools crowd both extremes.

Estimate: is the new prompt better, and how big must the eval be?

Your team evaluates a support assistant on a set of 300 real tickets, graded pass/fail. The current prompt passes 228 (76%). A new prompt, run on a different random sample of 300 tickets, passes 243 (81%). (a) Give a 95% interval for each pass rate and say whether the difference is convincing. (b) The team wants to detect improvements of 3 points reliably. Roughly how big should each evaluation sample be so that the standard error of the difference is at most 1 point? (c) What would make this cheaper?

Answer

(a) Current: SE = √(0.76 × 0.24/300) ≈ 0.0247, interval 0.76 ± 0.048, about 71% to 81%. New: SE = √(0.81 × 0.19/300) ≈ 0.0226, interval about 77% to 85%. The difference is 5 points with standard error √(0.0247² + 0.0226²) ≈ 0.0335 (variances of independent estimates add), so z ≈ 0.05/0.0335 ≈ 1.49, short of 1.96. Suggestive, not convincing: a difference this large would arise by chance alone about 14% of the time if the prompts were equal.

(b) With pass rates near 0.8, each estimate has variance about 0.16/n, the difference about 0.32/n. Setting √(0.32/n) ≤ 0.01 gives n ≥ 3,200 tickets per prompt. At that size a 3-point improvement is 3 standard errors, comfortably detectable. (Math7 gives the full sample-size formula with power, which asks for slightly different numbers but the same order.)

(c) Evaluate both prompts on the same tickets. Then the comparison is paired: only tickets where the two prompts disagree carry information, and the shared difficulty of each ticket cancels. If the prompts agree on most tickets, the standard error of the difference drops a lot, often needing several times fewer tickets for the same precision. Also stratify the sample by ticket category so the mix does not vary between runs.
