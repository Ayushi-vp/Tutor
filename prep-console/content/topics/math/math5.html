In 1943 Allied planners needed to know how many tanks Germany was building each month. Conventional intelligence, built from spies, aerial photographs and interrogations, put the figure for one key period at around 1,400 a month. A group of statisticians took a different route. Captured and destroyed tanks carried serial numbers on their gearboxes, chassis and wheels, and those numbers were assigned in sequence. If the highest gearbox number you have seen is 60, the enemy has built at least 60, and with a little reasoning about how the observed numbers are spread you can estimate how many more there are. The statisticians' estimates came out at a few hundred a month. After the war, German production records showed the statisticians had been close and the intelligence estimates several times too high.

That is estimation: using data to guess a number you cannot observe directly. The number of tanks, the click-through rate of a new ad, the average latency of a service, the probability that a model's prediction is correct, the weights of a neural network: each is a parameter, a fixed but unknown quantity, and every one is estimated from data. Machine-learning training is estimation on a grand scale. The loss you minimise, whether log loss, cross-entropy or squared error, is not arbitrary: each is what you get from one principle, maximum likelihood, applied to an assumption about how the data were generated. Regularisation is not a hack either: it is the same principle with a prior added, maximum a posteriori estimation.

Estimation also has a quality dimension that matters in practice. Two reasonable recipes can give different answers from the same data, so you need a way to say which is better. The tank problem is an example: the largest serial number seen is a perfectly sensible estimate, and it is always too low. A brand-new ad with 2 clicks in 3 impressions has an observed click rate of 67%, and no one believes it. A model's training accuracy is a sensible estimate of its quality, and always too optimistic. Each of these is about the bias and variance of an estimator, and the trade-off between them is the same trade-off that drives regularisation and model selection throughout ML.

This lesson defines estimators and how to judge them (bias, variance, mean squared error), then builds the likelihood function and maximum likelihood, worked by hand for a coin and for a Gaussian, and shows how log loss and squared error fall out of it. Then it adds a prior: MAP estimation with a Beta prior for click rates, and the derivation that a Gaussian prior is L2 regularisation. It closes with shrinkage, why a biased estimator can beat an unbiased one, and the German tank problem in full.

Imagine a detective with a list of suspects and a set of clues. For each suspect she asks: if this person did it, how likely is it that we would find exactly these clues? The suspect for whom the clues are most expected is her best guess. That is maximum likelihood. The suspects are candidate parameter values, the clues are the data, and "how likely the clues would be if this suspect did it" is the likelihood.

Concretely: you toss a coin 10 times and get 7 heads. The coin's probability of heads, p, is the unknown parameter. For each candidate p, the probability of exactly this sequence is p⁷(1 − p)³ (7 heads, 3 tails, independent tosses, math2). If p = 0.5, that is 0.5¹⁰ ≈ 0.00098. If p = 0.7, it is 0.7⁷ × 0.3³ ≈ 0.00222, more than twice as large. If p = 0.3, it is about 0.000075. Plot this number against p and you get a hump; its peak, at p = 0.7, is the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE). The answer is the obvious one, the observed fraction of heads, and that is reassuring: the principle reproduces common sense where common sense is clear, and then keeps working in situations where common sense runs out.

The figure shows a second idea for free. With 70 heads in 100 tosses the peak is in the same place but much narrower: more data rule out more candidates. The width of the likelihood around its peak is how uncertain the estimate is, and it shrinks like 1/√n, the same rule as in math4.

Where does the detective analogy break? A good detective also weighs how plausible each suspect was before the clues: a stranger with no motive needs much stronger evidence than the business partner who inherits everything. Maximum likelihood ignores that; it looks only at how well each candidate explains the clues. Adding the before-the-clues plausibility is Bayes' theorem from math1 applied to parameters: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. The peak of the posterior is the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate. When there are few clues, the prior matters a lot. Three heads in three tosses gives an MLE of p = 1, a coin that can never land tails, which is absurd; a prior that says most coins are roughly fair pulls the estimate back to something sensible. When clues are plentiful, the likelihood swamps the prior and MLE and MAP agree.

The last piece of the picture is how to judge an estimator. Think of an archer. Bias is whether the arrows are centred on the bullseye or systematically off to one side. Variance is how tightly they cluster. A good archer needs both, and the overall miss distance, the mean squared error, is bias² plus variance. A sight that is slightly off but holds the bow steady can beat a perfectly aligned sight on a shaky bow. That single trade-off explains why the "obviously correct" unbiased estimate is often not the best one, why priors and regularisation help with small data, and why ML theory talks so much about bias and variance (ml2).

1. Parameters, estimators and estimates

A parameter is a fixed number describing the population or the process that generates the data: the true click-through rate of an ad, the true mean latency of a service, the true probability that a coin lands heads, the weight a model should put on a feature. It is unknown, and it is not random: there is one true value. A statistic is any number computed from the data. An estimator is a statistic used to guess a parameter: a recipe such as "the fraction of heads" or "the largest serial number seen". Because the data are random, an estimator is a random variable with its own sampling distribution (math4). An estimate is the value the estimator gives on your actual data. The usual notation puts a hat on the parameter's symbol: p̂ ("p-hat") is an estimate of p, μ̂ of μ.

Keeping the three apart prevents a lot of confusion. Statements such as "unbiased", "has variance 0.03" or "is consistent" are about the estimator, describing how it behaves across all the datasets you might have drawn. Your particular estimate is just one number, and it is either close to the truth or not; you cannot tell which, but you can know how close the method usually gets.

2. Bias, variance and mean squared error

Let θ (theta) be a parameter and θ̂ an estimator of it. Three properties describe its quality:

  • Bias = E[θ̂] − θ: how far the estimator's average, over repeated samples, lands from the truth. An estimator with zero bias is unbiased.
  • Variance = E[(θ̂ − E[θ̂])²]: how much it scatters around its own average.
  • Mean squared error (MSE) = E[(θ̂ − θ)²]: the average squared distance from the truth.

The three are linked by the bias–variance decomposition:

MSE = bias² + variance

The derivation is a single step. Write θ̂ − θ = (θ̂ − E[θ̂]) + (E[θ̂] − θ), the random scatter plus the fixed offset. Square it and take expectations: the scatter term gives the variance, the offset term gives bias², and the cross term vanishes because the scatter averages to zero.

A worked comparison. You want the mean μ of a distribution with standard deviation σ = 10, from n = 4 observations. Estimator A is the sample mean: unbiased, variance σ²/n = 25, MSE 25. Estimator B is just the first observation: also unbiased, but variance σ² = 100, MSE 100. Estimator C is 0.8 × the sample mean, shrinking towards zero: its bias is −0.2μ and its variance 0.64 × 25 = 16, so its MSE is 0.04μ² + 16. If the true μ is 10, C's MSE is 4 + 16 = 20, better than the "obviously right" sample mean; if μ is 50, it is 100 + 16 = 116, much worse. Shrinkage helps when the truth is near where you shrink towards, and hurts when it is not. That sentence summarises the whole MAP section ahead.

Two more properties complete the vocabulary. An estimator is consistent if it converges to the truth as n grows; the sample mean is, by the law of large numbers. Among unbiased estimators, the one with the smallest variance is the most efficient, sometimes called the minimum-variance unbiased estimator (MVUE); the German tank section has one.

3. A biased estimator you already know: the variance

Math3 said the sample variance divides by n − 1. Here is why. The natural estimator Σ(xᵢ − x̄)²/n measures deviations from x̄, the sample mean, rather than from the true μ. The sample mean is, by construction, the point that makes the sum of squared deviations as small as possible, so the deviations from it are systematically a little too small. Working through the expectations shows E[Σ(xᵢ − x̄)²] = (n − 1)σ²: one σ² is "used up" by estimating the mean. So dividing by n gives an estimator whose average is (n − 1)/n × σ², biased low, and dividing by n − 1 gives exactly σ² on average. The lab confirms it by simulation: with n = 5 and true variance 225, the divide-by-n version averages 180.4 and the n − 1 version 225.5. For large n the difference is negligible; for a five-row table it is 20%.

4. The likelihood function

Suppose the data x₁, …, xₙ are independent draws from a distribution with parameter θ, written P(x | θ). The likelihood is the probability (or density) of the whole observed dataset, viewed as a function of θ:

L(θ) = P(x₁, …, xₙ | θ) = P(x₁ | θ) × P(x₂ | θ) × … × P(xₙ | θ)

The product comes from independence (math1). The crucial shift in viewpoint: the data are now fixed (you have them), and θ varies. L(θ) answers "if the parameter were θ, how probable would these exact data be?" It is not the probability that θ is the true value. It does not even add up to 1 across θ values. For 7 heads in 10, L(0.7) ≈ 0.00222 and L(0.5) ≈ 0.00098; the useful statement is the ratio: the data are about 2.3 times more probable under p = 0.7 than under p = 0.5. Ratios of likelihoods are evidence; that is the likelihood ratio from math1, now comparing parameter values instead of hypotheses.

In practice we almost always work with the log-likelihood, ℓ(θ) = log L(θ) = Σ log P(xᵢ | θ). Three reasons. The logarithm is increasing, so ℓ and L peak at the same θ. A sum is easier to work with than a product, especially when differentiating. And a product of thousands of probabilities underflows to 0 in floating point, while the sum of their logs is an ordinary number (math1's deep section). For the coin, ℓ(p) = 7 log p + 3 log(1 − p).

5. Maximum likelihood: the coin

The maximum likelihood estimate is the θ that maximises L(θ), equivalently ℓ(θ). For simple models you can find it by calculus: at the top of a smooth hill the slope is zero. (Math10 teaches derivatives; here you only need two facts: the slope of log p is 1/p, and the slope of log(1 − p) is −1/(1 − p).) For h heads in n tosses, ℓ(p) = h log p + (n − h) log(1 − p), so its slope is

h/p − (n − h)/(1 − p)

Setting it to zero: h(1 − p) = (n − h)p, so h = np, so p̂ = h/n. The MLE is the observed fraction. For 7 heads in 10, p̂ = 0.7. The lab finds the same answer by brute force: evaluate ℓ on a grid of 999 values of p and take the largest. Brute-force grids are a perfectly good way to understand an MLE; calculus is a shortcut, and for complex models (a neural network with a billion weights) neither works in closed form, so the maximum is found by gradient ascent on ℓ, or equivalently gradient descent on −ℓ (math11, ml4).

This estimator has a weakness that the next sections fix. With 3 heads in 3 tosses, p̂ = 1: the estimate says tails is impossible. With 0 failures in 50 test runs, the MLE failure rate is 0. Maximum likelihood trusts the data completely, which is right when data are plentiful and reckless when they are scarce.

6. Maximum likelihood: the Gaussian, and where squared error comes from

Now suppose latencies x₁, …, xₙ are modelled as Normal(μ, σ²). Each observation's density is (1/(σ√(2π))) exp(−(xᵢ − μ)²/(2σ²)) (math2). Taking logs and adding over observations:

ℓ(μ, σ) = −n log σ − (n/2) log(2π) − Σ(xᵢ − μ)² / (2σ²)

Look at the μ part. Only the last term involves μ, and it has a minus sign, so maximising ℓ over μ means minimising Σ(xᵢ − μ)², the sum of squared errors. The minimiser is the sample mean, μ̂ = x̄ (math3's least-squares fact). Maximising over σ gives σ̂² = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)²/n, the divide-by-n variance from section 3, which is the MLE and is biased low. Worked: latencies 100, 110, 120, 130 and 140 ms give μ̂ = 120 ms; squared deviations 400, 100, 0, 100, 400 sum to 1,000, so σ̂²_MLE = 200 (σ̂ ≈ 14.1 ms), while the unbiased version is 250 (≈ 15.8 ms).

This is the deeper reason squared error is the standard regression loss. Replace the single μ with a model's prediction f(xᵢ) for each input. If you assume y = f(x) + Gaussian noise with constant variance, the negative log-likelihood is Σ(yᵢ − f(xᵢ))²/(2σ²) plus terms that do not depend on the model, so minimising mean squared error is maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise. Change the noise assumption and the loss changes: Laplace noise (a sharper peak and heavier tails than Gaussian) gives absolute error, which is why absolute error is robust to outliers (ml5).

7. Maximum likelihood for classifiers: log loss and cross-entropy

For binary labels yᵢ ∈ {0, 1}, a classifier predicts a probability pᵢ that yᵢ = 1. Model each label as Bernoulli(pᵢ): its probability is pᵢ^yᵢ (1 − pᵢ)^(1 − yᵢ), which is pᵢ when the label is 1 and 1 − pᵢ when it is 0. The negative log-likelihood of all labels is

NLL = −Σ [ yᵢ log pᵢ + (1 − yᵢ) log(1 − pᵢ) ]

which is exactly log loss, also called binary cross-entropy. Worked on three examples: labels (1, 0, 1) with predictions (0.9, 0.2, 0.6) give −[log 0.9 + log 0.8 + log 0.6] = −[−0.105 − 0.223 − 0.511] ≈ 0.839, an average of 0.280 per example. A confident wrong prediction is punished heavily: predicting 0.01 for a true 1 costs −log 0.01 ≈ 4.6 by itself. For K classes the same argument with a categorical distribution gives categorical cross-entropy, −Σ log p(correct class), the loss that trains every classifier and every language model (math12 shows the information-theory view; ml5 compares losses; mc2 and mc10 implement them). The headline is worth memorising: training with log loss, cross-entropy or squared error is maximum likelihood; the loss encodes the assumed noise model.

8. The public case: the German tank problem

Tanks are numbered 1, 2, …, N in production order, and you have captured k of them, with largest serial number m. What is N? Treat the captured serials as a random sample without replacement from 1 to N. For any N ≥ m, every set of k serials is equally likely, with probability 1/C(N, k); for N < m the data are impossible. So the likelihood is zero below m and falls as N grows above m, and the MLE is N̂ = m, the largest serial seen. It is also obviously too small: the true N must be at least m, so this estimator can only err low. Its average is k(N + 1)/(k + 1); with N = 300 and k = 4 that is 240.8, a bias of about −59.

The fix uses the gaps. The k observed serials split the range 1…m into gaps, and on average the gap above m (between m and N) should be about as big as the average gap below. The average gap is roughly m/k − 1, so the estimator becomes

N̂ = m + m/k − 1

which is exactly unbiased, and has the smallest variance among unbiased estimators (it is the MVUE). With captured serials 19, 40, 42 and 60 (k = 4, m = 60): N̂ = 60 + 15 − 1 = 74. The lab compares three estimators on 50,000 simulated captures of k = 4 from N = 300: the maximum (bias −59.4, standard deviation 48.8, root mean squared error, RMSE, the square root of the MSE so it is in tanks rather than tanks², 76.9), twice the sample mean minus one (unbiased but standard deviation 86.5), and m + m/k − 1 (bias −0.2, standard deviation 61.0, root-MSE 61.0). The unbiased version wins on total error here, and the "twice the mean" estimator, also unbiased, is much noisier because it ignores the most informative number, the maximum.

In the war, the economists Richard Ruggles and Henry Brodie, working for the Allies in London, applied this kind of analysis to markings on gearboxes, chassis, engines and road wheels. Their 1947 paper in the Journal of the American Statistical Association compared the estimates with German records captured after the war: for June 1940 the statistical estimate was 169 tanks against an intelligence estimate of 1,000 and a recorded figure of 122; for June 1941, 244 against 1,550 and 271; for August 1942, 327 against 1,550 and 342. A widely quoted summary (in The Guardian, 2006) puts it as roughly 246 a month estimated by the statisticians, about 1,400 by conventional intelligence, and 245 in the records. Two lessons carry over to ML work. First, an estimator that ignores how the data were generated (the conventional approach) can be off by a factor of five, while a simple model of the generating process (uniform sampling of serial numbers) gives accurate answers from a handful of observations. Second, "maximum likelihood" is not automatically "best": here the MLE is biased, and a small correction gives a better estimator.

9. Adding a prior: MAP estimation

Maximum likelihood answers "which θ makes the data most probable?" Often you want "which θ is most probable, given the data?" That is P(θ | data), the posterior, and Bayes' theorem (math1) gives it:

P(θ | data) = P(data | θ) · P(θ) / P(data) ∝ likelihood × prior

The symbol ∝ means "proportional to": P(data) does not depend on θ, so it only rescales the curve and does not move its peak. The prior P(θ) encodes what is plausible before the data. The maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate is the θ at the posterior's peak. Taking logs, MAP maximises log-likelihood + log-prior: the data term plus a penalty for implausible θ.

For a probability like p, the natural prior is the Beta distribution, Beta(a, b), whose density is proportional to p^(a − 1)(1 − p)^(b − 1) on [0, 1]. You can read it as "a − 1 imaginary successes and b − 1 imaginary failures already seen" for the mode, or a and b pseudo-counts for the mean. Beta(1, 1) is flat (every p equally plausible); Beta(2, 2) is a gentle hump at 0.5; Beta(3, 97) is concentrated near 0.03. Multiply the binomial likelihood p^h(1 − p)^(n − h) by a Beta(a, b) prior and the posterior is p^(h + a − 1)(1 − p)^(n − h + b − 1), another Beta: Beta(a + h, b + n − h). The prior's pseudo-counts simply add to the real counts. A prior that keeps its family under updating like this is called conjugate. The posterior's peak and mean are:

MAP = (h + a − 1) / (n + a + b − 2) and posterior mean = (h + a) / (n + a + b)

Worked: 3 heads in 3. MLE: 3/3 = 1. With a Beta(2, 2) prior the posterior is Beta(5, 2): MAP = 4/5 = 0.8, posterior mean = 5/7 ≈ 0.714. The figure shows the three curves.

Worked: a new ad. An ad has 2 clicks in 3 impressions. The MLE click-through rate (CTR) is 67%, which would rank it above every established ad. Across your catalogue, ads average about 3%, and a Beta(3, 97) prior (mean 3%, worth 100 pseudo-impressions) captures that. The posterior mean is (2 + 3)/(3 + 100) ≈ 4.9% and the MAP is 4/101 ≈ 4.0%: the ad looks promising, but it does not jump the queue on three impressions. After 10,000 impressions with 400 clicks, the posterior mean is 403/10,100 ≈ 3.99%, almost the MLE of 4.0%: the data have taken over. This is the standard cold-start fix in recommendation and ads systems, and the same arithmetic as Laplace smoothing in Naive Bayes, which adds one pseudo-count to every word so that an unseen word does not get probability zero (that is the posterior mean under a flat Beta(1, 1) prior).

10. Priors on weights are regularisers

Apply MAP to a model's weights w. Suppose the prior says each weight is Normal(0, τ²): small weights are plausible, huge ones are not. Its log density is −w²/(2τ²) plus a constant. With Gaussian noise of variance σ² on the targets, maximising log-likelihood + log-prior is the same as minimising

Σ (yᵢ − f(xᵢ))² + λ Σ wⱼ², with λ = σ²/τ²

which is ridge regression, or L2 regularisation (a penalty on the sum of squared weights, named after the L2 norm in math8), or "weight decay" in deep learning. A strong prior (small τ, a firm belief that weights are near zero) means a large λ. No prior (τ → ∞) means λ = 0 and plain MLE. A Laplace prior, with density proportional to exp(−|w|/b), gives a penalty proportional to Σ|wⱼ|, which is L1 regularisation (a penalty on the sum of absolute weights; lasso); its sharp peak at zero is why L1 drives some weights exactly to zero (ml3). So regularisation is not an add-on trick: it is MAP estimation, and the regularisation strength is a statement about how large you believe the weights should be.

11. Shrinkage: trading bias for variance, worked

MAP estimates are pulled towards the prior's centre, so they are biased. Section 2 says that can still lower the error. Compare two estimators of a coin's p from n = 3 tosses: the MLE h/3, and the Beta(2, 2) posterior mean (h + 2)/7, which shrinks towards 0.5.

true pestimatorbiasvarianceMSE
0.5MLE h/300.08330.0833
0.5(h + 2)/700.01530.0153
0.9MLE h/300.03000.0300
0.9(h + 2)/7−0.2290.00550.0578

The variance of the shrunk estimator is always smaller (its range is squeezed from [0, 1] to [2/7, 5/7]). When the truth is near the prior's centre, that is pure gain: the MSE falls more than five-fold. When the truth is far away, the bias costs more than the variance saves, and the MSE nearly doubles. With more data, the prior's two pseudo-tosses count for less and both estimators converge. The practical rule: shrink when data are scarce and you have a sensible prior, ideally fitted from related data (other ads, other users, other products), and let the data dominate as they accumulate. The deep section shows that for many parameters at once, shrinkage provably wins.

12. Another recipe: the method of moments

Maximum likelihood is not the only way to build an estimator. The oldest alternative, the method of moments, matches averages: write the distribution's mean (and if needed its variance and higher averages, called moments) as a formula in the parameters, set those formulas equal to the sample's mean and variance, and solve. For the tanks, the serial numbers are uniform on 1…N, whose mean is (N + 1)/2; setting that equal to the sample mean x̄ gives N̂ = 2x̄ − 1, the second estimator in the lab. It is unbiased and easy to compute, but its standard deviation is 86.5 against 61.0 for the MVUE, because it uses the average serial rather than the maximum, and the maximum carries most of the information about where the range ends. A second example: for a Poisson count, both methods give λ̂ = x̄; for a gamma distribution, the method of moments gives closed-form estimates where the MLE needs an iterative solver.

Method-of-moments estimates are often used as quick first answers or as starting points for an iterative MLE, and they appear inside ML more than people notice: fitting a Beta prior to a set of click rates by matching its mean and variance (the empirical Bayes step in the deep section) is the method of moments, and so is setting batch-normalisation statistics from a batch's mean and variance. The general lesson of this lesson holds: several reasonable recipes exist for any parameter, and the way to choose between them is to compare their bias, variance and MSE, by formula where possible and by simulation otherwise, exactly as the lab does.

Fisher information and how precise the MLE is

The sharper the log-likelihood's peak, the more precisely the data pin down θ. Fisher information measures that sharpness: for one observation, I(θ) = −E[d²/dθ² log P(x | θ)], the expected curvature of the log-likelihood (math10 covers second derivatives). For n independent observations it adds up to n·I(θ). Two classical results use it. The MLE is asymptotically normal: for large n, θ̂ is approximately Normal(θ, 1/(n·I(θ))), so its standard error is 1/√(n·I(θ)). And the Cramér–Rao bound says no unbiased estimator can have variance below 1/(n·I(θ)), so for large samples the MLE is as precise as any unbiased estimator can be. For a Bernoulli, I(p) = 1/(p(1 − p)), which gives the standard error √(p(1 − p)/n) used for accuracies and conversion rates in math4 and math6. In deep learning, the Fisher information matrix (one entry per pair of parameters) underlies natural-gradient optimisation and elastic weight consolidation, which protects weights that carried a lot of information about an earlier task.

MLE minimises a divergence from the data

Maximising the average log-likelihood (1/n)Σ log P(xᵢ | θ) is, for large n, the same as maximising E_data[log P(x | θ)], and that equals −H(P_data) − KL(P_data ‖ P_θ), where H is the entropy of the data distribution and KL the Kullback–Leibler divergence (both in math12). The entropy does not depend on θ, so maximum likelihood finds the model distribution closest to the data distribution in KL divergence. This is why cross-entropy loss, negative log-likelihood and "minimise KL to the data" are three names for one objective, and why a language model trained by next-token prediction is fitting the distribution of its training text, including its quirks. The direction of the KL matters: this one punishes the model for putting low probability where data exist, which makes MLE-trained models "cover" all the data, sometimes by spreading probability too thinly.

When maximum likelihood misbehaves

Several failure modes recur. With perfectly separable classification data, the logistic-regression likelihood keeps increasing as weights grow, so the MLE is at infinity: the optimiser never converges and probabilities become absurdly confident. In mixture models, a Gaussian component can collapse onto a single point with variance shrinking to zero, sending the likelihood to infinity. With many parameters relative to data, the MLE overfits: it explains the noise in this sample as if it were signal. And as the tank problem showed, the MLE can be biased even in simple models. In each case a prior (regularisation), a constraint (a minimum variance) or a bias correction fixes the problem, and in each case the fix trades a little bias for a lot of stability.

MAP versus the full posterior

MAP keeps only the peak of the posterior, which loses two things. First, uncertainty: a sharp posterior and a flat one can have the same peak, and a decision that depends on risk needs to know which. The full Bayesian approach keeps the posterior and averages predictions over it: the probability that the next toss is heads is the posterior mean of p, (h + a)/(n + a + b), not the MAP. Second, invariance: the MLE does not care how you parameterise the model (the MLE of p², say, is p̂²), but the MAP does, because reparameterising stretches the prior's density and can move its peak. For most ML practice MAP is the right compromise, since full posteriors over millions of weights are expensive. Bayesian deep learning approximations (ensembles, Monte Carlo dropout, Laplace approximations) are attempts to recover some of the lost uncertainty cheaply.

Empirical Bayes and the James–Stein surprise

Where does a prior come from? Often from the data themselves: fit the Beta prior for click rates to the CTRs of all established ads, then use it to shrink the new ones. This is empirical Bayes. It is backed by one of the most surprising results in statistics. Charles Stein showed in 1956, and James and Stein made explicit in 1961, that when estimating three or more means simultaneously from normal data, the obvious estimator (each group's own sample mean) is inadmissible: shrinking all of them towards their common average gives lower total squared error, whatever the true means are. The intuition is the one from math4's small-schools example: extreme group means are partly noise, and pulling them in removes more noise than signal. Ratings systems that rank items by a "Bayesian average" (the item's mean blended with the site-wide mean according to how many votes it has), batting averages early in a season and per-user estimates in personalisation all use this idea.

Estimation when the likelihood has no closed form

For models with hidden variables, such as which cluster a point came from (ml12) or which topic produced a word, the likelihood involves a sum over all hidden configurations and has no simple maximiser. The expectation–maximisation (EM) algorithm alternates two easy steps: compute the probabilities of the hidden variables given current parameters, then re-estimate parameters as if those probabilities were observed counts. Each round cannot decrease the likelihood. Gaussian mixture models and the Baum–Welch algorithm for hidden Markov models are EM. In deep learning, variational autoencoders (dl10) maximise a lower bound on the likelihood instead, the ELBO (evidence lower bound), obtained from Jensen's inequality (math3).

Cold start and small counts

Every system that ranks items by an observed rate faces the 2-in-3 problem: new ads, new products, new sellers and new prompts have tiny samples and therefore extreme MLE rates. Without smoothing, rankings fill with lucky newcomers (the small-schools effect from math4) and recommendation systems oscillate as their rates crash back. The production fix is a Beta prior (or its equivalent, adding pseudo-counts) fitted to the population of established items, usually segmented by category, so a new item starts at its category's typical rate and earns its way up. Monitor how quickly items converge and whether the prior is still right for new categories. Exploration strategies (Thompson sampling, which samples a rate from each item's Beta posterior and shows the item with the highest draw) use the same posterior to decide what to test next.

Zero counts and the rule of three

"Zero incidents in 500 runs" is often reported as "a 0% failure rate". The MLE is indeed zero, but the data are consistent with small positive rates. If the true failure probability is q, the chance of zero failures in n runs is (1 − q)ⁿ; setting it to 5% and solving gives q ≈ 3/n for large n (because ln 0.05 ≈ −3). So zero failures in 500 runs supports a 95% upper bound of about 0.6%, and zero jailbreaks in 300 red-team prompts supports a bound of about 1%. Safety and reliability reports should quote that bound, and models that rely on estimated probabilities (Naive Bayes, n-gram models, frequency-based anomaly detectors) need smoothing so unseen events get a small, non-zero probability.

Training objectives as likelihoods

Seeing losses as negative log-likelihoods is practical, not only theoretical. When a regression target is a count, a duration or a heavy-tailed amount, squared error assumes the wrong noise model, and switching to a Poisson, gamma or log-normal likelihood often improves both accuracy and calibration. When targets are noisy in a known way (some labels from careful annotators, some from cheap ones), per-example weights or a noise model in the likelihood handle it. And when a model is overconfident, the likelihood view suggests the fixes: a prior (weight decay), label smoothing (a softened target distribution), or post-hoc calibration (ml15).

Biased estimates hiding in pipelines

Several common production numbers are biased estimators in disguise. Training accuracy estimates generalisation and is biased upward because the model was fitted to those examples; that is why held-out data exist (ml9). The best validation score from a hyperparameter search is biased upward by selection (the winner's curse in math4). The divide-by-n variance used in some streaming implementations is biased low for small windows. Rates computed only over completed requests exclude timeouts and are biased towards fast requests. Naming the bias is usually enough to fix it: hold out data, re-evaluate the winner, use n − 1, count the failures.

Sequential IDs leak volume

The German tank problem runs in reverse against modern companies. If order numbers, invoice IDs or user IDs are assigned sequentially and are visible to customers, anyone who places a few orders a week apart can estimate your order volume from the gaps, exactly as the Allies estimated tank production. Competitors, journalists and investors have done this with sequential identifiers. The fix is the one the Wikipedia article lists for the military case: use random or encrypted identifiers (UUIDs, universally unique identifiers drawn at random, or sequential IDs passed through a keyed permutation) for anything exposed externally, and keep sequential keys internal.

The public case, in production terms

The tank estimate worked because the statisticians modelled the data-generating process explicitly (serial numbers sampled uniformly from 1 to N) instead of aggregating opinions. That is the core move behind maximum likelihood, and it is the habit to bring to any estimation problem in production: write down how the data you see were produced, including what you cannot see (unsold inventory, users who never clicked, requests that timed out), and estimate the parameter of that process. When the generating story is right, small samples go a long way; when it is wrong, large samples give precise wrong answers.

Goal: find an MLE by brute force, see the divide-by-n variance bias, compare three German-tank estimators by bias, spread and RMSE, apply a Beta prior to a new ad's click rate, and measure when shrinkage helps. NumPy only (gpt_env); save as math5.py.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 1. MLE for a coin by brute force: 7 heads in 10 tosses
p = np.linspace(0.001, 0.999, 999)
loglik = 7 * np.log(p) + 3 * np.log(1 - p)
print("MLE of p from a grid:", p[np.argmax(loglik)].round(3))

# 2. Gaussian MLE of the variance divides by n, and is biased low
n, trials = 5, 200_000
x = rng.normal(120, 15, size=(trials, n))                 # true variance 225
print("mean of var/n     :", x.var(axis=1, ddof=0).mean().round(1), "(expect 225*(n-1)/n = 180)")
print("mean of var/(n-1) :", x.var(axis=1, ddof=1).mean().round(1), "(expect 225)")

# 3. German tank problem: N = 300 tanks, capture k = 4 serial numbers
N, k = 300, 4
serials = np.array([rng.choice(N, size=k, replace=False) + 1 for _ in range(50_000)])
m = serials.max(axis=1)
estimators = {
    "MLE: max m        ": m,
    "2*mean - 1        ": 2 * serials.mean(axis=1) - 1,
    "m + m/k - 1 (MVUE)": m + m / k - 1,
}
for name, est in estimators.items():
    bias, sd = est.mean() - N, est.std()
    print(f"{name}: bias {bias:6.1f}   sd {sd:5.1f}   RMSE {np.sqrt(bias**2 + sd**2):5.1f}")
print("serials 19, 40, 42, 60 ->", 60 + 60 / 4 - 1)

# 4. MAP / posterior mean with a Beta prior: a new ad with 2 clicks in 3 views
a, b = 3, 97                                              # prior: about 3% CTR, worth 100 views
clicks, views = 2, 3
print("MLE CTR           :", round(clicks / views, 3))
print("posterior mean CTR:", round((clicks + a) / (views + a + b), 4))
print("MAP CTR           :", round((clicks + a - 1) / (views + a + b - 2), 4))

# 5. Shrinkage trades bias for variance: coin, n = 3, MLE h/n vs (h+2)/(n+4)
for p_true in (0.5, 0.9):
    h = rng.binomial(3, p_true, 200_000)
    for name, est in (("MLE      ", h / 3), ("Beta(2,2)", (h + 2) / 7)):
        print(f"p={p_true} {name}: MSE {np.mean((est - p_true) ** 2):.4f}")

What to look for (seeded output from the test run):

  1. The grid search over p for 7 heads in 10 peaks at 0.7: the MLE is the observed fraction.
  2. With n = 5 and true variance 225, the divide-by-n estimator averages 180.4 (biased low, as (n − 1)/n × 225 = 180 predicts) and the divide-by-(n − 1) estimator 225.5.
  3. Tank estimators with N = 300, k = 4: the maximum has bias −59.4 and RMSE 76.9; 2·mean − 1 is unbiased but has sd 86.5; m + m/k − 1 has bias −0.2, sd 61.0 and the lowest RMSE, 61.0. Serials 19, 40, 42, 60 give 74.
  4. The new ad: MLE CTR 0.667, posterior mean 0.0485 and MAP 0.0396 with a Beta(3, 97) prior.
  5. Shrinkage with n = 3: at p = 0.5 the Beta(2, 2) estimator's MSE is 0.0153 versus the MLE's 0.0833; at p = 0.9 it is 0.0578 versus 0.0301. Shrinkage wins near the prior and loses far from it.

Design: ranking sellers by refund rate

A marketplace wants a "most reliable sellers" badge for the 5% of sellers with the lowest refund rate. Seller A has 0 refunds in 4 orders. Seller B has 3 refunds in 400. Seller C has 40 refunds in 4,000. Across all sellers with at least 1,000 orders, refund rates average 2% and most lie between 0.5% and 4%. (a) What are the MLE refund rates, and what would a naive ranking do? (b) Propose an estimator and compute it for A, B and C. (c) How would you choose its strength, and what would you monitor?

Answer

(a) MLEs: A 0/4 = 0%, B 3/400 = 0.75%, C 40/4,000 = 1.0%. A naive ranking puts A, and every other seller with a handful of clean orders, at the top. The badge would go mostly to new sellers with almost no history, which is the small-sample effect: their rates are extreme in both directions because they are noisy.

(b) Use a Beta prior that matches established sellers: mean 2%, with strength chosen so it spans roughly 0.5% to 4%. A Beta(2, 98) prior has mean 2% and acts like 100 pseudo-orders. Posterior means (refunds + 2)/(orders + 100): A (0 + 2)/104 ≈ 1.92%; B (3 + 2)/500 = 1.0%; C (40 + 2)/4,100 ≈ 1.02%. A is now ranked near average, which is all four orders can support; B and C are both a bit better than average, B slightly ahead. For the badge, a more conservative choice is to rank by an upper bound of each seller's posterior (for example its 95th percentile), so sellers need both a low rate and enough orders to be confident.

(c) Fit the prior from data (empirical Bayes): estimate the mean and spread of refund rates among sellers with plenty of orders, and solve for a and b that match them, possibly per category, since electronics and clothing have different norms. Monitor how badge holders' refund rates develop over the following months (regression to the mean would show the prior is too weak), the share of badges going to sellers with fewer than, say, 50 orders, and whether new sellers can still earn the badge in reasonable time.
