Your team trains a new retrieval model. On the 1,000-question evaluation set it answers 900 correctly; the current model answers 870. Three points better. Ship it? Someone asks whether the difference is "significant". Someone else runs a test and reports p = 0.036. A third person asks what that number means, and the room goes quiet, because half the people think it means there is a 96.4% chance the new model is better (it does not), and the other half are not sure what it means at all.

Hypothesis testing exists for exactly this situation: you have measured a difference, and you need to know whether it is larger than the random variation you would expect anyway. The first person to put it on a modern footing was a chemist at the Guinness brewery in Dublin, William Sealy Gosset. Brewing experiments were expensive, so he often had only a handful of measurements, and the large-sample methods of the day gave wrong answers for small samples. In 1908 he published the fix, the t-distribution, under the pen name "Student", because Guinness did not want competitors to know it employed statisticians. His paper illustrated the method on a tiny dataset: ten patients who each tried two sleeping drugs. That dataset is still the cleanest way to learn the subject, and this lesson uses it.

A century later, p-values are everywhere and widely misunderstood. In 2016 the American Statistical Association (ASA) took the unusual step of publishing a formal statement on what p-values do and do not mean, because misuse had become a factor in research that would not replicate. ML and product teams inherit both the method and the misunderstandings: A/B tests declared wins on noise, model comparisons on test sets too small to tell the models apart, "no significant regression" read as "no regression", and dashboards that test fifty metrics and celebrate the three that cross p < 0.05.

This lesson builds the machinery properly. It starts with the logic of a test (null hypothesis, test statistic, p-value, significance level, the two kinds of error), connects tests to confidence intervals, introduces the t-distribution and works the paired and two-sample t-tests on Student's own data by hand, then covers comparing proportions and classifiers, chi-squared tests for counts, statistical power and how to plan for it, and permutation tests. It ends with what p-values do not tell you, following the ASA's six principles, and with the production habits that keep model comparisons honest. Math7 then applies all of it to A/B testing at scale.

A hypothesis test works like a criminal trial. The defendant is presumed innocent: that presumption is the null hypothesis, written H₀, usually "there is no difference" or "the change had no effect". The prosecution presents evidence, the data. The jury asks one question: if the defendant really were innocent, how likely is evidence this damning? If very unlikely, they reject the presumption of innocence and convict. If not, they acquit, and the verdict is "not guilty", not "innocent": the evidence simply was not strong enough.

Mapping the analogy onto statistics gives the whole vocabulary:

  • The test statistic is the evidence condensed into one number, built so that its behaviour under the null is known. Typically it is (observed difference) ÷ (standard error): how many standard errors away from "no difference" the data landed.
  • The p-value is the jury's question answered: the probability, if H₀ were true, of a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed.
  • The significance level α (alpha) is the standard of proof set before the trial, conventionally 0.05: reject H₀ if p < α.
  • Convicting an innocent defendant is a Type I error (false positive); its rate is α. Acquitting a guilty one is a Type II error (false negative). The probability of convicting the guilty, of detecting a real effect, is the test's power.

Here is the picture for Student's sleep data, which the body works through. If the two drugs were equally effective, the test statistic t would follow the curve below, usually landing between about −2.3 and +2.3. The observed value was 4.06, far out in the tail. The p-value is the area of the tails beyond ±4.06: about 0.0028.

Where does the analogy break? A jury considers how plausible guilt was before the trial, informally: motive, opportunity, prior record. A p-value does not; it only asks how surprising the data are under the null. So a p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, any more than the probability of the evidence given innocence is the probability of innocence given the evidence. That is the prosecutor's fallacy from math1 again, and it is the most common misreading of p-values. Getting from "the data are surprising under H₀" to "H₀ is probably false" requires knowing how plausible H₀ was to begin with.

Every test you will meet follows the same five moves, whatever its name: state the null and alternative hypotheses; choose α (and ideally the smallest effect you care about) before looking at the data; compute a test statistic, almost always (estimate − value under H₀) ÷ standard error; find the p-value from the statistic's known distribution under H₀ (normal, t, chi-squared, or a permutation distribution); and decide, reporting the estimate and its interval alongside the verdict. The names of tests (z, t, Welch, McNemar, chi-squared) only tell you which standard error and which reference distribution to use for a given kind of data. Once the five moves are familiar, a new test is a small variation, not a new idea.

The second idea to carry from the primer is that every test has a twin: the confidence interval. Instead of asking "is the difference zero?", it asks "which differences are compatible with the data?" For the sleep drugs the 95% interval is 0.70 to 2.46 extra hours. Zero is outside it, which is the same verdict as the test, but the interval also tells you how big the effect plausibly is, which the p-value never does. When in doubt, report the interval.

1. The logic of a test, worked on a coin

Is a coin fair? Toss it 100 times and get 60 heads. Step by step:

  1. State the hypotheses. H₀: p = 0.5 (fair). The alternative H₁: p ≠ 0.5 (biased either way), which makes this a two-sided test. If you had a specific reason to look only for a bias towards heads, decided before seeing the data, you could use a one-sided test, H₁: p > 0.5.
  2. Choose α before looking: 0.05.
  3. Compute a test statistic. Under H₀ the number of heads is Binomial(100, 0.5), with mean 50 and standard deviation √(100 × 0.5 × 0.5) = 5 (math2), and by the central limit theorem (CLT, math4) approximately normal. So z = (60 − 50)/5 = 2: the observation is 2 standard errors above what H₀ predicts.
  4. Find the p-value. P(|Z| ≥ 2) under a standard normal is about 0.046: if the coin were fair, a result this lopsided (60+ heads or 40 or fewer) happens about 4.6% of the time.
  5. Decide. 0.046 < 0.05, so reject H₀ at the 5% level. The critical value for a two-sided 5% z-test is 1.96, and 2 exceeds it.

Two features of this recipe deserve emphasis. The test statistic is a ratio: effect divided by its standard error. Any test you meet later has this shape, and it explains everything about significance: bigger effects, larger samples (smaller SE) and less noise all push the statistic outward. And the p-value is computed entirely in the world where H₀ is true. It is a statement about data, not about hypotheses.

2. Significance, errors and what α controls

If you reject whenever p < α, then among all the tests you run where H₀ is actually true, you will wrongly reject about a fraction α of them. That is the precise guarantee a test gives: control of the Type I error rate. It says nothing about tests where H₀ is false; there the relevant rate is the Type II error rate β, the chance of missing the effect, and power = 1 − β.

H₀ true (no effect)H₀ false (real effect)
Reject H₀Type I error (false positive), rate αCorrect detection, rate = power
Do not rejectCorrect, rate 1 − αType II error (miss), rate β

The 0.05 convention dates from Ronald Fisher in the 1920s and is arbitrary; nothing special happens between p = 0.049 and p = 0.051. Fields choose differently: particle physics requires about 5 standard deviations (p around 3 × 10⁻⁷) before claiming a discovery, because it runs enormous numbers of searches. The deeper point, developed in section 9, is that lowering α to reduce false positives raises β unless you collect more data. You trade one error for the other, and the right balance depends on what each costs.

3. Confidence intervals, and why they are tests in disguise

Math4 built the 95% interval estimate ± 1.96 × SE. There is an exact link with testing: a 95% confidence interval is the set of null values that a two-sided 5% test would not reject. For the coin, the estimate is p̂ = 0.60 with standard error √(0.6 × 0.4/100) ≈ 0.049, giving 0.60 ± 0.096, roughly 0.50 to 0.70. The value 0.5 sits right at the edge, matching the borderline p = 0.046. (The small mismatch comes from using p̂ rather than 0.5 in the standard error; both are standard approximations.)

The interval is almost always more useful than the test, because it carries the effect size. "p = 0.03" says the effect is probably not zero; "+3 points, 95% CI +0.2 to +5.8" says the same thing and also that the gain could be tiny or quite large. Its correct interpretation is about the procedure: over many repeated samples, intervals built this way contain the true value 95% of the time. Any one interval either contains it or does not.

4. When σ is unknown: the t-distribution

The z-test uses the true standard deviation σ. In practice you estimate it from the data with s (math3), and with small samples s can be noticeably too small or too large. Dividing by a noisy s makes the statistic more variable than a standard normal. Gosset worked out its exact distribution for normally distributed data: the t-distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom, the number of independent deviations left after estimating the mean. It is bell-shaped and symmetric like the normal, with heavier tails, and it approaches the normal as the degrees of freedom grow.

degrees of freedom491829∞ (normal)

The lab shows what happens if you ignore this: with n = 5 and no real effect, comparing t to 1.96 rejects about 12% of the time instead of 5%. Above about 30 observations the difference hardly matters, which is why large-sample tests in A/B testing simply use z.

The one-sample t-test checks whether a mean differs from a value μ₀: t = (x̄ − μ₀)/(s/√n), compared with the t-distribution on n − 1 degrees of freedom. The matching 95% interval is x̄ ± t* × s/√n, where t* is the critical value from the table.

5. The paired t-test on Student's data

Cushny and Peebles (1904) gave ten patients each of two forms of a sleeping drug, hyoscyamine, and recorded the extra hours of sleep compared with no drug. Student reproduced their table in 1908:

patient12345678910mean
drug 1+0.7−1.6−0.2−1.2−0.1+3.4+3.7+0.80.0+2.0+0.75
drug 2+1.9+0.8+1.1+0.1−0.1+4.4+5.5+1.6+4.6+3.4+2.33
difference+1.2+2.4+1.3+1.30.0+1.0+1.8+0.8+4.6+1.4+1.58

Is drug 2 better? Each patient tried both, so the natural analysis looks at each patient's difference, a paired design. Then it is a one-sample t-test on the ten differences against μ₀ = 0:

  • Mean difference d̄ = 15.8/10 = 1.58 hours.
  • Squared deviations of the differences from 1.58 sum to 13.616, so s = √(13.616/9) ≈ 1.230.
  • Standard error = 1.230/√10 ≈ 0.389.
  • t = 1.58/0.389 ≈ 4.06 on 9 degrees of freedom.
  • Two-sided p ≈ 0.0028; critical value 2.262; 95% CI 1.58 ± 2.262 × 0.389 = 0.70 to 2.46 hours.

Strong evidence that drug 2 adds sleep relative to drug 1, somewhere between about 40 minutes and two and a half hours per night. Student's own calculation, in the conventions of his day (he divided by n rather than n − 1, giving an s.d. of 1.17), concluded that "the odds are about 666 to one that 2 is the better soporific", which corresponds to a one-sided p of about 0.0015, consistent with the modern one-sided p of 0.0014. He also put his finger on why the result is so strong: the low spread of the differences "is probably due to the different drugs reacting similarly on the same patient, so that there is correlation between the results". That correlation is exactly what pairing exploits.

6. The same data, ignored pairing: the two-sample t-test

Suppose instead you (wrongly) treated the two columns as two independent groups of ten patients. The two-sample t-test compares the means: t = (x̄₂ − x̄₁)/SE, with SE = √(s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂) because variances of independent estimates add (math3). The sample standard deviations are 1.79 and 2.00, so SE = √(1.79²/10 + 2.00²/10) ≈ 0.849, and t = (2.33 − 0.75)/0.849 ≈ 1.86. Welch's t-test, which does not assume the two groups have equal variances and is the recommended default, uses about 17.8 degrees of freedom here, giving p ≈ 0.079 and a 95% interval of −0.21 to +3.37 hours. Not significant; zero is inside the interval.

Same numbers, opposite conclusions. The difference is the standard error: 0.389 when paired, 0.849 unpaired. Patients differ from each other a lot (patient 7 sleeps 3.7 extra hours on the weaker drug; patient 2 loses 1.6), and in the unpaired analysis that patient-to-patient variation is counted as noise. In the paired analysis each patient is their own control and that variation cancels. Formally, Var(X₂ − X₁) = Var(X₁) + Var(X₂) − 2Cov(X₁, X₂) (math3): the positive covariance between a patient's two responses shrinks the variance of the difference.

This is directly relevant to ML. Comparing two models on the same test examples is a paired design: some examples are hard for both, some easy for both, and that shared difficulty is like the patient effect. Section 7 shows how big the gain is.

7. Comparing two proportions, and two classifiers

Back to the opening: model A scores 870/1,000 and model B 900/1,000. Treating them as independent samples, the two-proportion z-test uses the pooled rate under H₀, p = 1,770/2,000 = 0.885, standard error √(0.885 × 0.115 × (1/1000 + 1/1000)) ≈ 0.0143, and z = 0.03/0.0143 ≈ 2.10, p ≈ 0.036. The 95% confidence interval (CI) for the difference (using each model's own rate in the standard error, SE) is about +0.2 to +5.8 points. Just significant, and the gain might be anywhere from negligible to almost 6 points.

But both models were evaluated on the same 1,000 questions, so the right analysis is paired. Most questions are answered correctly by both or by neither; only the disagreements carry information about which model is better. McNemar's test uses exactly those. Suppose A is right and B wrong on 40 questions, and B right and A wrong on 70 (consistent with the totals: 900 − 870 = 70 − 40). Under H₀ each disagreement is equally likely to go either way, so the count favouring B is Binomial(110, 0.5), and z = (70 − 40)/√(70 + 40) ≈ 2.86, p ≈ 0.004. Same accuracies, ten times stronger evidence, because the shared difficulty of the questions has been removed. For metrics that are not simple proportions (F1, the harmonic mean of precision and recall; BLEU, a translation-overlap score; a judge score) the paired bootstrap does the same job: resample test examples with replacement, recompute both models' scores on each resample, and look at the distribution of the difference.

8. Chi-squared tests for counts

Many questions are about counts in categories: does a distribution fit, are two categorical variables related? The chi-squared statistic compares observed counts O with the counts E expected under H₀:

χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E

Each term is a squared difference scaled by its expected size, so a gap of 10 counts matters more in a cell expecting 20 than in one expecting 2,000. Under H₀, with expected counts not too small (a common rule: at least about 5 per cell), χ² follows the chi-squared distribution, whose degrees of freedom are the number of cells minus 1, minus one for each parameter estimated from the data. Its 5% critical value is 3.84 for 1 degree of freedom, 9.49 for 4.

Goodness of fit: Clarke's flying bombs (math2). Observed squares with 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5+ hits: 229, 211, 93, 35, 7, 1. Expected under Poisson(0.932): 226.74, 211.39, 98.54, 30.62, 7.14, 1.57. The terms are (229 − 226.74)²/226.74 ≈ 0.023, then 0.001, 0.311, 0.627, 0.003 and 0.207, total χ² ≈ 1.17. Degrees of freedom: 6 cells − 1 − 1 (λ was estimated) = 4. The p-value is about 0.88, Clarke's reported value: the observed counts are entirely typical of random scatter. (The last cell's expected count is small, so careful analyses merge cells; doing so here changes nothing.)

Independence: the spam table (math1). Of 1,000 emails, 300 spam; 155 contain "free"; 120 are both. If the word and the label were independent, the expected count in each cell would be (row total × column total)/1,000: 300 × 155/1,000 = 46.5 spam emails with "free", and 253.5, 108.5 and 591.5 in the other cells. The observed 120 versus 46.5 alone contributes (73.5)²/46.5 ≈ 116; the total χ² is about 196 on (2 − 1)(2 − 1) = 1 degree of freedom, an astronomically small p-value. The word is a real signal.

9. Power, and how to plan a test

Power is the probability that the test rejects H₀ when a real effect of a given size exists. A worked case: you will compare request latency between the current and a new serving stack, each with n requests (treat them as independent users), sd σ = 50 ms, and you care about an improvement of δ = 10 ms. The standard error of the difference in means is σ√(2/n). With n = 100, that is 50 × 0.1414 ≈ 7.07 ms, so a real 10 ms improvement sits at z ≈ 10/7.07 ≈ 1.41 on average. The test rejects when the observed z exceeds 1.96, which happens with probability P(Z > 1.96 − 1.41) = P(Z > 0.55) ≈ 0.29. Power is 29%: you would miss a genuine 10 ms gain about 7 times in 10.

To find the sample size for a target power, require the average z under H₁ to sit far enough beyond the cut-off: δ/(σ√(2/n)) = z_{α/2} + z_β, where z_{α/2} = 1.96 for α = 0.05 two-sided and z_β = 0.84 for 80% power. Solving:

n per group = 2 (z_{α/2} + z_β)² σ² / δ²

With the numbers: 2 × (1.96 + 0.84)² × 2,500/100 ≈ 392.4, so 393 per group. The lab confirms by simulation: power 0.289 at n = 100 and 0.796 at n = 393. The formula shows the four levers. Power rises with the effect size δ (quadratically: halving δ needs four times the data), with sample size, with a larger α (more false positives) and with lower noise σ. Noise reduction is often the cheapest lever: pairing, as section 6 showed, or adjusting for a pre-experiment covariate (CUPED, Controlled-experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data, in math7). The smallest δ you can detect with given n and power is called the minimum detectable effect; work it out before running the test, and if it is larger than any effect you could realistically produce, the test is not worth running at that size.

10. Permutation tests: p-values by reshuffling

The t-test relies on approximate normality. A permutation test replaces that assumption with a simpler one: under H₀, the labels are exchangeable. For the paired sleep data, if the drugs were equivalent, each patient's difference would be equally likely to be positive or negative. So enumerate all 2¹⁰ = 1,024 ways of flipping the signs of the ten differences, compute the mean difference for each, and ask what fraction is at least as extreme as the observed 1.58. Only the all-positive pattern and its mirror image reach it, each counted twice because patient 5's difference is zero, so 4 of 1,024: p ≈ 0.0039, close to the t-test's 0.0028. For two independent groups, shuffle the group labels many times instead. Permutation tests work for any statistic (a median, an AUC, a ratio) and are a strong default when sample sizes are small or the data are skewed. Their one requirement is that you permute the units that are actually exchangeable: whole users, not individual events, if events from one user are correlated (math4).

12. What a p-value does not tell you

In March 2016 the American Statistical Association published a statement with six principles, because misuse of p-values had become a recognised contributor to research that does not replicate:

  1. P-values can indicate how incompatible the data are with a specified statistical model.
  2. P-values do not measure the probability that the studied hypothesis is true, or the probability that the data were produced by random chance alone.
  3. Scientific conclusions and business or policy decisions should not be based only on whether a p-value passes a specific threshold.
  4. Proper inference requires full reporting and transparency.
  5. A p-value, or statistical significance, does not measure the size of an effect or the importance of a result.
  6. By itself, a p-value does not provide a good measure of evidence regarding a model or hypothesis.

Principle 2 is math1's base-rate lesson. Suppose 10% of the ideas a team tests have real effects, every test has 80% power, and α = 0.05. Of 1,000 ideas, 100 are real and 80 come out significant; 900 are null and 45 come out significant by chance. So 45 of the 125 significant results, 36%, are false, even though every test "controlled the false positive rate at 5%". The 5% is P(significant | null), not P(null | significant). Principle 5 is about scale: with a million requests per arm and sd 50 ms, a 0.2 ms difference gives z ≈ 2.8 and p ≈ 0.005, a real and worthless effect. Principle 4 is about selective reporting, taken up in math7: if you test twenty metrics and report the one with p < 0.05, the p-value no longer means what it says. The practical upshot is a habit: report the estimate, its confidence interval, the sample size and every comparison you ran, and make decisions against a pre-stated smallest effect that matters.

Fisher versus Neyman–Pearson, and why the hybrid confuses people

Two schools built modern testing. Ronald Fisher treated the p-value as a continuous measure of evidence against the null, to be weighed with other knowledge, with 0.05 a rough guide. Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson treated testing as a decision procedure: fix α and the power against a specific alternative before the experiment, then reject or not, and do not interpret the p-value beyond that decision. What is taught today is a hybrid: Neyman–Pearson's fixed α and power calculations, with Fisher's exact p-values reported and interpreted as evidence. Much confusion (is p = 0.049 strong evidence? is p = 0.06 "trending towards significance"?) comes from mixing the two. For engineering decisions the Neyman–Pearson framing usually fits better: decide α, the minimum effect and the power in advance, collect the planned sample, and act on the pre-registered rule, while reporting the interval for context.

The garden of forking paths

A p-value assumes the analysis was fixed before seeing the data. In practice analysts choose among outlier rules, metric definitions, segments, time windows and tests after looking, and each choice nudges the result. Even without any intent to cheat, picking the analysis that "looks right" after seeing the data inflates false positives; deliberate trying-until-significant is called p-hacking. Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn showed in 2011 that combining a few common flexibilities (two dependent variables, optional extra data, a covariate, dropping a condition) could push the false-positive rate from 5% to over 60%. The defences are pre-registration (write down the analysis before the data arrive), holdout confirmation, and correcting for every comparison actually considered (math7). Experimentation platforms enforce some of this by fixing the primary metric and analysis at experiment creation.

Underpowered tests exaggerate

When power is low, only estimates that happen to be far from zero pass the threshold. If the true effect is 1% and the standard error is 1%, a result is significant only if the estimate exceeds 1.96%, so significant estimates average well over twice the truth. Gelman and Carlin called this a Type M (magnitude) error, and with very low power significant estimates can even have the wrong sign (Type S). It is the winner's curse (math4) imposed by the significance filter. For ML teams it explains a familiar pattern: an A/B test of a model change shows +3% engagement, the launch forecast assumes +3%, and the measured long-run effect is +1%. Discount marginal results, run confirmation tests, and prefer designs with high power.

Non-inferiority and equivalence

"The cheaper model is not significantly worse" is not evidence that it is not worse; with a small test it is guaranteed. To show non-inferiority, define a margin Δ you can tolerate (say 1 point of accuracy) and test H₀: new − old ≤ −Δ against H₁: new − old > −Δ. Rejecting means the data rule out a loss as large as the margin; equivalently, the lower bound of the (one-sided 95%, or two-sided 90%) confidence interval for the difference lies above −Δ. Equivalence in both directions uses two one-sided tests (TOST). These framings are the honest way to approve model distillation, quantisation (lm12) and infrastructure migrations, where the goal is "no meaningful regression".

Non-parametric tests and robustness

The t-test compares means and is sensitive to outliers and heavy tails. The Mann–Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank-sum) for two groups and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired data replace values by their ranks, so one enormous latency cannot dominate; they test whether one group tends to produce larger values than the other. They answer a slightly different question from the t-test (about ranks, roughly medians, rather than means), which matters when the business cares about totals: a change that makes most requests a little faster and a few much slower can win on ranks and lose on mean. Choose the test that matches the quantity you care about, and use the bootstrap when you need a mean-based interval for heavy-tailed data.

Effect sizes

Because significance depends on sample size, comparisons across studies use effect sizes: the difference in natural units (hours of sleep, milliseconds, percentage points), or standardised by a standard deviation. Cohen's d is the mean difference divided by the standard deviation; for the paired sleep data, 1.58/1.23 ≈ 1.3, a very large effect by the conventional reading (0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large). Standardised effects are useful for power planning when you have no domain sense of the scale, but in engineering work the natural units are almost always better: "12 ms faster at p99" or "+1.4 points of accuracy" is what a decision needs. Report the effect, its interval and the sample size; the p-value adds little once those three are on the page.

Bayesian alternatives

A Bayesian analysis answers the question people usually want answered: given the data and a prior, what is the probability that B is better, and by how much? With a Beta prior on each conversion rate (math5), the posterior for each is Beta, and P(B > A) can be computed by sampling from both posteriors. A Bayes factor compares how well H₀ and H₁ predict the data, the likelihood ratio from math1 averaged over each hypothesis's parameters. These outputs are easier to explain, but they depend on the prior, and they do not remove the need for adequate sample sizes or protect against peeking unless designed to. Many experimentation platforms report both a frequentist interval and a Bayesian "probability to beat control"; when the prior is weak they usually agree.

Model comparisons on evaluation sets

The most frequent misuse of testing in ML work is reading differences on evaluation sets without any test. Two habits fix most of it. Always report a confidence interval on a metric: for an accuracy p on n examples, ±1.96√(p(1 − p)/n), and for anything else a bootstrap over examples. And compare models on the same examples with a paired method, McNemar's test for accuracy or a paired bootstrap for other metrics, which is often several times more sensitive than comparing two intervals. For large language model (LLM) evaluations with an LLM judge, add the judge's own noise: re-judging the same outputs gives different scores, and that variance belongs in the interval too (lm14).

Benchmarks and test-set reuse

A p-value on a test set is valid only for a comparison planned before the test set was used. Teams that iterate on a model while checking the same test set after every change are effectively running hundreds of comparisons and selecting the winner, so the final test score is biased upward and the significance claim is meaningless (the forking paths in the deep section). Keep a validation set for iteration and a test set touched rarely, rotate fresh test data in, and when publishing or reporting upward, state how many variants were compared.

Latency and heavy-tailed metrics

t-tests on mean latency are dominated by the tail and by single slow requests. Comparing latency distributions between two builds is better done with a test on the quantity you care about: a bootstrap interval on the difference in p99, a rank test if you care about typical requests, or a test on the fraction of requests over the service-level objective (SLO) threshold (a proportion, with a simple z-test). Requests from the same client or the same host are correlated, so bootstrap whole clients or hosts, not individual requests.

"No significant regression" in release gates

Release pipelines often gate on "no statistically significant regression" in quality or latency. With small canary samples that gate passes almost everything, including real regressions, because power is low. A sounder gate is non-inferiority: the release passes if the confidence interval for (new − old) excludes a regression larger than an agreed margin. That forces the canary to be big enough to be informative, and it makes the tolerated risk explicit.

A/A tests as a health check

If you run a test between two identical arms many times, about 5% should come out significant at α = 0.05, and the p-values should be spread evenly between 0 and 1. A much higher rate means the variance estimates are wrong, usually because the analysis treats correlated events as independent or the metric pipeline double-counts. Experimentation platforms run continuous A/A tests for exactly this reason (in10); for any home-grown analysis, simulate an A/A test on historical data before trusting it.

A reporting template

Teams that report results in a fixed shape argue less and ship fewer false wins. A template that works for model comparisons and experiments: the question and the primary metric, stated before the data; the design (paired or not, the unit of analysis, the sample size and why, the minimum effect of interest); the estimate with a 95% interval in natural units; the p-value from the pre-specified test; the number of other metrics or variants examined, with their results; and the decision with its rationale against the pre-stated threshold. The template makes multiple comparisons visible, separates statistical from practical significance, and records the power so that a null result can be read correctly as "too small to tell" or "ruled out effects above X".

The public cases, in production terms

Student's paper is the template for small-sample comparison: pair what can be paired, use the t-distribution when the spread is estimated from little data, and report the effect with its uncertainty. The ASA statement is the template for communicating results: an effect estimate with an interval, the sample size, the number of comparisons, and a decision rule set in advance, rather than a bare "p < 0.05". Teams that adopt both habits ship fewer false wins and argue less about borderline results.

NumPy only (gpt_env, which has no SciPy, so the t and chi-squared tail areas are computed by numerical integration); save as math6.py.

import itertools
import numpy as np
from math import lgamma, pi, sqrt

def t_tail(t, df):                       # P(T > t) for Student's t, by numerical integration
    x = np.linspace(abs(t), abs(t) + 200, 400_001)
    c = np.exp(lgamma((df + 1) / 2) - lgamma(df / 2)) / sqrt(df * pi)
    return np.trapezoid(c * (1 + x * x / df) ** (-(df + 1) / 2), x)

def chi2_tail(q, k):                     # P(X > q) for chi-squared with k degrees of freedom
    x = np.linspace(q, q + 200, 400_001)
    return np.trapezoid(np.exp((k / 2 - 1) * np.log(x) - x / 2 - lgamma(k / 2) - k / 2 * np.log(2)), x)

# 1. Student's sleep data (Cushny & Peebles 1904): extra hours of sleep, same 10 patients
drug1 = np.array([0.7, -1.6, -0.2, -1.2, -0.1, 3.4, 3.7, 0.8, 0.0, 2.0])
drug2 = np.array([1.9, 0.8, 1.1, 0.1, -0.1, 4.4, 5.5, 1.6, 4.6, 3.4])
d = drug2 - drug1
t_paired = d.mean() / (d.std(ddof=1) / sqrt(10))
print(f"paired: mean diff {d.mean():.2f}, t = {t_paired:.2f}, p = {2 * t_tail(t_paired, 9):.4f}")

v1, v2 = drug1.var(ddof=1) / 10, drug2.var(ddof=1) / 10        # Welch: ignore the pairing
t_welch = (drug2.mean() - drug1.mean()) / sqrt(v1 + v2)
df = (v1 + v2) ** 2 / (v1 ** 2 / 9 + v2 ** 2 / 9)
print(f"unpaired (Welch): t = {t_welch:.2f}, df = {df:.1f}, p = {2 * t_tail(t_welch, df):.4f}")

# 2. Exact sign-flip permutation test: under 'no difference' each sign is a coin flip
flips = np.array(list(itertools.product([1, -1], repeat=10)))
as_extreme = np.abs((flips * d).mean(axis=1)) >= d.mean() - 1e-12
print(f"sign-flip permutation p = {as_extreme.mean():.4f}  ({as_extreme.sum()} of 1024)")

# 3. Small samples: using z = 1.96 instead of the t critical value inflates false positives
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.normal(0, 1, size=(200_000, 5))                         # null is true: mean 0
t = x.mean(1) / (x.std(1, ddof=1) / sqrt(5))
print(f"n=5, null true: reject with 1.96 -> {(np.abs(t) > 1.96).mean():.3f}, "
      f"with t(4)=2.776 -> {(np.abs(t) > 2.776).mean():.3f}")

# 4. Power: latency drop of 10 ms, sd 50 ms, two groups of n, z-test at alpha = 0.05
for n in (100, 393):
    a = rng.normal(200, 50, (20_000, n)); b = rng.normal(190, 50, (20_000, n))
    z = (a.mean(1) - b.mean(1)) / sqrt(2 * 50**2 / n)
    print(f"n={n} per group: power = {(np.abs(z) > 1.96).mean():.3f}")

# 5. Chi-squared goodness of fit: Clarke's V-1 counts against Poisson(0.932)
obs = np.array([229, 211, 93, 35, 7, 1]); exp_ = np.array([226.74, 211.39, 98.54, 30.62, 7.14, 1.57])
q = ((obs - exp_) ** 2 / exp_).sum()
print(f"Clarke: chi-squared = {q:.2f} on 4 df, p = {chi2_tail(q, 4):.2f}")

  1. Paired test on the sleep data: mean difference 1.58, t = 4.06, p = 0.0028.
  2. Welch's unpaired test on the same numbers: t = 1.86 on 17.8 degrees of freedom, p = 0.0794. Pairing turns an inconclusive result into a strong one.
  3. The exact sign-flip permutation test: 4 of the 1,024 sign patterns are as extreme, p = 0.0039, close to the t-test without assuming normality.
  4. With n = 5 and no real effect, comparing t with 1.96 rejects 12.2% of the time; with the correct t(4) critical value 2.776 it rejects 5.0%.
  5. Power for a 10 ms improvement with sd 50 ms: 0.289 with 100 per group and 0.796 with 393 per group, matching 29% and 80% from the formula.
  6. Clarke's V-1 counts: χ² = 1.17 on 4 degrees of freedom, p = 0.88, his published value.

Diagnose: the "significant" prompt improvement

An engineer reports: "New prompt improves answer quality. Old prompt 71/100 judged correct, new prompt 79/100, two-proportion z-test p = 0.19, so not significant — but I also checked faithfulness, conciseness, tone, citation accuracy and refusal rate, and citation accuracy improved with p = 0.03. Shipping." The two prompts were run on the same 100 questions. What is wrong with the analysis, and what would you do instead?

Answer

Multiple comparisons. Six metrics were tested and the one with p < 0.05 was picked. If none of the six truly changed and they were independent, the chance at least one comes out below 0.05 is 1 − 0.95⁶ ≈ 26%. The 0.03 cannot be taken at face value. Math7 covers corrections; with Bonferroni the threshold for six metrics would be 0.05/6 ≈ 0.0083, which 0.03 does not meet. Better: name one primary metric before running, and treat the others as secondary or guardrails.

Wrong test for the design. The prompts ran on the same 100 questions, so the comparison should be paired. A two-proportion z-test treats them as independent samples and wastes the information. With McNemar's test, what matters is the disagreements: if, say, the new prompt fixed 12 questions the old one missed and broke 4 it had right (net +8), then z = (12 − 4)/√16 = 2.0, p ≈ 0.046, borderline, compared with 0.19 unpaired. The paired analysis might rescue the quality claim, or not; it depends on the disagreement counts, which the report omits.

Too small to be informative. At about 75% quality, 100 questions give a standard error near 4.3 points per prompt. Even a correct paired test can only detect large effects. Use a power calculation to size the evaluation for the smallest improvement worth shipping, report the estimated difference with a confidence interval, and confirm on a fresh question set before claiming the win, since the question set was used to develop the new prompt.
