Every modern ML system is, underneath, a long sequence of operations on lists of numbers. A sentence goes into a language model as a list of token IDs, each turned into a list of 4,096 numbers (an embedding). Every layer multiplies those lists by tables of learned weights. A search system turns a query into a list of 768 numbers and finds the stored documents whose lists point in the most similar direction. A recommendation model represents each user and each product as a list and scores a pair by multiplying the lists together element by element and adding up. Linear algebra is the language for all of it: vectors are the lists, matrices are the tables, and a handful of operations (dot products, norms, matrix products and projections) do almost all the work.

You can use libraries without the vocabulary, until something goes wrong. A retrieval system keeps returning the same few documents for every query, because its scores depend on the length of the vectors and not just their direction. A training run produces NaNs because a dot product over 4,096 dimensions overflowed in 16-bit floating point. A regression fit gives wild coefficients because two features are nearly the same column. A shape error appears deep in a model because a matrix was multiplied from the wrong side. Each of these is diagnosed in one sentence by someone fluent in this lesson, and is a day of confusion for someone who is not.

Linear algebra is also where some of the most famous results in representation learning live. In 2013 Tomas Mikolov and colleagues at Google showed that word vectors learned by their word2vec model supported arithmetic: the vector for "king", minus "man", plus "woman", landed closest to "queen". It became the emblem of embeddings, and the reason "vector arithmetic on meaning" entered the vocabulary of ML. It also turned out, on closer inspection, to depend on a small implementation detail in how the nearest word was chosen, which is a lesson about evaluation as much as about vectors.

This lesson starts from scratch: vectors as lists and as arrows, adding and scaling them, the dot product and what it measures, three ways to measure length (norms), cosine similarity, and projecting one vector onto another, worked by hand. Then matrices: as tables, as functions that transform vectors, how matrix–vector and matrix–matrix products work and what they cost, transposes, inverses and solving linear systems. It ends with least squares seen as a projection, worked on math3's 5-row table, and the word2vec story. Math9 continues with eigenvalues, the SVD and rank.

A vector is an ordered list of numbers, such as (3, 4) or (0.2, −1.5, 0.7, 2.0). That is the whole definition, and it is the one computers use. The power comes from a second way of seeing the same thing: as an arrow from the origin to the point with those coordinates. The list (3, 4) is the arrow that goes 3 units right and 4 units up. Both pictures are always available, and switching between them is most of the skill. The list view tells you how to compute; the arrow view tells you what the computation means.

In ML, the list view is how data arrive. A house is (bedrooms, square metres, age) = (3, 120, 15). A user is a list of 64 learned numbers. A word is a list of 300. A model's weights are a list of millions. The arrow view is how you reason about them. Two users whose arrows point in nearly the same direction have similar tastes. A weight vector's direction says which features matter; its length says how strongly. Moving the weights a small step in some direction is how training works.

A good analogy for directions in many dimensions is a recipe. Think of each dimension as an ingredient and a vector as a recipe's amounts: (flour, sugar, butter, eggs). Two cakes can be different sizes (one recipe is double the other) and still be "the same cake": same direction, different length. Cosine similarity, which compares directions while ignoring length, asks whether two recipes are the same cake; distance asks whether you would end up with the same amount of cake. The analogy breaks when ingredients can be negative, which embedding dimensions can; but the core idea, that direction captures "what kind" and length captures "how much", carries over.

Four operations do almost everything:

  • The dot product multiplies matching components and adds them up: (3, 4)·(2, 1) = 6 + 4 = 10. It measures how much two vectors point the same way, scaled by their lengths.
  • The norm is a vector's length: |(3, 4)| = √(9 + 16) = 5.
  • Projection finds the shadow of one vector on another: the part of a that points along b.
  • Matrix multiplication applies a table of numbers to a vector to produce a new vector, which is what every neural-network layer does.

The figure shows the projection worked by hand. Its key property, that what is left over (the residual) is perpendicular to the direction you projected onto, is the single idea behind least squares, PCA and many other methods. A reader who stops here should hold this picture: vectors are lists you can draw as arrows; the dot product measures alignment; norms measure size; projection splits a vector into "the part along this direction" plus "a perpendicular remainder"; and matrices are machines that turn vectors into other vectors.

1. Vectors, and the two basic operations

A vector with n components lives in n-dimensional space, written ℝⁿ (the set of all lists of n real numbers). We write vectors in bold or as lowercase letters (a, b, x) and their components with subscripts: a = (a₁, a₂, …, aₙ). A single number is called a scalar, to distinguish it from a vector.

Two operations are defined component by component. Addition: (3, 4) + (2, 1) = (5, 5). As arrows, place the second arrow's tail at the first arrow's head; the sum goes from the origin to the final head. Scalar multiplication: 2·(3, 4) = (6, 8), the same direction, twice as long; −1·(3, 4) = (−3, −4), the opposite direction. Subtraction combines them: a − b = a + (−1)b, the arrow from b's head to a's head. That last fact is worth remembering: the difference of two vectors is the arrow from one to the other, which is why "woman − man" can be read as a direction, the displacement that takes you from one word to the other.

A linear combination is a sum of scaled vectors, such as 2u − 3v. The set of everything you can reach by linear combinations of some vectors is their span. In 2-D, the vectors (1, 0) and (0, 1) span the whole plane: (3, 4) = 3·(1, 0) + 4·(0, 1). A set of vectors from which every vector in the space can be built in exactly one way is a basis; (1, 0) and (0, 1) are the standard basis, and the components of a vector are just its coefficients in that basis. Math9 shows that choosing a different basis (the eigenvectors, or the directions found by PCA) can make a problem much simpler.

2. The dot product

The dot product (or inner product) of two vectors of the same length is the sum of the products of matching components:

a·b = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ + … + aₙbₙ = Σ aᵢbᵢ

(3, 4)·(4, 3) = 12 + 12 = 24. (3, 4)·(4, −3) = 12 − 12 = 0. (1, 2, 3)·(4, 5, 6) = 4 + 10 + 18 = 32. The result is a scalar. You have already used dot products without the name: a weighted sum, such as a linear model's prediction w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + … + b, is w·x + b; a portfolio's value is prices·quantities; math3's covariance is a dot product of two deviation vectors divided by n − 1.

The geometric meaning is the surprising part. For any two vectors,

a·b = |a| |b| cos θ

where |a| and |b| are the lengths (next section) and θ (theta) is the angle between the arrows. The cosine is 1 when the arrows point the same way, 0 when they are perpendicular, and −1 when they point opposite ways. So the dot product measures alignment, scaled by both lengths. (The equality follows from the law of cosines in the triangle formed by a, b and a − b; it is the same school geometry, written with vectors.) Two vectors with a dot product of zero are called orthogonal, the general-dimension word for perpendicular. (3, 4) and (4, −3) are orthogonal.

The dot product is also the most-executed operation in machine learning. Each output of a linear layer is a dot product of a weight row with the input. An attention score in a transformer is a dot product of a query vector with a key vector (lm2). A recommendation score is a dot product of a user vector with an item vector. Retrieval ranks documents by their dot products or cosines with a query. When people talk about accelerators' "FLOPs", they are mostly counting the multiplies and adds inside dot products.

3. Norms: three ways to measure length

A norm assigns each vector a non-negative length. The familiar one is the L2 norm or Euclidean norm, straight-line length by Pythagoras:

|a|₂ = √(a₁² + a₂² + … + aₙ²) = √(a·a)

For (3, 4): √25 = 5. Two others appear constantly. The L1 norm, |a|₁ = Σ|aᵢ|, is the distance a taxi would drive on a grid: |3| + |4| = 7. The L∞ norm (max norm), |a|∞ = max |aᵢ|, is the largest single component: 4. The three always satisfy |a|∞ ≤ |a|₂ ≤ |a|₁.

Each has its uses. L2 is the default for distances and lengths, and its square is what squared-error losses and L2 regularisation (weight decay) penalise. L1 is what lasso regularisation penalises; because its "unit ball" (the set of vectors with norm 1) is a diamond with corners on the axes, the optimum often lands on a corner where some weights are exactly zero (ml3 draws this). L∞ bounds the worst component, which is the natural norm for adversarial robustness ("no pixel changes by more than 8/255") and for checking that a computation stays within a tolerance.

A vector of length 1 is a unit vector. Dividing any non-zero vector by its L2 norm gives the unit vector in the same direction: (3, 4)/5 = (0.6, 0.8). This is called normalising, and it is how you strip length away and keep only direction. The distance between two points is the norm of their difference: the L2 distance between (3, 4) and (4, 3) is |(−1, 1)| = √2 ≈ 1.41.

Norms also appear in training mechanics. Gradient clipping rescales the gradient vector whenever its L2 norm exceeds a threshold, so one bad batch cannot throw the weights far (dl3). Weight norms are monitored to catch runaway growth. Embedding norms in a trained model often carry information, such as word frequency, which matters for the next section.

4. Cosine similarity

Rearranging a·b = |a||b|cos θ gives the cosine of the angle directly:

cos θ = a·b / (|a| |b|)

This is cosine similarity. For (3, 4) and (4, 3): 24/(5 × 5) = 0.96, a small angle (about 16°). For (3, 4) and (2, 1): 10/(5 × √5) ≈ 0.894. For (3, 4) and (4, −3): 0. Cosine similarity compares directions and ignores lengths, which is what you want when length reflects something irrelevant. Two documents about the same topic, one ten times longer, have word-count vectors pointing the same way but of very different lengths; cosine says they are the same topic. Embedding search systems usually normalise all vectors to length 1, after which the cosine is just the dot product.

For unit vectors, distance and cosine carry the same information: |a − b|² = |a|² + |b|² − 2a·b = 2 − 2cos θ. Ranking by smallest distance and ranking by largest cosine give the same order. That identity is why approximate nearest-neighbour indexes built for Euclidean distance can serve cosine search on normalised vectors (lm10).

5. Projection, worked by hand

Given a vector a and a direction b, how much of a lies along b? The projection of a onto the line through b is

proj_b(a) = (a·b / b·b) · b

The fraction a·b / b·b is a scalar saying how many copies of b make up the shadow. Worked with a = (3, 4) and b = (2, 1):

  1. a·b = 3×2 + 4×1 = 10.
  2. b·b = 2² + 1² = 5.
  3. Coefficient 10/5 = 2, so the projection is 2·(2, 1) = (4, 2).
  4. The residual, what is left over, is a − proj = (3, 4) − (4, 2) = (−1, 2).
  5. Check: residual·b = −1×2 + 2×1 = 0. The residual is perpendicular to b.
  6. Check by Pythagoras: |proj|² = 16 + 4 = 20, |residual|² = 1 + 4 = 5, and 20 + 5 = 25 = |a|².

Why the formula? The projection must be some multiple c·b, and the residual a − c·b must be perpendicular to b (if it were not, you could move along b and get closer to a). Setting (a − c·b)·b = 0 gives a·b − c(b·b) = 0, so c = a·b / b·b. That one line of reasoning, "the best approximation leaves a residual perpendicular to everything you could have used", is the whole idea of least squares, which section 10 scales up.

Projections are everywhere in ML. Removing a direction from embeddings (for example, a direction that encodes gender, in some debiasing methods) subtracts the projection onto it. Principal component analysis (PCA) projects data onto the few directions with the most variance (ml13). The component of a gradient along a constraint is a projection. And a linear model's prediction w·x is, up to a scale, the projection of x onto the direction w.

6. Matrices: tables that transform vectors

A matrix is a rectangular table of numbers with m rows and n columns, called an m × n matrix. The entry in row i and column j is written Aᵢⱼ. The first view is the data view: a dataset of 1,000 examples with 20 features is a 1,000 × 20 matrix, one row per example. This is called the design matrix X in regression.

The second view is the function view. An m × n matrix A turns an n-component vector x into an m-component vector Ax. The rule is: each output component is the dot product of one row of A with x.

[[2, 1], [−1, 3]] · (3, 4) = (2×3 + 1×4, −1×3 + 3×4) = (10, 9)

The same product has a second reading that is often more illuminating: Ax is a linear combination of A's columns, weighted by the entries of x. Here 3·(2, −1) + 4·(1, 3) = (6, −3) + (4, 12) = (10, 9). This column picture says that the outputs Ax can only ever be combinations of A's columns: the set of reachable outputs is the span of the columns, called the column space. That idea is central to least squares and to math9's rank.

A neural network's linear layer is exactly this: y = Wx + b, where W is a weight matrix with one row per output unit, x the input vector and b a bias vector. A layer mapping 768 inputs to 3,072 outputs has a 3,072 × 768 weight matrix, 2,359,296 weights, and computes 3,072 dot products of length 768 for each input.

7. Matrices as transformations

Matrices do not just compute; they move space around in a structured way. A function f is linear if f(au + bv) = a·f(u) + b·f(v) for all vectors u, v and scalars a, b: it respects addition and scaling. Every matrix defines a linear function, and every linear function between spaces of fixed dimension is a matrix. The practical consequence is that a matrix is completely determined by what it does to the basis vectors: in 2-D, the first column of A is where (1, 0) lands, and the second column is where (0, 1) lands.

Reading the columns tells you what the matrix does. [[2, 0], [0, 1]] sends (1, 0) to (2, 0) and leaves (0, 1) alone: a horizontal stretch by 2. [[0, −1], [1, 0]] sends (1, 0) to (0, 1) and (0, 1) to (−1, 0): a 90° anticlockwise rotation. [[1, 1], [0, 1]] keeps (1, 0) and sends (0, 1) to (1, 1): a shear that slants squares into parallelograms. A diagonal matrix stretches each axis independently. The matrix (1/5)·[[4, 2], [2, 1]] projects every vector onto the line through (2, 1): it sends (3, 4) to (4, 2), the projection from section 5. Projection matrices squash space onto a line or plane, and they cannot be undone, which section 9 makes precise.

8. Matrix multiplication: composing transformations

If B transforms x and then A transforms the result, the combined effect A(Bx) is itself a linear transformation, so it is a matrix, called the product AB. To compute it: entry (i, j) of AB is the dot product of row i of A with column j of B. Equivalently, column j of AB is A times column j of B.

[[1, 2], [0, 1]] · [[0, −1], [1, 0]] = [[1×0 + 2×1, 1×(−1) + 2×0], [0×0 + 1×1, 0×(−1) + 1×0]] = [[2, −1], [1, 0]]

Four rules follow, and each prevents a class of bugs.

  • Shapes: an m × n matrix times an n × p matrix gives m × p. The inner dimensions must match and disappear. Most "shapes do not align" errors are violations of this rule, usually a missing transpose.
  • Order matters: AB ≠ BA in general. In the example, BA = [[0, −1], [1, 2]], different from AB. As transformations, AB means "B first, then A", and shearing then rotating differs from rotating then shearing.
  • Grouping does not matter: (AB)C = A(BC). This associativity is why a stack of linear layers without non-linearities collapses into a single matrix, and why the order of evaluation can be chosen for speed: for a matrix–matrix–vector product, computing A(Bx) costs two matrix–vector products, far cheaper than forming AB first.
  • Cost: multiplying m × n by n × p takes m·n·p multiplications and about as many additions, roughly 2mnp floating-point operations (FLOPs). A batch of 32 inputs through the 768 → 3,072 layer is a (32 × 768)·(768 × 3,072) product, about 151 million FLOPs. Training-cost estimates for large models (lm7, gpu1) are sums of exactly these counts.

Batching is matrix multiplication's quiet gift to deep learning. Stacking 32 input vectors as the rows of a 32 × 768 matrix X lets one product XWᵀ compute all 32 outputs at once, which hardware does far more efficiently than 32 separate matrix–vector products because each weight loaded from memory is reused 32 times (gpu1 covers why that matters).

9. Transpose, identity, inverse and solving systems

The transpose Aᵀ swaps rows and columns: the transpose of a 2 × 3 matrix is 3 × 2, and (Aᵀ)ᵢⱼ = Aⱼᵢ. A dot product can be written as a matrix product, a·b = aᵀb (a row times a column). The rule for products reverses order: (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ. A matrix equal to its own transpose is symmetric; covariance matrices (math3) and XᵀX are symmetric, which math9 shows gives them especially nice eigenvectors.

The identity matrix I has ones on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere; Ix = x. The inverse A⁻¹ of a square matrix undoes it: A⁻¹A = AA⁻¹ = I. For a 2 × 2 matrix [[a, b], [c, d]] the inverse is (1/(ad − bc))·[[d, −b], [−c, a]], provided the determinant ad − bc is not zero. For [[2, 1], [−1, 3]]: determinant 6 + 1 = 7, inverse (1/7)·[[3, −1], [1, 2]]. Check: it sends (10, 9) back to (1/7)·(30 − 9, 10 + 18) = (3, 4).

When is there no inverse? When the transformation loses information, so different inputs give the same output and there is no way back. The projection matrix (1/5)·[[4, 2], [2, 1]] sends both (3, 4) and (4, 2) to (4, 2); its determinant is (4 − 4)/25 = 0. Geometrically, the determinant is the factor by which the matrix scales areas, and zero means the plane has been squashed onto a line. Math9 makes "loses information" precise with the idea of rank.

Inverses solve systems of linear equations. "Find x with Ax = b" has the solution x = A⁻¹b when A is invertible. In practice libraries never form the inverse; they solve the system directly by elimination (np.linalg.solve), which is faster and more accurate. A rule worth adopting: if you find yourself writing inv(A) @ b, write solve(A, b) instead.

10. Least squares as a projection

Now everything combines. Math3 fitted a straight line to five points: prompt lengths x = (2, 4, 6, 8, 10) and latencies y = (150, 190, 200, 260, 250). Write the model for all five points at once as y ≈ Xw, where the design matrix X has a column of ones (for the intercept) and a column of x values:

X = [[1, 2], [1, 4], [1, 6], [1, 8], [1, 10]], w = (w₀, w₁)

There are five equations and two unknowns, so no w fits exactly: y is not in the column space of X (the set of all vectors Xw, a 2-dimensional plane inside 5-dimensional space). Least squares picks the w whose prediction Xw is closest to y, minimising the squared length of the residual |y − Xw|². By section 5's reasoning, the closest point is the projection of y onto the column space, and the residual must be perpendicular to every column of X:

Xᵀ(y − Xw) = 0, i.e. XᵀX w = Xᵀy

These are the normal equations ("normal" means perpendicular). Worked: XᵀX = [[5, 30], [30, 220]] (the count, Σx, Σx and Σx²), and Xᵀy = (1,050, 6,840) (Σy and Σxy). The determinant of XᵀX is 5 × 220 − 30 × 30 = 200, so by the 2 × 2 inverse:

w₀ = (220 × 1,050 − 30 × 6,840)/200 = 25,800/200 = 129, w₁ = (5 × 6,840 − 30 × 1,050)/200 = 2,700/200 = 13.5

The same line as math3, y = 129 + 13.5x, now derived as a projection. The residuals are (−6, +7, −10, +23, −14). Perpendicular to the column of ones means they sum to zero; perpendicular to the x column means Σxᵢrᵢ = 0. Both check out.

This picture generalises completely. With d features, X is n × d, the column space is d-dimensional, and the normal equations are a d × d system. Linear regression, the output layer of many models, and the closed-form solution of ridge regression (XᵀX + λI)w = Xᵀy are all this projection. Math10 and math11 derive the same equations a second way, by setting the gradient of the squared error to zero, and mc1 implements them, including why libraries solve them with a QR or SVD factorisation (two ways of splitting a matrix into simpler pieces, in this lesson's deep section and in math9) instead of inverting XᵀX.

11. The public case: word2vec's vector arithmetic

In 2013 Tomas Mikolov, Kai Chen, Greg Corrado and Jeffrey Dean published word2vec, a fast way to learn a vector for every word from billions of words of text (their main experiments used a Google News corpus of about 6 billion tokens). Words used in similar contexts end up with similar vectors. Their most striking claim was about differences between vectors. Recall from section 1 that a − b is the arrow from b to a. If the arrow from "man" to "king" is roughly the same as the arrow from "woman" to "queen", then king − man + woman should land near queen.

They turned that into a test: a set of 8,869 semantic questions (such as capital cities and currencies: "Athens is to Greece as Oslo is to ?") and 10,675 syntactic ones ("big is to biggest as small is to ?"). For each question they computed the vector X = vector(b) − vector(a) + vector(c) and searched the vocabulary for the word whose vector had the highest cosine similarity with X, counting the question correct only if that word was exactly right. In the paper's words, they "discard the input question words during this search". Their best models answered a large share of these questions correctly, and "king − man + woman ≈ queen" became the emblem of embeddings.

The detail in parentheses turned out to matter. In 2019 Malvina Nissim, Rik van Noord and Rob van der Goot re-ran the analogy test with the input words allowed as answers, and accuracy dropped sharply: very often the nearest vector to king − man + woman is king itself (and sometimes the third word, as in "short is to shorter as new is to new"), because the offset woman − man is small and noisy compared with the word vectors. They also showed how the constraint manufactures striking bias examples: "man is to doctor as woman is to X" cannot return "doctor", so it returns the next-nearest word, such as "nurse", and the embedding is charged with a bias that the query design forced. The lab reproduces the mechanism on toy 50-dimensional vectors built from a shared "royal" direction, a "gender" direction and per-word noise: the nearest word to king − man + woman is woman, an input; only when the inputs are excluded does queen come first.

Both halves of the story are worth keeping. Relations really are roughly linear directions in embedding spaces, which is why projections, differences and cosines on embeddings work as well as they do, and why techniques that find or remove a direction (for debiasing, steering or probing a model) are possible. And a single-number evaluation can hide a choice that makes the method look cleaner than it is. Every claim about embeddings ("these two concepts are related", "this model is biased") deserves the question: what exactly was compared, and what was excluded?

Geometry in high dimensions

Two facts make high-dimensional spaces behave differently from the plane. First, random directions are nearly orthogonal. The cosine between two random vectors with independent standard normal components is a sum of d small independent products divided by the lengths, so it concentrates around zero with standard deviation about 1/√d: about 0.58 in 3 dimensions, 0.14 in 50, 0.036 in 768 (the lab measures these). A 768-dimensional space therefore has room for a very large number of directions that are nearly independent of each other, which is one explanation for how models pack many features into fewer dimensions than features, a phenomenon interpretability research calls superposition. Second, lengths concentrate: a random vector with d independent standard-normal components has length very close to √d, with relative spread shrinking like 1/√d. Together these mean that distances between random points are all nearly the same, which weakens nearest-neighbour methods on unstructured high-dimensional data (the curse of dimensionality, ml11), while structured data such as embeddings, which live near lower-dimensional surfaces, escape the worst of it.

Why attention divides by √d

If query and key vectors have d independent components each with variance 1, their dot product is a sum of d terms with variance 1 each, so it has variance d and standard deviation √d. For d = 128, raw attention scores would typically be around ±11, and a softmax over such scores puts nearly all weight on the single largest one, with gradients that almost vanish elsewhere. Dividing by √d brings the scores back to unit scale. That is the "scaled" in scaled dot-product attention (lm2, mc16), and it is a direct application of math3's rule that variances of independent terms add.

Conditioning and numerical stability

The condition number of a matrix measures how much it can amplify relative errors when you solve a system with it: the ratio of the largest to the smallest factor by which it stretches vectors (math9 computes it from singular values). A condition number of 10⁶ means that relative errors in the data can grow up to a million-fold in the solution. Forming XᵀX squares the condition number, which is why the normal equations, perfect on paper, are avoided in numerical code: solving through a QR factorisation (X = QR with Q having orthonormal columns and R upper-triangular) or the SVD keeps the original conditioning. Near-duplicate features (height in cm and in inches, or two almost identical embeddings) make X badly conditioned and coefficients unstable; standardising features and adding L2 regularisation both improve conditioning, the second by adding λ to every eigenvalue of XᵀX.

Orthonormal bases and Gram–Schmidt

A basis whose vectors are mutually orthogonal and of unit length is orthonormal. In such a basis the coefficient of a vector along each basis vector is just a dot product, with no system to solve, and lengths and angles are preserved. The Gram–Schmidt process builds an orthonormal basis from any basis by repeatedly subtracting projections onto the vectors already accepted and normalising what remains: exactly section 5's projection, applied in a loop. A matrix with orthonormal columns, Q, satisfies QᵀQ = I, so its inverse is its transpose; rotations and reflections are such matrices. QR factorisation is Gram–Schmidt organised as a matrix decomposition, and orthogonal weight initialisation in deep networks uses such matrices because they preserve the length of signals passing through.

Inner products beyond the dot product

The dot product is one example of an inner product, any operation that is symmetric, linear in each argument and positive for non-zero vectors. A weighted inner product, aᵀMb with M symmetric and positive definite, measures alignment after stretching some directions; the Mahalanobis distance uses M = Σ⁻¹ (the inverse covariance matrix from math3) so that distances are measured in units of the data's own spread. Kernel methods go further: a kernel function k(x, y) acts as an inner product in a (possibly infinite-dimensional) feature space, which lets linear methods such as support vector machines fit non-linear boundaries without computing the features explicitly.

Matrix multiplication cost and why it dominates

Multiplying two n × n matrices with the schoolbook method takes about 2n³ operations, while reading and writing them takes about 3n² memory accesses. The ratio, about 2n/3 operations per number moved, grows with size, which is why large matrix multiplications can run near a processor's peak arithmetic rate while vector operations are limited by memory speed (gpu covers this as arithmetic intensity). Algorithms with lower asymptotic cost exist (Strassen's method, about n^2.81) but are rarely used in ML because they are less numerically stable and less suited to hardware. In practice the speed of deep learning is the speed of large, well-shaped matrix multiplications, which is why model dimensions are chosen as multiples of 64 or 128.

Dot product versus cosine in retrieval

Embedding-based retrieval systems must decide whether to score with dot product or cosine, and the choice must match how the embedding model was trained. Many sentence-embedding models are trained with cosine-similarity objectives and their norms carry little meaning; scoring them by dot product lets incidental norm differences reorder results. Some retrieval models are trained with dot-product objectives and encode a useful signal in the norm. The characteristic failure is a few documents (often long, generic or popular ones) appearing in the top results for almost every query. The diagnosis is to plot document norms and check whether the frequent winners are norm outliers; the fix is to normalise or to use the scoring the model was trained for. Vector databases expose the choice as a configuration parameter, so it is worth checking explicitly.

Precision and overflow in long dot products

A dot product over 4,096 dimensions sums 4,096 products. In 16-bit floating point (float16), whose largest value is 65,504, a few large activations can overflow to infinity, and long sums lose precision as small terms are rounded away against a large running total. Training frameworks and graphics-processor (GPU) kernels therefore accumulate matrix products in 32-bit precision even when inputs are 16-bit, and models use normalisation layers (dl4) to keep activations in range. Symptoms in production are sudden NaN or infinite losses or logits, usually in attention scores or the final layer; checking the maximum absolute activation per layer is the fastest way to locate them.

Shape and orientation bugs

The most common linear-algebra bug in ML code is a silent orientation error rather than a crash: a weight matrix stored as (out, in) applied as if it were (in, out) to a square shape, a vector broadcast along the wrong axis, or a batch dimension summed away. Square shapes are dangerous because the shape check passes. Defences: name dimensions in comments or with typed tensors, assert shapes at module boundaries, and test layers on inputs whose answer you can compute by hand, such as a one-hot input that should select exactly one column of W.

Ill-conditioned features in regression

Linear and logistic regression on raw features with very different scales (counts in millions next to rates between 0 and 1) or near-duplicate columns produce large, unstable, sign-flipping coefficients and slow or failed optimisation. The coefficients are then over-interpreted ("this feature has a huge negative effect") when they are artefacts of collinearity. Standardising features, dropping or combining duplicates, and using L2 regularisation make the fit stable; checking the condition number of the standardised design matrix (math9) tells you whether coefficients can be trusted individually.

The public case, in production terms

The word2vec analogy story is an evaluation lesson for anyone shipping embeddings. Offline tests that look clean can depend on choices hidden in the evaluation code (excluded candidates, vocabulary cut-offs, normalisation), and conclusions about what a model "knows" or how it is "biased" can flip when those choices change. When you report retrieval or similarity metrics, record exactly how candidates were generated and filtered; when someone shows a striking nearest-neighbour example, rerun it with the obvious constraints removed before drawing conclusions.

Goal: compute dot products, norms and cosines, project one vector onto another and check the residual is perpendicular, solve the 5-row least-squares problem two ways, see that matrix multiplication is not commutative and how many operations a layer costs, measure near-orthogonality in high dimensions, and reproduce the analogy-search artefact on toy vectors. NumPy only (gpt_env); save as math8.py.

import numpy as np

# 1. Dot product, norms, cosine
a, b = np.array([3.0, 4.0]), np.array([4.0, 3.0])
print("a.b =", a @ b, " |a|_2 =", np.linalg.norm(a), " |a|_1 =", np.abs(a).sum(),
      " cos =", round(a @ b / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b)), 3))

# 2. Projection of a = (3, 4) onto b = (2, 1); the residual is orthogonal to b
b = np.array([2.0, 1.0])
proj = (a @ b) / (b @ b) * b
resid = a - proj
print("projection", proj, " residual", resid, " residual . b =", resid @ b)

# 3. Least squares as a projection: the 5-row table from math3
x = np.array([2, 4, 6, 8, 10.0]); y = np.array([150, 190, 200, 260, 250.0])
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(5), x])                  # design matrix: intercept, x
w_normal = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)           # normal equations
w_lstsq = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]
print("X^T X =", (X.T @ X).tolist(), " X^T y =", (X.T @ y).tolist())
print("weights (normal eq):", w_normal.round(3), " (lstsq):", w_lstsq.round(3))
r = y - X @ w_normal
print("residual . each column of X:", (X.T @ r).round(9))

# 4. Matrix multiplication: shapes, cost, and order matters
A = np.array([[1, 2], [0, 1]]); B = np.array([[0, -1], [1, 0]])
print("AB =", (A @ B).tolist(), " BA =", (B @ A).tolist())
batch, d_in, d_out = 32, 768, 3072
print(f"one linear layer on a batch: {2 * batch * d_in * d_out:,} floating-point operations")

# 5. In high dimensions, random directions are nearly orthogonal
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for d in (3, 50, 768):
    u = rng.normal(size=(2000, d)); v = rng.normal(size=(2000, d))
    cos = (u * v).sum(1) / (np.linalg.norm(u, axis=1) * np.linalg.norm(v, axis=1))
    print(f"d={d:4d}: spread (sd) of cos between random vectors = {cos.std():.3f}  (1/sqrt(d) = {1/np.sqrt(d):.3f})")

# 6. Analogy arithmetic on toy 50-d "embeddings": shared royal and gender directions plus noise
rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
unit = lambda v: v / np.linalg.norm(v)
royal, gender = unit(rng.normal(size=50)), 0.6 * unit(rng.normal(size=50))
noise = lambda: 0.8 * unit(rng.normal(size=50))
words = {"king": royal + gender + noise(), "queen": royal - gender + noise(),
         "man": gender + noise(), "woman": -gender + noise(),
         "prince": royal + gender + noise(), "apple": noise()}
V = {w: unit(v) for w, v in words.items()}
target = V["king"] - V["man"] + V["woman"]
ranked = sorted(V, key=lambda w: -(V[w] @ target))
print("nearest to king - man + woman:", ranked[:3])
print("excluding the three input words:", [w for w in ranked if w not in ("king", "man", "woman")][0])

What to look for:

  1. (3, 4)·(4, 3) = 24, L2 norm 5, L1 norm 7, cosine 0.96.
  2. The projection of (3, 4) onto (2, 1) is (4, 2); the residual (−1, 2) has dot product exactly 0 with (2, 1).
  3. XᵀX = [[5, 30], [30, 220]], Xᵀy = (1,050, 6,840), and both the normal equations and lstsq give weights 129 and 13.5; the residual's dot product with each column of X is 0.
  4. AB = [[2, −1], [1, 0]] while BA = [[0, −1], [1, 2]]; one 768 → 3,072 layer on a batch of 32 costs about 151 million operations.
  5. The spread of cosines between random vectors is about 0.58, 0.14 and 0.037 in 3, 50 and 768 dimensions, tracking 1/√d.
  6. On the toy embeddings, the nearest word to king − man + woman is woman (an input word); excluding the inputs gives queen.

Diagnose: the recommender that likes everything

A two-tower recommendation model produces a 64-number vector for each user and each item and scores a pair by their dot product. After a retrain, the same 20 items appear in the top 10 for 70% of users. Offline recall looked fine. (a) Give a linear-algebra explanation. (b) What would you measure to confirm it? (c) Propose two fixes and their trade-offs.

Answer

(a) A dot product is |u||v|cos θ. If some item vectors became much longer than the rest, they score highly for any user whose vector is even loosely aligned with them, so a handful of long items dominate every list. Dot-product training objectives often let popular items grow longer, because increasing an item's norm raises its score for many users at once, which helps the loss when that item is clicked by many. Offline recall can still look fine if those popular items are also frequently relevant in the test set.

(b) Plot the distribution of item-vector norms and mark the 20 dominant items; check whether they are norm outliers. Recompute the top-10 lists with cosine similarity (normalise both towers' outputs) and measure how much the concentration drops. Measure diversity metrics such as catalogue coverage and the share of recommendations going to the top 1% of items, before and after the retrain.

(c) Normalise item (and user) vectors and score by cosine, possibly with a learned temperature, which removes the norm effect but may lose a genuine popularity signal the norm was carrying; you can reintroduce popularity explicitly as a separate, controllable feature. Alternatively, penalise item norms during training (an L2 penalty on embeddings) or correct for popularity in the training objective, for example by down-weighting popular items as negatives so that norm growth is not rewarded; this keeps the dot-product model but needs retuning and re-evaluation on diversity as well as recall.
