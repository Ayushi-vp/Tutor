In October 2006 Netflix offered a million dollars to anyone who could predict how customers would rate films 10% more accurately than its own system. It released 100,480,507 ratings that 480,189 users had given to 17,770 films. Arrange them as a table with one row per user and one column per film and you get about 8.5 billion cells, of which about 1.2% are filled. The challenge was to predict the other 98.8%.

The idea that carried the competition was an assumption about the shape of that table. Tastes are not 17,770 independent things. A small number of hidden factors, such as how much a person likes comedy versus drama, mainstream versus art-house, or violence, explain most of why people rate films as they do, and each film can be described by how much it has of those same factors. If that is true, the giant table is approximately a product of two thin tables: one row of, say, 50 numbers per user, and one row of 50 numbers per film. In linear-algebra language, the ratings matrix is approximately low rank. Fitting those thin tables to the observed ratings, a method the competitors called "SVD" after the singular value decomposition, became the backbone of the leading entries. When Netflix examined the first annual progress-prize solution, a blend of 107 algorithms, the matrix factorisation component on its own scored 0.8914 RMSE (root mean squared error, in stars), by Netflix's measurement; Netflix's own system, Cinematch, scored 0.9525 on the prize's test set, and the winning target was 0.8572.

The same three ideas, eigenvectors, the SVD and rank, run through modern ML. Principal component analysis (PCA, ml13) is the eigendecomposition of a covariance matrix. LoRA fine-tunes large language models by learning low-rank weight updates (lm11). Embedding compression, the condition number that slows gradient descent, the stability of recurrent networks, PageRank, spectral clustering and the analysis of what a network has learned all use them. They are also standard interview material, usually in the form "find the eigenvectors of this 2 × 2" or "explain the SVD and what low rank means".

This lesson builds them from math8. It defines eigenvectors as the directions a matrix only stretches, finds them by hand for a 2 × 2, shows why repeated multiplication finds the largest one (power iteration), and explains why symmetric matrices such as covariance matrices are especially well behaved. It defines rank and the null space precisely. Then it introduces the SVD, which works for every matrix, computes one by hand, and uses it for low-rank approximation (the Eckart–Young theorem), the condition number and the pseudo-inverse. It ends with matrix factorisation for recommendations and the Netflix Prize in detail.

Math8 showed that a matrix moves every vector in space: stretching, rotating, shearing. Under most matrices most vectors change direction. But almost every square matrix has a few special directions that it does not turn, only stretches or shrinks (or flips). Those directions are its eigenvectors, and the stretch factors are its eigenvalues (from the German "eigen", own: the matrix's own directions). For A = [[2, 1], [1, 2]], the direction (1, 1) is stretched by 3 and the direction (1, −1) is left alone (stretched by 1), while (1, 0) is turned to (2, 1).

Why care? Because in its eigenvector directions a matrix is just a set of independent stretches, and stretches are easy. Applying A ten times to the (1, 1) direction multiplies it by 3¹⁰; to the (1, −1) direction, by 1. Anything else can be split into those two components and handled separately. Eigenvectors are the natural coordinate system for a matrix. A useful picture is a piece of fabric pulled in two directions: most threads end up at a new angle, but the threads that run exactly along the pulls only get longer. Those threads are the eigenvectors.

The analogy breaks for rotations: if you twist the fabric, no thread keeps its direction, and the matrix has no real eigenvectors. That gap is filled by the singular value decomposition (SVD), which works for every matrix, square or not. The SVD says that any matrix acts in three steps: rotate (pick a special set of perpendicular input directions and line them up with the axes), stretch each axis by a singular value, then rotate again into a special set of perpendicular output directions. A circle of inputs always becomes an ellipse of outputs, and the ellipse's semi-axes are the singular values.

A tiny example makes rank concrete before the formal definition. Suppose four users rate three films, and every rating happens to equal (how much the user likes action) × (how much action the film has). Users with action-liking 1, 2, 0 and 3 and films with action content 5, 1 and 3 give the table [[5, 1, 3], [10, 2, 6], [0, 0, 0], [15, 3, 9]]. Twelve numbers, but every row is a multiple of (5, 1, 3) and every column a multiple of (1, 2, 0, 3): the whole table is determined by seven numbers, and it has rank 1. Add a second taste, say comedy, and the table becomes a sum of two such pieces, rank 2. Real ratings are noisier, but if a few tastes explain most of them, the table is close to low rank, and that is exactly what can be exploited.

The third idea is rank: how many genuinely different directions a matrix can produce. A 3 × 3 matrix might squash all of space onto a plane (rank 2) or a line (rank 1). In SVD terms, rank is the number of non-zero singular values. Low rank is the structural assumption behind the Netflix factorisation and LoRA: a big matrix whose action is mostly captured by a few directions can be stored and learned as a product of thin matrices. If you remember one sentence from this lesson, make it this: the SVD finds a matrix's most important directions, ordered by how much the matrix stretches along them, and keeping only the top few gives the best possible simpler approximation.

1. Eigenvectors and eigenvalues, defined

For a square matrix A, a non-zero vector v is an eigenvector with eigenvalue λ (lambda) if

Av = λv

The matrix maps v to a multiple of itself. λ can be greater than 1 (stretch), between 0 and 1 (shrink), negative (flip and scale) or zero (squash to nothing). Any non-zero multiple of an eigenvector is also an eigenvector with the same eigenvalue, so eigenvectors are really directions; by convention we often report them as unit vectors.

Check the example: A = [[2, 1], [1, 2]] and v = (1, 1). Av = (2 + 1, 1 + 2) = (3, 3) = 3·(1, 1). Eigenvalue 3. And v = (1, −1): Av = (2 − 1, 1 − 2) = (1, −1) = 1·v. Eigenvalue 1. And v = (1, 0): Av = (2, 1), not a multiple of (1, 0), so not an eigenvector.

2. Finding them by hand: the characteristic equation

Rewrite Av = λv as (A − λI)v = 0, where I is the identity. A non-zero v can satisfy this only if the matrix A − λI squashes some direction to zero, which (math8) happens exactly when its determinant is zero:

det(A − λI) = 0

This is the characteristic equation. For a 2 × 2 matrix [[a, b], [c, d]] it is (a − λ)(d − λ) − bc = 0, a quadratic in λ. For A = [[2, 1], [1, 2]]: (2 − λ)² − 1 = 0, so 2 − λ = ±1, giving λ = 1 or λ = 3. For each eigenvalue, solve (A − λI)v = 0:

  • λ = 3: A − 3I = [[−1, 1], [1, −1]]. The first row says −v₁ + v₂ = 0, so v₂ = v₁: v ∝ (1, 1), unit vector (1, 1)/√2.
  • λ = 1: A − I = [[1, 1], [1, 1]]. v₁ + v₂ = 0: v ∝ (1, −1), unit vector (1, −1)/√2.

Two quick checks always apply. The eigenvalues add up to the trace (the sum of the diagonal): 3 + 1 = 4 = 2 + 2. And they multiply to the determinant: 3 × 1 = 3 = 2 × 2 − 1 × 1. For larger matrices nobody solves characteristic polynomials by hand (beyond 4 × 4 there is no general formula); libraries use iterative algorithms, but the definition and the 2 × 2 calculation are what interviews test.

Some matrices have no real eigenvalues. The 90° rotation [[0, −1], [1, 0]] gives λ² + 1 = 0, so λ = ±i: no real direction survives a rotation unturned. Some have a repeated eigenvalue with too few eigenvectors, such as the shear [[1, 1], [0, 1]], whose only eigenvalue is 1 with the single eigenvector direction (1, 0). Section 4 shows the class of matrices where none of this goes wrong.

3. Repeated multiplication and power iteration

Eigenvectors make powers of a matrix easy. Write any vector as a combination of eigenvectors: (1, 0) = ½(1, 1) + ½(1, −1). Applying A multiplies each part by its eigenvalue, so Aᵏ(1, 0) = ½·3ᵏ(1, 1) + ½·1ᵏ(1, −1). For k = 10 that is ½·59,049·(1, 1) + ½·(1, −1) = (29,525, 29,524). The component along the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue grows fastest and soon dominates; the vector's direction converges to that eigenvector.

That observation is an algorithm, power iteration: start from a random vector, multiply by A, divide by its length, repeat. The direction converges to the eigenvector of the largest |λ|, and the Rayleigh quotient vᵀAv (for a unit vector v) converges to the eigenvalue. The error shrinks by a factor |λ₂/λ₁| each step, so the speed depends on the gap between the top two eigenvalues. For our A the ratio is 1/3, and after 10 steps the error is about (1/3)¹⁰ ≈ 2 × 10⁻⁵. The lab runs it on a random 50 × 50 symmetric matrix whose top two eigenvalues are close (ratio 0.982): after 200 steps the estimate is 175.33 against the true 175.35, and the direction still differs measurably from the true eigenvector, because 0.982²⁰⁰ is still about 3%.

Power iteration matters in practice because it needs only matrix–vector products, which are cheap for huge sparse matrices. Google's original PageRank is the dominant eigenvector of the web's link matrix, computed this way. The long-run behaviour of a Markov chain (a system that moves between states with fixed probabilities) is an eigenvector problem too: if a service is healthy and stays healthy with probability 0.9 per minute, or recovers from degraded with probability 0.5, the transition matrix has eigenvalue 1 with eigenvector (5/6, 1/6): in the long run it is healthy five-sixths of the time. Its second eigenvalue, 0.4, says how fast it forgets its starting state. Spectral normalisation in generative adversarial networks (GANs, dl10) uses a single power-iteration step per training step to estimate each weight matrix's largest singular value.

4. Symmetric matrices: the well-behaved case

A matrix equal to its transpose is symmetric. Covariance matrices (math3), XᵀX from least squares (math8), Hessians of loss functions (math10, math11) and kernel matrices are all symmetric, and symmetric matrices have three properties that make them easy to work with (the spectral theorem):

  1. All eigenvalues are real.
  2. Eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are perpendicular, and you can always choose a full set of n perpendicular unit eigenvectors.
  3. So A = QΛQᵀ, where Q's columns are the unit eigenvectors (Q is an orthogonal matrix, QᵀQ = I) and Λ is diagonal with the eigenvalues.

For our A: Q = (1/√2)·[[1, 1], [1, −1]] and Λ = diag(3, 1). The decomposition reads: rotate into the eigenvector coordinates (Qᵀ), stretch each axis by its eigenvalue (Λ), rotate back (Q). This is the eigendecomposition, and for symmetric matrices it is as clean as decompositions get.

A symmetric matrix with all eigenvalues positive is positive definite: then xᵀAx > 0 for every non-zero x, the quadratic "bowl" xᵀAx curves upward in every direction. Covariance matrices are positive semi-definite (eigenvalues ≥ 0), since a variance cannot be negative. The Hessian of a loss at a strict minimum is positive definite, and its eigenvalues are the curvatures along its eigenvector directions; math11 shows how their ratio decides how fast gradient descent converges.

5. Eigenvectors of a covariance matrix: the principal components

The covariance matrix of the 5-row prompt-length table from math3 is C = [[10, 135], [135, 2,050]]: variance 10 for x (hundreds of tokens), 2,050 for y (ms), covariance 135. Its eigenvalues solve λ² − 2,060λ + 2,275 = 0 (trace 2,060, determinant 10 × 2,050 − 135² = 2,275), giving λ ≈ 2,058.9 and 1.105. The top eigenvector is about (0.066, 0.998). That is the first principal component: the direction along which the data vary most, with variance 2,058.9 out of 2,060, or 99.95%.

That number is mostly an artefact of units. Latency is measured in milliseconds and varies by tens; prompt length in hundreds of tokens varies by a few. The direction of greatest variance is therefore almost exactly the latency axis. Standardise both variables (divide each by its standard deviation) and the covariance matrix becomes the correlation matrix [[1, 0.943], [0.943, 1]], whose eigenvalues are 1 ± 0.943 = 1.943 and 0.057, with eigenvectors (1, 1)/√2 and (1, −1)/√2 (the same as our A, since any matrix [[1, r], [r, 1]] has them). Now the first component is the diagonal, the shared trend of "longer prompts, slower responses", and it carries 97.1% of the variance. Same data, different answer: PCA's components depend on scaling, which is why ml13 insists on deciding scaling deliberately. Ml13 covers PCA in depth; here the point is that PCA is exactly the eigendecomposition of a covariance matrix, and its components are perpendicular because covariance matrices are symmetric.

6. Rank, null space and losing information

The rank of a matrix is the number of linearly independent columns: columns none of which can be built as a combination of the others. Equivalently it is the dimension of the column space, the set of all outputs Ax (math8), and remarkably it always equals the number of independent rows too. An m × n matrix has rank at most min(m, n); if it reaches that, it is full rank.

Examples. [[1, 2], [2, 4]] has rank 1: the second column is twice the first, so every output is a multiple of (1, 2) and the plane is squashed onto a line. Its determinant is 1 × 4 − 2 × 2 = 0. The outer product of two vectors, uvᵀ, always has rank 1: every column is a multiple of u. A data matrix with 1,000 rows and 20 features has rank at most 20, and less if some feature is a combination of others (height in centimetres and in inches; one-hot columns that always sum to 1 together with an intercept column). The lab builds a 100 × 4 matrix whose fourth column is 2 × the first minus the second: its rank is 3.

When the rank is less than the number of columns, some non-zero inputs map to zero. Those inputs form the null space. For [[1, 2], [2, 4]], every multiple of (2, −1) maps to (0, 0). Any vector in the null space can be added to a solution of Ax = b without changing Ax, so solutions are not unique, and the matrix cannot be inverted: information has been lost. In regression, that is why perfectly collinear features make XᵀX singular and the coefficients undefined: you can shift weight between the duplicate features without changing any prediction. The rank–nullity rule ties the two together: rank plus the dimension of the null space equals the number of columns.

7. The singular value decomposition

Eigenvectors need a square matrix and may not exist or may not be perpendicular. The singular value decomposition works for every matrix. For any m × n matrix A:

A = U Σ Vᵀ

where V (n × n) has orthonormal columns v₁, v₂, … (the right singular vectors, input directions), U (m × m) has orthonormal columns u₁, u₂, … (the left singular vectors, output directions), and Σ (m × n) is zero except for non-negative singular values σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ … on its diagonal. The meaning is: A sends each input direction vᵢ to the output direction uᵢ, stretched by σᵢ:

Avᵢ = σᵢuᵢ

Geometrically, every matrix is a rotation (Vᵀ), a stretch along the axes (Σ), and another rotation (U). The unit circle becomes an ellipse whose semi-axes have lengths σ₁, σ₂ and point along u₁, u₂.

Where do the pieces come from? AᵀA is symmetric and positive semi-definite, so it has perpendicular eigenvectors with non-negative eigenvalues. Those eigenvectors are the vᵢ, and the singular values are the square roots of the eigenvalues: AᵀAvᵢ = σᵢ²vᵢ. Then each uᵢ = Avᵢ/σᵢ.

Worked by hand: A = [[3, 0], [4, 5]].

  1. AᵀA = [[3·3 + 4·4, 3·0 + 4·5], [0·3 + 5·4, 0·0 + 5·5]] = [[25, 20], [20, 25]].
  2. Its eigenvalues: (25 − λ)² − 400 = 0, so 25 − λ = ±20, λ = 45 or 5. Singular values σ₁ = √45 = 3√5 ≈ 6.708, σ₂ = √5 ≈ 2.236.
  3. Eigenvectors of [[25, 20], [20, 25]] are (1, 1)/√2 for 45 and (1, −1)/√2 for 5 (the same pattern as section 2). These are v₁ and v₂.
  4. u₁ = Av₁/σ₁ = (3, 9)/√2 ÷ 3√5 = (1, 3)/√10. u₂ = Av₂/σ₂ = (3, −1)/√2 ÷ √5 = (3, −1)/√10.
  5. Check: u₁·u₂ = (3 − 3)/10 = 0, perpendicular; σ₁σ₂ = √225 = 15 = |det A| = |3 × 5 − 0 × 4|, the area scale factor.

The lab's np.linalg.svd returns singular values 6.7082 and 2.2361, matching (library singular vectors may differ by a sign, which is harmless: −u and −v together give the same product).

The SVD reveals rank directly: the number of non-zero singular values. In floating point nothing is exactly zero, so the numerical rank counts singular values above a tolerance relative to σ₁; the lab's dependent fourth column shows up as a singular value of 0.000000 next to 23.5, 10.5 and 9.2.

8. Low-rank approximation and the Eckart–Young theorem

The SVD writes A as a sum of rank-1 pieces, each an outer product scaled by a singular value:

A = σ₁u₁v₁ᵀ + σ₂u₂v₂ᵀ + … + σᵣuᵣvᵣᵀ

Keeping only the first k terms gives a rank-k matrix Aₖ. The Eckart–Young theorem (1936) says that no rank-k matrix is closer to A: Aₖ minimises the sum of squared differences between entries (the squared Frobenius norm of A − Aₖ), and the minimum error equals the sum of the squares of the discarded singular values.

Worked on A = [[3, 0], [4, 5]]: A₁ = σ₁u₁v₁ᵀ = 3√5 × (1, 3)/√10 × (1, 1)ᵀ/√2 = (3√5/√20)·[[1, 1], [3, 3]] = 1.5·[[1, 1], [3, 3]] = [[1.5, 1.5], [4.5, 4.5]]. The difference A − A₁ = [[1.5, −1.5], [−0.5, 0.5]] has squared entries summing to 2.25 + 2.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 = 5, so the error is √5 = σ₂, exactly as the theorem says. The rank-1 version keeps σ₁²/(σ₁² + σ₂²) = 45/50 = 90% of the matrix's squared size.

This is the mathematical basis for compression everywhere in ML. An embedding table of 50,000 words × 768 dimensions can be approximated by a rank-128 product if its singular values decay quickly. PCA keeps the top k components of a data matrix. Weight matrices can be factorised to shrink models. The singular value spectrum, plotted in decreasing order (a scree plot), tells you how much each extra rank buys.

9. Condition number and the pseudo-inverse

The SVD also answers "how sensitive is solving Ax = b?" The condition number is κ = σ_max/σ_min. Solving the system can amplify relative errors in b by up to κ: the inverse stretches the direction u_min by 1/σ_min, the largest stretch. For [[3, 0], [4, 5]], κ = 6.708/2.236 = 3, comfortably small. A design matrix with nearly collinear features has a tiny σ_min and a huge κ, which is the numerical face of math8's "unstable coefficients", and forming XᵀX squares it because the singular values of XᵀX are the squares of X's.

The SVD also gives the best substitute for an inverse when none exists. The pseudo-inverse A⁺ = VΣ⁺Uᵀ, where Σ⁺ inverts the non-zero singular values and leaves the zeros as zero. For an invertible matrix it is the ordinary inverse. For a rank-deficient or non-square one, x = A⁺b is the least-squares solution of Ax ≈ b, and among all least-squares solutions it is the one with the smallest length. That is what np.linalg.lstsq computes, and why it returns an answer for collinear features where solving the normal equations fails. Truncating tiny singular values before inverting (treating them as zero) is a form of regularisation: it refuses to amplify directions the data barely determine.

10. What determinant, trace and eigenvalues summarise

Two numbers computed directly from a square matrix are summaries of its eigenvalues, and they give quick sanity checks. The trace, the sum of the diagonal entries, equals the sum of the eigenvalues. For a covariance matrix that is the total variance, the quantity PCA splits among its components: 2,060 for the raw 5-row table, of which the first component takes 2,058.9. The determinant equals the product of the eigenvalues, and for any square matrix its absolute value equals the product of the singular values: the factor by which the matrix scales areas or volumes. For [[3, 0], [4, 5]] it is 15 = 6.708 × 2.236. A zero determinant means at least one eigenvalue (and singular value) is zero, so some direction is squashed flat and the matrix is not invertible. For a covariance matrix, the determinant measures the volume of the data cloud; it appears in the density of the multivariate normal distribution, and a near-zero determinant warns that some combination of features has almost no variance, the collinearity problem again.

Eigenvalues also describe what happens over repeated application. If every eigenvalue of a matrix has absolute value below 1, repeated multiplication shrinks every vector towards zero; if any exceeds 1, some direction blows up. That single fact explains the stability of linear dynamical systems, the convergence of Markov chains to their steady state (all eigenvalues except the one equal to 1 are smaller in size), and the vanishing and exploding gradients of recurrent networks (deep section).

11. Matrix factorisation for recommendations

Return to the Netflix table R, users by films. The low-rank hypothesis says R ≈ PQᵀ, where P is users × k and Q is films × k: each user u gets a vector pᵤ of k latent factors and each film m a vector qₘ, and the predicted rating is their dot product, r̂ᵤₘ = pᵤ·qₘ (usually plus a global mean, a user bias and a film bias, since some users rate everything high and some films are rated high by everyone). With k = 50 the model has about (480,189 + 17,770) × 50 ≈ 25 million numbers instead of 8.5 billion cells.

Eckart–Young would give the best rank-k fit if every cell were known, but 98.8% are missing, and treating them as zeros would teach the model that unrated means hated. So the factors are fitted only to the observed entries, with a penalty that keeps them small:

minimise Σ over observed (u, m) of (rᵤₘ − pᵤ·qₘ)² + λ(|pᵤ|² + |qₘ|²)

The penalty is the Gaussian prior of math5: a user with three ratings cannot support 50 free factors, so their vector is held near zero unless the data insist. Two algorithms fit it. Stochastic gradient descent loops over observed ratings and nudges pᵤ and qₘ to reduce each error (math10 and math11 explain gradients); Simon Funk's 2006 blog post describing this approach was widely copied during the competition. Alternating least squares fixes Q and solves a small ridge-regression problem (math8) for each user's vector, then fixes P and does the same for each film, and repeats; each step cannot increase the objective. The lab runs ALS on a synthetic 200 × 100 matrix of true rank 3 with only 20% of entries observed: after 30 rounds the error on the unseen 80% is about 0.03, against 1.76 for predicting zero everywhere. The low-rank assumption is what lets the observed fifth determine the missing four-fifths.

What do the learned factors mean? Nothing is labelled, so each of the k dimensions is whatever direction best explains the ratings, and any rotation of the factor space (P multiplied by an orthogonal matrix, Q by the same one) fits the data equally well. Individual dimensions therefore need not be interpretable. In practice, some top directions often line up with recognisable contrasts. Koren, Bell and Volinsky's article first illustrates the idea with two hand-picked axes (serious versus escapist, female- versus male-oriented), then plots films along the first two factors actually learned from the Netflix data, where recognisable clusters appear, such as films with strong female leads, fraternity humour and quirky independent films. The safe uses of factors are the ones that do not depend on rotation: dot products for prediction, cosine similarity between films for "more like this", and nearest neighbours between users.

Non-symmetric matrices and when eigenvectors mislead

For non-symmetric matrices, eigenvectors can be nearly parallel to each other, and then the eigenvalues give a misleading picture of behaviour over a few steps. A matrix such as [[1, 100], [0, 1]] has both eigenvalues equal to 1, which suggests nothing grows, yet it maps (0, 1) to (100, 1): a huge transient amplification that eigenvalues do not reveal and singular values (here about 100 and 0.01) do. Such matrices are called non-normal, and they matter in recurrent networks and iterative solvers, where short-term growth can cause instability even when long-run eigenvalue analysis says all is well. The practical rule: for questions about how much a matrix can stretch anything, look at singular values; for questions about long-run repeated application, look at eigenvalues; for symmetric matrices the two agree.

Why symmetric matrices have perpendicular eigenvectors

The proof is two lines and worth knowing. Suppose Av = λv and Aw = μw with λ ≠ μ, and A = Aᵀ. Then λ(v·w) = (Av)·w = vᵀAᵀw = vᵀAw = v·(Aw) = μ(v·w). So (λ − μ)(v·w) = 0, and since λ ≠ μ, v·w = 0. The same manipulation with complex numbers shows the eigenvalues are real. The consequence is practical: for symmetric matrices, numerical eigensolvers (np.linalg.eigh) are faster and more accurate than general ones (eig), and the results are guaranteed real and orthogonal, so always use the symmetric routine for covariance matrices, Gram matrices and Hessians.

Curvature, conditioning and optimisation

Near a minimum, a loss surface looks like the quadratic bowl ½xᵀHx, where H is the Hessian (the matrix of second derivatives, math10). Its eigenvectors are the bowl's principal axes and its eigenvalues are the curvatures along them. Gradient descent with step size η is stable only if η < 2/λ_max (ml4 derives this in one dimension), but then progress along the flattest direction shrinks by a factor of only about 1 − ηλ_min per step. The number of steps needed therefore grows roughly with κ = λ_max/λ_min, the Hessian's condition number. This is the deepest reason feature scaling helps (it reduces κ), why momentum and Adam help (they partly compensate for unequal curvatures), and why research on the "edge of stability" tracks the largest Hessian eigenvalue during training. Math11 picks this up.

Matrix completion: when can missing entries be recovered?

Recovering a matrix from a fraction of its entries is impossible in general (the missing entries could be anything), but possible when the matrix is low rank and its information is spread out rather than concentrated in a few rows or columns (a condition called incoherence). Theory by Candès, Recht and others showed that, under such conditions, roughly k·n·log n random observations suffice to recover an n × n matrix of rank k exactly, by minimising the nuclear norm (the sum of singular values), which is the convex relative of rank. Real ratings violate the assumptions in instructive ways: entries are not missing at random (people rate what they chose to watch, mostly films they expected to like), so models fitted only to observed ratings are biased towards optimism, which later recommendation research addresses with models of the missingness itself.

Randomized SVD and large matrices

A full SVD of an m × n matrix costs about mn·min(m, n) operations, impossible for a matrix with millions of rows. When only the top k singular vectors are needed, randomized algorithms multiply A by a random n × (k + p) matrix (p a small oversampling, such as 10), orthonormalise the result to get a basis that almost certainly captures A's top-k column space, and compute a small SVD within it. A few power-iteration steps sharpen the result when singular values decay slowly. These methods (Halko, Martinsson and Tropp, 2011) are what scikit-learn's randomized PCA and many large-scale embedding pipelines use.

Spectral norm and stability of deep networks

The largest singular value of a weight matrix, its spectral norm, is the most it can stretch any input. A deep network's output can change by at most the product of its layers' spectral norms (times the activations' slopes) when its input changes, which bounds its sensitivity. Spectral normalisation divides each weight matrix by an estimate of its spectral norm to keep that product controlled, stabilising GAN training. In recurrent networks, repeated multiplication by the same matrix behaves like power iteration: if its largest singular value is above 1, signals and gradients explode over time; below 1, they vanish (dl3, dl7). Orthogonal initialisation, whose singular values are all 1, is the linear-algebra answer to both.

Low rank inside large models

Many matrices in trained networks are approximately low rank or have rapidly decaying spectra: embedding tables, attention patterns, and especially the changes made by fine-tuning. LoRA (lm11, mc21) exploits the last of these by learning ΔW = BA with inner dimension r, so the update has rank at most r and needs 2dr parameters instead of d². For a 4,096 × 4,096 matrix with r = 8 that is 65,536 instead of 16,777,216. Grouped-query attention, low-rank compression of the key–value (KV) cache (lm5) and many pruning methods are also, at heart, low-rank approximations, and the Eckart–Young theorem tells you the best a rank-r approximation can possibly do.

Recommendation factorisations in practice

Production recommenders usually learn from implicit feedback (plays, clicks, purchases) rather than star ratings, so "missing" means "unknown or not interested" rather than "unrated". The standard adaptation, from Hu, Koren and Volinsky's implicit-feedback ALS, treats every user–item pair as a weak negative with low confidence and observed interactions as positives with confidence that grows with the amount of interaction, which ALS can still solve efficiently because the all-pairs term has a closed form. Characteristic failure modes: cold-start items and users with no interactions get near-zero vectors and are never recommended (fix with content features or a prior from metadata); popular items dominate because their factors are fitted to the most data (monitor catalogue coverage, math8's drill); and factors drift between retrains, so downstream systems that cache vectors need versioning.

Compressing embeddings and checking their rank

Before storing hundreds of millions of 768-dimensional embeddings, compute the singular value spectrum of a sample. If 95% of the squared Frobenius norm sits in the top 128 directions, projecting onto them (truncated SVD or PCA, fitted once and versioned with the model) cuts storage six-fold with little loss in retrieval quality, which should be confirmed on a retrieval benchmark rather than assumed. The same spectrum diagnoses dimensional collapse in contrastive models: if the effective rank is far below the embedding dimension, similarity scores depend on few directions, and the model is wasting capacity.

Collinearity diagnostics

When a regression or logistic model gives unstable coefficients, compute the singular values of the standardised design matrix. A condition number above roughly 30 suggests problematic collinearity; the right singular vectors belonging to the smallest singular values show which combination of features is nearly redundant (for example, total spend almost equals the sum of three category spends). Dropping or combining those features, or adding L2 regularisation, stabilises the fit. Libraries' warnings about singular matrices are usually this problem.

Watching spectra during training

Singular value spectra of weight matrices are a cheap health signal for large training runs. A rapidly growing largest singular value in one layer often precedes loss spikes, because that layer is amplifying some direction more each step; the attention logits blowing up in a transformer is a common case, and some training recipes add normalisation on queries and keys or clip spectral norms to prevent it. A collapse of the spectrum (most singular values near zero) in an embedding or output layer suggests the representation is losing dimensions. Logging the top few singular values per layer, computed with a few power-iteration steps, costs little and makes such problems visible before the loss curve does.

Numerical choices

Use symmetric eigensolvers for symmetric matrices; use the SVD rather than eigendecomposing AᵀA, which squares the condition number; compute PCA from the SVD of centred data rather than from an explicitly formed covariance matrix when the number of features is large; and remember that singular vectors are defined only up to sign (and up to rotation within a group of equal singular values), so code that compares components across runs must align signs first. When eigen-decomposing large sparse matrices (graphs, co-occurrence counts), use iterative methods that need only matrix–vector products.

The public case, in production terms

Netflix's 2012 account of the prize is a production lesson. The team took the two strongest components of the first progress-prize solution, matrix factorisation ("SVD", 0.8914 RMSE) and restricted Boltzmann machines (0.8990), blended them to about 0.88, and put them into production after engineering them to handle billions of ratings rather than 100 million. The final grand-prize solution, a blend of hundreds of models that reached 0.8567 on the test set, was evaluated offline but not deployed: the extra accuracy did not seem to justify the engineering effort, and by then the business had moved from DVD rentals to streaming, where what to show on a page mattered more than predicting a star rating. The low-rank model survived because it was accurate, compact (25 million numbers for the whole table) and simple to retrain; the lesson is to measure the marginal value of complexity against its operating cost.

Goal: find eigenvalues and eigenvectors, run power iteration and see how the eigenvalue gap sets its speed, confirm the hand-computed SVD and the Eckart–Young error, read rank from singular values, and fill in a mostly missing low-rank matrix with alternating least squares. NumPy only (gpt_env); save as math9.py.

import numpy as np

# 1. Eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a symmetric 2x2
A = np.array([[2.0, 1.0], [1.0, 2.0]])
vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(A)                       # eigh: for symmetric matrices
print("eigenvalues:", vals, " eigenvectors (columns):\n", vecs.round(4))

# 2. Power iteration finds the top eigenvector by repeated multiplication
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
M = rng.normal(size=(50, 50)); S = M @ M.T                # a symmetric 50x50 matrix
v = rng.normal(size=50)
for step in range(1, 201):
    v = S @ v
    v /= np.linalg.norm(v)
    if step in (1, 10, 50, 200):
        print(f"step {step:3d}: Rayleigh quotient {v @ S @ v:.4f}")
w, U = np.linalg.eigh(S)
print("largest eigenvalue (eigh):", w[-1].round(4), "| gap ratio lambda2/lambda1:", (w[-2] / w[-1]).round(3),
      "| |cos| with eigh vector:", abs(v @ U[:, -1]).round(4))

# 3. SVD of A = [[3, 0], [4, 5]] by library, matching the hand calculation
A = np.array([[3.0, 0.0], [4.0, 5.0]])
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A)
print("singular values:", s.round(4), "(hand: sqrt(45) = 6.7082, sqrt(5) = 2.2361)")
A1 = s[0] * np.outer(U[:, 0], Vt[0])
print("rank-1 approximation:\n", A1.round(3), "\nerror (Frobenius):", np.linalg.norm(A - A1).round(4))

# 4. Rank from singular values: a column that is a combination of others adds nothing
X = rng.normal(size=(100, 3))
X = np.column_stack([X, 2 * X[:, 0] - X[:, 1]])          # 4th column is dependent
print("singular values:", np.linalg.svd(X, compute_uv=False).round(6),
      "-> rank", np.linalg.matrix_rank(X))

# 5. Matrix factorisation: fill in a 200 x 100 rating matrix of true rank 3 from 20% of entries
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n_users, n_items, k = 200, 100, 3
R = rng.normal(size=(n_users, k)) @ rng.normal(size=(k, n_items))
seen = rng.random(R.shape) < 0.2
P = rng.normal(scale=0.1, size=(n_users, k)); Q = rng.normal(scale=0.1, size=(n_items, k))
lam = 0.1
for _ in range(30):                                       # alternating least squares
    for u in range(n_users):
        Qi = Q[seen[u]]
        P[u] = np.linalg.solve(Qi.T @ Qi + lam * np.eye(k), Qi.T @ R[u, seen[u]])
    for i in range(n_items):
        Pu = P[seen[:, i]]
        Q[i] = np.linalg.solve(Pu.T @ Pu + lam * np.eye(k), Pu.T @ R[seen[:, i], i])
pred = P @ Q.T
rmse = lambda m: np.sqrt(np.mean((pred[m] - R[m]) ** 2))
print(f"RMSE on seen entries {rmse(seen):.3f}, on unseen {rmse(~seen):.3f};"
      f" predicting 0 everywhere gives {np.sqrt(np.mean(R[~seen] ** 2)):.3f}")

What to look for:

  1. eigh on [[2, 1], [1, 2]] returns eigenvalues 1 and 3 with eigenvectors ±(1, −1)/√2 and (1, 1)/√2 (signs may differ).
  2. Power iteration on a random 50 × 50 symmetric matrix climbs 119.6 → 167.3 → 173.6 → 175.33 towards the true 175.35. The gap ratio λ₂/λ₁ is 0.982, which is why it needs hundreds of steps; the direction agrees with eigh to a cosine of about 0.997.
  3. The SVD of [[3, 0], [4, 5]] gives 6.7082 and 2.2361; the rank-1 approximation is [[1.5, 1.5], [4.5, 4.5]] with error 2.2361 = σ₂.
  4. A 100 × 4 matrix whose fourth column is a combination of two others has singular values about 23.5, 10.5, 9.2 and 0, so rank 3.
  5. ALS on a rank-3, 200 × 100 matrix with 20% of entries seen reaches an error of about 0.016 on seen and 0.032 on unseen entries, against 1.76 for predicting zero: the missing 80% is recovered.

Set the regularisation lam to 0 and see what happens to users with few ratings. Finally, run power iteration on [[2, 1], [1, 2]] and confirm the error falls by about a factor of 3 per step.

Estimate and design: compressing a recommendation model

A marketplace has 20 million users and 2 million items. The current model stores a 128-dimensional float32 vector for every user and item and scores pairs by dot product. Storage and serving cost are too high, and the team proposes cutting to 32 dimensions. (a) How much memory do the vectors take now and after? (b) How would you decide whether 32 dimensions lose too much? (c) A colleague suggests instead running a standard SVD on the full user–item interaction matrix. What is wrong with that?

Answer

(a) 22 million vectors × 128 numbers × 4 bytes ≈ 11.3 GB now; at 32 dimensions, 2.8 GB, a four-fold saving. Each dot product also costs a quarter of the operations, which matters when scoring thousands of candidates per request.

(b) Two complementary checks. Spectrum: stack a large sample of item vectors (and user vectors) and compute their singular values; if the top 32 directions hold, say, 95% of the squared Frobenius norm, a rank-32 projection preserves most of the geometry, and Eckart–Young guarantees it is the best rank-32 linear compression. But geometry is not the goal, so second, measure the task: project both towers onto the top-32 subspace (or retrain at 32 dimensions) and compare offline recall@k and ranking metrics on held-out interactions, sliced by popular versus long-tail items, since compression often hurts the tail first. Confirm with an A/B test (math7) before switching.

(c) The interaction matrix has 4 × 10¹³ cells, almost all unobserved; a standard SVD treats missing as zero, so it learns "unobserved means disliked" and is dominated by popular items. It is also far too large to decompose directly. Use a factorisation fitted to observed interactions with confidence weighting (implicit ALS) or the existing two-tower training, and if needed a randomized truncated SVD of the learned vectors, not of the raw matrix.
