Three teams at the same company start "an ML project" in the same week. The first wants to flag fraudulent card payments; they have two years of transactions, and for each one the bank eventually learned whether it was fraud. The second wants to understand its customers; it has millions of purchase histories and nobody has ever said what "kinds" of customer there are. The third wants a model that drafts replies to support tickets; it has fifty million old tickets and replies, but only two thousand have been carefully tagged by topic.

All three will say they are "training a model", and all three may even use the same library. But they are doing fundamentally different things, because the thing that teaches the model — the learning signal — comes from a different place in each case. The fraud team has an answer for every example. The customer team has no answers at all. The support team has a handful of answers and an ocean of raw text from which it can manufacture its own. Get this framing wrong and nothing downstream rescues the project: a team that thinks it has labels when it really has a noisy proxy, or that spends its whole budget hand-labelling when free structure was sitting in the data, loses months.

This framing is also the story of modern AI in one sentence. Large language models exist because researchers found a way to turn unlabelled text into a supervised problem — predict the next word — so that the internet became a training set of trillions of labelled examples with no human in the loop. Nearly every interview for an AI-engineering role eventually asks some version of "where does the signal come from, and what would you do if you had more data but no labels?"

This lesson builds the vocabulary the whole track uses: what a model, a parameter, a feature and a label are; what "training" means as a calculation (with a least-squares fit done by hand); why we care about new data rather than the training data; and then each learning regime — supervised, unsupervised, self-supervised, semi-supervised, weak supervision, active learning, transfer learning and reinforcement learning — with a worked example of each, the workflow that ties them together, and a public case (Snorkel's weak-supervision study) showing what the choice of signal is worth.

Three ways to learn a language

Imagine three people learning French. The first has a tutor who shows them an English sentence and then the correct French translation, thousands of times; after each attempt the tutor says exactly what the right answer was. That is supervised learning: every example comes with the correct answer, and learning means reducing the difference between your answer and the given one.

The second person is dropped into Paris with no tutor and no dictionary. Nobody tells them what anything means, but after a few weeks they notice that certain sounds occur together, that some shops are like other shops, that conversations fall into a few recognisable types. That is unsupervised learning: no answers at all, only structure found in the raw experience — groups, patterns, the few directions along which things mostly vary.

The third person has a huge pile of French books but no teacher. They invent a game: cover the next word with a finger, guess it, then lift the finger and check. Nobody labelled anything, yet every word of every book has become a question with a known answer. That is self-supervised learning, and it is exactly how GPT-style language models are pretrained: predict each next word from the words before it, across trillions of words.

The analogy breaks in one useful place: a person who plays the covering game for years learns an enormous amount about French, but still doesn't know which of their abilities you want. That is why self-supervised models are usually followed by a smaller, supervised stage — a few thousand labelled examples of the actual task — and why "pretrain, then fine-tune" is the standard recipe.

The vocabulary in one example

Take the smallest possible problem: predict a flat's monthly rent from its floor area. One flat is one example (also called a sample, an instance or a row). Its floor area is a feature — a measurable input; real problems have dozens or thousands of features, arranged as a table called X with one row per example and one column per feature. The rent is the label or target, written y. A model is a function that maps features to a prediction, written ŷ ("y-hat"). The simplest model here is a straight line, ŷ = w × area + b, where w (a slope) and b (an intercept) are its parameters, also called weights: the adjustable numbers inside the model. Training means choosing parameter values that make predictions close to the labels on the examples we have. Inference means using the trained model on a flat it has never seen.

Predicting a number, like rent, is called regression. Predicting a category, like "spam" or "not spam", is called classification — binary with two classes, multi-class with more, and multi-label when several can be true at once (an article can be about both sport and politics).

A reader who stops here has the correct picture: learning is adjusting numbers inside a function so its outputs match some target, and the regimes differ only in where that target comes from. The rest of this lesson makes each part precise.

1. Training as a calculation: minimising average loss

"Make predictions close to the labels" needs a number that says how close. That number is a loss function: a formula that takes one prediction and one label and returns a penalty, zero when they match and larger the more they disagree. For numbers, the most common choice is squared error, (y − ŷ)²: an error of 100 costs 10,000, an error of 50 costs 2,500. (ml5 compares loss functions and what each one punishes; for categories the standard choice is cross-entropy, which ml6 derives.)

Training then means: choose the parameters that make the average loss over all training examples as small as possible. In symbols, with n training examples:

training loss(w, b) = (1/n) · Σᵢ (yᵢ − (w·xᵢ + b))²       ← mean squared error (MSE)
training            = find the w, b that make this as small as possible

Statisticians call that average the empirical risk, and the recipe "pick the parameters that minimise it" is called empirical risk minimisation. Almost everything in this track, from logistic regression to gradient-boosted trees, is this recipe with a different model and a different loss. For deep networks the minimum is found by repeatedly nudging the parameters downhill — gradient descent, which ml4 covers — but for a straight line with squared error there is a direct formula, which makes it perfect for doing by hand.

Worked example. Three flats: 40 m² at £1,000, 60 m² at £1,500, 80 m² at £1,700. The least-squares line has slope w = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) / Σ(x − x̄)² and intercept b = ȳ − w·x̄, where the bars mean averages.

  1. Averages: x̄ = (40 + 60 + 80)/3 = 60; ȳ = (1000 + 1500 + 1700)/3 = 1400.
  2. Deviations from the averages: areas −20, 0, +20; rents −400, +100, +300.
  3. Numerator: (−20)(−400) + (0)(100) + (20)(300) = 8,000 + 0 + 6,000 = 14,000. Denominator: 400 + 0 + 400 = 800.
  4. So w = 14,000 / 800 = 17.5 pounds per square metre, and b = 1,400 − 17.5 × 60 = 350.
  5. Predictions: 17.5 × 40 + 350 = £1,050; 17.5 × 60 + 350 = £1,400; 17.5 × 80 + 350 = £1,750. Errors: −50, +100, −50. MSE = (2,500 + 10,000 + 2,500)/3 = 5,000.

Compare a line someone might guess, ŷ = 20 × area + 200: predictions £1,000, £1,400, £1,800, errors 0, +100, −100, MSE = (0 + 10,000 + 10,000)/3 ≈ 6,667. The guessed line fits the first flat perfectly but is worse on average; training's job is to find the line with the smallest average, and no other straight line beats 5,000 on these three points. For a new 70 m² flat, the trained model predicts 17.5 × 70 + 350 = £1,575 — that step is inference.

The square root of the MSE, about £71 here, is in pounds and is easier to talk about: "the model is typically off by about £70 on the flats it trained on". Which raises the real question.

2. Generalisation: the point is the next flat, not these three

Nobody needs a model to tell them the rent of flats whose rent they already know. The value is in predicting the next flat. Doing well on new examples is called generalisation, and it is the whole point of machine learning. A model can have zero training loss and be useless: a lookup table that memorises each training flat's rent fits perfectly and has nothing to say about a flat of 70 m².

Generalisation only makes sense under an assumption about where new examples come from. The standard one is that all examples — training and future — are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.): each is drawn separately from one fixed, unknown process, called the distribution. If that holds, performance on examples the model never trained on is an honest forecast of performance in use. Much of the practical difficulty in this track (leakage in ml9, the training/serving gap in ml14, drift in ml16) comes from situations where it quietly doesn't hold: next year's flats are not drawn from last year's distribution, and several rows from the same building are not independent.

So data is split before any modelling:

  • The training set fits the parameters.
  • The validation set (or development set) chooses between models and hyperparameters — settings that the engineer picks rather than training, such as how complex the model may be.
  • The test set is used once, at the end, for an unbiased estimate. Once you have made decisions based on it, it is no longer unbiased: the more times you check it, the more you are fitting to it.

A typical split is 70/15/15 or 80/10/10 of the examples, chosen at random when the i.i.d. assumption is reasonable, and by time or by group when it isn't. ml2 turns the gap between training and validation error into a diagnostic, and ml9 covers splitting and cross-validation properly.

3. Supervised learning

In supervised learning every training example carries a label, and the model learns the mapping from features to label. Most business ML is supervised: fraud (label: was it fraud?), churn (did the customer leave?), demand forecasting (how many sold?), ranking (did the user click?), and the classifier heads on top of modern language models.

The labels are where supervised learning's costs and risks live. They cost money: a medical image may need a specialist's time; a contract clause needs a lawyer. They are noisy: two annotators given the same 1,000 support tickets might agree on only 85% of them, which caps any model's measurable accuracy near that level, because on the other 15% the "right answer" is itself uncertain. And they are defined by someone. A "churned" customer might mean one who cancelled, one who has not logged in for 30 days, or one who downgraded; each definition gives a different model. Stating the label definition, when it becomes known and how noisy it is, is the first thing a strong candidate does when asked to design a supervised system.

A small classification example shows the shape. A spam filter might represent each email by two features: the number of links and whether the sender is in the contacts. The labels come from users pressing "report spam". A model might learn the rule "spam if links > 5 and sender not in contacts" — not by being told that rule, but by searching for parameter values that make its predictions match the labels. ml6 builds the standard model for this, logistic regression.

4. Unsupervised learning

In unsupervised learning there are no labels, only features. The model looks for structure in X itself. The main kinds:

  • Clustering groups similar examples (customer segments, topics among documents). ml12.
  • Dimensionality reduction finds a few directions that capture most of the variation, compressing hundreds of features into a handful (PCA in ml13).
  • Density estimation and anomaly detection model which inputs are typical, so that unusual ones — a strange transaction, a failing machine — stand out.

Worked example. Six customers' monthly spend: £10, £12, £14, £50, £55, £60. Nobody labelled them, but split them into two groups and the natural split is {10, 12, 14} with average £12 and {50, 55, 60} with average £55; every customer is much closer to their own group's average than to the other's. A clustering algorithm finds this split by trying to make each point close to its group's centre. Now scale matters: add a second feature, "visits per month", ranging from 1 to 3, and measured in raw units it barely affects distances next to spend in pounds; measured in pence, spend would dominate even more. Change the units and the "natural" groups change — which is why unsupervised results are hypotheses that depend on your choices, not discoveries.

Because there is no label, there is also no simple accuracy. Unsupervised models are judged by whether their output helps something downstream: do the segments respond differently to a campaign, does the anomaly score catch the fraud that was later confirmed, do the compressed features make a supervised model better?

5. Self-supervised learning

Self-supervised learning takes unlabelled data and invents a supervised task from it, so that the label is part of the input. The model is then trained exactly like a supervised model — same loss functions, same optimisers — but the dataset can be as large as all the raw data you can collect, because no human has to label anything.

The objective behind GPT-style models is next-token prediction. Text is split into tokens — words or pieces of words — and the model is asked to predict each token from all the tokens before it.

From the six-token sentence "the cat sat on the mat" come five examples: predict "cat" from "the", "sat" from "the cat", and so on. A sequence of L tokens gives L − 1 targets. Scale that up: Meta reports pretraining Llama 3 on over 15 trillion tokens, which is on the order of 15 trillion labelled examples. The largest human-labelled datasets are in the millions — ImageNet, the dataset that drove the deep-learning boom in vision, has about 14 million images labelled by paid crowd workers. Self-supervision is a difference of six orders of magnitude, and that is the most important fact in this lesson.

Why does guessing the next word teach anything useful? Because to predict well across all text, a model must capture whatever makes text predictable: grammar, facts ("the capital of France is…"), arithmetic, the structure of code, how arguments proceed. The representations it builds to do that turn out to be useful for many other tasks.

Two other self-supervised families appear constantly:

  • Masked prediction. Hide some tokens and predict them from both sides. BERT masked 15% of tokens; this gives representations that see the whole sentence, good for understanding tasks such as classification and search, but no natural way to generate text left to right. Vision has an analogue: hide patches of an image and reconstruct them.
  • Contrastive learning. Instead of predicting a hidden piece, learn to tell matching pairs from non-matching ones. CLIP was trained on 400 million image–caption pairs from the web: for a batch of images and captions, it learns to score each image highest with its own caption. The result is an embedding space — each input becomes a vector of numbers, with similar things close together — used for image search and classifying images by comparing them with text such as "a photo of a dog".

Some textbooks file self-supervised learning under unsupervised, because no human labels are involved. The more useful description is the mechanical one: it is supervised training with manufactured labels.

6. Between the extremes: using a few labels well

Real projects rarely sit in one regime. The support-ticket team from the introduction has fifty million unlabelled tickets and two thousand labelled ones. Four techniques bridge that gap.

Transfer learning reuses a model trained on a large task as the starting point for a smaller one. Take a model pretrained on text, use it to turn each ticket into an embedding, and fit a simple classifier on the two thousand labelled embeddings. The expensive knowledge — what words mean, how sentences work — came free from pretraining; the labels only have to teach the last step. This is the default for text, images and audio today (dl11 covers fine-tuning in depth).

Semi-supervised learning uses the unlabelled examples directly in training. The simplest method is pseudo-labelling: train on the labelled set, predict the unlabelled set, keep predictions the model is very confident about (say, above 95% probability) as if they were labels, and retrain. Suppose the model labels 400,000 unlabelled tickets with over 95% confidence and 97% of those pseudo-labels are right; the model now trains on far more examples, at the cost of 12,000 wrong labels that reinforce its own mistakes. That trade is why the confidence threshold matters.

Weak supervision writes rules instead of labels. A domain expert writes labelling functions — "if the ticket mentions 'refund' or 'charged twice', label it billing" — each noisy and covering only some tickets. A label model estimates how accurate each rule is from how the rules agree and disagree, and combines them into probabilistic labels for millions of examples. The Snorkel system, the public case in this lesson, is the canonical example.

Active learning spends a labelling budget where it helps most. The model scores the unlabelled pool and asks a human to label the examples it is least sure about. With uncertainty sampling, a ticket the model rates 51% billing / 49% technical is a much better use of a pound than one it rates 99% billing; the second label would teach it almost nothing. Labels are gathered in rounds: label a batch, retrain, re-score, repeat.

7. Reinforcement learning, briefly

Reinforcement learning (RL) is the fourth regime. An agent takes actions in an environment and receives rewards; it learns a policy — a rule for choosing actions — that maximises total reward. A game-playing agent is rewarded for winning, not told the right move in each position. Two features make RL genuinely different from supervised learning. First, credit assignment: a reward at the end of a game has to be traced back to the moves that caused it. Second, the data depends on the learner: an agent that never tries a move never learns what it does, so it must balance exploration (trying things) against exploitation (doing what seems best). The simplest production form is the contextual bandit — choose one action per request (which article to show), observe one reward (a click) — used in recommendation and ads. For LLMs, RL appears in post-training: the model generates answers, a reward model or automatic checker scores them, and the model is updated to make high-scoring answers more likely.

8. The workflow that ties it together

A baseline is the simplest reasonable predictor: for classification, always predict the most common class; for regression, always predict the training mean. If 97% of transactions are legitimate, "never fraud" is 97% accurate, and a model that scores 96% is worse than doing nothing. After the trivial baseline comes a simple model (a linear or logistic regression), and only then complex ones. Each step must beat the previous one on validation data by enough to justify its cost — Google's Rules of Machine Learning go further, and Rule 1 is not to be afraid to launch without machine learning at all.

The label is a design decision

A label is a definition, a time window and a measurement process, and each can be wrong. "Churn" could be "cancelled within 60 days of the prediction date" or "no login for 30 days"; the first is observable but rare, the second common but noisy (people go on holiday). The label also has a label delay — how long after the prediction moment it becomes known. Chargebacks arrive weeks after a fraudulent payment, so the newest training data is always incomplete: recent transactions look legitimate simply because their fraud hasn't been reported yet. Senior answers state the label definition, the delay and the noise level, and build the training set only from examples whose labels have had time to mature.

Selective labels and feedback loops

When the model's decision determines whether a label is ever observed, the data is censored. A lending model sees repayment only for loans it approved; a fraud model that blocks a payment never learns whether it was fraud. Retraining on observed outcomes then teaches the model only about the population it already lets through, and its false positives can never be corrected. The standard remedy is a small exploration slice — approve a random few applications just below the threshold — plus logging the probability with which each decision was made, so that later training can reweight the observed outcomes (inverse propensity weighting). The same structure appears in recommendation: items never shown never get clicks, so the system's past choices shape its future training data.

The self-supervised objective shapes the representation

Masked prediction sees both sides of a gap, so every token's representation reflects its whole sentence — excellent for classification and search, but a masked model has no natural way to generate text left to right. Next-token prediction sees only the past, which is exactly what generation needs, and every token is a target, so it extracts more training signal per sequence than masking 15%. Contrastive objectives never reconstruct anything; they only learn which things belong together, so they produce embedding spaces ideal for retrieval but no generator at all. Choosing the objective chooses what the model will be good at — the reason retrieval systems use contrastively trained embedding models and chat systems use next-token models.

Why self-supervision scales where supervision doesn't

Three properties compound. The labels are free, so the dataset can grow with the raw data. The labels are exact — the next token is the next token — so there is no annotator noise ceiling. And the task is hard enough that it keeps rewarding larger models: loss on next-token prediction kept falling smoothly as models, data and compute grew, which is the empirical basis for scaling laws. A supervised task with a fixed labelled set eventually saturates; a self-supervised task over a growing corpus keeps paying back more capacity.

Pseudo-labels and distillation inherit errors

Semi-supervised methods that train on a model's own confident predictions reinforce its biases: if it confidently mislabels a class of examples, pseudo-labelling makes that class more wrong. Consistency regularisation — requiring predictions to stay the same under small changes to the input, like cropping an image or paraphrasing a sentence — is a gentler alternative. The LLM-era version is labelling data with a stronger model (distillation); it is cheap and effective, but the labels carry that model's blind spots, and a student cannot learn what the teacher gets wrong.

Weak supervision as a statistical problem

Labelling functions are noisy, overlapping and correlated. The insight behind Snorkel is that their agreements and disagreements on unlabelled data carry information about their accuracies — if rule A usually agrees with B and C but rule D often contradicts all three, D is probably less reliable — so a generative label model can estimate each rule's accuracy without any ground truth, and output a probability for each example's label. A discriminative model (for example, a neural network) is then trained on those probabilistic labels and generalises beyond the rules, labelling examples no rule covered.

The cost framing of labels

The interview question "we have budget for N more labels" is really "where is the marginal value of a label highest?". The usual ranking is: first improve the representation (free); then fix label noise in the existing set, which raises the ceiling; then label actively (uncertain or disagreeing examples) and label rare classes deliberately; and only then label randomly. Always reserve part of the budget for a random, never-trained-on test set, because every other labelled set has been chosen by the model and is biased.

The public case: Snorkel and the cost of hand labelling

Snorkel was built at Stanford to test whether experts could produce training data by writing rules rather than labelling rows. In the paper's user study (Ratner et al., 2018), subject-matter experts using Snorkel built models 2.8× faster and improved predictive performance by an average of 45.5% compared with seven hours of hand labelling. Across its benchmark datasets, models trained on Snorkel's labels came within 3.60% of models trained on large hand-curated training sets. Google then adapted the approach as Snorkel DryBell (Bach et al., 2019): using existing organisational resources — older rule-based systems, knowledge graphs, internal classifiers — as labelling functions, it produced content classifiers of comparable quality to ones trained on tens of thousands of hand-labelled examples. The lesson for interviews is not that weak supervision always wins, but that how you get the signal is a first-order engineering decision: an hour of an expert's time spent on rules can be worth many hours spent on rows, when the rules capture patterns that generalise.

How framing goes wrong in real systems

The label is a proxy for the goal. A recommender trained on clicks optimises clicks, which rewards clickbait; the goal was satisfaction. The usual fix is a better label (dwell time, explicit ratings, long-term retention) or a combination, checked against the business metric (ml16).

Label leakage from the framing. Imagine a hospital model meant to flag pneumonia at admission, trained on records that include which antibiotics the patient received. It will look excellent — the antibiotic column practically spells out the diagnosis — and be useless at admission, when no antibiotic has been given yet. Any feature recorded after the prediction moment is part of the answer. The defence is to fix the prediction moment in the label definition and only use features known before it (ml9 on leakage, ml14 on point-in-time features).

Label noise capping accuracy. If annotators agree on 85% of items, a model scoring 88% against one annotator's labels may be as good as possible. Measure inter-annotator agreement on a sample early; it tells you the ceiling and whether to invest in clearer guidelines rather than a bigger model.

Maturing labels. Fraud, churn and conversion labels arrive late. Training on the last week's data teaches the model that recent examples are all "negative". Use a cutoff older than the label delay, or model the delay explicitly.

What teams monitor

Label-side metrics deserve the same attention as model metrics: the rate of positive labels over time (a sudden change may be a pipeline bug, not the world), annotator agreement, the fraction of production traffic covered by exploration, and the delay between prediction and label. On the unlabelled side, the distribution of input features compared with training data is the earliest warning that the i.i.d. assumption has broken (ml14, ml16).

The modern default recipe

For text and images, the production default is: start from a pretrained model; get embeddings or fine-tune on a few thousand labelled examples; spend further labels actively; and consider continued self-supervised pretraining on in-domain unlabelled data when the domain is unusual (legal, medical, code in a niche language). For tabular data — transactions, sensor readings, customer records — pretrained representations help less, and supervised gradient-boosted trees (ml10) remain the strong default.

import numpy as np

# 1. Supervised regression: least squares by the formula, then by NumPy
x = np.array([40., 60., 80.]); y = np.array([1000., 1500., 1700.])
w = ((x - x.mean()) * (y - y.mean())).sum() / ((x - x.mean())**2).sum()
b = y.mean() - w * x.mean()
mse = lambda w, b: ((y - (w * x + b))**2).mean()
print(f"w={w}, b={b}, MSE={mse(w, b):.0f}, guessed line MSE={mse(20, 200):.0f}")
print("numpy polyfit:", np.polyfit(x, y, 1), " predict 70 m2:", w * 70 + b)

# 2. Self-supervised: manufacture (context, target) pairs from raw text
tokens = "the cat sat on the mat".split()
pairs = [(tokens[:i], tokens[i]) for i in range(1, len(tokens))]
for ctx, tgt in pairs:
    print(" ".join(ctx), "->", tgt)

# 3. Unsupervised: split 1-D spend into two groups by trying every cut
spend = np.sort(np.array([10., 12., 14., 50., 55., 60.]))
cost = [((spend[:k] - spend[:k].mean())**2).sum() + ((spend[k:] - spend[k:].mean())**2).sum()
        for k in range(1, len(spend))]
k = int(np.argmin(cost)) + 1
print("groups:", spend[:k], spend[k:])

What to look for. Part 1 prints w=17.5, b=350, MSE 5,000 against 6,667 for the guessed line, and polyfit agrees — training is just finding the minimum. Part 2 prints five pairs from six tokens: labels with no human. Part 3 finds the {10, 12, 14} / {50, 55, 60} split with no labels at all. Work it out

You have 2,000 labelled support tickets and 50M unlabelled ones. Labelling costs £1.20 each and you have £3,600. Your current model is a bag-of-words classifier (word counts fed into logistic regression) at 71% macro-F1 — an accuracy-like score between 0 and 100% that weights every class equally, covered in ml7. Spend the budget.

Answer

Do not spend it on 3,000 random labels. Two cheaper moves come first.

1. Replace the representation, free. Swap the word counts for a pretrained sentence-embedding model and refit the same logistic regression on top. On text classification this commonly adds several points of F1 at zero labelling cost, because the embedding already encodes what your 2,000 labels were being spent learning (that "refund" and "money back" mean the same thing).

2. Check label quality. Have a second person label 200 of the existing tickets. If they agree only 80% of the time, clearer guidelines and relabelling the confusing categories will raise the ceiling more than new labels would.

3. Then spend the budget on the right 3,000. Uncertainty sampling — label the tickets where the model's top-two class probabilities are closest — usually reaches a target score with substantially fewer labels than random sampling. Batch it: label 500, retrain, re-score, repeat, because labels chosen against the first model are wasted once the model improves.

Keep a random slice. Reserve about 400 labels for a randomly sampled, never-trained-on test set. An actively sampled set is biased by construction — it over-represents hard cases — and cannot honestly measure performance.
