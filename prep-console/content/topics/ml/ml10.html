A lender has a spreadsheet of past loans: income, debt, age of credit history, number of late payments, employment type, region, loan amount — and whether each loan was repaid. It wants a model that predicts default for new applicants. The data is tabular: rows of mixed columns, some numeric and skewed, some categorical, some missing, with effects that switch on at thresholds ("more than two late payments") and interact ("high debt matters more for short credit histories"). A linear model (ml6) can capture these only with a lot of hand-crafted features. A neural network could learn them but is fiddly to tune on data like this and often loses.

The models that dominate this kind of data are built from decision trees: flowcharts of yes/no questions learned from data. A single tree is easy to read but unreliable — change a few training rows and it can change completely. The breakthrough was combining many trees. Random forests average hundreds of deliberately different trees to cancel out their individual errors. Gradient boosting adds small trees one at a time, each fixing what the previous ones still get wrong. Gradient-boosted trees, through libraries such as XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost, are the default winning approach for tabular problems in industry and on Kaggle, and a benchmark across 45 datasets in 2022 still found them ahead of deep learning on medium-sized tabular data.

These models also produce some of the most instructive stories in applied machine learning. The Netflix Prize, a million-dollar contest to improve Netflix's rating predictions by 10%, was won by a team that blended hundreds of models — and then Netflix chose not to deploy the winning solution, because the extra accuracy wasn't worth the engineering cost.

This lesson builds from a single split to a production ensemble: how a tree partitions the feature space; one split's Gini gain and entropy gain computed by hand; why trees overfit and how depth and pruning restrain them; bagging and random forests with the variance formula from ml2 and the out-of-bag trick; gradient boosting worked round by round on four houses; what XGBoost's regularised objective adds (with the leaf-weight formula computed); LightGBM and CatBoost; hyperparameters; feature-importance traps; and the Netflix Prize.

A decision tree is a flowchart learned from data

A tree asks a sequence of yes/no questions about the features and ends at a prediction: "Late payments > 2? → yes → Debt-to-income > 0.4? → yes → predict high risk." Each question tests one feature against one threshold; each end point, a leaf, holds a prediction — the majority class (or class proportions) of the training examples that reached it, or their mean value for regression.

The analogy is the game of Twenty Questions: a good player asks first the question that splits the possibilities most evenly and informatively, then continues within the answer. A tree learner does the same, greedily: at each step it picks the question that best separates the training examples, then repeats inside each branch. The analogy breaks in one important way. A Twenty Questions player keeps asking until certain; a tree that keeps splitting until every leaf is pure ends up with a question for almost every training example and has memorised the data — the overfitting problem that ensembles exist to solve.

What a tree does to the feature space

Every split cuts the space with a line perpendicular to one feature's axis. A tree is therefore a way of carving the feature space into rectangles (boxes, in more dimensions), each with its own prediction.

Why trees are attractive, and their weakness

Trees need no feature scaling (a split on "income > 40,000" works the same in pounds or pence), handle numeric and categorical features together, capture thresholds and interactions naturally (a split inside a branch is an interaction), and can be read directly when shallow. Their weakness is variance (ml2): deep trees fit noise, and a small change in the data can change the first split and everything below it.

A tree also makes a particular kind of prediction: one constant per rectangle. Its predictions are step functions, flat within each region and jumping at the boundaries. With enough leaves the steps approximate any shape, including curves and interactions, which is why trees are so flexible — but a single tree's steps sit exactly where its training data happened to put them, which is part of why it overfits and why averaging many trees (whose steps fall in slightly different places) produces a smoother, more reliable function.

Two ways to combine trees

  • Random forest: grow hundreds of deep trees, each on a random resample of the rows and considering a random subset of features at each split, and average their predictions. Each tree overfits in its own way; averaging cancels much of it.
  • Gradient boosting: build small trees one after another; each new tree predicts the errors the current ensemble still makes, and is added with a small weight (the learning rate). Hundreds of small corrections add up to a very accurate model.

1. Measuring how mixed a node is

To choose splits, we need a number for how "impure" a set of examples is. For classification with class proportions pₖ in a node:

Gini impurity  = 1 − Σₖ pₖ²          (0 for a pure node; 0.5 for a 50/50 binary node)
Entropy        = −Σₖ pₖ log₂ pₖ      (0 for a pure node; 1 bit for a 50/50 binary node)

Gini has a plain meaning: the probability that two examples drawn at random from the node belong to different classes. For regression, impurity is the variance of the target in the node (equivalently, the mean squared error of predicting the node's mean). A split's quality is the impurity decrease: the parent's impurity minus the children's impurities weighted by their share of the examples. With entropy this decrease is called information gain.

2. One split's Gini gain, by hand

A node contains 100 loans: 50 good, 50 bad. Its Gini impurity is 1 − (0.5² + 0.5²) = 0.50. Compare two candidate splits.

Split A, "late payments > 2": left child 50 loans (40 good, 10 bad), right child 50 loans (10 good, 40 bad).

  • Left Gini: 1 − (0.8² + 0.2²) = 1 − (0.64 + 0.04) = 0.32. Right Gini: the same, 0.32.
  • Weighted: 0.5 × 0.32 + 0.5 × 0.32 = 0.32. Gain = 0.50 − 0.32 = 0.18.

Split B, "loan amount > £50k": left child 80 loans (50 good, 30 bad), right child 20 loans (0 good, 20 bad).

  • Left Gini: 1 − (0.625² + 0.375²) = 1 − (0.390625 + 0.140625) = 0.469. Right Gini: 0 (pure).
  • Weighted: 0.8 × 0.469 + 0.2 × 0 = 0.375. Gain = 0.50 − 0.375 = 0.125.

Split A wins even though split B creates a perfectly pure child, because B's pure child is small and its other child is still very mixed. With entropy the ranking is the same: split A's children each have entropy −(0.8 log₂ 0.8 + 0.2 log₂ 0.2) = 0.722 bits, a gain of 1 − 0.722 = 0.278 bits. The learner evaluates every feature and, for numeric features, every threshold between consecutive sorted values, and takes the best — a greedy choice, since it doesn't look ahead to what later splits could achieve.

For regression the same logic applies with variance. Four houses priced 100, 200, 300 and 400 (thousands) have variance 12,500 around their mean of 250. Splitting them {100, 200} versus {300, 400} leaves each child with variance 2,500, a weighted decrease of 10,000 — the best possible split here.

3. Growing, stopping and pruning

The tree repeats the search in each child until a stopping rule: a maximum depth, a minimum number of examples per leaf (say 20), a minimum impurity decrease, or pure leaves. Unrestricted, it grows until leaves are pure, reaching zero training error on any data without exact duplicates — pure memorisation. The main controls are therefore capacity controls (ml2): max depth (a depth-d binary tree has at most 2^d leaves), min samples per leaf, and pruning — growing a large tree, then cutting back branches whose improvement doesn't justify their complexity, judged on validation data or by a penalty per leaf (cost-complexity pruning).

Even a well-pruned single tree is unstable: the top split is chosen from near-ties among features, and a different choice there changes everything below. That instability — high variance — is exactly what averaging fixes.

4. Bagging and random forests: cutting variance

Bagging (bootstrap aggregating) trains each tree on a bootstrap sample — n rows drawn with replacement from the n training rows — and averages the trees' predictions (or takes a majority vote). A bootstrap sample contains on average 1 − (1 − 1/n)ⁿ ≈ 1 − 1/e ≈ 63.2% of the distinct rows, some repeated, so each tree sees a different version of the data and overfits differently.

How much does averaging help? From ml2, averaging B models each with variance σ² and pairwise correlation ρ gives variance ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ²/B. Worked: deep trees with σ² = 1.0 and correlation 0.6 — bagged trees trained on similar data are quite correlated. With 500 trees: 0.6 + 0.4/500 ≈ 0.60. The second term is gone, but the first remains; adding trees can't push variance below ρσ². A random forest attacks ρ: at each split, only a random subset of features is considered (√d for classification, d/3 for regression are the classic defaults, for d features). If one feature is very strong, most bagged trees would split on it first and make similar mistakes; feature sampling forces variety. Suppose it drops ρ to 0.3 while raising each tree's variance to 1.2: 0.3 × 1.2 + 0.7 × 1.2/500 ≈ 0.36. Each tree is slightly worse, the forest is clearly better.

Two practical consequences. Adding trees never hurts accuracy — the variance of the average only falls — so the number of trees is set by compute, not tuned against overfitting. And every tree has rows it didn't see (about 36.8%), so for each row you can average the predictions of just the trees that omitted it: the out-of-bag (OOB) estimate, a validation score with no separate holdout — valid when rows are independent (ml9).

5. Gradient boosting: cutting bias, round by round

Boosting takes the opposite approach. Instead of many independent deep trees, it builds a sequence of shallow trees (depth 3–8), each fitted to what the ensemble so far still gets wrong, and adds them with a small learning rate η (shrinkage):

F₀(x) = constant (the mean for squared error)
for m = 1 … M:
    rᵢ = −∂L(yᵢ, F)/∂F at F_{m−1}(xᵢ)       ← negative gradient; for squared error, the residual yᵢ − F_{m−1}(xᵢ)
    fit a small tree hₘ to the rᵢ
    F_m(x) = F_{m−1}(x) + η · hₘ(x)

This is gradient descent (ml4), but in the space of functions: each tree is a step in the direction that most reduces the loss, and the learning rate is the step size.

Worked, four houses. Prices 100, 200, 300, 400 (thousands) for houses 1–4, sorted by size. η = 0.1, trees are stumps (one split).

  1. F₀ = mean = 250 for every house. Residuals: −150, −50, +50, +150. MSE = (22,500 + 2,500 + 2,500 + 22,500)/4 = 12,500.
  2. Tree 1 splits {1, 2} from {3, 4} (the variance-reducing split of section 2) and predicts each side's mean residual: −100 and +100. Update: 250 ∓ 0.1 × 100 → 240, 240, 260, 260. Residuals −140, −40, +40, +140; MSE = (19,600 + 1,600 + 1,600 + 19,600)/4 = 10,600.
  3. Tree 2 fits the new residuals with the same split: means −90 and +90. Update → 231, 231, 269, 269. Residuals −131, −31, +31, +131; MSE = 9,061.

Each round removes 10% of the explainable part of the error along that split. A later tree will eventually split {1} from {2} to fix the within-pair residuals. The small learning rate means it takes many trees — which sounds wasteful, but many small steps generalise better than a few large ones, because no single tree's quirks dominate. Since boosting keeps reducing training error, it does overfit if run too long: the number of trees is chosen by early stopping — stop when validation error hasn't improved for, say, 50 rounds.

For classification, the ensemble predicts log-odds (as logistic regression does, ml6) and each tree fits the negative gradient of log loss, y − p (ml5).

6. XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost

XGBoost (Chen and Guestrin, 2016) made boosting fast and regularised. It approximates the loss around the current predictions using each row's gradient g and second derivative (Hessian) h, and penalises the number of leaves (γ per leaf) and the size of leaf values (λ, an L2 penalty as in ml3). For a leaf containing rows with gradient sum G and Hessian sum H, the best leaf value and a split's gain are:

w* = −G / (H + λ)
gain = ½ [ G_L²/(H_L + λ) + G_R²/(H_R + λ) − (G_L + G_R)²/(H_L + H_R + λ) ] − γ

For squared error written as ½(y − ŷ)², g = ŷ − y and h = 1. At F₀ = 250 in the house example, g = +150, +50, −50, −150. The left leaf {1, 2} has G = 200, H = 2; with λ = 1, w* = −200/3 ≈ −66.7 — the mean residual (−100) shrunk towards zero by λ. The split's gain is ½ [200²/3 + 200²/3 − 0²/5] = ½ × 26,667 ≈ 13,333, minus γ; if γ exceeded that, the split would be pruned. The same formulas work for any differentiable loss, which is how one library handles regression, classification, ranking and custom objectives.

LightGBM (Microsoft) is built for speed on large data: it bins each feature into histograms (typically up to 255 bins), so split finding scans bins rather than sorted values; grows trees leaf-wise (always splitting the leaf with the largest gain, so trees are deep and unbalanced where the data warrants, controlled by num_leaves); and adds sampling tricks that keep rows with large gradients. CatBoost (Yandex) focuses on categorical features: it encodes each category with ordered target statistics — computed from rows earlier in a random permutation, so a row's own label never leaks into its encoding (ml9) — and uses ordered boosting to avoid the related shift in residuals. All three handle missing values by learning, at each split, which direction missing values should go.

Row subsampling (each tree sees, say, 80% of rows) and column subsampling add randomness to boosting — borrowing the forest's decorrelation idea — and usually improve generalisation.

8. Feature importance and its traps

  • Impurity-based importance (the default) sums the impurity decrease of every split on a feature. It's computed on training data and is biased towards continuous and high-cardinality features, which offer many split points — a random ID column can rank highly.
  • Permutation importance shuffles one feature in held-out data and measures how much the score drops. More trustworthy, but correlated features share and hide importance: shuffle one of two copies and the model uses the other.
  • SHAP values attribute each prediction to features with a consistent method from game theory; TreeSHAP computes them exactly and quickly for tree ensembles. They're the standard for explaining individual decisions.

None of these is causal: a feature can be important because it's a symptom of the outcome, or a proxy for something else. A practical sanity check is to add a column of random noise before training: any feature ranked below it has no demonstrable value, and if the noise column ranks highly under impurity importance, that ranking method is being fooled.

Trees can't extrapolate

A tree predicts a constant in each leaf, the average of training targets there, so a tree ensemble's predictions are bounded by the range of training targets. If house prices rise 5% a year and the model is trained on past years, it will under-predict every new record price; a demand model will flatten any growing trend. Remedies: model the trend separately (detrend, or predict ratios or growth), add a linear component (some libraries offer linear leaves), or predict a transformed target such as log-price relative to a regional index.

Monotonic and interaction constraints

Regulated domains often require that risk never decreases as debt increases, all else equal. XGBoost and LightGBM support monotonic constraints per feature, restricting splits so predictions move in one direction along that feature, at a small cost in accuracy and a large gain in plausibility and auditability. Interaction constraints limit which features may appear together in one tree, making models easier to explain.

Calibration of tree ensembles

Random forests average votes or leaf proportions, which pulls predictions away from 0 and 1: a case that is certainly positive still gets, say, 0.9 because some trees trained without similar examples disagree. Boosted models trained with log loss are often reasonably calibrated but can become over-confident with many rounds. If probabilities are used, check calibration on held-out data and correct it with Platt scaling or isotonic regression (ml15).

Histogram tricks and scale

Exact split finding sorts each feature at each node, O(n log n) per feature. Histogram-based methods bin each feature once into up to a few hundred bins and accumulate gradient statistics per bin, so each split search is O(bins); a child's histogram can be computed by subtracting its sibling's from the parent's, halving the work. LightGBM adds gradient-based one-side sampling (keep all rows with large gradients, sample the rest and reweight) and exclusive feature bundling (merge sparse features that are rarely non-zero together). These are why boosting scales to hundreds of millions of rows on a single machine, and GPU implementations go further.

Serving cost

A model with 2,000 trees of depth 8 performs 16,000 comparisons per prediction — fast for one request, significant at millions per second or on small devices. Options: fewer, shallower trees at a small accuracy cost; compiling the ensemble into native code or optimised formats (Treelite, ONNX, GPU inference libraries); or distilling it into a smaller model. Facebook's ad-click paper (ml6) reported that almost all the accuracy gain came from the first 500 of 2,000 trees — a common pattern worth measuring before deploying thousands.

Stacking and blending

Ensembles of different model families — boosted trees, linear models, neural networks, nearest neighbours — make different errors, so their correlation ρ is lower than that of similar models, and a second-level model trained on their out-of-fold predictions (stacking) can beat each one. The out-of-fold part is essential: training the combiner on in-sample predictions leaks (ml9). The Netflix Prize is the canonical example of blending; its cost in complexity is the canonical warning.

Categorical features with many levels

One-hot encoding a feature with 10,000 levels (postcodes, merchant IDs) creates 10,000 sparse columns, each useful for only a few rows; trees split on them inefficiently and memorise rare levels. Alternatives are out-of-fold target encoding with smoothing (ml9), frequency encoding, grouping rare levels into "other", native categorical splits (LightGBM groups categories by their gradient statistics and splits the sorted list; CatBoost uses ordered statistics), or learned embeddings from a neural model used as features. Whatever the choice, it must be frozen and shipped with the model, since a different encoding at serving time sends rows down the wrong branches.

When neural networks win on tables

Grinsztajn et al. identify why neural networks struggle on typical tabular data: they're not robust to uninformative features, they don't preserve the data's orientation (rotating the feature space is harmless for a network but destroys meaningful axes that trees exploit), and they're biased towards smooth functions while tabular targets are often irregular. Networks win with very large datasets, when inputs include text, images or high-cardinality IDs whose embeddings should be learned, and in multi-task settings. Pretrained tabular models that learn from many datasets and predict in context on small new ones are an active research area and competitive on small data.

The public case: the Netflix Prize

In October 2006 Netflix offered $1 million to anyone who could improve the accuracy of its film-rating predictions by 10%. Contestants got about 100 million ratings and were scored by root mean squared error (RMSE) on hidden ratings; Netflix's own system, Cinematch, scored 0.9525 on the test set, so winning meant reaching 0.8572 or better. In 2007 team KorBell won the first Progress Prize with an 8.43% improvement; Netflix later wrote that the team reported more than 2,000 hours of work to produce a final combination of 107 algorithms. On 21 September 2009, BellKor's Pragmatic Chaos was awarded the Grand Prize with a test RMSE of 0.8567, a 10.06% improvement. The Ensemble, a rival coalition, matched the score exactly — and lost because BellKor had submitted 20 minutes earlier.

The winning entries were ensembles in the fullest sense: hundreds of predictors — matrix factorisation models, neighbourhood methods, restricted Boltzmann machines, and many variants — blended together, with gradient-boosted decision trees among the tools used to combine them. The whole contest was a live demonstration of this lesson's variance argument: diverse models with partly independent errors, combined carefully, beat any single model.

The second half of the story is the one interviewers value. Netflix did not deploy the Grand Prize solution. In its 2012 engineering blog post "Netflix Recommendations: Beyond the 5 stars", the company explained that it had put algorithms from the Progress Prize work into production, but that the additional accuracy gains it measured for the Grand Prize solution did not justify the engineering effort needed to bring them into a production environment. The contest dataset had 100 million ratings while the production system had over 5 billion; and the business had shifted from DVD rental to streaming, changing both the data collected and what "good recommendations" meant. A 10% gain in rating RMSE had become less relevant than ranking what to show on a homepage.

Lessons for production: measure the marginal value of accuracy against its cost in latency, compute, maintenance and complexity; make sure the offline metric still matches the product; and prefer the simplest ensemble that captures most of the gain.

Failure modes with tree ensembles

Leaky high-cardinality features. IDs, timestamps and target-encoded categories give trees easy ways to memorise; importance rankings dominated by such features are a warning sign (ml9).

Silent extrapolation failures. Predictions flat-line when inputs or targets move outside the training range — prices, volumes, new product sizes.

Training/serving mismatch in categorical handling. A new category at serving time, or a different encoding order, sends rows down the wrong branches. Freeze encoders with the model (ml14).

Too many trees at inference. Latency budgets exceeded by ensembles whose last thousand trees add almost nothing; measure accuracy against tree count before deploying.

What to monitor

Prediction distribution and calibration over time, input feature distributions (especially the top-importance features), the fraction of inputs outside training ranges, and — for boosted models retrained regularly — the best iteration chosen by early stopping; a sudden change often signals a data problem.

Goal: compute a Gini split by brute force, then build gradient boosting from scratch with stumps and compare it to a single deep tree. NumPy only; written for Python 3.12.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

gini = lambda y: 1 - sum(np.mean(y == c) ** 2 for c in np.unique(y)) if len(y) else 0.0
def best_split(x, y):                        # try every threshold on one feature
    best = (0.0, None)
    for t in np.unique(x)[:-1]:
        L, R = y[x <= t], y[x > t]
        gain = gini(y) - (len(L) * gini(L) + len(R) * gini(R)) / len(y)
        best = max(best, (gain, t), key=lambda b: b[0])
    return best

x = rng.normal(size=200); y = (x + 0.5 * rng.normal(size=200) > 0).astype(int)
print("best Gini gain, threshold:", best_split(x, y))

def stump(x, r):                             # regression stump on residuals r
    best = (np.inf, 0, 0, 0)
    for t in np.unique(x)[:-1]:
        l, rr = r[x <= t], r[x > t]
        sse = ((l - l.mean())**2).sum() + ((rr - rr.mean())**2).sum()
        if sse < best[0]: best = (sse, t, l.mean(), rr.mean())
    _, t, a, b = best
    return lambda z: np.where(z <= t, a, b)

X = np.sort(rng.uniform(0, 10, 300)); Y = np.sin(X) + 0.3 * rng.normal(size=300)
tr, va = np.arange(300) % 4 != 0, np.arange(300) % 4 == 0
F, lr = np.full(tr.sum(), Y[tr].mean()), 0.1
Fv = np.full(va.sum(), Y[tr].mean())
for m in range(1, 301):
    h = stump(X[tr], Y[tr] - F)
    F += lr * h(X[tr]); Fv += lr * h(X[va])
    if m in (1, 10, 50, 100, 300):
        print(f"round {m:3d}: train MSE {np.mean((Y[tr]-F)**2):.3f}  val MSE {np.mean((Y[va]-Fv)**2):.3f}")

What to look for. The best split lands near x = 0 with a large Gini gain. Boosting's training error falls steadily; validation error falls, then flattens near the noise level (0.09) and eventually creeps up — the point where early stopping would stop. Try lr = 1.0: training error drops faster and validation error is worse.

Work it out

A random forest has 500 trees, each 80% accurate on a binary task. If their errors were independent, how accurate would the majority vote be? Then explain why real forests are nowhere near that, and what the forest does about it.

Answer

The vote is wrong only if 251 or more trees are wrong. With the number of correct trees X ~ Binomial(500, 0.8), mean 400 and standard deviation √(500 × 0.8 × 0.2) = 8.94, the failure threshold of 250 correct sits 16.8 standard deviations below the mean. The failure probability is vanishingly small — effectively 100% accuracy.

Real forests land far lower, because the independence assumption is false. Trees trained on bootstrap samples of the same data, with the same dominant features available, make highly correlated mistakes: the hard examples are hard for all of them. Averaging B models with pairwise correlation ρ leaves variance ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ²/B; as B → ∞ the second term vanishes but ρσ² never does.

That residual term is the whole reason a random forest samples a random subset of features at every split: it's not a speed optimisation, it's a deliberate attack on ρ. It's also why adding trees past a few hundred stops helping, while decorrelating them further — or blending in different model families with different errors — still does.
