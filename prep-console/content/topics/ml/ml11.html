A support team wants to route incoming tickets automatically. They have 20,000 past tickets, each labelled with the team that resolved it. The simplest possible approach: when a new ticket arrives, find the past tickets most similar to it and send it where they went. No model to train, no parameters to fit — just a notion of "similar" and a search. This is k-nearest neighbours (k-NN), the oldest idea in machine learning, and it is also, with a modern twist, how retrieval works in almost every large-language-model application: convert text into an embedding vector, find the nearest stored vectors, and use what's found.

Two questions decide whether it works. First, what does "similar" mean? k-NN is only as good as its distance function, and naive distances are easily dominated by whichever feature has the biggest numbers. Second, can "nearest" still mean anything when data has hundreds or thousands of dimensions? The curse of dimensionality says that in high-dimensional space everything is far from everything, neighbourhoods stop being local, and the nearest point is barely nearer than a random one. Taken at face value, that would make vector databases useless. They aren't — and understanding why is a favourite interview question for AI engineers.

There is also a practical question of scale. Comparing a query with every stored vector is fine for 20,000 tickets and impossible for a billion product images at millisecond latency. The answer is approximate nearest-neighbour search: index structures that find probable neighbours far faster, trading a little recall for orders of magnitude in speed and memory. This lesson's public case, the FAISS library from Facebook AI Research, showed nearest-neighbour search at the scale of a billion vectors on a handful of GPUs.

This lesson covers the algorithm and a worked example where scaling flips the answer; distance and similarity measures, including why cosine and Euclidean agree on normalised vectors; choosing k as a bias–variance dial; the curse of dimensionality with concrete numbers; why embeddings escape it; exact and approximate indexes (KD-trees, IVF, product quantisation, HNSW) with memory arithmetic; measuring recall; and the theory result that 1-NN is asymptotically within a factor of two of the best possible classifier.

Predict like your neighbours

To predict for a new point, k-NN finds the k training points closest to it and takes a majority vote among their labels (classification) or averages their values (regression). There's no training step beyond storing the data; all the work happens at prediction time, which is why it's called a lazy or instance-based learner.

The everyday analogy is estimating a house price from comparable sales: an estate agent looks at the few most similar houses sold recently nearby and averages their prices. The quality of the estimate depends on two things — what counts as "similar" (size? street? condition?) and how many comparables to use. Too few and one odd sale skews the estimate; too many and you're averaging in houses that aren't comparable at all. k-NN makes exactly these two choices: a distance function and k. The analogy breaks when there are hundreds of features: an estate agent comparing on three attributes can find close matches, but with three hundred attributes no house is close to any other on all of them — the curse of dimensionality.

Distance

  • Euclidean distance: straight-line distance, √Σ(aᵢ − bᵢ)².
  • Manhattan distance: Σ|aᵢ − bᵢ|, distance along a grid, less dominated by one large difference.
  • Cosine similarity: a·b / (‖a‖‖b‖), the cosine of the angle between two vectors, ignoring their lengths — the standard for text embeddings. Worked: a = (1, 2, 2) and b = (2, 1, 2) have dot product 2 + 2 + 4 = 8 and lengths 3 and 3, so cosine similarity = 8/9 ≈ 0.889.

A worked example where scaling changes the answer

Unscaled, weight differences (2 to 10 grams) swamp colour differences (at most 0.4), so the nearest fruit is orange 3, which weighs almost the same but has a completely different colour: 1-NN says orange. Rescale each feature by its typical spread — weight in units of 10 g, colour in units of 0.1 — so the new fruit is (15, 7), apple 1 is (15.5, 7.5) at distance √(0.25 + 0.25) = 0.71, and orange 3 is (15.2, 3) at distance √(0.04 + 16) = 4.00. Now the two apples are nearest and 1-NN says apple. With k = 3 both versions happen to vote apple (2 to 1), but the unscaled one does so for the wrong reasons. In practice you standardise every feature to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 (ml3), or weight features deliberately.

Choosing k

k = 1 follows the training data exactly: noisy, high variance (one mislabelled neighbour decides the answer). Large k smooths predictions: lower variance, but more bias, blurring genuine boundaries and drifting towards the overall majority. Choose k by cross-validation (ml9); odd k avoids ties in binary problems.

1. k as a bias–variance dial

k-NN's capacity is controlled by k. With k = 1 the decision boundary bends around every training point: training error is zero (each point is its own nearest neighbour) and the model has maximal variance. With k = n every query gets the same prediction, the overall majority class — maximal bias. A useful rough measure is that k-NN behaves like a model with about n/k parameters. In between, validation error traces the U-curve of ml2 (reversed along the k axis). Two refinements are common: distance weighting, where each neighbour's vote is weighted by 1/distance so closer points count more, and radius neighbours, which use all points within a fixed distance rather than a fixed count, useful when data density varies.

For regression, k-NN averages neighbours' targets, producing a step-like function that follows local trends — and, like trees (ml10), it can't extrapolate beyond the range of the training targets.

2. Choosing the distance

The distance is where domain knowledge enters. Beyond Euclidean and Manhattan:

  • Cosine similarity for embeddings and text vectors, where direction matters and length often reflects something irrelevant (document length, word frequency).
  • Dot product, which some embedding models are trained with; it also rewards vector length, which can encode popularity or confidence.
  • Hamming distance (count of differing positions) for binary codes, and Jaccard distance for sets (one minus the overlap divided by the union) — for example, sets of products bought.
  • Learned metrics: embedding models trained so that distance reflects the task, such as contrastive models (ml1) where matching pairs are pulled together.

For vectors normalised to length 1 the three standard measures agree on ranking: ‖a − b‖² = ‖a‖² + ‖b‖² − 2a·b = 2 − 2·cos(a, b). With the example above normalised, cos = 0.889 gives ‖a − b‖² = 2 − 1.778 = 0.222. So normalise once, and you can use the fastest inner-product search while getting cosine rankings. If the embedding model was trained with dot product and its vector lengths carry meaning, don't normalise — use the similarity the model was trained with.

3. The cost of exact search

Brute-force k-NN compares the query with every stored vector: n × d multiply-adds per query. For 20,000 tickets with 768-dimensional embeddings that's about 15 million operations — a millisecond. For 100 million vectors with 1,536 dimensions it's 150 billion operations per query, plus 614 GB of memory just to hold the vectors in 32-bit floats (1,536 × 4 bytes = 6 KB each). That's why large systems need indexes. In low dimensions, exact spatial indexes such as KD-trees and ball trees prune the search by splitting space into regions and skipping regions that can't contain a closer point. Above roughly 20 dimensions the pruning stops working — every region is close enough to the query to need checking — and they degrade to brute force. The reason is the subject of the next section.

4. The curse of dimensionality, with numbers

Three effects, each with arithmetic you can do in your head.

Neighbourhoods stop being local. Spread points uniformly in a unit hypercube and ask for a cube-shaped neighbourhood that captures 10% of them. Its volume must be 0.1, so each side must span 0.1^(1/d) of the axis:

Dimensions d1210100
Side length for 10% of data0.100.320.790.98

In 10 dimensions, a neighbourhood holding just 10% of the data already spans 79% of the range of every feature. It isn't a neighbourhood any more; the "nearest" points differ from the query in almost every feature.

Almost everything is near the edge. The fraction of a unit cube within 0.05 of a face is 1 − 0.9^d: 19% in 2 dimensions, 65% in 10, 99.997% in 100. Nearly every point is near a boundary, so predicting at almost any point is extrapolation rather than interpolation between surrounding examples.

Distances concentrate. For two points drawn uniformly in [0, 1]^d, each coordinate contributes an independent squared difference with mean 1/6 and variance 7/180. The squared distance therefore has mean d/6 and standard deviation √(7d/180), so its relative spread is √(7d/180) / (d/6) = √(1.4/d):

At d = 1,000 almost all squared distances lie within a few per cent of each other. Beyer et al. proved the general version: under broad conditions, as dimension grows the ratio of the farthest to the nearest distance from a query tends to 1. For k-NN this means the neighbours are barely more similar to the query than random points; for exact indexes it means no region can be skipped. And a data-efficiency corollary: to keep the same density of examples as you add dimensions, the number of examples must grow exponentially.

5. Why embeddings escape (mostly)

The curse describes data spread evenly across all dimensions. Real data isn't. Images of faces occupy a tiny, structured region of all possible pixel arrays; text embeddings from a trained model lie near a structure whose intrinsic dimension — the number of independent directions the data really varies along — is far smaller than the 768 or 1,536 coordinates used to store it. Within that structure, distances remain informative: genuine neighbours (paraphrases, related documents) are much closer than unrelated items. That's why retrieval over embeddings works, and why a good embedding model matters more than the index: it is the embedding that makes "near" mean "similar".

The curse still applies in two places. Exact spatial indexes still fail, because the ambient dimension is high. And data needs still grow with intrinsic dimension, which is why k-NN on raw high-dimensional features (thousands of raw pixels or word counts) performs poorly, while k-NN on learned embeddings is a strong baseline.

6. Approximate nearest-neighbour indexes

Approximate indexes accept that they will sometimes miss a true neighbour in exchange for enormous speed and memory savings. The three building blocks:

IVF (inverted file index). Run k-means (ml12) on the vectors to get, say, 65,536 centroids, and assign each vector to its nearest centroid's list. At query time, find the nprobe centroids nearest the query and search only their lists. With nprobe = 32 you scan about 32/65,536 = 0.05% of the data. A true neighbour sitting just across a cluster boundary can be missed; raising nprobe raises recall and cost.

Product quantisation (PQ). Split each 1,536-dimensional vector into 96 sub-vectors of 16 dimensions, learn a codebook of 256 centroids for each sub-space, and store each sub-vector as the one-byte index of its nearest centroid. Each vector shrinks from 6,144 bytes to 96 bytes — a 64× compression — so 100 million vectors need about 9.6 GB instead of 614 GB. Distances are approximated from the codes using precomputed tables, quickly. The compression loses precision, so systems usually re-rank the top few hundred candidates with full vectors. IVF-PQ combines both ideas.

HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World). Build a graph where each vector links to some of its near neighbours (M links, for example 16–32), arranged in layers: the top layer has few nodes with long-range links, lower layers are denser. Search starts at the top, greedily moves to whichever neighbour is closest to the query, drops a layer, and repeats, keeping a candidate list of size efSearch at the bottom. It gives excellent recall at low latency and is the default in most vector databases, at the cost of memory (full vectors plus links) and awkward deletions.

7. Measuring an index: recall, latency, memory

An approximate index is only as good as its measured recall at production settings. Recall@k is the fraction of the true k nearest neighbours the index returns: if brute force says the true top-10 is a set S and the index returns 10 items of which 9 are in S, recall@10 = 0.9. Measure it on a sample of real queries (not synthetic ones), together with latency percentiles (p50, p95, p99), memory and throughput, and sweep the knobs — nprobe for IVF, efSearch for HNSW — to find the cheapest setting that meets the recall target. "We use HNSW" is not a quality statement; "HNSW with efSearch 128 gives recall@10 of 0.96 at 8 ms p95 and 40 GB" is.

8. k-NN beyond classification: regression and anomaly scores

For regression, k-NN predicts the average target of the k neighbours. Worked: a flat of 70 m² whose three nearest comparable flats rented for £1,500, £1,600 and £1,650 gets a prediction of £1,583; with distance weighting, if those flats are 2, 5 and 10 m² away, weights 1/2, 1/5 and 1/10 (0.5, 0.2, 0.1) give (0.5 × 1,500 + 0.2 × 1,600 + 0.1 × 1,650) / 0.8 = (750 + 320 + 165) / 0.8 ≈ £1,544 — pulled towards the most similar flat. For anomaly detection, the distance from a point to its k-th nearest neighbour among normal examples is a simple and effective anomaly score: a transaction far from every normal transaction scores high. Both inherit everything above: they need scaled features, they suffer in high raw dimensions, and they work well on good embeddings.

9. Sizing a retrieval system: a worked plan

A company wants semantic search over 10 million documents, split into 30 million passages, embedded with a 768-dimensional model, answering 200 queries per second with a p95 latency of 50 ms.

  • Memory: 30 million × 768 × 4 bytes ≈ 92 GB of raw vectors. HNSW adds graph links (with M = 16, roughly 32 neighbour ids per node at the bottom layer, about 128 bytes), so around 96 GB — feasible on one large machine, or split across a few shards. IVF-PQ with 64-byte codes would need about 2 GB, at some recall cost.
  • Exact search as a baseline: 30 million × 768 ≈ 23 billion multiply-adds per query — tens of milliseconds on a GPU, too slow on a CPU at 200 queries per second, but perfect for measuring recall offline on a sample.
  • Tuning: sweep efSearch (HNSW) or nprobe (IVF) on 1,000 real queries, pick the cheapest setting with recall@10 ≥ 0.95, and confirm p95 latency under load.
  • End to end: check that retrieval recall translates into answer quality for the downstream application, and plan re-indexing for when the embedding model changes.

The Cover–Hart bound

Cover and Hart (1967) proved that as the number of training examples goes to infinity, the error rate of the 1-nearest-neighbour classifier is at most twice the Bayes error — the lowest error any classifier could achieve on the problem (for two classes, the bound is 2R*(1 − R*), where R* is the Bayes error). So a method with no training and no parameters is asymptotically within a factor of two of optimal, and k-NN with k growing suitably with n is asymptotically optimal. The catch is the rate of convergence, which slows exponentially with dimension. k-NN's weakness is data efficiency, not what it could achieve in the limit.

Hubness

In high dimensions, the distribution of "how many other points list this point among their k nearest neighbours" becomes very skewed. A few points — typically those near the centre of the data — become hubs that appear in many neighbour lists, while others (anti-hubs) appear in none. In retrieval this means some documents are returned for many unrelated queries and others are almost never found. Mitigations include centring embeddings (subtracting the mean vector), local scaling of similarities such as CSLS (cross-domain similarity local scaling, which subtracts each point's average similarity to its own neighbours), and mutual-proximity measures. Counting how often each item appears in others' top-k lists is a simple diagnostic.

Estimating intrinsic dimension

Before assuming the curse applies, estimate how many dimensions the data really uses. The simplest estimate is from PCA (ml13): how many components explain, say, 95% of the variance. Nonlinear estimators (for example, maximum-likelihood estimators based on distances to the nearest few neighbours) handle curved structures. Text embeddings stored in 768 or more dimensions often have intrinsic dimensions in the tens, which explains why they can be truncated or compressed heavily with modest quality loss — and why some embedding models are trained so that their first coordinates carry most of the information (Matryoshka-style embeddings).

Filters, updates and real vector databases

Production vector search rarely asks for "the nearest neighbours" alone; it asks for "the nearest neighbours that belong to this customer, in this language, published after this date". Filtering after the search can leave too few results; filtering during graph traversal changes the graph's connectivity and can hurt recall; partitioning the index by a common filter field avoids both at a cost in flexibility. Updates matter too: HNSW handles inserts well, but deletions leave tombstones that degrade the graph until it's rebuilt; IVF centroids trained on old data become unbalanced as the distribution drifts and need periodic retraining. These operational details decide real-world recall as much as the index type does.

Choosing the similarity to match the model

Embedding models are trained with a specific similarity — often cosine on normalised outputs, sometimes dot product. Using a different one at search time silently changes the ranking. When a model's vector lengths carry information (popularity, confidence), normalising discards it; when they don't, not normalising lets irrelevant length differences affect ranking. The rule is simple: search with the similarity the model was trained with, and if unsure, check recall of known related pairs under each option.

Locality-sensitive hashing

An older family of approximate methods, locality-sensitive hashing (LSH), uses hash functions designed so that similar vectors are likely to collide. For cosine similarity, the classic choice is random hyperplanes: each bit of the hash records which side of a random hyperplane the vector falls on, and two vectors at angle θ agree on each bit with probability 1 − θ/π. Vectors are stored in buckets by hash, and a query searches only its own bucket (using several hash tables to improve recall). LSH comes with clean theoretical guarantees and is simple to distribute, but in practice graph-based and quantisation-based indexes usually achieve better recall for the same memory and speed, which is why HNSW and IVF-PQ dominate today's vector databases.

k-NN as a component, not only a classifier

Nearest-neighbour search appears throughout modern systems: retrieval for retrieval-augmented generation, candidate generation in recommendation (find items near the user's embedding, then re-rank with a heavier model), deduplication of training corpora, few-shot classification with embeddings, and anomaly scores (distance to the k-th nearest normal example). In most of these, the embedding model decides quality, the index decides cost, and the recall of the index sets an upper bound on everything downstream.

The public case: FAISS and billion-scale search

By 2017 Facebook needed similarity search over collections of hundreds of millions to billions of image and text embeddings — for deduplication, recommendation and content understanding — at a scale where brute force was impossible and where approximate methods on CPUs were too slow. Johnson, Douze and Jégou's paper "Billion-scale similarity search with GPUs" introduced the GPU implementation of the FAISS library, built from exactly the pieces in this lesson: brute-force search, IVF clustering and product-quantisation compression, redesigned for GPUs. They reported:

  • A nearest-neighbour implementation 8.5× faster than the prior GPU state of the art, including a k-selection routine (finding the smallest k distances) that ran at up to 55% of the GPU's theoretical peak.
  • A high-accuracy k-NN graph over 95 million images from the YFCC100M dataset built in 35 minutes.
  • A graph connecting 1 billion vectors in under 12 hours on 4 GPUs (Maxwell Titan X).

FAISS was open-sourced and became one of the standard libraries behind vector search, including many retrieval systems for language-model applications. The engineering lessons carry over directly: memory, not arithmetic, is usually the first constraint (hence product quantisation); the index must be chosen and tuned against measured recall; and at scale, the choice of index type, its parameters and its hardware together decide cost.

Failure modes in production retrieval

Unmeasured recall. An index configured once for speed silently returns 70% of the true neighbours; downstream quality drops and nobody knows why. Monitor recall against brute force on a daily sample of real queries.

Embedding model changes. Re-embedding with a new model version without re-indexing everything mixes incompatible vectors; queries embedded with the new model find nonsense among old vectors. Version the embedding model with the index and re-index atomically.

Filter-induced empty results. Strict metadata filters applied after an approximate search leave too few candidates. Increase the candidate count, filter during search, or partition by the filter.

Drift and index decay. New content arrives with a different distribution; IVF lists become unbalanced and HNSW accumulates deletions. Schedule rebuilds and track recall and latency over time.

How teams choose an index

A practical decision rule: below about a million vectors, exact search (optionally on a GPU) is often fast enough and has perfect recall — start there. From millions to hundreds of millions of vectors with enough memory, HNSW gives the best recall–latency trade-off. When memory is the binding constraint, or at billions of vectors, IVF with product quantisation, plus re-ranking of the top candidates with full vectors, is the standard answer. Managed vector databases wrap these same structures; the questions to ask a vendor are which index they use, how filters are applied, how updates and deletions are handled, and what recall they deliver at your latency target on your own data. Sizes in this lesson use gigabytes (GB, 10⁹ bytes) and kilobytes (KB, 10³ bytes).

What to monitor

Recall@k on a brute-force sample, latency percentiles at actual query load, memory per shard, index freshness (time from new item to searchable), result-set size after filters, and — for retrieval-augmented systems — end-to-end answer quality on a fixed evaluation set, which catches problems anywhere in the embedding–index–ranking chain.

Goal: see distance concentration appear as dimension grows, then compare exact search with a tiny IVF index and measure its recall. NumPy only; written for Python 3.12.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 1. Distance concentration: (farthest - nearest) / nearest for one query
for d in (2, 10, 100, 1000):
    X = rng.uniform(size=(2000, d)); q = rng.uniform(size=d)
    dist = np.linalg.norm(X - q, axis=1)
    print(f"d={d:5d}: relative contrast {(dist.max() - dist.min()) / dist.min():.2f}")

# 2. Clustered "embeddings" (low intrinsic dimension) and a toy IVF index
n, d, k = 20000, 64, 10
centres = rng.normal(size=(50, d)) * 3
X = centres[rng.integers(0, 50, n)] + rng.normal(size=(n, d))
X /= np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1, keepdims=True)          # normalise: cosine = dot
Q = X[rng.integers(0, n, 100)] + 0.05 * rng.normal(size=(100, d))
Q /= np.linalg.norm(Q, axis=1, keepdims=True)

C = X[rng.choice(n, 100, replace=False)]                # 100 "centroids" (no k-means, for brevity)
assign = np.argmax(X @ C.T, axis=1)
def ivf_search(q, nprobe):
    cells = np.argsort(-(C @ q))[:nprobe]
    idx = np.where(np.isin(assign, cells))[0]
    return idx[np.argsort(-(X[idx] @ q))[:k]], len(idx)

for nprobe in (1, 5, 20):
    rec, scanned = [], []
    for q in Q:
        exact = set(np.argsort(-(X @ q))[:k])
        found, m = ivf_search(q, nprobe)
        rec.append(len(exact & set(found)) / k); scanned.append(m / n)
    print(f"nprobe={nprobe:2d}: recall@10 {np.mean(rec):.2f}, scanned {np.mean(scanned):.1%} of data")

What to look for. The relative contrast shrinks sharply from d = 2 to d = 1,000 — the nearest point becomes barely nearer than the farthest. On clustered, normalised data the toy IVF index reaches high recall while scanning a small fraction of the vectors, and recall rises with nprobe as the scanned fraction grows — the core trade-off of every approximate index.

Work it out

Take a unit hypercube in d dimensions and define the "shell" as everything within 0.05 of a face. What fraction of the volume is shell at d = 2, 10 and 100? Then say what that means for k-NN, and answer the follow-up: why does search over 1,536-dimensional embeddings still work?

Answer

The interior is a cube of side 1 − 2 × 0.05 = 0.9, so the shell fraction is 1 − 0.9^d.

  • d = 2: 1 − 0.81 = 19%
  • d = 10: 1 − 0.349 = 65%
  • d = 100: 1 − 0.0000266 = 99.997%

At d = 100 essentially every point is near a boundary, so nearly every query is an extrapolation rather than an interpolation among surrounding examples — and k-NN is built on the assumption that you're interpolating among close neighbours. Paired with distance concentration (the farthest-to-nearest ratio tends to 1), "nearest" stops carrying information and any index that partitions space degenerates to a full scan.

The follow-up: embeddings aren't uniformly spread through 1,536 dimensions. A trained model maps similar items close together, and the vectors lie near a structure with a far lower intrinsic dimension, so genuine neighbours are much closer than random items. The ambient dimension still defeats exact spatial indexes, which is why approximate indexes (IVF, PQ, HNSW) are used — with recall measured against brute force.
