A subscription service has two million customers and no labels saying what "kind" of customer each one is. The marketing team wants segments so it can tailor offers; the product team wants to know what distinct ways people use the product; the fraud team wants to find accounts that behave unlike any normal group. Nobody can hand-label two million customers, and nobody even knows what the categories should be. The data has to reveal its own groups.

That is clustering: grouping examples so that members of a group are similar to each other and different from other groups, without labels (ml1's unsupervised learning). It shows up everywhere in AI engineering — customer segmentation, grouping support tickets or search queries by theme, discovering topics among millions of documents by clustering their embeddings, deduplicating near-identical training examples, compressing data, and building the coarse partitions that vector indexes search (ml11's IVF is k-means under the hood).

The subtlety interviewers probe is that "similar" and "group" aren't defined by the data; they're defined by the algorithm and by the features you give it. k-means says a cluster is the set of points nearest to a centre, so it finds compact blobs. DBSCAN says a cluster is a dense region connected through dense neighbourhoods, so it finds arbitrary shapes and leaves sparse points as noise. Gaussian mixture models say the data was generated by overlapping bell-shaped distributions, so every point gets a probability of belonging to each. Give the same data to all three and you can get three different answers, each correct under its own definition. Knowing which assumption a problem needs — and which one breaks — is the skill.

This lesson works through each family by hand: k-means iterations on six points with the inertia computed; DBSCAN's core, border and noise points on a line of numbers; a Gaussian mixture's soft memberships; hierarchical clustering and linkage; choosing the number of clusters (and why the lowest inertia is always wrong); a silhouette computed by hand; evaluating without labels; clustering embeddings at scale; and the public case of Airbnb, which learned embeddings for millions of listings from search sessions and used k-means clustering to check that they captured real-world structure.

What clustering does

Clustering assigns each example to a group — or, in soft clustering, a probability of belonging to each group — using only the features, not labels. The output is useful when the groups correspond to something you can act on: a segment you can market to, a topic you can route, an unusual account you can investigate.

An analogy: sorting a pile of photographs

Hand someone a box of unlabelled holiday photos and ask them to sort it into piles. One person sorts by location, another by who is in the picture, another by season. All three produce sensible groupings, and none is "the" right one — it depends on what the sorting is for. Clustering algorithms are like sorters with fixed habits: k-means always makes piles of roughly equal size around a typical photo; DBSCAN puts together photos connected through chains of very similar ones and leaves oddballs aside; a mixture model allows a photo to be 70% "beach" and 30% "city". And the features you give the algorithm are like telling the sorter what to look at. The analogy's limit: a person can explain their piles and change their mind; an algorithm will produce piles whether or not meaningful groups exist.

k-means in one dimension

  1. Choose k, the number of clusters, and place k starting centres (centroids).
  2. Assign every point to its nearest centroid.
  3. Update each centroid to the average of the points assigned to it.
  4. Repeat steps 2–3 until assignments stop changing.

Points 1, 2, 3, 10, 11, 12 with k = 2 and starting centroids 1 and 2. Assign: {1} to c₁; {2, 3, 10, 11, 12} to c₂ (2 is closer to 2 than to 1). Update: c₁ = 1, c₂ = (2 + 3 + 10 + 11 + 12)/5 = 7.6. Assign again: 2 and 3 are now closer to 1 than to 7.6, so {1, 2, 3} and {10, 11, 12}. Update: c₁ = 2, c₂ = 11. Assign again: nothing changes. Two clean clusters in three iterations.

The other families in a sentence each

  • DBSCAN: clusters are dense regions; points in sparse regions are labelled noise. No k needed; finds odd shapes.
  • Gaussian mixture models: the data comes from several overlapping bell-shaped blobs; each point gets a probability of belonging to each.
  • Hierarchical (agglomerative): start with every point as its own cluster and repeatedly merge the closest pair, producing a tree you can cut at any level.

How do you know it worked?

There are no labels, so there's no accuracy. Evaluation uses internal scores such as the silhouette (how much closer points are to their own cluster than to the next), stability across resamples, and above all whether the clusters are useful for the purpose — can the marketing team act on these segments, do the topics make sense to a reader?

One more warning belongs at the start: clustering algorithms always return an answer. Ask k-means for five clusters of perfectly uniform random data and it will return five tidy-looking groups. Whether the groups reflect real structure is a separate question the algorithm can't answer for you.

1. k-means: the objective and why the loop works

k-means chooses k centroids μ₁…μ_k and an assignment of points to them that minimises the inertia, or within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS):

WCSS = Σ over clusters j  Σ over points x in cluster j  ‖x − μⱼ‖²

The algorithm (Lloyd's algorithm) alternates two steps, each of which can only lower WCSS. The assignment step gives each point to its nearest centroid, which minimises its squared distance given the centroids. The update step moves each centroid to the mean of its points, which is the point minimising the sum of squared distances to them (the same "mean minimises squared error" fact as in ml5). Since WCSS can't go up and there are finitely many assignments, the loop stops — but at a local optimum that depends on where it started.

2. k-means worked in two dimensions

Six points: (1, 1), (1.5, 2), (2, 1) in the lower left and (8, 8), (9, 8.5), (8.5, 9.5) in the upper right. Take k = 2 and a deliberately poor start: c₁ = (1, 1), c₂ = (1.5, 2) — both in the same true group.

  1. Assign. (2, 1) is at distance 1 from c₁ and √(0.25 + 1) = 1.12 from c₂, so it joins c₁. The three upper-right points are all nearer c₂. Clusters: {(1, 1), (2, 1)} and {(1.5, 2), (8, 8), (9, 8.5), (8.5, 9.5)}.
  2. Update. c₁ = mean of its two points = (1.5, 1). c₂ = ((1.5 + 8 + 9 + 8.5)/4, (2 + 8 + 8.5 + 9.5)/4) = (6.75, 7).
  3. Assign. (1.5, 2) is now at distance 1 from c₁ but √(5.25² + 5²) ≈ 7.25 from c₂, so it switches to c₁.
  4. Update. c₁ = ((1 + 1.5 + 2)/3, (1 + 2 + 1)/3) = (1.5, 1.33); c₂ = (8.5, 8.67). Assigning again changes nothing: converged.

Final inertia: in cluster 1, the squared distances are 0.25 + 0.11 = 0.36, 0 + 0.44 = 0.44 and 0.36, totalling 1.17; in cluster 2, 0.69, 0.28 and 0.69, totalling 1.67. WCSS = 2.83.

Initialisation matters. Here the bad start recovered, but in general k-means converges to whatever local optimum is nearest its start, and two centroids starting inside one true cluster can stay there. k-means++ seeding fixes most of this: choose the first centroid at random, then choose each next one with probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest centroid already chosen, which spreads the starting centroids out. Arthur and Vassilvitskii proved its expected cost is within a factor O(log k) of optimal before any iterations, and libraries also run several initialisations (n_init) and keep the lowest inertia.

What k-means assumes. Because points go to the nearest centroid, the boundary between two clusters is the perpendicular bisector between their centroids: every cluster is a convex region around its centre. That works for compact, roughly spherical clusters of similar spread and fails for rings, crescents, elongated streaks and clusters of very different sizes or densities. And because the objective uses squared Euclidean distance, feature scaling decides the result (ml11): a feature with a larger range dominates.

3. DBSCAN: clusters as dense regions

DBSCAN (Ester et al., 1996) needs two parameters: a radius ε and a count minPts. Every point is classified:

  • A core point has at least minPts points (counting itself) within distance ε.
  • A border point has fewer, but lies within ε of a core point.
  • A noise point is neither.

A cluster is a maximal set of core points connected through overlapping ε-neighbourhoods, plus the border points attached to them. Worked, on a line: points 1, 2, 3, 7, 12, 13, 14 with ε = 1.5 and minPts = 3. Point 2's neighbourhood [0.5, 3.5] contains {1, 2, 3}: three points, so 2 is core. Point 1's neighbourhood contains {1, 2}: only two, so 1 is not core, but it's within ε of core point 2, so it's a border point; the same for 3. Point 7 has only itself nearby: noise. Likewise 13 is core and 12 and 14 are border. Result: clusters {1, 2, 3} and {12, 13, 14}, and 7 left unassigned. k-means with k = 2 would have forced 7 into one of the clusters, dragging its centroid.

Setting ε. The standard heuristic is the k-distance plot: for every point, compute the distance to its minPts-th nearest neighbour, sort these distances and plot them. Most points sit in dense regions with small k-distances; the curve bends upward where points start being outliers. Choose ε near the bend. minPts is often set to about twice the number of dimensions for noisy data.

Limits. One ε can't fit clusters of very different densities: an ε small enough to separate two nearby dense clusters dissolves a sparse cluster into noise, and one large enough to keep the sparse cluster merges the dense ones. HDBSCAN considers all density levels at once, building a hierarchy and keeping the clusters that persist over the widest range of densities; its main parameter, the minimum cluster size, is easier to choose. Density methods also struggle in high dimensions, where distances concentrate (ml11), so embeddings are usually reduced first.

4. Gaussian mixture models: soft, elliptical clusters

A Gaussian mixture model assumes each point was generated by first picking a component j with probability πⱼ (the mixing weight), then drawing from a Gaussian with that component's mean μⱼ and covariance Σⱼ. Fitting it means finding the weights, means and covariances that make the data most likely — maximum likelihood again (ml6) — using expectation–maximisation (EM):

  • E-step: for each point, compute each component's responsibility — the probability that the point came from that component: πⱼ × (density of component j at the point), normalised over components.
  • M-step: re-estimate each component from all points, weighting each point by its responsibility: new weight = average responsibility; new mean = responsibility-weighted mean; new covariance = responsibility-weighted spread.

Worked responsibility. Two one-dimensional components with equal weights and standard deviation 1, centred at 0 and 4. For the point x = 1.5, the Gaussian densities are proportional to exp(−1.5²/2) = exp(−1.125) = 0.325 and exp(−2.5²/2) = exp(−3.125) = 0.044. Responsibilities: 0.325 / (0.325 + 0.044) = 0.88 for the first component and 0.12 for the second. For x = 2, exactly halfway, they're 0.5 and 0.5. k-means would assign both points wholly to the first cluster; the mixture says the second is genuinely ambiguous.

k-means is the special case of a GMM with hard (0 or 1) responsibilities and identical spherical covariances. Allowing each component its own full covariance lets clusters be elongated, tilted ellipses of different sizes. The price is parameters: in d dimensions each full covariance has d(d + 1)/2 numbers — 2,080 for d = 64 — so data must be plentiful, or covariances restricted to diagonal, tied or spherical forms. A component can also collapse onto a single point with near-zero variance, sending the likelihood to infinity; adding a small constant to each covariance's diagonal prevents it. The number of components is usually chosen with the BIC (Bayesian information criterion), which rewards likelihood and penalises the number of parameters.

5. Hierarchical clustering

Agglomerative clustering starts with each point as its own cluster and repeatedly merges the two closest clusters until one remains, recording the merges as a tree called a dendrogram; cutting the tree at a chosen height gives any number of clusters. "Closest" is defined by the linkage: single (closest pair of points — can chain distinct groups together through a thin bridge of points), complete (farthest pair — compact clusters), average (mean pairwise distance), or Ward (the merge that least increases total within-cluster variance — similar in spirit to k-means and a good default). Its cost is at least quadratic in the number of points, so it suits thousands rather than millions of examples, but the dendrogram is valuable when groups are naturally nested (product categories, species, document themes).

6. Choosing the number of clusters

Inertia can't choose k: adding a centroid never increases it, and at k = n it's zero. Common tools:

  • Elbow: plot inertia against k and look for the point where the decrease flattens. Often ambiguous on real data.
  • Silhouette: for each point, a = mean distance to the other points in its cluster, b = mean distance to the points of the nearest other cluster, and s = (b − a)/max(a, b). Worked for (1.5, 2) in the converged example: a = mean of its distances to (1, 1) and (2, 1) = 1.118; b = mean of its distances to the three far points = (8.85 + 9.92 + 10.26)/3 = 9.68; s = (9.68 − 1.12)/9.68 = 0.88. Average s over points; values near 1 mean well-separated clusters, near 0 overlapping ones, negative likely misassigned points. Unlike inertia it can decrease as k grows.
  • BIC for mixture models; the gap statistic, which compares inertia with that of random data.
  • Stability: cluster bootstrap samples and check the same structure reappears.
  • Usefulness: the number of segments a team can act on, or the k that most improves a downstream metric.

7. When a few labels exist: external evaluation

Sometimes a small labelled sample exists — a hundred documents tagged by topic, a few customers with known types. Then compare the clustering with the labels using scores that don't care about cluster numbering. The adjusted Rand index (ARI) counts pairs of points: a pair is "agreed" if both the clustering and the labels put the two points together, or both put them apart. The raw proportion of agreed pairs is then adjusted so that a random clustering scores 0 and a perfect match scores 1. Worked on four points with labels (A, A, B, B): a clustering {1, 2} {3, 4} agrees on all six pairs (ARI = 1); a clustering {1, 3} {2, 4} gets the two "apart" pairs 1–4 and 2–3 right and the other four wrong — no better than chance, and in fact slightly worse, so its ARI is negative (−0.5). Normalised mutual information measures how much knowing the cluster tells you about the label. Both are also used to measure stability: cluster two bootstrap samples, assign all points with each model, and compute ARI between the two assignments.

8. Clusters for anomaly detection

Because clustering models describe where normal data lives, they give simple anomaly scores: the distance from a point to its nearest k-means centroid (large means unusual), DBSCAN's noise label, or a low likelihood under a Gaussian mixture. Worked: with the converged centroids above, a new point at (5, 5) is √((5 − 1.5)² + (5 − 1.33)²) ≈ 5.07 from c₁ and √((5 − 8.5)² + (5 − 8.67)²) ≈ 5.07 from c₂ — far from both compared with the training points, whose largest distance to their centroid is about 0.83. It would be flagged. These scores are cheap and interpretable; dedicated methods such as isolation forests are often stronger, but cluster-based scores remain a good baseline.

Why k-means is hard, and what restarts buy

Minimising within-cluster sum of squares exactly is NP-hard even for two clusters in general dimension, so every practical algorithm is a heuristic. Lloyd's loop is fast — each iteration costs O(n·k·d) — and usually converges in tens of iterations, but only to a local optimum. k-means++ gives a provable O(log k) approximation in expectation from seeding alone; several restarts plus keeping the best inertia makes bad local optima rare in practice. For very large data, mini-batch k-means updates centroids from small random batches, trading a little quality for large speed-ups, and approximate nearest-centroid search (ml11) accelerates the assignment step.

Scaling and feature choice are the model

In clustering there's no label to tell the algorithm which features matter, so every feature you include, and its scale, is a statement about what "similar" means. Clustering customers on raw spend (in pounds) and visit count (single digits) groups them by spend alone. Standardising gives every feature equal say — which may also be wrong if some features are noisy or irrelevant. Senior practice is to choose features for the purpose, weight them deliberately, check sensitivity to those choices, and prefer learned representations (embeddings) when raw features are high-dimensional.

Clustering in high dimensions

Distance concentration (ml11) blurs the difference between near and far, which weakens every distance-based method; density-based methods suffer most, because "dense" loses meaning. The standard remedy is to reduce dimensionality first: PCA (ml13) to remove noise directions, or UMAP, a non-linear method that preserves local neighbourhoods and is widely used before HDBSCAN on embeddings (the BERTopic pipeline). Be aware that non-linear projections can create or exaggerate apparent clusters; validate on the original representation where possible.

Spectral clustering

Spectral clustering builds a similarity graph between points (for example, connecting each point to its nearest neighbours), computes eigenvectors of the graph's Laplacian matrix, and runs k-means on the points' coordinates in those eigenvectors. It effectively clusters by connectivity, so it separates concentric rings and interlocking crescents that defeat k-means. Its cost — an eigen-decomposition of an n × n matrix — limits it to moderate data sizes unless approximations are used.

EM more carefully

EM increases the log-likelihood at every iteration and converges to a local maximum, so GMMs share k-means' sensitivity to initialisation; seeding with k-means centroids is standard. The covariance type trades flexibility for stability: full (each component any ellipse), diagonal (axis-aligned ellipses), tied (all components share one shape) and spherical. Bayesian GMMs place priors on the mixing weights and can drive unneeded components' weights towards zero, partially automating the choice of the number of components. And a GMM's likelihood gives a principled anomaly score: points with low density under every component are unusual.

Mixed and categorical data

k-means needs means, and the mean of "London" and "Leeds" doesn't exist. For categorical features, k-modes replaces means with modes and Euclidean distance with a count of mismatches; k-prototypes combines k-means on numeric columns with k-modes on categorical ones. More generally, Gower distance scales each feature's contribution to between 0 and 1 (range-normalised difference for numbers, match or mismatch for categories) and averages them, so it can be used with any distance-based method such as hierarchical clustering or DBSCAN. With many or high-cardinality categories, the usual modern answer is to learn an embedding for each record and cluster those.

Clustering at the scale of training corpora

Clustering appears throughout the preparation of data for large models. Embedding-based clustering groups billions of documents or images so that datasets can be deduplicated (near-duplicates fall in the same cluster and can be compared cheaply), balanced (sampling evenly across clusters so that dominant themes don't swamp rare ones) and inspected (reading samples cluster by cluster reveals spam, boilerplate or low-quality regions). At that scale the algorithms are the same — k-means with approximate nearest-centroid search, often run hierarchically (cluster, then cluster within clusters) — and the engineering challenges are the ones of ml11: memory, approximate search and recall.

Clusters as features

Clustering often pays off indirectly. Cluster IDs, distances to each centroid, or mixture responsibilities can be features for a supervised model; k-means on image patches or embeddings can compress data (vector quantisation, the idea behind ml11's product quantisation); and clustering training data lets you deduplicate near-copies or balance a dataset across themes before fine-tuning a model.

The public case: clustering Airbnb's listing embeddings

Airbnb's search ranking and similar-listing recommendations are, in the words of its KDD 2018 paper by Grbovic and Cheng, channels that "drive 99% of conversions" on the platform. To personalise them, the team learned an embedding for every listing: a short vector (32 numbers) trained from users' search sessions, on the idea that listings clicked in the same session are similar, in the same way word embeddings learn that words appearing in the same contexts are similar. Nothing in the training told the model where a listing was, how much it cost or what style it had.

How do you check that such embeddings mean anything, when there are no labels to evaluate them against? Clustering is one of the tools the team used. As the paper and Airbnb's accompanying write-ups describe, they ran k-means on the learned embeddings of listings in California, producing 100 clusters, and plotted the clusters on a map: listings from the same areas fell into the same clusters, showing that the embeddings had absorbed geography purely from user behaviour. They complemented this with checks of similarity by listing type and price, and ultimately with online experiments on the similar-listings feature and search ranking.

The case illustrates how clustering is most often used in modern AI systems — not as the final product, but as an unsupervised lens on a learned representation, and as a sanity check before the representation goes into production. It also shows the evaluation principle of this lesson: the clusters were judged by whether they matched something meaningful (real neighbourhoods), not by an internal score alone.

Failure modes in production clustering

Unstable segment IDs. Re-running k-means next month renumbers the clusters (and may split or merge some), breaking dashboards and downstream rules keyed on "segment 3". Match new clusters to old ones by centroid similarity (the Hungarian algorithm finds the best one-to-one matching), and treat splits and merges as explicit events.

Drift. Customer behaviour and content change; clusters fitted a year ago describe a population that no longer exists. Track how far new points are from their nearest centroid and the size of each cluster over time, and refit on a schedule.

Clusters driven by one feature. An unscaled or dominant feature (spend, document length) determines the segments; inspect cluster profiles feature by feature before presenting them.

Over-interpreting. Presenting k-means segments as natural "customer types" when the data has no real cluster structure; k-means always returns k groups. Check silhouettes and stability, and compare with clustering random data of the same shape.

A practical workflow

  1. Define the purpose and the features (or embedding) that encode the relevant similarity; scale deliberately.
  2. Reduce dimension if needed; try k-means and HDBSCAN (or a GMM) as complementary views.
  3. Choose settings with silhouette or BIC plus stability, then read samples from each cluster.
  4. Validate against the use — a campaign test, a downstream metric, expert review.
  5. In production, version the model, keep IDs stable across refits, and monitor cluster sizes and distances.

Goal: implement k-means with k-means++ seeding and the silhouette score, and watch k-means fail on concentric rings. NumPy only; written for Python 3.12.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def kmeans(X, k, iters=100, seed=0):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    C = [X[r.integers(len(X))]]                      # k-means++ seeding
    for _ in range(k - 1):
        d2 = np.min(((X[:, None] - np.array(C)) ** 2).sum(-1), axis=1)
        C.append(X[r.choice(len(X), p=d2 / d2.sum())])
    C = np.array(C)
    for _ in range(iters):
        a = np.argmin(((X[:, None] - C) ** 2).sum(-1), axis=1)
        newC = np.array([X[a == j].mean(0) if np.any(a == j) else C[j] for j in range(k)])
        if np.allclose(newC, C): break
        C = newC
    return a, C, ((X - C[a]) ** 2).sum()

def silhouette(X, a):
    D = np.linalg.norm(X[:, None] - X[None], axis=-1); s = []
    for i in range(len(X)):
        own = a == a[i]; own[i] = False
        if not own.any(): s.append(0.0); continue
        ai = D[i, own].mean()
        bi = min(D[i, a == j].mean() for j in set(a) if j != a[i])
        s.append((bi - ai) / max(ai, bi))
    return np.mean(s)

blobs = np.vstack([rng.normal(c, 0.6, size=(100, 2)) for c in [(0, 0), (5, 0), (2.5, 4)]])
for k in (2, 3, 4, 6):
    a, C, wcss = kmeans(blobs, k)
    print(f"blobs k={k}: WCSS={wcss:7.1f}  silhouette={silhouette(blobs, a):.2f}")

t = rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi, 300); r = np.where(np.arange(300) < 150, 1.0, 3.0)
rings = np.c_[r * np.cos(t), r * np.sin(t)] + rng.normal(0, 0.1, (300, 2))
a, _, _ = kmeans(rings, 2)
inner = np.arange(300) < 150
print("rings: share of inner ring in cluster 0 =", round(np.mean(a[inner] == 0), 2),
      "| outer =", round(np.mean(a[~inner] == 0), 2))

What to look for. On the three blobs, WCSS keeps falling as k grows, but the silhouette peaks at k = 3 — the true number. On the rings, both rings are split roughly half-and-half between the two clusters (shares near 0.5), because k-means cuts through the shared centre. Work it out

Two questions. (a) You plot k-means inertia for k = 1…20 and it falls monotonically. Why can't you just pick the k with the lowest inertia, and what would you do instead? (b) Your data is two concentric rings. Sketch what k-means with k = 2 returns, and name two methods that would recover the rings.

Answer

(a) Inertia is within-cluster sum of squares, and it cannot increase when you add a centroid — the new centroid can always take over the points it's closest to — and at k = n every point is its own cluster with inertia exactly 0. So minimising it always selects the most clusters allowed. Look for the elbow (where the marginal improvement drops off), use the average silhouette score, which penalises clusters that are close together, the gap statistic, or BIC on a Gaussian mixture; check stability across resamples; and prefer a downstream metric where one exists. None of the internal criteria is authoritative — say so.

(b) k-means splits the rings down the middle: two half-moon clusters, each containing half of the inner ring and half of the outer. It can't do otherwise, because it assigns every point to the nearest centroid and the rings share a centre, so no pair of centroids separates them.

DBSCAN (or HDBSCAN) recovers the rings, because it grows clusters through chains of dense neighbourhoods and never assumes a shape; spectral clustering also works, because it clusters by graph connectivity. The transferable point: every clustering algorithm encodes an assumption about what a cluster is, and naming the assumption that breaks is the skill being tested.
