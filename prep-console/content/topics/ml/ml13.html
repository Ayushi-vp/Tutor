A manufacturer records 400 sensor readings from every machine each minute: temperatures, pressures, vibration at several frequencies, currents. Engineers want to spot machines drifting towards failure, and data scientists want to predict failures a day ahead. But the 400 readings aren't 400 independent pieces of information. Temperatures in neighbouring parts rise together; vibration at related frequencies moves together; many readings are mostly noise. A model given all 400 has far more inputs than real degrees of freedom, which invites overfitting (ml2), slows everything down, and makes the data impossible to look at.

Dimensionality reduction re-expresses each example with far fewer numbers while keeping what matters. The most important method, principal component analysis (PCA), finds the directions along which the data actually varies. If 20 directions capture 95% of the variation in those 400 readings, each minute can be summarised by 20 numbers; the other 380 directions are mostly redundancy and noise. The summary can feed a model, be plotted, be compressed, or be used to detect anomalies — a reading that can't be rebuilt from the 20 main patterns is one that doesn't fit the machine's normal behaviour.

PCA appears everywhere in AI engineering: compressing embeddings before indexing them (ml11), reducing features before clustering (ml12), removing noise, visualising high-dimensional data, and as the conceptual ancestor of autoencoders. It is also a favourite interview topic because it ties together variance, correlation, eigenvectors and the singular value decomposition, and because its misuses — forgetting to scale, fitting before splitting, reading meaning into components, trusting t-SNE pictures — are common and revealing.

This lesson builds PCA from the idea of spread: the intuition, one complete PCA on a 3×2 matrix by hand (covariance, eigenvalues, eigenvectors, projections and reconstruction error), why the solution is an eigenvector, PCA through the SVD, choosing the number of components, scaling and leakage, uses and failure modes, other methods (t-SNE, UMAP, autoencoders, feature selection), and a public case in which PCA applied to the DNA of 3,000 Europeans produced a picture closely resembling the map of Europe.

Why reduce dimensions

Data often has many features that are redundant — height in centimetres and height in inches, or twenty survey questions that all measure satisfaction. Reducing dimensions can make models faster and less prone to overfitting, remove noise, let distance-based methods work better (ml11), and let you plot high-dimensional data in two dimensions.

An analogy: photographing a statue

You're allowed one photograph of a statue and want it to show as much of the statue's shape as possible. You'd walk around it and choose the angle where it looks widest — the view that shows the most spread. If you're allowed a second photograph, you'd take it from the side, at right angles to the first, to capture the depth the first missed. PCA does exactly this with data: the first principal component is the viewing direction along which the points are most spread out, the second is the most spread-out direction at right angles to the first, and so on. The analogy breaks in one place: a photograph shows the statue from a direction, while PCA's components are directions to measure along; each point's position along a component is one number, its score.

PCA in pictures

Picture a cloud of points shaped like a tilted cigar in two dimensions. PC1 runs along the cigar; PC2 runs across it. Projecting each point onto PC1 alone — replacing its two coordinates with one number, its position along the cigar — loses only the small sideways scatter.

Explained variance

Each component comes with an explained variance ratio: the share of the data's total variance it captures. If PC1 explains 70% and PC2 20%, two components keep 90% of the variance. A common rule is to keep enough components for 90–95%, or to look for the "elbow" where extra components add little.

A small example

Students' scores in maths and physics are highly correlated. PCA gives PC1 ≈ "overall science ability" (both scores rising together), explaining most of the variance, and PC2 ≈ "maths minus physics", explaining the rest. One number summarises most of what two did, and the second number captures the smaller, still-real difference between subjects.

What is kept and what is lost

PCA keeps directions, not features. Each component is a weighted blend of all the original features — its weights are called loadings — so after PCA you no longer have "temperature 3" and "pressure 7"; you have "component 1", which might be mostly a mix of all the temperatures. What you lose is whatever varied only in the discarded directions: small, independent quirks of individual features, which are often noise but occasionally the one signal you needed. That trade — compactness and denoising in exchange for interpretability and possibly some signal — is the decision you make every time you apply it.

The practical rule to remember

Standardise features first (mean 0, standard deviation 1) when they're in different units; otherwise the feature with the biggest numbers dominates the components. And fit PCA on training data only, then apply the same fitted transformation to everything else.

1. The ingredients: centring, variance and covariance

PCA works on centred data: subtract each feature's mean so the cloud sits around the origin. Then the spread of the data is described by the covariance matrix Σ. Its diagonal entries are each feature's variance; its off-diagonal entries are covariances, which are positive when two features rise together, negative when one rises as the other falls, and zero when they're unrelated. For centred data with n rows, Σ = XᵀX / (n − 1).

Now take any direction, given by a unit vector w. Each point's projection onto that direction is the dot product x·w — its coordinate along w. The variance of those projections turns out to be wᵀΣw. PCA asks: which direction w makes this as large as possible?

2. Why the answer is an eigenvector

We want to maximise wᵀΣw subject to w having length 1 (otherwise we could inflate the variance just by making w longer). Using a Lagrange multiplier λ for the constraint, the maximum satisfies:

Σw = λw

That is the definition of an eigenvector: a direction the matrix only stretches, by the factor λ, the eigenvalue. And the variance achieved along it is wᵀΣw = wᵀ(λw) = λ. So the best direction is the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue, and that eigenvalue is the variance along it. The second component maximises the same variance subject to being at right angles (orthogonal) to the first, which gives the eigenvector with the second-largest eigenvalue, and so on. Since Σ is symmetric, its eigenvectors are orthogonal and its eigenvalues are non-negative, and the eigenvalues add up to the total variance (the sum of the diagonal). The explained variance ratio of component j is λⱼ divided by that total.

3. A full PCA on a 3×2 matrix, by hand

Three points in two dimensions: (1, 1), (2, 3), (3, 2).

  1. Centre. The mean is (2, 2). Centred points: (−1, −1), (0, 1), (1, 0).
  2. Covariance (divide by n − 1 = 2). Sum of x² = 1 + 0 + 1 = 2, so var(x) = 1. Sum of y² = 1 + 1 + 0 = 2, so var(y) = 1. Sum of x·y = 1 + 0 + 0 = 1, so cov(x, y) = 0.5. 
    Σ = [[1.0, 0.5],
     [0.5, 1.0]]
  3. Eigenvalues. Solve det(Σ − λI) = (1 − λ)² − 0.25 = 0, so 1 − λ = ±0.5: λ₁ = 1.5, λ₂ = 0.5. The total variance is 1 + 1 = 2 = 1.5 + 0.5, as it must be.
  4. Eigenvectors. For λ₁ = 1.5: (1 − 1.5)a + 0.5b = 0 gives a = b, so PC1 = (1, 1)/√2 ≈ (0.707, 0.707). For λ₂ = 0.5: a = −b, so PC2 = (1, −1)/√2. They're at right angles.
  5. Explained variance. PC1 explains 1.5/2 = 75%, PC2 the remaining 25%.
  6. Project onto PC1. Scores are the dot products with (0.707, 0.707): (−1, −1) → −1.414; (0, 1) → 0.707; (1, 0) → 0.707. Their variance is (2 + 0.5 + 0.5)/2 = 1.5 = λ₁. Check.
  7. Reconstruct from PC1 only. Multiply each score by PC1 and add back the mean. −1.414 × (0.707, 0.707) = (−1, −1), plus (2, 2) gives (1, 1): exact. 0.707 × (0.707, 0.707) = (0.5, 0.5), plus (2, 2) gives (2.5, 2.5) for both remaining points, whose true values are (2, 3) and (3, 2). Each is off by (±0.5, ∓0.5), a squared error of 0.5. Total squared reconstruction error = 1.0, which equals the discarded eigenvalue times n − 1: 0.5 × 2.

This small example contains every idea in PCA: the covariance captures how features move together; the eigenvectors are the directions of spread; the eigenvalues are the variance along them; projecting keeps the top directions; and the variance you discard is exactly what reconstruction can't recover.

A general rule hiding in the example. For any two standardised features with correlation r, the covariance matrix is [[1, r], [r, 1]], whose eigenvalues are 1 + r and 1 − r with eigenvectors (1, 1)/√2 and (1, −1)/√2 — whatever r is. So PC1 is always "the two features together", and it explains (1 + r)/2 of the variance. Our example had r = 0.5 and PC1 explained 75%. Maths and physics scores correlated at r = 0.8 give PC1 = "overall ability" explaining 90% and PC2 = "maths minus physics" explaining 10%; uncorrelated features (r = 0) give 50/50, and PCA has nothing to compress. Redundancy is what PCA feeds on: the more correlated the features, the fewer components you need.

4. PCA through the SVD, as software does it

Forming the covariance matrix squares the numbers and loses precision, so implementations use the singular value decomposition of the centred data matrix instead: X = U·S·Vᵀ, where U and V have orthonormal columns and S is diagonal with non-negative singular values. Then:

principal components   = rows of Vᵀ
scores (projections)   = U·S        (= X·V)
eigenvalues            = S² / (n − 1)

In the example, eigenvalues 1.5 and 0.5 with n − 1 = 2 mean singular values √3 ≈ 1.73 and 1. For large matrices you never need the full decomposition: truncated or randomised SVD computes only the top k components, in roughly O(n·d·k) time, and incremental PCA processes data in batches that don't fit in memory. For sparse matrices such as word counts, truncated SVD without centring (to preserve sparsity) is the standard variant, known in text analysis as latent semantic analysis.

5. Choosing the number of components

Suppose PCA on standardised data with six features gives eigenvalues 4.2, 2.1, 1.3, 0.8, 0.4 and 0.2 (total 9.0). The cumulative explained variance is 46.7%, 70.0%, 84.4%, 93.3%, 97.8%, 100%.

  • Variance threshold: 95% needs five components; 90% needs four.
  • Kaiser rule (standardised data): keep eigenvalues above 1 — components that explain more than one original feature's worth of variance — giving three.
  • Scree elbow: the bars flatten after the third, also suggesting three.
  • Downstream validation: sweep k and pick the value with the best validation score for the actual task. When the reduced data feeds a model, this is the rule that matters.

6. Scaling and leakage

Because PCA follows variance, units decide the answer. With income in pounds (standard deviation £20,000, variance 4 × 10⁸) and age in years (standard deviation 15, variance 225), PC1 is essentially "income" regardless of how the two relate. After standardising both to unit variance, the components reflect their correlation instead. Standardise unless all features share units and their raw variances are meaningful (pixel intensities, or embedding coordinates, which are usually left unscaled).

PCA learns means, scales and directions from data, so fitting it on validation or test rows before splitting leaks information about them (ml9). Put it in the pipeline, fit on training data, and apply the fitted transform to new data — including in production, where the stored means and components must travel with the model (ml14).

7. Reading loadings

The loadings of a component say how much each original feature contributes to it, and inspecting them is how you judge whether a component means anything. Suppose PCA on six standardised body measurements (height, arm span, leg length, shoulder width, weight, waist) gives PC1 loadings all positive and roughly equal, about 0.4 each: PC1 is "overall size", and a person's PC1 score says how big they are overall. PC2 might load positively on weight and waist and negatively on height and leg length: "stocky versus slender". That is the best case. More often, later components mix many features with no clear story, and forcing a name on them misleads. A useful check is to list the handful of features with the largest absolute loadings for each kept component and ask a domain expert whether the grouping makes sense; if nobody can tell a coherent story, treat the component as a compression device, not a concept. Two cautions: loadings are only comparable when features were standardised, and a component's sign is arbitrary — a refit may flip all its loadings and scores together.

8. Compressing embeddings, worked

A retrieval system stores 10 million 1,536-dimensional embeddings in 32-bit floats: 1,536 × 4 bytes = 6 kilobytes (KB) each, about 61 gigabytes (GB) in total — too much to keep comfortably in memory on one ordinary server. Fitting PCA on a sample of 200,000 embeddings shows that the first 256 components explain, say, 90% of the variance. Projecting every stored vector (and every query) onto those 256 components cuts storage to 1 KB per vector — about 10 GB — and speeds up distance computations six-fold. Whether 90% of the variance is enough is not a PCA question: measure recall@10 of nearest-neighbour search with the reduced vectors against exact search with the full ones on real queries (ml11), and look at end-to-end answer quality. If recall drops too far, keep more components or use product quantisation instead. Remember to re-normalise the reduced vectors if the index uses cosine similarity, and to refit the PCA whenever the embedding model changes.

9. What PCA is used for

  • Preprocessing for models that suffer from many correlated features or high dimension — k-NN and clustering especially — and for speed.
  • Compression of embeddings before indexing (for example 1,536 to 256 dimensions), validated by retrieval recall (ml11).
  • Denoising: reconstructing data from the top components drops the low-variance directions where much of the noise lives.
  • Anomaly detection: a point with a large reconstruction error doesn't fit the main patterns of normal data — a standard monitor for sensor streams.
  • Visualisation of the first two or three components, which, unlike t-SNE, preserves global distances linearly.
  • Diagnosing structure, as in population genetics (the public case), where the top components can reveal the main axes of variation among thousands of variables.

10. Beyond PCA

PCA is linear. Other methods cover what it can't:

  • t-SNE places points in 2-D or 3-D so that each point's nearest neighbours stay nearby, using a heavy-tailed distribution in the low-dimensional map that pushes moderately distant points apart; its perplexity setting (roughly, the effective number of neighbours, typically 5–50) changes the picture.
  • UMAP builds a neighbour graph and optimises a low-dimensional layout; it's faster, preserves somewhat more global structure and can map new points, but shares t-SNE's distortions.
  • Autoencoders learn non-linear compression with neural networks; a linear autoencoder trained with squared error learns the same subspace as PCA.
  • Kernel PCA, NMF and ICA: non-linear components via kernels; parts-based non-negative factors for counts and spectra; statistically independent sources for separating mixed signals.
  • Feature selection — filter methods such as mutual information, wrapper methods such as recursive elimination, and L1 regularisation (ml3) — when you must keep original, interpretable features.

Variance is not signal

PCA is unsupervised: it never sees the target. If the feature that predicts churn is a small, low-variance flag, PCA may bury it in the 50th component and a model trained on the top 20 will never see it. Partial least squares (PLS) finds components that both explain the features and correlate with the target; supervised dimensionality reduction more generally exists for this reason. In many modern pipelines the simpler answer is to skip PCA and let a regularised model or a tree ensemble cope with all the features.

Whitening and its cost

Whitening divides each component's scores by its standard deviation (√λ), so every direction has unit variance and the data is uncorrelated with equal spread. Some algorithms benefit — it makes the covariance an identity, which helps certain optimisation and density methods — but whitening amplifies the low-variance directions, which are often mostly noise, by large factors. It's usually combined with dropping the smallest components or adding a small constant to each eigenvalue before dividing.

Instability of components

When two eigenvalues are nearly equal, the data spreads about equally in every direction within their plane, and the individual eigenvectors are poorly determined: small changes in the data rotate them arbitrarily, even though the plane they span is stable. Component signs are also arbitrary (v and −v are equally valid). Code that stores components and compares them across refits must align signs and, for near-ties, compare subspaces rather than individual vectors. Outliers can dominate components too; robust PCA variants separate a low-rank structure from sparse large errors.

Connections: ridge, the SVD and linear autoencoders

Ridge regression (ml3) shrinks the fitted coefficient along each principal direction by the factor d²/(d² + λ), where d is that direction's singular value: directions the data barely explores are shrunk hardest. Principal component regression instead keeps the top k directions fully and drops the rest; ridge is the smooth version. And a linear autoencoder with a k-dimensional bottleneck, trained to minimise squared reconstruction error, learns exactly the subspace spanned by the top k components (though not necessarily the individual orthogonal axes) — the link between classical dimensionality reduction and learned representations.

Probabilistic PCA and factor analysis

PCA can be recast as a probabilistic model: each observation is generated from a few hidden factors through a linear map, plus Gaussian noise that is the same size in every direction (probabilistic PCA). Maximum likelihood recovers the principal subspace, and the model gives a likelihood for new points — a principled anomaly score — and handles missing values. Factor analysis relaxes the assumption so that each feature gets its own noise level, which suits survey and psychometric data where some questions are noisier than others; followed by a rotation (such as varimax) that pushes loadings towards zero or large values, it often yields more interpretable factors than PCA. Both are useful when the goal is to explain correlations with hidden causes rather than just to compress. In interviews, the distinction to state is that PCA summarises total variance, while factor analysis explains shared variance (covariance) and treats each feature's unique variance as noise.

Random projections

The Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma says that projecting n points onto about O(log n / ε²) random directions preserves all pairwise distances to within a factor 1 ± ε, with no fitting at all. Random projections are fast, data-independent and useful as a first reduction for enormous datasets or streaming data. They need more dimensions than PCA for the same fidelity, because they ignore where the variance actually lies.

t-SNE and UMAP, more carefully

t-SNE converts distances into neighbour probabilities in both the original and the low-dimensional space and minimises the KL divergence between the two distributions; because this heavily penalises putting true neighbours far apart but barely penalises putting non-neighbours close, local structure is kept and global structure is not. Results vary with perplexity and random seed, and naive implementations scale as O(n²) (Barnes–Hut tree approximations and fast-Fourier-transform-based interpolation scale further, to millions of points). UMAP optimises a similar objective on a fuzzy nearest-neighbour graph; its n_neighbors setting trades local against global structure and min_dist controls how tightly points pack. Both are for looking. Distill's "How to Use t-SNE Effectively" shows random data forming apparent clusters and cluster sizes that mean nothing — a useful exhibit before presenting such a plot.

The public case: PCA redraws the map of Europe

In 2008 Novembre and colleagues published "Genes mirror geography within Europe" in Nature. They characterised genetic variation in a sample of 3,000 Europeans genotyped at 500,568 positions in the genome (single-nucleotide polymorphisms, or SNPs) — a data matrix with half a million columns in which, at any single position, differences between European populations are tiny. Before analysis they filtered samples carefully: removing people whose ancestry came from outside Europe, balancing sample sizes across countries, and excluding people whose grandparents came from more than one place; they also removed SNPs that are strongly correlated with their neighbours in the genome, which would otherwise dominate the components.

They then ran PCA. Plotting 1,387 individuals by their scores on the first two principal components — rotated to line up with compass directions — produced a figure that, in the authors' words, "bears a notable resemblance to a geographic map of Europe": individuals clustered by country, and countries sat roughly where they are on a map. The two strongest directions of genetic variation corresponded to perpendicular geographical axes, as population-genetics theory predicts for spatially structured populations. Using the components in a regression, they could place 50% of individuals within 310 km of their reported origin and 90% within 700 km.

Two lessons for engineers. First, PCA can reveal genuine low-dimensional structure hidden in an enormous number of individually weak, noisy features — the same reason it works for sensor streams and embeddings. Second, the result depended on choices that aren't visible in the plot: which samples were included, which correlated features were removed, and a rotation applied for display. Components reflect the data you give them, so the provenance of that data belongs in any interpretation.

Failure modes in production

Refitting silently changes the space. Refitting PCA on new data rotates components and can flip their signs, so models trained on old component scores receive inputs in a different coordinate system. Version the PCA transform with the model, and retrain downstream models whenever it's refitted.

Leaky fitting. PCA fitted on all data before cross-validation or before a time split gives optimistic results (ml9).

Mismatched preprocessing. Serving code that forgets the centring or scaling step, or uses means computed on live data, feeds the model shifted inputs (ml14).

Over-reading plots. Decisions made from t-SNE cluster sizes or gaps; or stakeholders told a component "is" a business concept.

Monitoring with reconstruction error

A PCA fitted on normal operating data gives a cheap drift and anomaly monitor: track the average reconstruction error of incoming data over time. A rising error means new data contains patterns outside the fitted subspace — a new failure mode, a sensor fault, or a shift in the input distribution — and is often visible before any downstream metric moves. Set the alert threshold from the distribution of errors on held-out normal data (for example, its 99th percentile), and when it fires, look at which original features contribute most to the error to see what changed.

Goal: reproduce the 3×2 PCA by hand with NumPy, confirm the SVD relationships, then compress correlated data and use reconstruction error to spot anomalies. NumPy only; written for Python 3.12.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 1. The worked example
X = np.array([[1., 1.], [2., 3.], [3., 2.]])
Xc = X - X.mean(0)
cov = Xc.T @ Xc / (len(X) - 1)
vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(cov)                 # ascending order
print("cov", cov.tolist(), "eigenvalues", vals.round(3), "PC1", vecs[:, -1].round(3))
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(Xc, full_matrices=False)
print("singular values", S.round(3), "-> S^2/(n-1) =", (S**2 / 2).round(3))
scores = Xc @ Vt[0]
recon = np.outer(scores, Vt[0]) + X.mean(0)
print("PC1 scores", scores.round(3), "recon error", ((X - recon)**2).sum().round(3))

# 2. 50 correlated features driven by 3 hidden factors, plus noise
n, d = 1000, 50
Z = rng.normal(size=(n, 3)); W = rng.normal(size=(3, d))
D = Z @ W + 0.3 * rng.normal(size=(n, d))
mu, sd = D[:800].mean(0), D[:800].std(0)          # fit on "training" rows only
Dtr, Dte = (D[:800] - mu) / sd, (D[800:] - mu) / sd
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(Dtr, full_matrices=False)
ratio = S**2 / (S**2).sum()
print("explained by first 5 PCs:", ratio[:5].round(3), "cumulative 3:", ratio[:3].sum().round(3))

# 3. Reconstruction error as an anomaly score
k = 3
def err(A): return (((A - (A @ Vt[:k].T) @ Vt[:k]) ** 2).sum(1))
odd = Dte.copy(); odd[:10] += rng.normal(0, 3, size=(10, d))   # corrupt 10 rows
e = err(odd)
print("mean error normal:", e[10:].mean().round(1), " corrupted:", e[:10].mean().round(1))

What to look for. Part 1 prints the covariance [[1, 0.5], [0.5, 1]], eigenvalues 0.5 and 1.5, PC1 ≈ ±(0.707, 0.707) (the sign is arbitrary), singular values √3 and 1, and reconstruction error 1.0. Part 2's first three components explain most of the variance and the rest are near the noise level — the three hidden factors. Part 3's corrupted rows have far larger reconstruction errors than normal ones.

Work it out

PCA on a standardised dataset gives eigenvalues 4.2, 2.1, 1.3, 0.8, 0.4, 0.2. How many components for 95% of the variance? What does the Kaiser rule (keep eigenvalues > 1) suggest? And how would you actually decide?

Answer

Total = 9.0 — the number of standardised features, since each contributes variance 1. Cumulative explained variance:

PC1 46.7% · PC2 70.0% · PC3 84.4% · PC4 93.3% · PC5 97.8% · PC6 100%

So 5 components for 95%. The Kaiser rule keeps 3 (4.2, 2.1 and 1.3 exceed 1): on standardised data each original feature contributes variance 1, so a component with an eigenvalue below 1 explains less than a single raw feature.

The two rules disagree, which is the point: 3 components lose 15.6% of the variance and 5 keep almost everything. Neither number is correct on its own — choose by the downstream task, sweeping the number of components as a hyperparameter against validation performance.

Two traps to mention unprompted: standardise first or the largest-unit feature dominates; and fit PCA on the training fold only, because fitting it on train and test together is a quiet form of leakage.
