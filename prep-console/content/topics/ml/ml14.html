A delivery company wants to predict which orders will arrive late. The raw data is an order table — timestamp, warehouse, destination postcode, product weight — and an event log of every scan, dispatch and delivery. Given the raw columns, a model learns almost nothing: a timestamp is just a large number, a postcode is an arbitrary string. Given features — hour of day and day of week, distance from warehouse to destination, whether the date is near a holiday, the warehouse's late rate over the past week, the current backlog — the same model predicts well. Feature engineering, turning raw data into inputs that expose the pattern, is where most of the performance of tabular models comes from.

Then the model ships, and something subtler goes wrong. Offline, the feature "warehouse late rate over the past 7 days" was computed by a nightly warehouse query over complete daily tables. Online, the serving system computes it from a live counter that covers a rolling 168 hours, ignores cancelled orders differently, and lags by an hour when the event stream is busy. Same name, two different features. The model, trained on one and served the other, quietly performs worse than every offline test promised. This is training/serving skew, and it's one of the most common and least visible causes of production ML failure.

Google's "Rules of Machine Learning" — this lesson's public case, alongside the "Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems" paper — devotes a whole section to it, reporting that teams who measured their skew "were sometimes surprised by the results" and that YouTube's home page gained significant quality improvements, and simpler code, by logging the features actually used at serving time and training on those. Feature stores, shared transformation code and skew monitoring all exist because of this problem.

This lesson covers both halves. First, the feature toolkit with worked numbers: encoding categories (one-hot, smoothed target encoding, hashing with its collision arithmetic, embeddings), scaling and log transforms, cyclical time features, point-in-time aggregates, and missing values. Then the gap: how skew arises, how to detect it (including a population stability index computed by hand), the architectural fixes — logging served features, shared code, feature stores — and how to judge whether a feature is worth its cost.

Models only see numbers

A model's input is a vector of numbers. Everything in the real world — a product category, a timestamp, a paragraph, a history of clicks — has to be turned into numbers first, and how you do it determines what the model can learn. A good feature makes the pattern simple: if late deliveries happen on Friday evenings, a "day of week" and an "hour" feature let even a simple model see it, while a raw Unix timestamp hides it.

An analogy: preparing ingredients

A chef can't cook a good meal from unprepared ingredients: vegetables must be washed and chopped, meat trimmed, spices measured. The same ingredients prepared differently produce different dishes. Features are prepared ingredients, and the model is the cooking. The analogy extends to the second half of this lesson: imagine the recipe was developed in a test kitchen where onions were diced finely, but the restaurant kitchen slices them thickly because a different cook wrote down "prepare the onions". The recipe is the same, the result is worse, and nobody notices until customers complain. That is training/serving skew.

The common transformations

  • Categories to numbers: one-hot encoding turns "colour = red/green/blue" into three 0/1 columns.
  • Scaling: standardise (subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation) so features are comparable in size (ml3).
  • Logarithms for skewed values such as income: in natural logs, £20k, £40k and £200k become 9.90, 10.60 and 12.21, so doubling income is the same step (0.69) at any level, and extreme values stop dominating.
  • Dates and times: extract day of week, hour, month, "days since last purchase", holidays.
  • Aggregates: "number of purchases in the last 30 days", "average basket value", "failed logins in the last hour".
  • Ratios and interactions: debt ÷ income; price per square metre.
  • Text: word counts, TF-IDF weights, or embeddings from a language model.
  • Missing values: fill with the training median and add a "was missing" flag — missingness itself is often informative.

A worked example

Predicting late deliveries from order timestamp, warehouse, destination postcode and product weight. Useful engineered features: hour of day and day of week of the order; distance between warehouse and destination; whether the date is within three days of a public holiday; the warehouse's late-delivery rate over the past 7 days; its current order backlog. The raw timestamp alone tells the model almost nothing; the engineered features carry the signal.

Which models need what

Different model families need different preparation. Linear and logistic regression need scaled numeric features, encoded categories and hand-made interactions and non-linear transforms. Distance-based methods (k-NN, clustering) need careful scaling above all. Tree ensembles need none of the scaling but benefit most from aggregates, ratios and history features. Neural networks need normalised inputs and vocabularies for IDs, and learn many interactions themselves. Knowing the model tells you where to spend feature effort.

Training/serving skew in one sentence

If a feature is computed one way when training (from a historical batch table) and another way when serving (from live data), the model sees different inputs in production from the ones it learned from, and quietly performs worse.

1. Encoding categorical features

One-hot encoding creates one 0/1 column per level. It's safe and interpretable for a few dozen levels and the default for linear models. Its problems grow with cardinality: thousands of sparse columns, trees splitting on one rare level at a time, and no column for a level first seen in production. Always define an "unknown" bucket.

Ordinal encoding maps levels to integers. Use it only when the order is real (small < medium < large); imposing a fake order on nominal categories misleads linear models, though trees tolerate it.

Target encoding replaces a level with the average target among its training rows, smoothed towards the global average so rare levels aren't trusted too much:

encoding(level) = (sum of targets in level + m × global rate) / (count of level + m)

Worked: a merchant with 3 transactions, 1 of them fraud, when the global fraud rate is 2% and the smoothing strength m = 20. The raw rate would be 1/3 = 0.33; the smoothed encoding is (1 + 20 × 0.02) / (3 + 20) = 1.4 / 23 = 0.061 — pulled most of the way back towards 0.02, because three transactions is weak evidence. A merchant with 3,000 transactions and 300 frauds gets (300 + 0.4) / 3,020 = 0.0995, essentially its own rate. As ml9 showed, the encoding must be computed out-of-fold for training rows, or each row's own label leaks into its feature; scikit-learn's TargetEncoder does this internally. Unseen levels get the global rate.

Frequency encoding replaces a level with how often it occurs — useful when popularity itself is predictive. The hashing trick maps each level to one of m columns with a hash function, so memory is fixed however many levels appear, and new levels need no vocabulary update. The price is collisions: with n levels hashed into m buckets, the expected number of colliding pairs is about n² / (2m). For 10,000 levels in 2²⁰ ≈ 1,048,576 buckets, that's 10⁸ / 2,097,152 ≈ 48 pairs — a tiny fraction, usually harmless. Learned embeddings give each level a small dense vector trained with a neural network; they're the standard for user, item and word IDs, and need a plan for new IDs (a shared "unknown" vector, or features that describe the new item).

2. Scaling and transforming numbers

Standardisation — (x − mean) / standard deviation, with the mean and standard deviation learned on training data — is needed for regularised linear models (ml3), distance-based methods (ml11, ml12), PCA (ml13) and neural networks. Trees don't need it (ml10). Robust scaling uses the median and interquartile range instead, so a few extreme values don't set the scale. Clipping (capping values at, say, the 1st and 99th percentiles of training data) limits the influence of outliers and protects against absurd serving values.

Log transforms suit positive, right-skewed quantities — money, counts, durations, sizes — where effects are multiplicative. Worked: incomes of £20k, £40k and £200k are 20, 40 and 200 thousand apart on the raw scale, dominated by the largest; their natural logs 9.90, 10.60 and 12.21 put each doubling at the same 0.69 step. Use log(1 + x) when zeros occur. Binning turns a continuous feature into ranges so a linear model can learn a non-linear effect (young drivers and elderly drivers both riskier), at the cost of discarding within-bin detail.

All fitted statistics — means, standard deviations, clip bounds, bin edges, category vocabularies, target encodings — are part of the model. They are learned on training data only (ml9) and must be applied, unchanged, at serving time.

3. Time features

Timestamps carry several kinds of signal. Calendar parts: hour, day of week, month, holiday flags, paydays. Periodic ones should usually be cyclically encoded, because 23:00 and 00:00 are adjacent in reality but 23 apart as numbers:

Worked: hour 23 maps to (sin(2π × 23/24), cos(2π × 23/24)) = (−0.259, 0.966); hour 0 maps to (0, 1). Their distance is √(0.259² + 0.034²) ≈ 0.26, the same as the distance between any two adjacent hours, instead of 23.

Recency — time since the last event (last purchase, last login, last failed payment) — is often among the most predictive features in churn, fraud and marketing. Aggregates over windows — counts, sums, averages, distinct counts over the last hour, day, 7 or 30 days — summarise behaviour. Both must be computed point-in-time (ml9): as of each training example's timestamp, using only events before it.

4. Missing values

Missing values need a decision, and the reason they're missing matters. If income is missing because applicants with irregular income leave it blank, missingness predicts risk. The standard recipe: impute with a statistic from the training data (median for numbers, a dedicated "missing" level for categories) and add a missingness indicator, so the model can learn from both the value and the fact that it was absent. XGBoost and LightGBM learn, at each split, which branch missing values should follow, which often works well. Whatever the rule, serving must apply exactly the same one — a serving path that sends 0 instead of "missing" is a classic skew.

5. Text and embedding features

Text can enter a tabular model in three ways. Counts and TF-IDF weights of words or word pairs are cheap and interpretable, and with a linear model they make a strong baseline for classification. Hand-made text features — length, number of links, presence of certain phrases — capture specific signals. Embeddings from a pretrained language model turn each text into a dense vector that captures meaning; they can be fed to a model directly, reduced with PCA (ml13) first, or used to compute similarity features ("how close is this ticket to known billing tickets?"). Embedding features add their own skew risk: the embedding model's version is part of the feature definition, and serving must use exactly the same model and preprocessing (tokenisation, truncation) as training.

6. A feature review, worked

A team proposes five new features for the late-delivery model. A quick review against the questions of this lesson:

  • "Driver's delivery count today." Available at prediction time? Only up to the moment of prediction — and the training version must use the count as of the order time, not the end-of-day total. Keep, with point-in-time computation.
  • "Delivered on time (yes/no) for the previous order to this address." Useful history, available — keep.
  • "Customer complaint filed." Complaints are filed after late deliveries: target leakage. Drop.
  • "Weather at destination in the next 6 hours." Only available if a forecast (not the observed weather) is used, and training must use the forecast as it was issued, not what actually happened. Keep only with archived forecasts.
  • "Warehouse backlog." Online it comes from a live queue service with a few minutes' lag; offline from end-of-hour snapshots. Two implementations — define it once, or log the served value and train on that.

Every question in the review is about when and how the value is computed, not about whether it's predictive. That's the habit that prevents most leakage and skew.

7. How training/serving skew happens

Google's Rules of Machine Learning defines training/serving skew as a difference between performance during training and performance during serving, caused by (1) a discrepancy between how data is handled in the training and serving pipelines, (2) a change in the data between training and serving, or (3) a feedback loop between the model and the data. The first is the engineering bug this lesson's opening story describes, and it has many forms:

  • Two implementations. A SQL job in the warehouse for training, Java or Python code in the online service for serving, written by different people: window boundaries (calendar days versus rolling hours), whether the current event is included, time zones, rounding, and how nulls and cancellations are treated all drift apart.
  • Freshness. Offline, a feature is computed from complete data; online, the latest events haven't arrived yet, so counts are systematically lower.
  • Preprocessing mismatch. Serving forgets to apply the scaler, uses a different category vocabulary, or maps unseen values differently.
  • Tables that change. A training join reads today's version of a table rather than the version at prediction time (Rule 31 warns that data in a joined table may change between training and serving).

8. Detecting skew

The most direct check: log the feature values actually used for each prediction, then recompute the same features offline for the same entities and timestamps and compare. Any difference is a pipeline discrepancy. The second check compares distributions of each served feature with its training distribution. A common measure is the population stability index (PSI): split the training values into bins, and sum over bins of (actual share − expected share) × ln(actual share / expected share).

Worked. A feature split into quartiles of the training data has expected shares 0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25. This week's served values fall into those bins with shares 0.10, 0.20, 0.30, 0.40.

bin 1: (0.10 − 0.25) × ln(0.10/0.25) = (−0.15)(−0.916) = 0.137
bin 2: (0.20 − 0.25) × ln(0.20/0.25) = (−0.05)(−0.223) = 0.011
bin 3: (0.30 − 0.25) × ln(0.30/0.25) = ( 0.05)( 0.182) = 0.009
bin 4: (0.40 − 0.25) × ln(0.40/0.25) = ( 0.15)( 0.470) = 0.071
PSI = 0.228

Common rules of thumb treat a PSI below 0.1 as stable, 0.1–0.25 as a moderate shift worth investigating, and above 0.25 as a major shift; 0.23 is a clear signal. PSI doesn't say whether the cause is a bug or real change in the world — the logged-versus-recomputed comparison does. Google's Rule 37 adds a model-level view: track performance on training data, holdout data, next-day data and live data, since each gap points to a different problem (overfitting, time drift, or skew).

9. Preventing skew

Three architectural fixes, strongest first. Train on logged serving features (Rule 29): save the feature values used at prediction time and join them to labels later, so training sees exactly what serving saw. Share transformation code between training and serving (Rule 32), for example by exporting the fitted preprocessing pipeline with the model. Use a feature store: one definition per feature, materialised into an offline store that answers point-in-time queries for training and an online store that serves the latest values in milliseconds, kept in sync by the same logic, streaming updates and backfills. And whichever you choose, monitor: logged-versus-recomputed comparisons, per-feature distribution checks, and performance by data slice over time.

Logging and waiting: the trade-off

Training on logged serving features removes pipeline skew by construction, and Google reports that YouTube's home page gained significant quality and simpler code by doing so. Its costs are real. A new feature has no history until it has been logged for a while, so you can't train on months of it immediately; logs must be stored, versioned and joined to labels that arrive later; and a serving bug is faithfully preserved in the training data. A common hybrid: backfill a new feature offline with carefully point-in-time code, verify it against a few days of logged values, then switch to training on logs.

Streaming features and event time

Features such as "clicks in the last five minutes" are computed by stream processors (Flink, Spark Streaming) online and backfilled from event logs offline. Two notions of time complicate this: event time (when something happened) and processing time (when the system saw it). Late-arriving events change a window's value after the fact; offline backfills that use event time see complete data, while online computations using processing time don't. Unless both paths use the same windowing semantics and the same rules for late data, the feature is skewed by construction — often by a few per cent, always in the same direction.

Feature crosses and learned interactions

Linear models can't learn interactions unless you create them: a cross of "city × device" lets a model learn that mobile users in one city behave differently. Crosses explode in number and sparsity, which is why they're usually hashed. Tree ensembles learn interactions through nested splits, and neural architectures for tabular and recommendation data (wide-and-deep, deep-and-cross networks) learn crosses automatically — shifting feature work from designing crosses to preparing clean inputs and ID vocabularies.

Features that leak or discriminate

Two kinds of feature are dangerous even when they help offline. Leaky features are updated after the prediction moment — status fields, "last modified" timestamps, aggregates that include the label — and they inflate offline metrics (ml9). Proxy features reproduce the effect of protected attributes the model isn't allowed to use: postcode, name, school or device can stand in for ethnicity or income. Removing the protected attribute doesn't remove the effect; measuring outcomes by group does, and some features must be dropped for legal or ethical reasons regardless of accuracy.

Feature cost and technical debt

"Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems" (Sculley et al., 2015) argues that ML systems incur "massive ongoing maintenance costs", and names risks that live in features: entanglement (changing one input changes everything — "changing anything changes everything"), unstable data dependencies (an upstream team changes a signal's meaning), underutilised dependencies (legacy features that add risk without value), and feedback loops. The practical response is a feature inventory with owners and definitions, regular ablation to remove features that no longer earn their keep, contracts with upstream data producers, and alerting on upstream changes. Every online feature is also a serving dependency: if the service providing it is slow or down, predictions degrade.

Automated feature learning versus engineering

Deep learning replaced hand-engineered features for images, audio and text because networks learn better representations from raw inputs when data is plentiful. For tabular and event data the division of labour is different: the most valuable features encode domain knowledge about time and entities — "rate of failed payments for this merchant over 30 days", "days since this customer's last complaint" — that a model can't invent from a raw event table without enormous data. Automated feature generation tools enumerate aggregates and transforms systematically, which helps coverage but multiplies leakage and skew risks and the number of dependencies. The practical balance is to automate breadth, then prune hard with ablations and the feature-review questions.

Feedback loops

The third cause of skew in Google's list is the hardest to see. When a model's decisions affect future data — which items are shown, which transactions are blocked, which customers are contacted — features built from that data reflect the model's own past behaviour. Retraining then reinforces the old policy. Logging the probability with which each action was chosen, keeping a small randomised exploration slice, and correcting for exposure (for example, position bias in rankings) are the standard defences (ml1, ml16).

The public case: Google on training/serving skew

Google's "Rules of Machine Learning", written from the experience of its production ML teams, devotes a section to training/serving skew. It defines skew as a difference between performance during training and during serving, with three causes: discrepancies in how the training and serving pipelines handle data, changes in the data between training and serving, and feedback loops between the model and the data. Its rules read like a checklist for this lesson:

  • Rule 29: "The best way to make sure that you train like you serve is to save the set of features used at serving time, and then pipe those features to a log to use them at training time." Even if not for every example, do it for a fraction to verify consistency. The guide reports that teams at Google who measured this were "sometimes surprised by the results", and that the YouTube home page switched to logging features at serving time "with significant quality improvements and a reduction in code complexity".
  • Rule 31: beware joining data from a table at training and serving time, because the table may change in between.
  • Rule 32: re-use code between the training pipeline and the serving pipeline whenever possible.
  • Rule 37: measure skew by tracking the differences between performance on training data, holdout data, next-day data and live data.

The companion paper, Sculley et al.'s "Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems" (NeurIPS 2015), explains why these problems are structural: the ML code is a small part of a real system, surrounded by data collection, feature extraction, configuration, serving and monitoring, and the risks — data dependencies, entanglement, hidden feedback loops, undeclared consumers — accumulate there. Together they're the reason feature stores, shared preprocessing and skew monitoring became standard parts of ML platforms.

What goes wrong, and how it shows

A gap between offline and online metrics that appears from day one points to pipeline skew; one that grows over weeks points to drift (ml16).

A feature that is always a little lower online (counts, sums) usually means late-arriving events or a window that ends earlier online.

Sudden jumps in a feature's distribution after a deployment of an upstream service — the meaning of a signal changed without the model's owners knowing.

Predictions that ignore a feature online because serving sends a default value (0, empty string) where training had real values — often a failed lookup silently caught by error handling. Counting how often each feature falls back to its default in production is one of the cheapest and most revealing monitors there is.

A production checklist for every feature

  1. Is it available at prediction time, with the same freshness as in training?
  2. Is it computed by one definition in both paths, point-in-time for training?
  3. How are missing values and unseen categories handled, identically?
  4. Is it logged at serving time and compared with offline recomputation?
  5. Is its distribution monitored (PSI or similar) with alerts?
  6. Does it carry leakage or fairness risk, and who owns it?

Goal: compute a smoothed target encoding, a cyclical hour encoding and a PSI by hand-checked code, then simulate a skewed feature and watch a model's accuracy drop. NumPy only; written for Python 3.12.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 1. Smoothed target encoding
def target_encode(n_pos, n, prior, m=20):
    return (n_pos + m * prior) / (n + m)
print("merchant 1/3:", round(target_encode(1, 3, 0.02), 3),
      " merchant 300/3000:", round(target_encode(300, 3000, 0.02), 4))

# 2. Cyclical hour encoding
enc = lambda h: np.array([np.sin(2 * np.pi * h / 24), np.cos(2 * np.pi * h / 24)])
print("dist(23h, 0h) =", round(np.linalg.norm(enc(23) - enc(0)), 3))

# 3. Population stability index
def psi(expected, actual, eps=1e-6):
    e, a = np.asarray(expected) + eps, np.asarray(actual) + eps
    return float(np.sum((a - e) * np.log(a / e)))
print("PSI:", round(psi([.25, .25, .25, .25], [.10, .20, .30, .40]), 3))

# 4. Skew: train with a 7-day window feature, serve a lagged / undercounted version
n = 20000
spend7 = rng.gamma(2.0, 50.0, n)                        # the feature as computed offline
y = (rng.random(n) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(spend7 - 150) / 40))).astype(int)
thr = np.quantile(spend7[:15000], 0.5)                   # a one-feature "model": flag above median
acc = lambda f: np.mean((f[15000:] > thr) == y[15000:])
served = spend7 * rng.uniform(0.75, 0.95, n)             # online counter misses late events
print(f"accuracy offline={acc(spend7):.3f}  served={acc(served):.3f}")
bins = np.quantile(spend7[:15000], [0, .25, .5, .75, 1])
share = lambda f: np.histogram(np.clip(f[15000:], bins[0], bins[-1]), bins)[0] / 5000
print("PSI train vs served:", round(psi(share(spend7), share(served)), 3))

What to look for. The encodings print 0.061 and about 0.0995, the hours' distance 0.261 and the worked PSI 0.228. In part 4 the served feature is systematically 5–25% low, so accuracy drops even though the model didn't change, and the PSI between training and served values flags the shift — the kind of silent damage skew does in production.

Work it out

A feature is avg_spend_7d. The training pipeline computes it in the warehouse as the mean over the last 7 calendar days including today. The serving path computes it from a Redis counter updated on each transaction. Offline AUC 0.89, online 0.76. What happened, and how would you fix it for good?

Answer

Two distinct bugs hiding behind one symptom.

1. The training feature includes the future. "Including today" means that for a transaction at 09:00, the average covers spending that happened at 14:00 — after the prediction point. For a fraud or churn model this is target leakage (ml9): a spending spike caused by the very event you're predicting is inside the feature. Offline AUC is inflated by information that can't exist at serving time.

2. The two paths compute different things anyway. Warehouse rows are batch-loaded with a lag, so the training feature covers calendar days, while the Redis counter covers a rolling 168 hours ending now, possibly missing late events. Different windows, different boundaries, different late-arrival handling — the same name, two features.

The fix is architectural, not a patch. One definition, executed in both paths (a feature store, or shared transformation code), with an explicit as-of timestamp so training joins the value as it stood at prediction time. Log the values actually served and compare them with offline recomputation for the same users and times, and monitor each feature's distribution (PSI) against the training snapshot. This class of bug is silent otherwise, and it's the most common cause of an offline/online gap.
