A recommendation team spends a quarter building a new ranking model. Offline it's clearly better: on last month's logged data its ranking metric is 4% higher, a large gain by the team's standards. They launch it to half of users in an A/B test. After three weeks, the metric the company actually cares about — purchases per user — hasn't moved. The confidence interval straddles zero. A quarter's work, a solid offline improvement, and nothing to show. This is one of the most common experiences in applied machine learning, and understanding why it happens is what this lesson is about.

Offline metrics (ml7) are computed on historical data. That data was collected under the old system — users could only click what the old ranker showed them — and the offline metric measures a proxy (did the model rank the clicked item highly?) rather than the goal (did the user buy, return, stay?). Offline evaluation also can't see latency, presentation, novelty, how users adapt, or how the change interacts with everything else in the product. The only reliable way to learn whether a change improves the outcome you care about is to expose real users to it under controlled conditions and measure: an online controlled experiment, or A/B test.

Experiments are powerful. In this lesson's first public case, a Bing engineer tested a small, deprioritised idea — making ad headlines longer — and found it increased revenue by 12%, over $100 million a year in the United States alone, without hurting user-experience metrics. Experiments are also easy to run badly: stopping early when the numbers look good, testing twenty metrics and reporting the one that moved, ignoring a broken randomisation. And the world keeps changing after launch. In the second public case, Zillow's home-buying business relied on price forecasts; when the housing market moved faster than its models anticipated, it ended up buying homes for more than it could sell them, took a $304 million inventory write-down and shut the business down.

This lesson covers the path from offline metric to business outcome: why offline and online disagree; the evaluation ladder (offline, shadow, canary, A/B, holdout); A/B testing statistics worked by hand — sample size, a z-test with a confidence interval, sample-ratio mismatch, the false-positive inflation from peeking, multiple comparisons; variance reduction; interference, novelty and long-term effects; faster alternatives such as interleaving and off-policy evaluation; monitoring and drift after launch; and the two public cases.

Offline versus online evaluation

Offline evaluation scores a model on historical data before launch: fast, cheap and safe, but it only tells you how well the model predicts past outcomes under past conditions. Online evaluation shows the model to real users and measures what happens — purchases, retention, time saved, complaints. The business cares about the second; the first is only a proxy, useful for filtering ideas before spending users' attention on them.

An analogy: a drug trial

A new drug that kills bacteria in a laboratory dish still has to be tested on patients, because the body adds absorption, side effects and interactions that the dish can't show. Medicine settled on the randomised controlled trial: randomly assign patients to the drug or a placebo, decide in advance what outcome counts, and compare. A/B testing is the same design applied to software: users are randomly assigned to the current system (control) or the new one (treatment), and the difference in outcomes, beyond what chance would produce, is the effect of the change. The analogy also warns about the same failures: stopping the trial the moment results look good, measuring many outcomes and reporting the best, and patients in one group affecting those in the other.

Why not just compare before and after?

The tempting shortcut is to launch the new model and compare this month with last month. It fails because everything else changes too: seasons, marketing campaigns, competitors, news, other product changes. A 3% rise after launch might be the model, or December. Randomisation solves this by splitting users at the same time, so every outside influence hits both groups equally and the only systematic difference is the change being tested.

A/B testing basics

  1. Randomly split users into control (A: old model) and treatment (B: new model).
  2. Choose a primary metric in advance (for example conversion rate) and guardrail metrics that must not get worse (latency, errors, complaints).
  3. Compute the sample size needed and run until you have it — at least one full week.
  4. Compare with a statistical test, and report the effect with a confidence interval.

Statistical significance in plain words

Even with no real difference, two random groups will show slightly different conversion rates. A p-value is the probability of seeing a difference at least this large if there were truly no effect. The usual convention treats p < 0.05 as "significant", which means accepting a 5% false-positive rate when there's no effect. Power is the probability of detecting a real effect of a given size; tests are usually designed for 80% power. A confidence interval gives the range of effect sizes consistent with the data — more useful than a yes/no verdict.

A worked example

Control converts at 5.0%. You want to be able to detect a lift to 5.25% (a 5% relative improvement). A rough sample size per group is 16 × p(1 − p) / Δ², where Δ is the absolute difference: 16 × 0.05 × 0.95 / 0.0025² ≈ 122,000 users per group. With 10,000 users a day in total, that's about 3.5 weeks. Stopping after three days because the numbers "look good" is how teams ship changes that do nothing.

1. Why offline and online disagree

  • The data was collected by the old system. Logged clicks exist only for items the old model showed, mostly in top positions. A new model that promotes different items is evaluated on items with no feedback, and position bias (people click what's on top) contaminates the labels.
  • The label is a proxy. Offline you predict clicks or ratings; the business wants purchases, satisfaction, retention. A model that's better at predicting clicks can promote clickbait.
  • Offline ignores the system. Latency (a slower model loses users), presentation, diversity, and interactions with other components (ads, search, notifications) don't appear in an offline metric.
  • Distribution differences. The offline gain may be concentrated in queries or users rare in live traffic; training/serving skew (ml14) or leakage (ml9) may have inflated the offline number.
  • Time. Users adapt: a novel interface gets explored at first (novelty), a changed one resisted at first (primacy), and some effects only show up over months.

2. The evaluation ladder

Shadow deployment runs the new model on live requests without using its outputs: you compare its predictions with the current model's, check latency and error rates, and catch training/serving skew before any user is affected. A canary release sends a small share of traffic (1–5%) to the new version, mainly to catch crashes and regressions quickly. The A/B test then measures the outcome, and a long-term holdout — a small share of users kept on the old version for months — measures effects that take time to appear.

3. Designing an A/B test

Randomisation unit. Usually the user, so each person gets a consistent experience and carry-over between visits doesn't mix the groups. Randomise by request only when there's no memory between requests. The analysis must use the same unit: if users are randomised but the metric is per page view, page views from one user are correlated and naive standard errors are too small.

Metrics. One primary metric that decides the test (Microsoft calls it the overall evaluation criterion), chosen because it predicts long-term value; guardrails that must not degrade (latency, errors, crashes, complaints, revenue); and diagnostic metrics that explain what happened.

Sample size. Decide the minimum detectable effect — the smallest effect worth knowing about — then compute the sample size. For conversion rates, a good approximation at 80% power and a two-sided 5% significance level is:

n per arm ≈ 16 · p̄(1 − p̄) / δ²          (16 ≈ 2 × (1.96 + 0.84)²)

Worked: baseline 2.0%, target 2.2% (δ = 0.002, p̄ ≈ 0.021): n ≈ 16 × 0.0206 / 0.000004 ≈ 82,000 per arm. Because δ is squared, halving the detectable effect quadruples the sample. Duration: run whole weeks, because weekday and weekend behaviour differ, and long enough for novelty to fade.

4. Analysing the result, by hand

Control: 100,000 users, 5,000 conversions (5.00%). Treatment: 100,000 users, 5,250 conversions (5.25%).

  1. Difference: 0.25 percentage points, a 5% relative lift.
  2. Standard error of the difference: √(0.05 × 0.95 / 100,000 + 0.0525 × 0.9475 / 100,000) = √(9.72 × 10⁻⁷) ≈ 0.000986.
  3. z-statistic: 0.0025 / 0.000986 ≈ 2.54, giving a two-sided p-value of about 0.011.
  4. 95% confidence interval: 0.0025 ± 1.96 × 0.000986 = [0.057, 0.443] percentage points — a relative lift somewhere between about 1% and 9%.

The p-value says a difference this large would be unusual if the change did nothing. The interval says what matters for the decision: the lift is probably real but could be as small as 1% or as large as 9%. Whether to ship depends on whether even a 1% lift justifies the change's costs, and on the guardrails.

The same rates with a tenth of the users. Had each arm had 10,000 users (500 versus 525 conversions), the standard error would be √10 ≈ 3.16 times larger, about 0.00312; z would be 0.0025 / 0.00312 ≈ 0.80, a p-value of about 0.42, and the 95% interval [−0.36, 0.86] percentage points. That result is inconclusive, not evidence of no effect: it's consistent with a 7% drop and a 17% lift. "Not significant" from an underpowered test is one of the most misreported outcomes in experimentation — the honest summary is the interval, and the remedy is the sample size calculation done before the test.

5. The classic ways tests mislead

Sample-ratio mismatch. Before looking at any metric, check the split. In a 50/50 test of 200,000 users, observing 101,600 versus 98,400 gives a chi-squared statistic of (1,600²/100,000) × 2 = 51.2, with a p-value far below 0.001: something is broken — a variant that crashes before logging, bot filtering that differs between arms, assignment that fails for some browsers. The groups are no longer comparable, and no result from the test can be trusted until the cause is found.

Peeking. Looking at the p-value every day and stopping when it dips below 0.05 gives noise many chances to cross the line. A simulation of A/A tests (no real difference) checked at equally spaced points shows false-positive rates of about 14% with 5 looks, 20% with 10 and 25% with 20, instead of 5%. Either fix the sample size and analyse once, or use sequential methods (group-sequential designs with alpha spending, or always-valid p-values) designed for monitoring.

Multiple comparisons. Test twenty independent metrics or segments at 5% and the chance that at least one is "significant" by luck is 1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ 64%. Declare one primary metric, treat the rest as diagnostic, and correct (for example Bonferroni or false-discovery-rate methods) when several decisions are made from one test.

Novelty and primacy. Users click on something new because it's new, or avoid it because they're used to the old way. Plot the daily treatment effect: a lift that decays week by week is novelty.

Interference. When treatment users affect control users — shared drivers in a marketplace, shared advertiser budgets, content spreading in a network — the comparison is biased. Randomise by cluster (city, network community) or alternate whole markets over time (switchback designs).

6. Making experiments cheaper

CUPED (Controlled-experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data) adjusts each user's metric by their own pre-experiment value: Y′ = Y − θ(X − X̄), with θ the regression coefficient of Y on X. The pre-period value X is unaffected by treatment but explains part of the variance, which falls by a factor of 1 − ρ² for correlation ρ between X and Y. With ρ = 0.7, variance roughly halves (1 − 0.49 = 0.51), so the same precision needs about half the users or half the time. Interleaving, for rankers, merges the two rankers' results into one list for each user and credits each click to the ranker that contributed the item; because every user compares both, it detects ranking differences with far fewer users than an A/B test. Off-policy evaluation estimates a new policy's value from logs of the old one by reweighting logged outcomes by the ratio of new to old action probabilities (inverse propensity scoring) — possible only if the old system logged the probability of each action it took, which is a strong argument for logging them.

7. Choosing the metrics

The primary metric deserves as much thought as the model. It should be sensitive (it moves when the product gets better), attributable (the tested change can plausibly affect it within the test window) and aligned with long-term value. Raw revenue per user is aligned but noisy; clicks are sensitive but gameable; "sessions per user" or "successful searches per user" are often good compromises. Worked: a search team's candidate metrics for a ranking change are clicks per query (a better ranker can reduce clicks, because users find the answer faster), time to first successful click (sensitive, aligned with satisfaction) and queries per user per week (aligned, slow to move). They choose time to first successful click as primary, queries per user as a guardrail against users giving up, and latency and error rate as further guardrails.

Many useful metrics are ratios — clicks per session, revenue per visit — where the randomisation unit (the user) differs from the unit in the denominator (sessions). Sessions from the same user are correlated, so the naive variance is too small; the delta method or a bootstrap over users gives correct intervals. Heavy-tailed metrics such as revenue benefit from capping extreme values (winsorising) before analysis, decided in advance.

8. Deciding whether to ship

A test result is an input to a decision, not the decision itself. Combine the interval with costs. Suppose the lift in section 4 applies to 10 million annual users with an average order value of £40: each 0.1 percentage point of conversion is worth 10,000,000 × 0.001 × £40 = £400,000 a year. The interval of 0.057 to 0.443 points is therefore worth roughly £230,000 to £1.8 million a year. If the new model costs £300,000 a year more to serve and maintain, the lower end of the interval doesn't cover it; the expected value (at the 0.25-point estimate, about £1 million) does. A reasonable team ships, with a holdout to confirm the effect persists — or extends the test to narrow the interval if the downside matters. Writing this reasoning down, with guardrail results, is what makes the decision reviewable.

9. After launch: monitoring and drift

A launched model's performance changes because the world does. Data drift is a change in the input distribution (new users, new products); concept drift is a change in how inputs relate to outcomes (the same features now mean a different risk). Monitor input distributions (with the population stability index of ml14), prediction distributions, calibration (ml15), and — most important — realised outcomes once labels arrive, by segment and by cohort. Keep a small long-term holdout for comparison. And watch for the model's own influence: decisions change which outcomes get observed and which cases the model "wins", which is where the second public case went wrong.

What a p-value doesn't tell you

If most ideas don't work — Microsoft reports that about a third of its experiments improve metrics, a third are neutral and a third are negative, and many teams see lower success rates — then among tests with p < 0.05 a meaningful share are false positives, and the observed effects of true winners are exaggerated, because the ones that happened to measure high are the ones that crossed the line (the "winner's curse" of experimentation). Kohavi and colleagues recommend treating surprising results with extra scepticism (Twyman's law: any figure that looks interesting or different is usually wrong), replicating important wins, and using lower significance thresholds or Bayesian shrinkage when prior success rates are low.

Sequential testing

Business pressure makes continuous monitoring inevitable, so it should be done validly. Group-sequential designs pre-plan a few interim analyses with stricter thresholds that spend the overall 5% error budget across looks (alpha spending). Always-valid inference (mixture sequential probability ratio tests, confidence sequences) produces p-values and intervals that remain valid however often you look. Bayesian analysis reports the probability that treatment beats control under an explicit prior, but it's not immune to optional stopping in terms of error rates; it needs the same discipline about decisions.

Heterogeneous and long-term effects

Averages hide structure: a change can help new users and hurt experienced ones, or help one market and hurt another. Pre-registering a few key segments avoids the fishing that multiple comparisons punish; methods such as causal forests estimate effect heterogeneity more systematically. For long-term effects, short tests can mislead in a predictable direction: tactics that raise short-term engagement (more notifications, attention-grabbing content) can erode long-term retention. Long-running holdouts, and short-term surrogate metrics validated against long-term outcomes, are the standard answers.

Interference designs

In two-sided marketplaces and networks, user-level randomisation is biased because units share resources or influence each other. Cluster randomisation assigns whole cities or graph communities, reducing spillover at the cost of fewer effective units and more variance. Switchback designs alternate a whole market between variants over short time blocks, suited to dispatch and pricing algorithms, but require blocks long enough for effects to settle and analysis that respects time correlation. Budget-split designs in ad systems split each advertiser's budget between variants so that treatment can't spend control's money.

Adverse selection and the winner's curse in models

Whenever a model makes offers or bids that the other side can accept or reject, the accepted cases are selected: sellers accept offers that are too high, borrowers with private information accept loans that are underpriced, auctions are won by the bidder who most overestimates value. Average error measured offline is then the wrong metric; what matters is the error on the cases where the model's decision was taken up. Monitoring outcomes by accepted cohort, and treating a sudden rise in acceptance as a warning rather than a success, are the defences — both visible in hindsight in the Zillow case.

Experiments on ML systems specifically

Machine-learning changes have quirks that ordinary product tests don't. Models interact: a new ranking model and a new ads model tested separately may each win, and conflict when launched together, so layered experiments need interaction checks. Models learn from the data their own decisions produce, so a treatment model retrained on data from all users is contaminated by the control's behaviour and vice versa; clean comparisons sometimes require separate data pipelines per arm. And a model that wins by changing what users see also changes what future training data will contain, so the effect of the first launch can compound or fade over retraining cycles — another reason for long-term holdouts. Finally, model tests should always report cost and latency alongside quality, since a more accurate model that doubles serving cost may be a net loss.

Evaluating LLM features

LLM products make the offline–online gap wider. Offline evaluation relies on curated test sets and automated judges (LLM-as-judge), which must themselves be calibrated against human ratings; online signals — thumbs up or down, edits, retries, escalations — are sparse and biased towards unhappy users. Model or prompt updates change behaviour broadly, so layered evaluation matters: a versioned offline suite for regressions, shadow runs comparing outputs, A/B tests on outcome and cost metrics with the user as the unit, and holdouts for slow effects such as user trust.

Public case 1: the Bing headline test

Kohavi and Thomke describe it in "The Surprising Power of Online Experiments" (Harvard Business Review, 2017). In 2012 a Bing employee proposed changing how ad headlines were displayed — lengthening the title line by combining it with text from the line below. The idea was judged low priority and sat in the backlog for months, until an engineer ran a quick online controlled experiment. The test showed the change increased revenue by 12% — more than $100 million a year in the United States alone — without hurting key user-experience metrics. It became the best revenue-generating idea in Bing's history. The same article reports that large companies such as Amazon, Facebook, Google and Microsoft each run more than 10,000 online controlled experiments a year, and that at Microsoft as a whole about one-third of experiments prove effective, one-third are neutral and one-third are negative.

Two lessons follow. Intuition, including expert intuition, is a poor judge of which ideas will work, so cheap, trustworthy experiments have enormous value. And the flip side: since most ideas don't move metrics, a surprising success deserves careful verification — the Bing result was examined for exactly the kinds of errors this lesson lists before it was trusted.

Public case 2: Zillow Offers

Zillow Offers made cash offers on homes, renovated them and resold them, relying on the company's ability, in its own words, "to forecast the price of homes accurately three to six months into the future". In its November 2021 letter to shareholders, Zillow announced it would wind the business down, with a workforce reduction of about 25%. It reported that it had been "unable to accurately forecast future home prices at different times in both directions by much more than we modeled as possible", after a pandemic, a temporary freeze and then a surge in prices "at an unprecedented rate". In the third quarter it had seen higher acceptance rates than before "as a result of unintentionally purchasing homes at higher prices than our current estimates of future selling prices", leading to a $304 million inventory write-down.

In this lesson's terms: the model's offline accuracy on historical sales did not describe its error at the decision horizon in a fast-changing market (concept drift); the homes Zillow bought were selected by sellers accepting its offers, so errors on accepted offers were biased upward (adverse selection); and a rising acceptance rate — which looks like success — was actually the leading indicator of mispricing. Outcome monitoring by purchase cohort, guardrails that throttle activity when realised margins drift, and stress tests of forecast error under regime changes are the practices this case argues for.

Running an experimentation practice

  • Platform hygiene: consistent assignment by hashing, exposure logged at the point of treatment, automatic sample-ratio checks, metrics defined once, and A/A tests to validate the pipeline.
  • Pre-registration: hypothesis, primary metric, guardrails, sample size and stopping rule written down before launch.
  • Reporting: effect sizes with confidence intervals, guardrail results, segment checks declared in advance, and null results recorded — they prevent the same idea being retried endlessly.
  • Layering: many concurrent experiments with independent randomisation, with checks for interactions between those that touch the same surface.
  • After launch: holdouts, outcome and drift monitoring, and a rollback plan.

Goal: compute a sample size and a test result by hand, then simulate A/A tests to see how peeking inflates false positives. NumPy only; written for Python 3.12.

import numpy as np
from math import erf, sqrt
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
Phi = lambda z: 0.5 * (1 + erf(z / sqrt(2)))

# 1. Sample size per arm for a 2.0% -> 2.2% lift (80% power, 5% two-sided)
p1, p2 = 0.020, 0.022
pbar = (p1 + p2) / 2
print("n per arm ≈", round(16 * pbar * (1 - pbar) / (p2 - p1) ** 2))

# 2. Analyse a finished test
n, c1, c2 = 100_000, 5_000, 5_250
r1, r2 = c1 / n, c2 / n
se = sqrt(r1 * (1 - r1) / n + r2 * (1 - r2) / n)
z = (r2 - r1) / se
print(f"lift={r2 - r1:.4f}  z={z:.2f}  p={2 * (1 - Phi(abs(z))):.4f}  "
      f"95% CI=[{r2 - r1 - 1.96 * se:.4f}, {r2 - r1 + 1.96 * se:.4f}]")

# 3. Sample-ratio mismatch check
a, b = 101_600, 98_400; exp = (a + b) / 2
print("SRM chi-squared =", round((a - exp) ** 2 / exp + (b - exp) ** 2 / exp, 1))

# 4. Peeking: A/A tests (no true effect), checked at K equally spaced points
def false_positive_rate(K, sims=4000, users_per_look=500):
    hits = 0
    for _ in range(sims):
        diffs = rng.normal(size=(K, users_per_look)).sum(1).cumsum()
        z = diffs / np.sqrt(np.arange(1, K + 1) * users_per_look)
        hits += np.any(np.abs(z) > 1.96)
    return hits / sims
for K in (1, 5, 10, 20):
    print(f"{K:2d} looks: false-positive rate ≈ {false_positive_rate(K):.3f}")

What to look for. Part 1 prints about 82,000 per arm; part 2 a lift of 0.0025 with z ≈ 2.54, p ≈ 0.011 and an interval of roughly [0.0006, 0.0044]; part 3 a chi-squared of 51.2 — a broken split. Part 4 shows the false-positive rate climbing from about 5% with one look to roughly 14%, 20% and 25% with 5, 10 and 20 looks, although nothing changed between the groups.

Work it out

Baseline conversion is 2%. You want to detect a 10% relative lift (2.0% → 2.2%) at 80% power and 5% significance. How many users per arm, and how long at 10,000 visitors a day split evenly? What would you say next?

Answer

For two proportions the usual approximation is n ≈ 16 · p̄(1 − p̄) / δ² per arm, where 16 ≈ 2 × (1.96 + 0.84)² packages the z-values for a two-sided 5% test and 80% power.

p̄ = 0.021, so p̄(1 − p̄) = 0.0206. The absolute effect is δ = 0.002, so δ² = 4 × 10⁻⁶.

n = 16 × 0.0206 / 0.000004 ≈ 82,000 per arm, about 164,000 in total. At 10,000 a day: about 17 days.

Three things to say next, because the number alone isn't the answer:

  • Run it for whole weeks. 17 days ends mid-week and weekday and weekend traffic differ; run 21.
  • Halving the detectable effect quadruples the sample. A 5% relative lift needs about four times as long — over two months. That's usually the moment to ask whether a 5% lift is worth detecting at all, or whether variance reduction (CUPED) can shorten the test.
  • Don't peek. Checking daily and stopping at the first significant result inflates the false-positive rate from 5% to roughly 20% or more. Fix the horizon in advance, or use a sequential test designed for continuous monitoring — and check the sample ratio before reading any metric.
