A subscription company sends a 20%-off coupon to the customers its churn model rates most likely to renew if offered a discount. The response model is good: among the customers who received the coupon, 30% of the top decile renewed, against 8% across everyone. The campaign is declared a success, and next quarter the budget doubles. Then a data scientist runs a small test: in the top decile, half the customers get the coupon and half don't. The ones without the coupon renew at 29%. The coupon bought one extra renewal per hundred customers, and paid for a 20% discount on the other twenty-nine who would have renewed anyway. The model was accurate; it was answering the wrong question.

The model predicted who will renew if given the coupon. The business needed who will renew because of the coupon. These sound alike, but they differ in the one way that matters: the second is about what would have happened if we had acted differently, and that outcome is never in the data. A customer who got the coupon and renewed tells us nothing about whether they'd have renewed without it. This is the domain of causal inference: estimating the effect of an action, not just predicting outcomes in data where actions were taken for reasons of their own.

The same gap appears everywhere a model's output turns into an action. Did the notification bring the user back, or do active users get more notifications? Did the price rise cut demand, or did it happen in the season demand always falls? Did the new feature improve retention, or did enthusiastic users adopt it first? A/B tests (ml16) answer these questions cleanly when you can run them. Often you can't — the decision was already made, randomising would be unethical or too slow, or the question is about a past policy — and then you need to know which observational methods can stand in, and what each one silently assumes.

This lesson starts with a real dataset in which a treatment looks worse overall but better in every subgroup, and uses it to build the vocabulary: potential outcomes, average effects for the whole population, for the treated and for subgroups, and why randomisation fixes everything. It then works through the observational toolkit — propensity scores and inverse-probability weighting, matching, difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity and instrumental variables — with a hand calculation and the key assumption for each. Finally it covers uplift modelling: estimating the effect for each customer so you can decide whom to treat, the S-, T- and X-learners that build it from ordinary models, the Qini curve that evaluates it, and the marketing, notification and pricing settings where interviewers ask about it.

Prediction versus intervention

Most of machine learning answers the question "given what I see, what will happen?" A churn model, a click model or a fraud model learns the pattern between features and outcomes in historical data, and predicts the outcome for a new case under the same conditions. Causal inference answers a different question: "if I do something, what will change?" That question is about an intervention — sending the coupon, changing the price, showing the feature — and the data was usually collected while someone else was deciding when to intervene. The pattern in the data mixes the effect of the intervention with the reasons it was chosen.

An analogy: hospitals and sick patients

People who go to hospital are more likely to die in the next month than people who don't. Nobody concludes that hospitals kill people, because we know why: sick people go to hospital. Illness causes both the "treatment" (hospital) and the outcome (death). If you only had the data and no medical knowledge, though, the association would point the wrong way. Every causal question in product work has a version of this. Heavy users get more notifications (because the system sends to active users) and retain better (because they're heavy users); looking at notifications and retention together, you'd conclude notifications cause retention — maybe they do, but the data as collected can't tell you. The analogy breaks in one way: in product work the hidden cause is rarely as obvious as illness, which is why the problem is so often missed.

The missing column

The cleanest way to see the difficulty is a table. For each customer, imagine two columns: whether they'd renew with the coupon, and whether they'd renew without it. The effect of the coupon on that customer is the difference between the columns. But every customer either got the coupon or didn't, so one of the two columns is always blank. This is called the fundamental problem of causal inference: individual effects are never observed. Everything in this lesson is a way of filling in averages of the blank column using a comparison group that stands in for it — and every method is only as good as the argument that its comparison group really is comparable.

Three routes to a fair comparison

There are only three ways to get a comparison group you can trust. Randomise: flip a coin for who gets treated, so the groups differ only by chance. Adjust: when treatment wasn't random, compare treated and untreated units that look alike on the features that drove the decision, by weighting, matching or regression. Exploit a natural experiment: find something in the world that assigned treatment almost randomly — a policy that began in one region, a threshold that decides eligibility, a randomised nudge. The first is the gold standard; the second and third are what you do when you can't run it.

From one average to targeting

Knowing that a coupon raises renewals by 3 points on average answers "should we run the campaign?". It doesn't answer "for whom?". The effect varies: price-sensitive customers respond, loyal ones don't, and a few customers react badly to being contacted at all. Uplift modelling estimates the effect for each kind of customer, so you can target only those whose behaviour the treatment changes. It's causal inference turned into a ranking problem — and evaluating it needs causal thinking too, because the "label" (an individual's effect) never exists.

1. A worked confounding example: kidney stones

In 1986 Charig and colleagues published the results of treating kidney stones with open surgery and with percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL, a less invasive keyhole procedure). Here are 700 of their patients, 350 per treatment, with "success" meaning the stones were gone or reduced to under 2 mm three months after treatment:

StonesOpen surgeryPCNL
Small (< 2 cm)81 / 87 = 93%234 / 270 = 87%
Large (≥ 2 cm)192 / 263 = 73%55 / 80 = 69%
All273 / 350 = 78%289 / 350 = 83%

Open surgery wins for small stones and for large stones, yet loses overall. That reversal is Simpson's paradox, and the explanation is in the counts. Doctors chose open surgery mostly for large stones (263 of its 350 patients) and PCNL mostly for small ones (270 of 350). Large stones are harder to clear whatever you do. So the "open surgery" column is dominated by hard cases and the "PCNL" column by easy ones; the overall comparison is mostly comparing stone sizes, not treatments. Stone size is a confounder: a common cause of the treatment chosen and the outcome.

A diagram like this is a causal DAG (directed acyclic graph): arrows mean "causes", and there are no loops. A path from treatment to outcome that begins with an arrow into the treatment, like T ← Z → Y, is a back-door path; it creates association without causation. Adjusting for Z — comparing within levels of Z — blocks it. The DAG is where the domain knowledge goes: the data alone can't tell you whether stone size is a cause of treatment choice or a consequence of it. That matters because the same arithmetic reversal can happen with variables you must not adjust for, which section 5 returns to.

The adjusted answer, by hand. Ask: "what would the success rate be if everyone got open surgery, versus if everyone got PCNL?" Of the 700 patients, 357 (51%) had small stones and 343 (49%) large. Apply each treatment's within-group success rate to that same mix — this is called standardisation:

everyone open:  0.51 × 0.931 + 0.49 × 0.730 = 0.833
everyone PCNL:  0.51 × 0.867 + 0.49 × 0.688 = 0.779
adjusted difference = +5.4 points in favour of open surgery

The raw comparison said PCNL was 5 points better; the adjusted one says open surgery is 5.4 points better. (In the real study, other differences between the procedures — recovery time, complications — also mattered to clinicians; the point here is only how the comparison of success rates flips.)

2. Potential outcomes: the vocabulary

The potential outcomes framework makes the "missing column" precise. Each unit i (a patient, a customer) has two potential outcomes: Yi(1), the outcome if treated, and Yi(0), the outcome if not. The individual effect is Yi(1) − Yi(0). We only ever observe one: Yi = Yi(Ti), where Ti is 1 if treated. The unobserved one is the counterfactual.

Since individual effects are never seen, we estimate averages:

  • ATE, the average treatment effect: E[Y(1) − Y(0)] over the whole population. "Should we treat everyone?"
  • ATT, the average treatment effect on the treated: the same average over the units who actually got treated. "Was the treatment worthwhile for those who received it?" In the kidney data, standardising to the open-surgery patients' mix (87 small, 263 large) gives an ATT of +4.8 points rather than +5.4, because those patients were mostly large-stone cases, where the gap is smaller.
  • CATE, the conditional average treatment effect, τ(x) = E[Y(1) − Y(0) | X = x]: the average effect for units with features x. "For whom does it work?" — the quantity uplift models estimate.

Why would the naive difference, average outcome of the treated minus average outcome of the untreated, be wrong? Write it out: E[Y | T=1] − E[Y | T=0] = E[Y(1) | T=1] − E[Y(0) | T=0]. Add and subtract E[Y(0) | T=1]:

naive difference = E[Y(1) − Y(0) | T=1]            ← ATT, the effect
                 + E[Y(0) | T=1] − E[Y(0) | T=0]    ← selection bias

The second line is the difference between treated and untreated units had neither been treated. In the kidney data it's negative (open-surgery patients had harder stones, so would have done worse anyway). In the coupon story it's positive (customers picked for coupons were more likely to renew anyway). Every method that follows is a way of making that second line zero.

3. Why randomisation works

If a coin decides T, then T is independent of everything about the unit — including both potential outcomes. Treated and untreated groups are then exchangeable: E[Y(0) | T=1] = E[Y(0) | T=0], so selection bias vanishes and the naive difference is the effect. Crucially, this holds for factors you never measured: a hidden trait like "intends to cancel anyway" is spread evenly by chance. That's why the randomised test of ml16 is the gold standard, and why the cheapest fix for a causal question you'll face repeatedly — "do our coupons work?" — is to build a permanent random holdout into the system rather than argue about observational data every quarter.

Randomisation's costs are practical: it takes time, it withholds a possibly beneficial treatment from the control group, it sometimes can't be done (you can't randomise a past decision, or a country's tax policy), and it can be compromised by interference or non-compliance (ps6). When you can't randomise, you have to replace the coin with an assumption.

4. Adjusting for measured confounders: propensity scores and IPW

Stratifying by stone size worked because there was one confounder with two values. With twenty confounders, many continuous, there are no cells to stratify into. The propensity score solves this: e(x) = P(T = 1 | X = x), the probability of being treated given the covariates. Rosenbaum and Rubin showed in 1983 that if adjusting for X removes confounding, adjusting for the single number e(X) does too. You can estimate it with any probabilistic classifier — logistic regression (ml6) is the classic choice.

Two assumptions make the method valid. No unmeasured confounding (also called conditional exchangeability): X includes every common cause of treatment and outcome, so within units with the same x, treatment is as good as random. Overlap (positivity): every kind of unit had some chance of each treatment, 0 < e(x) < 1. If all large-stone patients got open surgery, nothing in the data could say how PCNL does on large stones.

Inverse-probability weighting (IPW) uses the score to build a pseudo-population in which treatment no longer depends on X. Each treated unit gets weight 1/e(x) and each control unit 1/(1 − e(x)). In the kidney data, a small-stone patient had e = 87/357 = 0.244 of getting open surgery, a large-stone patient 263/343 = 0.767. Then:

GroupCountWeightWeighted count
Open, small871 / 0.244 = 4.10357
Open, large2631 / 0.767 = 1.30343
PCNL, small2701 / 0.756 = 1.32357
PCNL, large801 / 0.233 = 4.29343

After weighting, each treatment arm has 357 small and 343 large stones — the full population's mix — so stone size no longer predicts treatment. The rare cases (open surgery on a small stone) count four times because each stands in for about four similar patients who got the other treatment. The weighted success rates are (81 × 4.10 + 192 × 1.30) / 700 = 0.833 for open surgery and 0.779 for PCNL — exactly the standardised numbers, as they must be when the propensity model is just the observed proportions in each cell.

The weakness of IPW is in the weights. If some unit has e(x) = 0.01 and was treated, its weight is 100, and a handful of such units can dominate the estimate and its variance. Near-zero or near-one propensities are also a warning that overlap is poor: the data barely contains comparable units. Practical responses are to plot the propensity distributions of both arms and check they overlap, to use stabilised weights (multiply by the marginal treatment probability, which keeps the mean weight near one), to trim extreme scores (which changes the population you're describing — say so), and to check balance: after weighting, the standardised mean difference of each covariate between arms should be small (a common rule of thumb is below 0.1).

5. Matching, regression and what not to adjust for

Matching pairs each treated unit with one or more untreated units with similar covariates or similar propensity scores, discards the unmatched, and compares outcomes within pairs. It estimates the ATT naturally (every treated unit gets a counterpart) and is easy to explain to stakeholders, but throws data away and depends on choices — how close is close enough (the caliper), with or without replacement. Regression adjustment fits an outcome model including treatment and covariates and reads off the treatment coefficient, or more flexibly fits μ1(x) and μ0(x) and averages their difference (standardisation again, done with a model). It's efficient when the outcome model is right and quietly extrapolates when overlap is poor.

All adjustment methods share a question: which covariates? The answer comes from the causal structure, not from "include everything":

  • Confounders (common causes of T and Y) must be adjusted for.
  • Mediators (T → M → Y) must not be, if you want the total effect. If a coupon works by increasing app sessions, "controlling for sessions" erases most of the coupon's effect.
  • Colliders (T → C ← Y) must not be. If you analyse only users who contacted support, and both a bad experience and a new feature raise support contact, you can create an association between them that doesn't exist in the population.
  • Post-treatment variables in general — anything measured after treatment that treatment could have changed — are risky to adjust for.

6. Difference-in-differences

Sometimes a change rolls out to one group and not another. Suppose a redesigned notification system launched in Germany in March but not in France. Weekly orders per active user:

Feb (before)Apr (after)Change
Germany (treated)2.002.30+0.30
France (comparison)1.802.00+0.20
Difference+0.10

A before-after comparison in Germany alone says +0.30, but spring lifted France by +0.20 without any change. Difference-in-differences (DiD) subtracts the comparison group's change from the treated group's: 0.30 − 0.20 = +0.10 orders per user per week. It removes anything constant over time within a country (Germany simply orders more) and anything that changed equally for both (season, a global campaign).

The key assumption is parallel trends: without the launch, Germany would have changed by the same amount as France. It can't be verified for the post-period, but it can be made plausible by plotting several pre-launch months and checking the two lines moved together. Things that break it: the treated group was chosen because it was declining or growing unusually (regression to the mean then looks like an effect), something else changed in Germany at the same time (a competitor's exit, a holiday), or the treatment spilled into the comparison group. DiD is usually run as a regression, Y = α + β·treated + γ·post + δ·(treated × post), with δ the effect and standard errors clustered by group — and with only two groups, honest uncertainty is large. Card and Krueger's 1994 study of a minimum-wage rise in New Jersey, with neighbouring Pennsylvania as comparison, is the textbook example.

7. Regression discontinuity

Many treatments are assigned by a threshold on a score: a loyalty tier at £500 of annual spend, a credit offer above a risk score, free shipping above a basket value. Customers who spent £495 and £505 are almost identical in every respect except that one got the tier. Regression discontinuity (RD) compares outcomes just either side of the cutoff. Suppose 12-month retention is 61% among customers who spent £480–£500 and 66% among those who spent £500–£520, with retention rising smoothly with spend elsewhere: the jump of about 5 points at £500 estimates the tier's effect for customers near £500. In practice you fit a line (or a low-degree curve) on each side within a bandwidth and measure the gap at the cutoff, rather than comparing raw averages, because the outcome trends with the score.

RD's assumptions are that nothing else changes at the cutoff and that units can't precisely sort themselves across it. If customers learn that £500 unlocks the tier and buy a little extra to cross it, those just above differ from those just below (they're more engaged), and the comparison is biased. The tell-tale sign is a spike in the number of customers just above the threshold; checking the score's histogram for bunching is the first diagnostic. RD's answer is also local: it says nothing about the tier's effect for a £2,000 spender.

8. Instrumental variables

You want the effect of adopting a new budgeting feature on 90-day retention. Adopters are self-selected — keen users adopt — so comparing adopters with non-adopters is confounded, and you can't force anyone to adopt. But you can randomise an encouragement: half of users get an email promoting the feature. Results:

                  adoption   retention
email (random 50%)   30%        42%
no email             10%        40%
difference          +20 pts    +2 pts

Wald estimate = 2 / 20 = 0.10  → adopting raises retention by 10 points (for compliers)

The email is an instrumental variable: it's random (so unconfounded), it changes adoption (relevance), and it plausibly affects retention only through adoption (the exclusion restriction). The email raised retention by 2 points by moving 20% of users into adoption, so adoption itself must be worth about 10 points for those users. That's the local average treatment effect (LATE): the effect for compliers, users who adopt if and only if emailed — not for keen users who adopt anyway or users who never would. Two further assumptions are needed: no one is put off adopting by the email (monotonicity), and the email has no direct effect on retention (if it also reminded people the app exists, the exclusion restriction fails and 10 points is an overestimate). Weak instruments — an email that moves adoption by half a point — make the division explode any small violation into a large bias.

9. From average effects to uplift

Back to the coupon. A randomised holdout tells you the ATE: say coupons raise 90-day renewal by 3 points on average. To decide whom to send them to, you need the CATE, τ(x): the expected uplift for a customer with features x. Marketers describe four kinds of customer by their two potential outcomes:

Renews if treatedDoesn't renew if treated
Renews if not treatedSure thing — coupon wastedSleeping dog — contact makes it worse
Doesn't renew if not treatedPersuadable — the only group worth treatingLost cause — coupon wasted

A response model scores P(renew | treated) high for both persuadables and sure things. An uplift model aims to score persuadables highest and sleeping dogs lowest. Sleeping dogs are real: a retention call can remind a customer that they meant to cancel; a notification can prompt an uninstall.

Uplift models are usually built with meta-learners — recipes that turn any supervised learner (gradient-boosted trees, logistic regression) into a CATE estimator, trained on randomised data or on observational data with the adjustments above:

  • S-learner ("single"): one model μ(x, t) with treatment as a feature; τ(x) = μ(x, 1) − μ(x, 0). Simple, but regularisation can shrink the treatment feature's influence to nearly nothing, biasing the uplift toward zero — trees may never split on it.
  • T-learner ("two"): separate models μ1(x) on treated units and μ0(x) on controls; τ(x) = μ1(x) − μ0(x). Flexible, but each model's errors land in the difference, and a small arm (a 5% holdout) gives a poor model whose noise dominates.
  • X-learner (Künzel et al.): fit μ0, μ1 as in the T-learner; then impute individual effects — for each treated unit D = Y − μ0(x), for each control D = μ1(x) − Y; fit τ1(x) on the treated units' imputed effects and τ0(x) on the controls'; combine τ(x) = g(x)·τ0(x) + (1 − g(x))·τ1(x), usually with g = the propensity score. When controls are scarce, μ0 is poor but τ1 is fitted on the large treated arm, and the weighting leans on it; that's exactly the unbalanced case where Künzel et al. show it beats the T-learner.

Other approaches estimate τ directly: uplift trees split to maximise the difference in treatment effect between child nodes rather than purity, causal forests average many such trees with honest splitting, and the transformed-outcome trick builds a label whose expectation equals τ(x) on randomised data (with 50/50 assignment, Z = 2Y for treated and −2Y for controls), so any regression model can be fit to it, at the cost of high variance.

10. Evaluating uplift: the Qini curve

There is no ground-truth uplift per customer, so no AUC. Instead, on a randomised validation set, sort customers by predicted uplift, take the top k%, and ask: how many extra conversions did treatment produce in this slice? With NT, NC the treated and control counts in the slice and YT, YC their conversions, the Qini value is YT − YC · NT/NC — treated conversions minus the conversions they'd have had, estimated from the control group scaled to the same size. Worked, with 1,000 customers per arm split into quartiles of predicted uplift (250 treated and 250 controls in each):

QuartileTreated conv.Control conv.IncrementalCumulative (Qini)Random targeting
Top 25%50 (20%)20 (8%)+30308.75
25–50%35 (14%)25 (10%)+104017.5
50–75%25 (10%)22 (8.8%)+34326.25
Bottom 25%15 (6%)23 (9.2%)−83535

Treating everyone yields 35 extra conversions — the ATE times the population. Treating the top quarter yields 30, for a quarter of the cost; the top three-quarters yields 43, more than everyone, because the bottom quarter contains sleeping dogs. The area between the curve and the random line (here, with x as the share targeted, about 32.6 − 17.5 ≈ 15.1) summarises the model in one number for comparing models. The business decision then needs costs: if each coupon costs £2 and each incremental renewal is worth £40, the top quartile earns 30 × £40 = £1,200 against 250 × £2 = £500 of coupons, a net £700, and so on down the curve; the optimum is where the next slice's incremental value no longer covers its cost.

11. Where interviewers use this

Marketing offers: whom to give a discount, which is the coupon story. Notifications (hd19): a notification that brings back a user who'd have returned anyway wastes attention and risks an opt-out, so good systems estimate the incremental effect of sending versus not sending, keep a random holdout, and treat unsubscribes as a guardrail. Pricing: demand falls as price rises, but prices are usually set in response to demand (raised in peak season), so naive regressions of quantity on price can even show demand rising with price; randomised price tests, or instruments such as cost shocks, are the fix. In each, the interviewer is listening for the same three moves: name the confounding, say how you'd get a fair comparison, and say what you'd do with the effect estimate.

Doubly robust estimation and double machine learning

IPW needs a correct propensity model; regression adjustment needs a correct outcome model. The augmented IPW (AIPW) estimator combines them. For the treated potential outcome, it averages μ1(x) + T·(Y − μ1(x))/e(x) over all units: the outcome model's prediction, plus a weighted correction from the treated units' residuals. If μ1 is right, residuals average zero and the correction does nothing; if e is right, the weighted residuals remove μ1's bias. Either being correct is enough — hence "doubly robust". With flexible machine-learning models for both, overfitting can bias the result, so double/debiased machine learning uses cross-fitting: models are trained on one fold and used to predict on another, which, together with the AIPW form's insensitivity to small errors in either model, gives estimates with valid confidence intervals. None of this relaxes the core assumption; it reduces dependence on model specification, not on having measured the confounders.

Sensitivity analysis for unmeasured confounding

"No unmeasured confounding" can't be tested, so a senior answer quantifies how wrong it would need to be. The E-value (VanderWeele and Ding) is the minimum strength of association, measured as a risk ratio (RR, the outcome rate in one group divided by that in the other), that a hidden confounder would need with both treatment and outcome to explain away an observed effect: for an observed risk ratio RR > 1 it's RR + √(RR(RR − 1)). An observed RR of 2 has an E-value of 3.4: a hidden factor would need to triple both the odds of treatment and of the outcome. If plausible confounders are that strong, the result is fragile. Simpler versions — adding the strongest measured covariates one at a time and watching how much the estimate moves — make the same point informally.

Target trial emulation

Hernán and Robins argue that observational analyses go wrong less from statistics than from design: comparing users at different points in time, conditioning on things that happen after treatment, or defining "treated" by behaviour that only survivors could show (immortal time bias — users who "used the feature for a month" must have stayed a month). Their remedy is to write down the randomised trial you would run — eligibility, treatment strategies, assignment, start of follow-up, outcome, analysis — and make the observational analysis imitate it, especially by starting follow-up at the moment of treatment decision for both arms. In product analytics this catches most of the classic mistakes before any model is fitted.

Heterogeneous effects without fishing

Looking at twenty segments and reporting the one where the treatment worked is the multiple-comparisons trap of ml16 in another form. Methods that estimate τ(x) — causal forests, X-learners — reduce it by fitting effect heterogeneity with honest sample splitting (the data that chooses the splits isn't the data that estimates the effects), and their output should be validated like a model: on held-out randomised data, does the predicted-high-uplift group really show a larger measured effect than the predicted-low group? A calibration check for uplift is to bin by predicted τ and compare with the measured treated-minus-control difference in each bin.

Policy learning and the cost of the decision

An uplift model is a means; the end is a policy — a rule mapping x to treat or not. The best policy treats where τ(x) × value > cost, and the value of a policy can be estimated on randomised data with inverse-propensity scoring: average outcomes of units whose random assignment happened to agree with the policy, reweighted by the assignment probability (the same off-policy idea as in ml16). With multiple treatments (10% off, 20% off, free month) the problem becomes choosing the best treatment per user, and a model that only estimates uplift versus control for each is the natural extension. Contextual bandits go one step further and keep randomising a little to learn as they act.

When the treatment changes who is measured

Many product outcomes are only observed for some units: spend among purchasers, time-to-next-session among returning users. If treatment changes who purchases, comparing spend among purchasers is a collider-style bias — treatment pulls in marginal, lower-spending customers, and average spend among purchasers can fall even when the treatment raises total revenue. The fix is to analyse outcomes defined for everyone (revenue per user, including zeros) or to use principal-stratification arguments explicitly. This catches out many experiment readouts, not just observational studies.

Interference and equilibrium effects

Potential outcomes assume one unit's treatment doesn't affect another's (the stable unit treatment value assumption, SUTVA). Coupons in a two-sided marketplace violate it: a discount that increases riders' demand also changes driver availability for everyone. Uplift estimated in a small test may then not survive scaling: target 1% of users and you measure the effect with everything else held fixed; target 50% and prices, supply or competitors respond. The cluster and switchback designs of ps6 are the experimental answer; in observational work, the honest answer is to state that the estimate is a small-scale, partial-equilibrium effect.

Characteristic failures

Targeting by response instead of uplift. The symptom is a campaign with impressive conversion among recipients and no measurable lift against a holdout — or no holdout at all, so nobody can tell. The fix is cultural as much as technical: no campaign without a randomised holdout, and a dashboard that reports incremental conversions rather than conversions.

The model poisons its own training data. Once an uplift (or response) model chooses whom to treat, treated and untreated users differ systematically, and the next model trained on that data inherits the confounding. Production systems keep a small random slice — say 5–10% of eligible users assigned at random, including some who'd never be targeted — which provides unbiased training data, an ongoing estimate of the policy's real effect, and a guard against drift. CausalML and similar libraries assume such randomised or well-understood assignment data.

Propensity estimates that look fine but aren't. Symptoms: a few units with weights in the hundreds; an effect estimate that swings when a covariate is added; covariate balance that's good on means but bad on tails. Diagnostics to run every time: overlap histograms of e(x) by arm, the maximum and distribution of weights, standardised mean differences before and after weighting, and the effective sample size, (Σw)²/Σw², which shows how many units the weighted estimate is really based on.

Post-treatment features leak in. In uplift and propensity models alike, features computed after the treatment decision (sessions in the week after the email) make the model look excellent and the estimates wrong. Freeze features at the decision time, exactly as in leakage prevention (ml9).

Uplift collapses at scale. Effects measured on a small test cohort can shrink when the policy is rolled out — saturation (users get offers from three campaigns), competitor response, or interference. Roll out in stages with the holdout kept, and compare the realised incremental effect with the model's prediction for the treated population.

Metrics and tooling

For uplift: Qini and uplift curves and their areas on randomised validation data, uplift by predicted-decile bins (a calibration plot for τ), and the realised incremental profit of the deployed policy against the holdout. For observational estimates: balance tables, overlap plots, effective sample size, pre-trend plots for DiD, score-density plots for RD bunching, first-stage strength for IV (the effect of the instrument on treatment). Common open-source tools include CausalML (Uber), EconML (Microsoft) and DoWhy for estimation and refutation tests.

The public case: kidney stones, revisited

The Charig et al. study is used in textbooks because the reversal is real data, not a contrived example, and because the explanation required clinical knowledge: stone size affected both which procedure a surgeon chose and how likely it was to succeed. The lesson for product teams is identical. Whenever the people who received a treatment were chosen — by a surgeon, a sales rep, a targeting model or the users themselves — the raw comparison describes the choosing as much as the treatment, and the analysis has to say what drove the choice. If the drivers were logged, adjustment can work; if they weren't (a surgeon's judgement, a rep's intuition), only randomisation can.

Practical playbook

  • Build randomised holdouts into every repeated intervention: campaigns, notifications, offers, recommendations.
  • When an observational question comes up, first look for a design (a rollout boundary, a threshold, a random nudge), then for adjustment, and write down the assumption each relies on.
  • Report effects with intervals and with the population they apply to (ATE, ATT, compliers, units near a cutoff).
  • For uplift, decide on profit, not on Qini area alone: include treatment cost and the value of an incremental outcome.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 200_000

# 1. Confounded data: "engaged" users get more coupons AND renew more anyway.
engaged = rng.random(n) < 0.4
p_treat = np.where(engaged, 0.8, 0.2)            # targeting favours engaged users
T = rng.random(n) < p_treat
base = np.where(engaged, 0.60, 0.20)             # renewal without coupon
effect = np.where(engaged, 0.01, 0.08)           # true uplift: small for engaged
Y = rng.random(n) < base + T * effect
true_ate = 0.4 * 0.01 + 0.6 * 0.08
print(f"true ATE        {true_ate:.4f}")

# 2. Naive comparison
print(f"naive diff      {Y[T].mean() - Y[~T].mean():.4f}")

# 3. Stratified comparison (Simpson's paradox check)
for g in (True, False):
    m = engaged == g
    print(f"engaged={g!s:5}  treated {Y[m & T].mean():.3f}  control {Y[m & ~T].mean():.3f}")

# 4. IPW with a propensity estimated from the data
e = np.where(engaged, T[engaged].mean(), T[~engaged].mean())
w = np.where(T, 1 / e, 1 / (1 - e))
ipw = (w * Y * T).sum() / (w * T).sum() - (w * Y * ~T).sum() / (w * ~T).sum()
print(f"IPW estimate    {ipw:.4f}")
print("max weight", round(w.max(), 2), " effective n", round(w.sum()**2 / (w**2).sum()))

# 5. Uplift by segment: who should get the coupon?
print("uplift engaged ≈", round(Y[engaged & T].mean() - Y[engaged & ~T].mean(), 3),
      " not engaged ≈", round(Y[~engaged & T].mean() - Y[~engaged & ~T].mean(), 3))

What to look for. The true ATE is 0.052. The naive difference is far larger (around 0.26), because coupon recipients are mostly engaged users who renew anyway — selection bias, not effect. Within each stratum the treated-minus-control gap is close to the true segment effect (≈0.01 and ≈0.08), and the IPW estimate lands near 0.052. The effective sample size is well below 200,000 because weights of 5 on the rare cases inflate variance. Step 5 is the uplift lesson: the response rate is highest among engaged users, but the coupon's effect is eight times larger among the others. Try setting p_treat to 0.99 for engaged users and watch the maximum weight and the estimate's stability.

Diagnose and design

A growth team reports: "Users who received our win-back push notification had 35% 30-day retention versus 12% for users who didn't — a 23-point lift." The push was sent by a model to lapsed users it scored as 'likely to return'. Is the claim valid, and what would you do?

Answer

The claim is confounded. The model chose recipients precisely because they were likely to return, so recipients would have had higher retention without any push. The 23 points is the ATT plus selection bias, and the selection bias is probably most of it. Name the confounder: the model's score (and the features behind it — recent activity, tenure).

Rescue what you can from existing data. If sends were decided by a score threshold, compare users just above and just below it (regression discontinuity), after checking that nothing else happens at the threshold. If not, estimate propensities from the score's inputs, weight or match, and check overlap: if almost everyone above some score was sent the push and almost no one below, there are no comparable controls in large regions and the answer is fragile. Report the estimate with the assumptions spelled out.

Fix it going forward. Add a randomised holdout: among users the model selects, withhold the push from a random 10%. The retention difference within the selected group is then the true ATT for the policy. Also randomly send to a small slice of users the model didn't select, which gives uplift-model training data across the whole score range — the model may be targeting sure things, while the persuadables are mid-score users. Track unsubscribes and uninstalls as guardrails: for some users the push is a sleeping-dog trigger.
