A team has 1,800 labelled protein samples, each described by 400 measured properties, and needs a classifier that separates two functional families. Gradient-boosted trees overfit and wobble from fold to fold; a neural network has far too little data to learn anything. Someone trains a support vector machine with a radial kernel, tunes two numbers by cross-validation, and gets the most accurate and most stable model of the lot in an afternoon. Twenty years ago this was the default outcome on almost every classification benchmark, and support vector machines (SVMs) were the method to beat.

Today they're rarely the first thing anyone reaches for, but they keep appearing in interviews for two reasons. First, the ideas inside them are some of the most reusable in machine learning: the margin as a measure of how robust a classifier is, hinge loss as an alternative to logistic loss, the view of a model as "loss plus regulariser", Lagrangian duality, and the kernel trick, which shows up again in Gaussian processes, kernel approximations and the theory of wide neural networks. Second, the history is itself a lesson in trade-offs: SVMs lost their crown not because they were wrong but because their cost grows with the square of the dataset size, and because deep networks learn features instead of relying on a fixed kernel. A senior candidate should be able to explain both why SVMs work and why they faded.

Interviewers probe this topic in predictable ways. Can you explain the maximum-margin idea with a picture? What does C do, and which direction is "more regularised"? How is hinge loss different from logistic loss? Why do only some training points matter? What exactly is the kernel trick, and what does γ do? Why don't people train kernel SVMs on ten million rows? Each of those has a crisp answer that rests on one or two steps of real reasoning, and this lesson builds them in order.

The lesson starts with the geometry of a linear classifier and the margin, solves a tiny maximum-margin problem by hand, relaxes it to the soft margin, and rewrites that as hinge loss plus an L2 penalty — the form that connects it to logistic regression (ml6). It then derives the dual with Lagrange multipliers (math11), shows why only support vectors matter, and turns inner products into kernels, with worked polynomial and RBF examples. It finishes with the computational cost, the reasons SVMs faded at scale, and the places they still win.

The widest street

Picture two neighbourhoods on a map, red houses on one side and blue on the other, and the job of building a straight road that separates them. Many roads would separate them; some pass a few metres from a house, others leave plenty of room on both sides. The support vector machine's rule is: build the widest possible road, with the dividing line down its middle. The width of the road is the margin. The houses touching the road's edges — the only ones that stop it from being wider — are the support vectors. Move any other house around, as long as it stays off the road, and the road doesn't change.

Why wide is good

A boundary that skims past training points is fragile: a new point that's slightly different from a training point — a little noise in a measurement — can land on the wrong side. A wide margin leaves room for that noise. There's also a theoretical version of this intuition: classifiers with wide margins relative to the spread of the data are "simple" in a precise sense, whatever the number of features, and simple classifiers generalise. The analogy breaks in one place: real data overlaps, so a perfectly empty road usually doesn't exist; the SVM then allows some houses on or across the road and charges a price for each one. That price is the parameter C.

Three ideas in one model

An SVM combines three ideas, which this lesson takes in order. The margin: a linear classifier chosen to be as far as possible from the nearest training points. Hinge loss: rewritten as "loss plus regularisation", the SVM is a linear model like logistic regression, trained with a different loss that ignores points that are already comfortably correct. Kernels: the training problem can be rewritten so that the data only appears through similarities between pairs of points, and swapping in a different similarity function — a kernel — gives curved boundaries while the mathematics stays that of a linear classifier.

The two knobs, before any maths

Almost every practical question about an RBF SVM comes down to two numbers. C answers "how much do I care about classifying every training point correctly?" — turn it up and the street narrows to squeeze past awkward points; turn it down and the street stays wide while a few points are allowed onto it or across it. γ (gamma) answers "how far does one training point's influence reach?" — turn it up and each point only affects its immediate neighbourhood, so the boundary can curl around individual points; turn it down and every point influences a broad area, so the boundary is smooth. Turning both up at once is the classic way to memorise a training set. Keep these two pictures in mind; the body of the lesson explains where each one comes from.

What it costs

Those pairwise similarities are the catch. A dataset of n points has n² pairs. At 10,000 points that's 100 million kernel values, comfortable; at a million points it's a trillion, which is why kernel SVMs are a small-to-medium-data tool and why the deep-learning era passed them by. Linear SVMs, which don't need the pairs, scale to millions of examples and are still used.

1. The geometry of a linear classifier

A linear classifier computes a score f(x) = w·x + b — a weighted sum of the features plus an offset, as in logistic regression (ml6) — and predicts +1 if f(x) > 0 and −1 otherwise. (SVMs label classes +1 and −1 rather than 1 and 0 because it makes the formulas tidy.) The set of points where f(x) = 0 is a hyperplane: a line in two dimensions, a plane in three. The vector w is perpendicular to it.

How far is a point from the boundary? The distance is |w·x + b| / ‖w‖, where ‖w‖ is the length of w. Worked: with w = (1, 1) and b = −3, the boundary is the line x₁ + x₂ = 3. The point (3, 2) has f = 3 + 2 − 3 = 2, and ‖w‖ = √2, so it sits 2/√2 ≈ 1.41 units from the line. The score alone isn't a distance: doubling w and b to (2, 2) and −6 describes the same line but gives f = 4. Dividing by ‖w‖ undoes the scale.

Multiplying the score by the true label y gives the functional margin y·f(x), positive when the point is on the correct side. Dividing that by ‖w‖ gives the geometric margin, the signed distance. The classifier's margin on a dataset is the smallest geometric margin over all points — the distance to the nearest point.

2. The hard-margin SVM, solved by hand

We want the w and b that maximise the distance to the nearest point. Because rescaling w and b doesn't move the boundary, we're free to fix the scale: choose it so the closest points have y·f(x) = 1 exactly. Then every point satisfies y·f(x) ≥ 1, the nearest ones lie at distance 1/‖w‖ on each side, and the margin — the full width of the street — is 2/‖w‖. Maximising 2/‖w‖ is the same as minimising ‖w‖, or more conveniently ½‖w‖²:

minimise   ½‖w‖²
subject to yᵢ(w·xᵢ + b) ≥ 1   for every training point i

This is the hard-margin SVM: a convex quadratic objective with linear constraints, so it has a single global optimum and no local minima to get stuck in.

A two-point example. Take one negative point at (1, 1) and one positive at (3, 3). By symmetry the best boundary is the perpendicular bisector of the segment between them, the line x₁ + x₂ = 4, so w points along (1, 1): w = (a, a) and b = −4a. The constraint for (3, 3) is 6a − 4a = 2a ≥ 1, and for (1, 1) it's −(2a − 4a) = 2a ≥ 1. The smallest ‖w‖ satisfying both has a = 0.5: w = (0.5, 0.5), b = −2. Check: f(3, 3) = 1.5 + 1.5 − 2 = 1 and f(1, 1) = −1, both exactly on the margin. ‖w‖ = √0.5 ≈ 0.707, so the margin is 2/0.707 ≈ 2.83 — exactly the distance between the two points, 2√2. Both points are support vectors.

3. The soft margin and C

Real data overlaps, so no boundary satisfies every constraint, and the hard-margin problem has no solution. A single mislabelled point can also drag a hard-margin boundary far from where it should be. The soft-margin SVM gives each point a slack variable ξᵢ ≥ 0, how far it falls short of the margin requirement, and charges for it:

minimise   ½‖w‖² + C · Σ ξᵢ
subject to yᵢ(w·xᵢ + b) ≥ 1 − ξᵢ,   ξᵢ ≥ 0

A point beyond the margin on the correct side has ξ = 0. A point inside the margin but correctly classified has 0 < ξ ≤ 1. A misclassified point has ξ > 1. The parameter C sets the exchange rate between margin width and violations. With C = 1,000, a single unit of slack costs as much as a big change in ‖w‖², so the SVM bends to classify nearly every training point correctly, narrowing the margin: low bias, high variance. With C = 0.01, violations are cheap, so the SVM keeps a wide margin and lets many points sit inside it or on the wrong side: more bias, less variance. Large C means less regularisation — the most commonly confused direction in interviews.

4. Hinge loss: the SVM as regularised empirical risk

At the optimum, each slack is as small as its constraint allows: ξᵢ = max(0, 1 − yᵢf(xᵢ)). Substituting that back removes the constraints entirely:

minimise   ½‖w‖² + C · Σ max(0, 1 − yᵢ(w·xᵢ + b))

Divide by Cn and write λ = 1/(Cn):

minimise   (λ/2)‖w‖² + (1/n) Σ max(0, 1 − yᵢ f(xᵢ))

That's empirical risk minimisation — average loss on the training data — plus an L2 penalty (ml3), exactly the form of regularised logistic regression. The only difference is the loss: the hinge loss max(0, 1 − m), where m = y·f(x) is the margin of one point. Here it is next to logistic loss, log(1 + e−m) (ml5), and the 0/1 loss that we actually care about but can't optimise directly:

Two consequences follow. Hinge loss ignores points that are already safely correct, which is why the solution depends only on support vectors. And because the hinge minimiser targets the sign of the best decision rather than the probability, an SVM's scores aren't probabilities; logistic regression's are estimates of P(y = 1 | x). In practice the two losses usually give similar accuracy on the same features; the choice is about whether you need probabilities and whether you want a sparse kernel solution.

The unconstrained form also gives a simple training algorithm. Hinge loss has a kink at m = 1 and no gradient there, but it has a subgradient: −y·x when m < 1, zero when m > 1 (and either at the kink). Stochastic subgradient descent on this objective — the Pegasos algorithm — updates w ← (1 − ηλ)w + η·y·x for each sampled point with margin below 1, and w ← (1 − ηλ)w otherwise, with step size η = 1/(λt) at step t. The (1 − ηλ) factor is the L2 penalty shrinking w; the second term pushes w towards misclassified or too-close points. The lab implements exactly this.

5. The dual, and why only support vectors matter

The constrained form has another life through Lagrangian duality (math11). Attach a multiplier αᵢ ≥ 0 to each margin constraint of the hard-margin problem:

L(w, b, α) = ½‖w‖² − Σ αᵢ [ yᵢ(w·xᵢ + b) − 1 ]

Setting the derivatives with respect to w and b to zero gives two facts:

∂L/∂w = 0  →  w = Σ αᵢ yᵢ xᵢ
∂L/∂b = 0  →  Σ αᵢ yᵢ = 0

Substituting w back into L eliminates w and b and leaves the dual problem, an optimisation over the α's only:

maximise   Σ αᵢ − ½ Σᵢ Σⱼ αᵢ αⱼ yᵢ yⱼ (xᵢ · xⱼ)
subject to αᵢ ≥ 0,  Σ αᵢ yᵢ = 0        (soft margin: 0 ≤ αᵢ ≤ C)

For a convex problem like this one, the dual's optimum equals the primal's (strong duality), and the optimality (KKT) conditions include complementary slackness: αᵢ · [yᵢf(xᵢ) − 1] = 0 for every point. So either a point's constraint is tight — it sits exactly on the margin — or its multiplier is zero. Points comfortably beyond the margin get αᵢ = 0 and vanish from w = Σ αᵢyᵢxᵢ. The points with αᵢ > 0 are the support vectors. (In the soft margin, points inside the margin or misclassified also have αᵢ > 0, capped at C.)

The two-point example, from the dual side. The constraint Σ αᵢyᵢ = 0 forces α₁ = α₂ = α. Then w = α·(+1)·(3, 3) + α·(−1)·(1, 1) = α·(2, 2). The dual objective is 2α − ½‖w‖² = 2α − ½·8α² = 2α − 4α², maximised at α = 0.25. That gives w = (0.5, 0.5), the same answer as section 2, and the dual objective value 0.5 − 0.25 = 0.25 equals the primal ½‖w‖² = ½ × 0.5 = 0.25. Strong duality, checked by hand.

Prediction uses the same form: f(x) = Σ αᵢyᵢ (xᵢ·x) + b, a weighted vote of the support vectors, each weighted by how similar it is to the new point (as measured by the inner product). Two features of the dual set up everything that follows. It has one variable per training point, regardless of how many features there are. And the data appears only through inner products xᵢ·xⱼ.

6. The kernel trick

Some data isn't linearly separable in its original features. The classic case is XOR: positives at (1, 1) and (−1, −1), negatives at (1, −1) and (−1, 1). No line separates them. Add one feature, the product x₁x₂, and the positives have x₁x₂ = +1 and the negatives −1: in the new feature, a threshold at zero separates them perfectly.

In general, mapping x to a richer feature vector φ(x) — squares, products, higher powers — makes more problems linearly separable, but the feature vector explodes: all products of degree up to 3 of 1,000 features is about 170 million features. Here the dual pays off. Training and prediction only need inner products φ(xᵢ)·φ(xⱼ). If some function K(x, z) computes that inner product directly from x and z, we never need φ at all. Such a function is a kernel, and substituting it for every inner product is the kernel trick:

dual:        maximise Σ αᵢ − ½ Σᵢⱼ αᵢαⱼyᵢyⱼ K(xᵢ, xⱼ)
prediction:  f(x) = Σ αᵢ yᵢ K(xᵢ, x) + b

Polynomial kernel, checked by hand. For two-dimensional x and z, K(x, z) = (x·z)² equals φ(x)·φ(z) with φ(x) = (x₁², √2·x₁x₂, x₂²). With x = (1, 2) and z = (3, 1): x·z = 3 + 2 = 5, so K = 25. Explicitly, φ(x) = (1, 2√2, 4) and φ(z) = (9, 3√2, 1), and φ(x)·φ(z) = 9 + 12 + 4 = 25. One multiplication-and-square replaces building and multiplying three-dimensional (and, for larger degree and dimension, enormous) vectors. The general polynomial kernel (γ·x·z + r)d covers all monomials up to degree d.

The RBF kernel. The radial basis function (Gaussian) kernel is K(x, z) = exp(−γ‖x − z‖²): similarity 1 for identical points, decaying towards 0 with squared distance. Its feature space is infinite-dimensional, yet each evaluation costs one distance and one exponential. With an RBF kernel, f(x) = Σ αᵢyᵢ exp(−γ‖xᵢ − x‖²) + b is a sum of bumps centred on the support vectors, and γ sets their width. For two points at squared distance 4:

So an RBF SVM has two knobs: γ (how local each support vector's influence is) and C (how much violations cost). Both large means a wiggly boundary that memorises the training set — with almost every point a support vector, a red flag you can read off the fitted model. Not every function is a valid kernel: K must produce a positive semi-definite Gram matrix (the n × n table of K(xᵢ, xⱼ)) for any set of points, which is Mercer's condition. That guarantees an underlying feature space exists and keeps the dual convex. Sums, products and positive multiples of valid kernels are valid, which is how custom kernels for strings, graphs and molecules were built.

7. Practicalities: scaling, multiclass, probabilities

Scale the features. The RBF kernel and the margin are both distance-based (ml11): a feature measured in thousands swamps one measured in fractions. The LIBSVM authors' practical guide recommends scaling each feature to [−1, 1] or [0, 1], using the training set's ranges for the test set too, starting with the RBF kernel, and choosing C and γ by cross-validated grid search over exponential grids — they suggest C = 2⁻⁵, 2⁻³, …, 2¹⁵ and γ = 2⁻¹⁵, 2⁻¹³, …, 2³. scikit-learn's SVC defaults are C = 1 and γ = 'scale', which sets γ = 1/(n_features × variance of X) (the default since version 0.22).

Multiclass. SVMs are binary. One-vs-rest trains one classifier per class against all others; one-vs-one trains one per pair of classes and takes a vote — LIBSVM's approach, and so scikit-learn SVC's. For kernel SVMs, one-vs-one's many small problems are cheaper than a few large ones because cost grows faster than linearly with n.

Probabilities. Scores aren't probabilities. scikit-learn's probability=True fits Platt scaling (ml15) — a logistic regression on the scores — using an internal five-fold cross-validation, which is expensive and can produce probabilities that disagree with the predicted class near 0.5. If you only need a ranking, use the decision function directly.

Imbalance. With rare positives, the margin can be placed to sacrifice the minority class. Class weights (a larger C for the minority class) are the standard fix, as in ml8.

8. Cost, and why SVMs faded at scale

A kernel SVM needs kernel values between all pairs of training points. For n = 100,000 points that's 10¹⁰ entries — 80 GB in 64-bit floats — so solvers compute kernel rows on demand with a cache (scikit-learn's default cache is 200 MB). The standard solver, SMO, repeatedly optimises two multipliers at a time; scikit-learn documents its scaling as between O(n_features × n²) and O(n_features × n³) depending on how well the cache works, and warns that SVC fit time "may be impractical beyond tens of thousands of samples". Prediction costs one kernel evaluation per support vector, and on noisy data the number of support vectors grows roughly in proportion to n, so a big model is also slow to serve.

Three changes around 2010–2015 moved the field elsewhere. Datasets grew to millions of examples, where O(n²) is prohibitive and minibatch SGD's O(n) per epoch isn't. Deep networks learn their representation, while a kernel SVM's representation is fixed by the choice of kernel, so on images, audio and text the learned features won decisively. And GPUs made dense matrix arithmetic on minibatches extremely cheap, which suits neural networks and suits pairwise kernel computations much less. Linear SVMs escaped the first problem — liblinear and SGD train them in time roughly linear in the number of non-zero features — but they're then essentially interchangeable with logistic regression.

9. Where SVMs still win

  • Small, clean tabular or scientific datasets — hundreds to tens of thousands of rows, well-chosen features. The convex objective gives the same answer every run, there are only two hyperparameters, and the margin regularises well when data is scarce.
  • Sparse, high-dimensional text with a linear kernel. Joachims (1998) showed why: many relevant features, few irrelevant ones, and documents that are close to linearly separable. A linear SVM or logistic regression on TF-IDF features remains a strong, cheap baseline.
  • A classifier on frozen embeddings. With a few hundred labelled examples and embeddings from a pretrained model, a linear or RBF SVM on the embeddings is a fast, stable head.
  • Novelty detection. The one-class SVM learns a boundary around normal data and flags points outside it — a standard method alongside isolation forests (ts10).
  • Regression with outlier tolerance. Support vector regression ignores errors smaller than ε and penalises larger ones linearly, which is robust to some outliers.

The representer theorem and the kernel view of learning

The fact that w = Σ αᵢyᵢφ(xᵢ) isn't special to SVMs. The representer theorem says that for any loss on the training points plus an increasing function of ‖w‖² (in the feature space defined by a kernel), the optimum is a weighted sum of kernel functions centred at the training points. So kernel ridge regression, kernel logistic regression and Gaussian-process regression (whose posterior mean has the same form) all inherit the kernel trick — and all inherit the n × n matrix and its cost. What distinguishes the SVM is hinge loss, which makes most weights exactly zero; kernel logistic regression keeps every point, so its predictions are slower even when its accuracy is similar.

Why the margin controls overfitting

Generalisation bounds based on counting parameters or VC dimension in d dimensions are useless in an infinite-dimensional RBF feature space. Margin bounds avoid dimension entirely: for data inside a ball of radius R separated with margin γ_m, the capacity of the class of such classifiers is bounded by roughly (R/γ_m)², whatever the dimension. A second, simpler argument: the leave-one-out error of a hard-margin SVM is at most (number of support vectors)/n, because removing a non-support vector leaves the solution unchanged, so it's still classified correctly. Both match what practitioners see: a model where nearly every point is a support vector is overfitting, and a model with a wide margin and few support vectors usually generalises.

Logistic regression secretly wants the max-margin solution

On linearly separable data, unregularised logistic regression has no finite optimum: scaling w up always lowers the loss. Soudry and colleagues (JMLR 2018) proved that gradient descent's iterates nonetheless converge in direction to the hard-margin SVM solution — the loss on the closest points dominates the gradient as w grows, just as only support vectors matter. This "implicit bias" result is one of the starting points of modern theory about why overparameterised networks trained by gradient descent generalise: the optimiser itself prefers large-margin solutions among the many that fit the data.

Solving the problem: SMO, coordinate descent and the primal

Platt's sequential minimal optimisation (SMO) exploits the dual's structure: the equality Σ αᵢyᵢ = 0 means you can't change one α alone, but you can change two at once, and that two-variable subproblem has a closed-form solution. SMO repeatedly picks a pair violating the optimality conditions, solves it exactly, and updates a cache of errors; with heuristics for choosing pairs and kernel caching it made kernel SVMs practical in the late 1990s. For linear SVMs, dual coordinate descent (liblinear) updates one α at a time while maintaining w explicitly, so each update costs the number of non-zero features of one point — this is why linear SVMs scale to millions of sparse documents. Primal subgradient methods like Pegasos converge in a number of steps that doesn't depend on n at all for a given accuracy, which suits streaming data.

Kernel approximation: getting the RBF without the n²

Random Fourier features (Rahimi and Recht, 2007) use the fact that the RBF kernel is the Fourier transform of a Gaussian: sampling D frequency vectors ωⱼ from a Gaussian and offsets bⱼ uniformly on [0, 2π] gives features z(x) = √(2/D)·cos(ωⱼ·x + bⱼ) whose inner products approximate K(x, z) with error shrinking like 1/√D. The Nyström method instead picks m landmark training points and builds features from their kernel values, adapting to the data. Either way you train a linear model on D or m features — linear in n — and trade a little accuracy for orders of magnitude of speed. This is the practical answer to "how would you use an RBF SVM on 10 million rows?".

ν-SVM, one-class SVM and SVR

C is hard to interpret, so the ν-SVM replaces it with ν ∈ (0, 1], which is an upper bound on the fraction of margin errors and a lower bound on the fraction of support vectors — a more intuitive knob. The one-class SVM (Schölkopf et al.) separates the training data from the origin in feature space with maximum margin; with an RBF kernel this draws a boundary around the dense region, and ν sets the expected fraction of training points treated as outliers. Support vector regression uses the ε-insensitive loss max(0, |y − f(x)| − ε): errors inside an ε-tube cost nothing, giving sparse solutions for regression too.

Kernels, Gaussian processes and wide networks

A kernel is also a covariance function: a Gaussian process with covariance K has a posterior mean of the same form as a kernel machine's prediction, with uncertainty estimates attached. And as neural networks become infinitely wide, training by gradient descent becomes equivalent to kernel regression with a specific kernel (the neural tangent kernel). These links are why kernels remain a live theoretical tool even where SVMs themselves are rarely deployed, and why an interviewer may follow an SVM question with "how is this related to Gaussian processes?".

The public case: Cortes and Vapnik, and the LIBSVM recipe

Cortes and Vapnik's 1995 paper "Support-Vector Networks" introduced the soft-margin SVM with kernels and evaluated it on handwritten digit recognition, the benchmark of the day, where polynomial-kernel SVMs were competitive with the best purpose-built methods without any hand-designed image features. Over the next decade SVMs became the default classifier in text categorisation, bioinformatics and computer vision pipelines built on hand-crafted features, and LIBSVM (Chang and Lin) became the standard implementation — it's still what scikit-learn's SVC wraps. The LIBSVM authors' practical guide exists because so many users got poor results by skipping steps: their recommended procedure is to scale features, start with the RBF kernel, cross-validate C and γ over exponential grids, retrain on the whole training set with the best pair, and switch to a linear kernel when the number of features is very large.

Characteristic failures

Unscaled features. Symptom: an RBF SVM near chance accuracy, or one feature dominating, or wildly different results after a unit change. Fix: standardise or min-max scale using training statistics inside the cross-validation pipeline (fitting the scaler on all data is leakage, ml9).

Too many support vectors. Symptom: support-vector count close to n, training accuracy near 100%, slow predictions. Cause: γ or C too large, or very noisy labels. Fix: lower γ and C, clean labels, or accept a linear model. The count is a free diagnostic — log it.

Training time explodes. Symptom: a job that took a minute at 20,000 rows takes hours at 200,000 — the quadratic-to-cubic scaling. Fix: subsample for model selection, increase cache size, use a linear kernel, or use kernel approximation (Nyström, random features) with a linear solver.

Probabilities that don't match predictions. Symptom: predict says positive while predict_proba says 0.45. Cause: Platt scaling fitted separately by internal cross-validation. Fix: use the decision function for ranking and thresholds, or calibrate explicitly on a held-out set (ml15).

Imbalanced classes. Symptom: high accuracy, near-zero recall on the minority class. Fix: class weights, then choose the operating threshold on the decision function with the metrics of ml7.

Serving

A linear SVM serves as a dot product, as cheap as logistic regression. A kernel SVM stores its support vectors and computes a kernel against each per request: 20,000 support vectors × 400 features is 8 million multiply-adds per prediction — fine for batch scoring, painful at high request rates. Teams that need kernel accuracy at low latency either approximate the kernel with explicit features or distil the SVM into a smaller model.

Tuning cost in practice

A 2-D grid of 11 values of C by 10 values of γ with five-fold cross-validation is 550 training runs. At 20,000 rows, where a single RBF fit can take a minute or more, that's an afternoon of compute for one model — another reason SVMs suit small data. Practical shortcuts: tune on a stratified subsample of a few thousand rows to find the right region, then refine on the full set; search on a coarse grid first (steps of 2² as the LIBSVM guide does) and zoom in; and parallelise, since every (C, γ) pair is independent. Record the support-vector count and fit time alongside the validation score for each pair, because the cheapest model within noise of the best is usually the one to ship.

When to pick an SVM today

Reach for it when data is small and features are meaningful (a few thousand labelled rows, or embeddings from a pretrained model), when you want a deterministic convex fit with two hyperparameters, or for novelty detection. Prefer gradient-boosted trees for medium-to-large tabular data with mixed feature types, logistic regression when probabilities matter, and fine-tuned neural models when raw text, images or audio and plenty of labels are available.

Goal: train a linear SVM by stochastic subgradient descent on hinge loss (the Pegasos algorithm), find the support vectors, and see what C does. NumPy only; Python 3.12 with the gpt_env NumPy.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# Two overlapping Gaussian blobs, labels in {-1, +1}
n = 400
X = np.vstack([rng.normal([-1.5, -1.0], 1.0, (n // 2, 2)),
               rng.normal([1.5, 1.0], 1.0, (n // 2, 2))])
y = np.r_[-np.ones(n // 2), np.ones(n // 2)]
# The blobs are symmetric about the origin, so we train without a bias term
# (Pegasos' 1/(lam*t) steps make an unregularised bias very noisy).
Xb = X

def pegasos(C, steps=50_000):
    lam = 1 / (C * n)                # lambda = 1 / (nC)
    w = np.zeros(2)
    for t in range(1, steps + 1):
        i = rng.integers(n)
        eta = 1 / (lam * t)
        margin = y[i] * Xb[i] @ w
        w *= (1 - eta * lam)         # L2 shrink
        if margin < 1:
            w += eta * y[i] * Xb[i]  # hinge subgradient step
    return w

for C in (0.01, 1, 100):
    w = pegasos(C)
    m = y * (Xb @ w)
    print(f"C={C:<6} w={np.round(w, 2)}  margin width={2 / np.linalg.norm(w):.2f}  "
          f"support vectors={(m <= 1 + 1e-9).sum():3d}  train acc={(m > 0).mean():.3f}")

# Hand check from the lesson: hinge vs logistic loss at a few margins
for mm in (-1, 0, 0.5, 1, 2):
    print(mm, max(0, 1 - mm), round(np.log1p(np.exp(-mm)), 3))

What to look for. As C grows, ‖w‖ grows, the margin width shrinks and the number of support vectors (points with margin ≤ 1) falls, while training accuracy changes only slightly — C trades margin width for fit. With C = 0.01 a large share of points sit inside the wide margin. The weight direction should point roughly along (1.5, 1.0), the line between the blob centres. The loss table should reproduce the lesson's numbers (hinge 2, 1, 0.5, 0, 0; logistic 1.313, 0.693, 0.474, 0.313, 0.127). Pegasos is stochastic, so expect small run-to-run variation; with a very large C the 1/(λt) step size starts huge and the result can be noisy — increase the steps if so.

Diagnose

An RBF SVM trained on 5,000 rows with C = 1000 and γ = 50 reaches 100% training accuracy and 61% validation accuracy. The fitted model has 4,870 support vectors. Predictions take 40 ms each. What is happening, and what do you do?

Answer

It's memorising. γ = 50 makes each support vector's influence a tiny bump — two points at squared distance 0.1 already have similarity exp(−5) ≈ 0.007 — so the decision function is a patchwork around individual training points. C = 1000 makes every training error expensive, so the SVM uses that freedom to fit all of them. The tell is the support-vector count: 97% of the training set, meaning almost no point is "safely" classified; leave-one-out error can be as high as that fraction.

First check the features are scaled. If one feature is in thousands, γ = 50 is effectively infinite for that feature and meaningless for the others; standardise inside the cross-validation pipeline.

Then search properly. Grid-search C and γ on log scales (for example C from 2⁻⁵ to 2¹⁵ and γ from 2⁻¹⁵ to 2³) with cross-validation, expecting the best γ to be far smaller. Watch the support-vector count fall as validation accuracy rises.

Latency follows. Prediction cost is proportional to the number of support vectors, so a well-regularised model with a few hundred support vectors is also an order of magnitude faster. If accuracy still needs a complex kernel and latency is tight, approximate the kernel (Nyström with a linear model) or compare against gradient-boosted trees, which are usually the stronger default for 5,000 tabular rows.
