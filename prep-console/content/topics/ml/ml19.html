An analyst at a subscription app is asked, "How long does a typical customer stay?" They pull every customer who has cancelled, compute the average time between signup and cancellation, and report 5.6 months. Finance builds a revenue forecast on it. A year later, the forecast has underestimated revenue badly. What went wrong is that the calculation only included customers who had already left. Half the customer base was still subscribed — some for two years and counting — and none of them were in the average. The customers most likely to stay a long time were exactly the ones excluded.

The fix isn't to include current customers with their tenure so far, either: a customer who joined three weeks ago and is still active hasn't "stayed three weeks", they've stayed at least three weeks. Their story isn't finished. This kind of incomplete observation is called censoring, and it's everywhere in product data. Trial users who haven't converted yet. Disks that haven't failed yet. Loans not yet repaid or defaulted. Any question of the form "how long until…?" runs into it, and the ordinary tools — averages, regression, binary classifiers — all mishandle it in ways that look reasonable and give wrong answers.

Survival analysis is the set of methods built for exactly this. It was developed for medical studies (how long do patients live after treatment, when some are still alive at the end of the study?) and reliability engineering (how long do components last?), and it carries straight over to churn, conversion, retention and failure prediction. It answers questions about the whole distribution of time — the chance of lasting past 6 months, the median lifetime, how risk changes with tenure — while using every subject's information, censored or not.

This lesson starts with censoring and the specific biases of ignoring it. It defines the survival and hazard functions, computes a Kaplan–Meier curve by hand on a 10-row table, and compares two groups with the log-rank test. It then builds the Cox proportional hazards model — the workhorse of survival regression — explains how its partial likelihood sidesteps the unknown baseline, and shows how to check its central assumption. It moves on to discrete-time models (survival as classification) and machine-learning approaches, explains the concordance index used to evaluate them, and ends with how churn, time-to-convert and failure-prediction systems use all of this in production.

The question and the missing endings

Survival analysis models a time to event: the time from a starting point (signup, purchase, installation) until something happens (cancellation, conversion, failure). The "event" is whatever you define it to be — "survival" is just the historical name for "the event hasn't happened yet". The defining difficulty is that at the moment you analyse the data, many subjects haven't had the event. For them you know a lower bound on the time, not the time itself. That's right-censoring: the true time lies somewhere to the right of what you observed.

An analogy: reading a race before it ends

Imagine estimating how long a marathon takes while the race is still on. The runners who have finished are the fastest ones; averaging their times says the marathon takes two and a half hours. Waiting until everyone finishes isn't possible — some will finish hours from now, and in product data some "runners" never finish. What you can say is: at the 3-hour mark, 40% of runners had finished out of those who had been running at least that long. Survival analysis does exactly this bookkeeping, step by step. The analogy breaks in that marathon runners all start together; customers start at different calendar times, so the comparison is always by time since their start — tenure, not date.

Two curves that describe everything

The survival function S(t) is the probability that the event hasn't happened by time t: S(0) = 1 and it falls over time. "70% of customers are still subscribed after 6 months" is S(6) = 0.7. The hazard function h(t) is the event rate at time t among those still at risk: "of customers who made it to month 6, 3% cancel during month 6". The hazard is the more natural thing to model, because it's about what happens to the people still around, and it's often the shape of the hazard that tells the business story: a hazard that falls with tenure means "if they survive the first months, they stay"; a hazard that rises means wear-out, as with ageing hardware.

The methods, in one paragraph

The Kaplan–Meier estimator draws S(t) from the data without assuming any shape: at each event time, it multiplies in the fraction of at-risk subjects who survived. The log-rank test asks whether two Kaplan–Meier curves differ by more than chance. The Cox proportional hazards model adds covariates — plan, country, usage — by saying each subject's hazard is a baseline hazard multiplied by a factor that depends on their features. Machine-learning extensions relax that structure. Every one of them treats a censored subject correctly: as someone who was at risk, and survived, up to their censoring time, and is simply not counted after that.

1. Censoring, and three wrong ways to handle it

Here is the 10-row table used throughout this lesson: months from signup until a customer churned (event = 1) or until the data was cut while they were still active (event = 0, censored).

Three tempting approaches, all wrong:

  • Drop the censored rows. The mean of the five churn times (2, 4, 5, 7, 10) is 5.6 months. But the censored customers include the two who've lasted 12 months and counting; removing them removes the long tail.
  • Treat the censoring time as the event time. Now everyone "churns", and the mean of all ten times is 6.8 months — still too short, and it says S(12) = 0, which is false: two customers are still here at 12 months.
  • Use a binary label. "50% of customers churned." But customer 2 was only observed for 3 months and customer 9 for 12; a 50% churn rate over wildly different windows is not a meaningful number, and a classifier trained on it learns that recent signups "don't churn" because they haven't had time to.

The right approach uses each row for exactly what it tells us: customer 2 was at risk, and survived, for 3 months. After month 3, we don't know, so customer 2 stops contributing. Everything below follows from this principle.

It rests on an assumption: non-informative censoring. Being censored must not be related to the risk of the event (given any covariates in the model). A fixed data cut-off usually satisfies it. "We lose track of users who stop opening the app" usually doesn't — those users were about to churn, and treating them as ordinary censored subjects makes survival look better than it is.

Two other kinds of incompleteness come up. Left truncation (delayed entry): if your data only includes customers active on 1 January, a customer who signed up two years earlier enters observation at tenure 24 months, and must only be counted in risk sets from month 24 — otherwise you overcount survival, because customers who churned before 1 January are invisible. Interval censoring: the event is only known to have happened between two check-ins (a disk found dead at a monthly inspection).

2. Survival, hazard and cumulative hazard

Let T be the random time to the event. The survival function is S(t) = P(T > t). The hazard is the event rate among those who've made it to t: in discrete time (months), h(t) = P(T = t | T ≥ t), the share of customers still subscribed at the start of month t who cancel during it. Surviving to the end of month t means surviving each month in turn, so:

S(t) = (1 − h(1)) × (1 − h(2)) × … × (1 − h(t))

In continuous time the hazard is a rate per unit time, h(t) = f(t)/S(t) where f is the density, and the product becomes S(t) = exp(−H(t)), with the cumulative hazard H(t) the area under h up to t.

The simplest model has a constant hazard λ: every month, the same fraction of remaining customers leaves. Then S(t) = e−λt, the exponential distribution (math2), with mean 1/λ and median ln 2/λ. Its maximum-likelihood estimate (math5) with censored data is beautifully simple: λ̂ = number of events / total time at risk. For our table, 5 events over 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 5 + 7 + 8 + 10 + 12 + 12 = 68 customer-months: λ̂ = 5/68 ≈ 0.074 per month, a median of ln 2/0.074 ≈ 9.4 months and a mean of about 13.6 months. Note how censored customers contribute: their months at risk go in the denominator, but they add no events. Constant hazards are rare in practice — churn hazards usually fall with tenure, hardware failure hazards often form a "bathtub" (high early from defects, low in mid-life, rising with wear) — which is why the methods below avoid assuming a shape.

3. Kaplan–Meier by hand

The Kaplan–Meier (KM) estimator estimates the discrete hazard at each event time as (events at t) / (number at risk just before t), and multiplies the survival fractions. "At risk" means not yet churned and not yet censored. When an event and a censoring happen at the same recorded time, the convention is that the event comes first (the censored subject was at risk at that moment).

Uncertainty comes from Greenwood's formula, which sums d/(n(n − d)) over event times; the variance grows as risk sets shrink. In practice you plot KM curves with confidence bands and number-at-risk tables under the x-axis, so readers can see when the curve is resting on a handful of subjects. A related estimator, Nelson–Aalen, estimates the cumulative hazard as the running sum of d/n; exp(−H) gives a survival curve very close to KM.

4. Comparing groups: the log-rank test

Suppose customers 1, 3, 4, 7 and 10 in the table are on monthly plans and 2, 5, 6, 8 and 9 on annual plans. Their KM curves: monthly drops to 0.8, 0.6 and 0.4 by month 5; annual stays at 1 until month 7, then 0.67, then 0.33 at month 10. Is the difference real or chance?

The log-rank test works event time by event time. At each, if plan made no difference, the expected number of monthly-plan events would be (total events) × (monthly customers at risk) / (all at risk). Sum observed minus expected over event times, and divide the square by the summed variance to get a chi-squared statistic with one degree of freedom (math6):

5. Cox proportional hazards

To handle many covariates at once, the Cox model (Cox, 1972) assumes:

h(t | x) = h₀(t) · exp(β₁x₁ + β₂x₂ + … + βₚxₚ)

h₀(t) is a baseline hazard of any shape — the model doesn't specify it — and the covariates multiply it by exp(β·x). Two customers' hazards therefore have a constant ratio over time: exp(β·(x_A − x_B)). That's the proportional hazards assumption. Each exp(βⱼ) is a hazard ratio: the factor by which the hazard is multiplied for a one-unit increase in xⱼ, holding the others fixed. A coefficient of 0.69 for "monthly plan" (exp(0.69) ≈ 2) would mean monthly customers churn at twice the rate of annual customers at every tenure.

The partial likelihood. How do you fit β without knowing h₀? Cox's insight: at each event time, condition on the fact that someone in the risk set had the event, and ask how likely it was to be the one who did. The probability is that subject's hazard divided by the sum of hazards in the risk set:

P(subject i fails | one failure among risk set R) =
      h₀(t)·exp(β·xᵢ) / Σⱼ∈R h₀(t)·exp(β·xⱼ)  =  exp(β·xᵢ) / Σⱼ∈R exp(β·xⱼ)

h₀(t) cancels. Multiply these probabilities over all event times and maximise over β: that's the partial likelihood. Only the ordering of event times matters, censored subjects contribute by being in the denominators of risk sets until they leave, and the baseline can be recovered afterwards (the Breslow estimator) if you need absolute survival predictions. Worked with one binary covariate, monthly plan (1) or annual (0), at month 2 in our table: the risk set has 5 monthly and 5 annual customers, and a monthly customer churned. With hazard ratio r = exp(β), the probability is r / (5r + 5). At β = 0 that's 0.10; at r = 2, 2/15 ≈ 0.13. The likelihood rewards values of β that make the actual failures look likely; the fitted β balances all five event times.

The public case. lifelines' documentation fits a Cox model to the Rossi recidivism data: 432 released prisoners followed for a year, event = re-arrest, with covariates including financial aid (fin), age and number of prior convictions (prio). The fitted coefficients include fin −0.38 (hazard ratio 0.68), age −0.06 (0.94 per year) and prio 0.09 (1.10 per prior conviction), with a concordance of 0.64. Read: at any week, people who received financial aid had about 32% lower re-arrest hazard, adjusting for the other covariates; each prior conviction raised it by about 10%. "Lower hazard" is about rates, not "lived 32% longer". And, as ml17 would insist, these are associations adjusted for the listed covariates; in this study financial aid was in fact randomly assigned, which is why its coefficient can be read causally.

6. Checking proportional hazards

The assumption fails when an effect changes with time: a discount that keeps customers in the first three months but has no effect later; a disk model with more early failures but fewer late ones. Diagnostics:

  • Look at the curves. KM curves that cross, or log(−log S(t)) curves for groups that aren't roughly parallel, suggest non-proportional hazards.
  • Scaled Schoenfeld residuals. At each event, compare the failing subject's covariate with the risk-weighted average covariate in the risk set. If the effect is constant, these residuals show no trend over time; a test of correlation with time flags violations. lifelines' check_assumptions() runs this and suggests remedies.

Remedies: stratify on the offending covariate (each stratum gets its own baseline hazard, and the covariate's effect is no longer estimated); add a time interaction x·g(t) so the coefficient changes with time; or split follow-up into periods with separate coefficients. If the goal is prediction rather than an interpretable hazard ratio, a mild violation matters less — the lifelines docs make this point — and a flexible model that doesn't assume proportionality may simply be better.

7. Discrete-time models: survival as classification

Most product data is naturally discrete: billing is monthly, churn is recorded by month. A discrete-time survival model reshapes the data into person-period form — one row per customer per month at risk — with label 1 in the month of churn and 0 in every month before. Customer 4 (churned in month 5) contributes rows for months 1–5 with labels 0, 0, 0, 0, 1; customer 2 (censored at 3) contributes three rows of 0. Now fit any classifier for P(churn in month t | still here at start of t, features): logistic regression (ml6) with tenure dummies gives the discrete analogue of a Cox model, and gradient boosting (ml10) gives a flexible one. Predicted values are hazards; survival is the product of (1 − hazard).

The appeal is practical: time-varying features (this month's usage) are just columns, the whole classification toolbox applies, and censoring is handled automatically — censored customers stop contributing rows. The costs: the dataset is multiplied by the average tenure in periods, and train/test splits must be by customer (and ideally by time), never by row, or the model sees a customer's future rows in training (ml14).

8. Machine-learning survival models

Random survival forests (Ishwaran et al., 2008) grow trees whose splits maximise the difference in survival between child nodes — for example by the log-rank statistic — and estimate a cumulative hazard in each leaf; the forest averages them. They capture interactions and non-proportional effects without specification. Gradient boosting with a Cox loss fits trees to the gradient of the negative partial likelihood, so the boosted score plays the role of β·x (XGBoost offers a Cox objective and an accelerated-failure-time objective). Deep survival models put a neural network where β·x was: DeepSurv (Katzman et al., 2018) trains a network as the Cox log-risk function; discrete-time networks predict a hazard per period and can take images, text or event sequences as input. These shine on large or unstructured data; on small tabular data a Cox model or random survival forest is usually as good and easier to trust.

A different family, accelerated failure time (AFT) models, assumes covariates stretch or shrink time itself: log T = β·x + error, so exp(β) is a time ratio ("monthly-plan customers' lifetimes are 0.6 times as long"). AFT coefficients are often easier to explain to business stakeholders than hazard ratios, and the model needs a distribution for the error (Weibull, log-normal, log-logistic).

9. Evaluation: the concordance index and calibration

A survival model's output is a risk score or a predicted survival curve per subject. Accuracy against a single label doesn't work, because censored subjects have no event time. The standard ranking metric is Harrell's C-index: over all comparable pairs — pairs where the subject with the shorter time had the event, so we know who failed first — the fraction where the model gave higher risk to the one who failed first (ties count half). Comparable pairs: (A, B), (A, C), (A, D), (B, C), (B, D) — A and B churned before the other member of each pair. (C, D) is not comparable: C was censored at 8, so we don't know whether C would have churned before 10. Concordant: A over B, C and D (0.9 is highest); B over D (0.4 > 0.2). Discordant: (B, C), since C had higher predicted risk but B churned first. C = 4/5 = 0.8. 0.5 is random ranking; the Rossi Cox model's 0.64 is typical for noisy behavioural outcomes.

The C-index measures ranking only. Check absolute accuracy with calibration at a horizon — group customers by predicted 90-day survival and compare with the KM estimate in each group — and with the Brier score at chosen times (squared error between predicted survival and observed status, weighted by the inverse probability of being uncensored so censored subjects don't bias it). Time-dependent AUC answers "how well does the model separate those who churn by day 90 from those who don't?", which often matches the business decision better than one global C-index.

10. Product uses

Churn. Survival curves by signup cohort show whether retention is improving; Cox or boosted models identify risk factors; predicted survival curves feed customer lifetime value, which is expected revenue summed over the survival curve. Time to convert. Trial-to-paid or signup-to-first-purchase: many users haven't converted yet, and a survival model separates "slow" from "never" in a way a binary label can't; cumulative conversion is 1 − S(t). Failure prediction. Disks, vehicles and machines: hazards rising with age call for preventive replacement before the hazard climbs, and models on telemetry predict which units are near failure. Backblaze publishes daily drive statistics and failures for its fleet, a public dataset that is a natural survival problem — most drives are censored (still working) at any snapshot.

Competing risks

A customer can leave in different ways — voluntary cancellation, payment failure, account closure for fraud — and one event prevents the others. Treating other events as censoring for the event of interest gives a cause-specific hazard, which is right for asking "what drives voluntary cancellation?", but 1 − KM then overstates the probability of voluntary cancellation by time t, because it imagines a world where nobody can leave any other way. The correct quantity for "what share will cancel voluntarily by month 12?" is the cumulative incidence function, which accounts for the competing events (estimated by Aalen–Johansen); the Fine–Gray model regresses directly on it. In interviews, recognising that "payment failure" and "chose to cancel" should be separate events with different interventions is often the key insight.

Time-varying covariates and the leakage trap

Covariates change: usage this month, support tickets, a price change. The Cox model handles time-varying covariates with a counting-process (start, stop] data format — the same idea as person-period rows — where each row carries the covariate values valid during that interval. The trap is using covariates measured over a customer's entire history: "total support tickets" is higher for customers who stayed longer, and "used the feature at least once" can only be true for customers who survived long enough to use it. This is immortal time bias, and it can make a useless feature look protective. Every covariate must be defined as of the time at which it would be known (ml14).

Landmarking: predicting from now

Production systems predict from the current moment: "given this customer is active at month 7 with this recent behaviour, what's their chance of churning in the next 90 days?" Landmark analysis builds training sets at fixed landmark times (month 3, 6, 9…), including only subjects still at risk at the landmark and covariates measured up to it, and models the residual time. It avoids immortal time bias by construction and matches how the model is used. Stacking several landmarks into one dataset, with the landmark time as a feature, gives one model usable at any tenure.

Why the partial likelihood works so well

Cox's partial likelihood is not a full likelihood — it discards information about when events happen, keeping only their order — but it loses remarkably little efficiency, and in exchange avoids specifying h₀(t), whose shape is often the hardest thing to get right. The estimates are consistent and asymptotically normal, which is why Cox regression gives standard errors and confidence intervals for hazard ratios. Ties (several events at the same recorded time, common with monthly data) need an approximation — Breslow's is the classic default, Efron's is more accurate — or a discrete-time model, which handles ties naturally.

Frailty and unobserved heterogeneity

Population hazards can fall over time even if every individual's hazard is constant, because high-risk customers leave first and the survivors are increasingly low-risk. This selection effect means a falling population churn hazard doesn't prove that "customers become more loyal with tenure". Frailty models add a random effect per subject (or group, like a region) multiplying the hazard, separating individual heterogeneity from genuine time effects. The practical lesson: be careful interpreting the shape of a population hazard as a statement about individuals.

Choosing the time axis and the origin

Every survival analysis silently picks a clock. Tenure (time since signup) is the usual choice for churn, because risk changes most with tenure; calendar time matters when an outside event (a price change, a competitor launch) hits everyone on the same date; age of the device matters for hardware even if monitoring began later, which turns into left truncation. The origin must be a moment every subject passes through and that marks the start of risk: "signup" for churn, "trial start" for conversion, "installation" for disks. Choosing a later origin, such as "first payment", silently excludes everyone who left before paying and changes the question being answered. When two clocks both matter, use one as the time axis and the other as a covariate — for example tenure as the axis, with calendar-period indicators as time-varying covariates.

Survival and causal questions

Hazard ratios have a subtle causal problem: even in a randomised trial, the treated and control groups still at risk at later times are no longer comparable, because the treatment changed who survived. Hernán has argued for reporting differences in survival curves or restricted mean survival time rather than hazard ratios for causal questions, because those compare the whole randomised groups. For uplift on retention (ml17), the target is therefore usually "retained at 90 days" or "expected months retained up to a horizon", estimated per arm, rather than a hazard ratio.

The public case: Rossi recidivism in lifelines

lifelines, the open-source Python survival library, uses the Rossi et al. dataset as its main regression example: 432 people released from Maryland state prisons followed for 52 weeks, with re-arrest as the event and most subjects censored at week 52. Its CoxPHFitter output shows financial aid with coefficient −0.38 (hazard ratio 0.68), prior convictions with 0.09 (1.10 per conviction), marriage with −0.43 (0.65) and a concordance of 0.64; the documentation then runs check_assumptions() and discusses how to respond to violations by stratifying or adding time-varying terms. The example is worth copying as a template: a clear event definition, administrative censoring at a fixed horizon, hazard ratios with intervals, concordance, and an explicit assumption check.

Characteristic failures

Censoring ignored. Symptom: average lifetime estimates that keep rising as the dataset ages, because each new snapshot includes more long-lived customers; forecasts that underestimate retention. Fix: Kaplan–Meier or a survival model, never averages of completed durations.

Informative censoring. Symptom: survival curves that look too good; customers "lost to observation" (deleted accounts, stopped app use) are really churners. Fix: define the event so it's observable for everyone, such as billing cancellation, and check sensitivity by treating ambiguous censoring as events.

Immortal time and leakage. Symptom: features like "number of logins" or "used feature X" with enormous protective hazard ratios. Fix: compute features as of the prediction time; use landmarking.

Cohort drift. Customers who signed up during a promotion behave differently from organic signups, and recent cohorts may churn faster or slower than old ones. Fix: include cohort or signup-channel covariates, plot KM curves by cohort, and validate on later cohorts (time-based splits), not random splits.

Over-reading the tail. Symptom: decisions based on the far right of a KM curve resting on a dozen subjects. Fix: show number-at-risk tables and confidence bands, and report restricted mean survival up to a horizon with enough data.

Operating a survival model

Scores are typically produced daily or weekly for active customers as "probability of churning in the next 30/90 days" from the predicted survival curve conditional on current tenure. Monitor the C-index and horizon calibration on each new cohort once outcomes arrive (which takes as long as the horizon, so keep shorter-horizon proxies too), and monitor input drift as in ml14. Pair the risk score with an uplift estimate before acting: the customers at highest churn risk are not necessarily those a retention offer can save (ml17), and contacting some of them can backfire.

From survival curves to decisions

A survival curve becomes money through expected lifetime: the expected number of future paid months for a customer is the sum of their predicted monthly survival probabilities over a horizon, and multiplying by margin gives a lifetime-value estimate that properly discounts customers likely to leave soon. Comparing that number with the cost of an intervention, and with the intervention's estimated uplift on survival, decides who gets a retention offer, which disks are replaced in the next maintenance window, or which trial users get a sales call. Keep the horizon explicit in every number you report — "12-month restricted mean lifetime", not "lifetime" — because the tail beyond the observed data is an extrapolation.

Tools

In Python, lifelines covers Kaplan–Meier, Nelson–Aalen, Cox and AFT models, and assumption checks; scikit-survival adds random survival forests, boosted Cox models and censoring-aware metrics; XGBoost and LightGBM have Cox or AFT objectives. In R, the survival package is the reference implementation that many others are checked against.

Goal: compute Kaplan–Meier, the exponential rate and the log-rank statistic for the lesson's 10-row table, and compare with the naive answers. NumPy only; Python 3.12 with the gpt_env NumPy.

import numpy as np

time  = np.array([2, 3, 4, 5, 5, 7, 8, 10, 12, 12])
event = np.array([1, 0, 1, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 0])
plan  = np.array(list("MAMMAAMAAM"))             # M = monthly, A = annual

def kaplan_meier(t, e):
    S, rows = 1.0, []
    for u in np.unique(t):
        n = (t >= u).sum()                         # at risk just before u
        d = ((t == u) & (e == 1)).sum()            # events at u
        if d:
            S *= 1 - d / n
        rows.append((int(u), int(n), int(d), round(S, 4)))
    return rows

for r in kaplan_meier(time, event):
    print("t=%2d  at risk=%2d  events=%d  S=%.4f" % r)

print("naive mean of churners:", time[event == 1].mean())
print("naive mean of all times:", time.mean())
lam = event.sum() / time.sum()
print(f"exponential rate {lam:.4f}/month, median {np.log(2) / lam:.1f} months")

# Log-rank test, monthly vs annual
O = E = V = 0.0
for u in np.unique(time[event == 1]):
    risk = time >= u
    n, n1 = risk.sum(), (risk & (plan == "M")).sum()
    d = ((time == u) & (event == 1)).sum()
    d1 = ((time == u) & (event == 1) & (plan == "M")).sum()
    O += d1; E += d * n1 / n
    V += d * (n1 / n) * (1 - n1 / n) * (n - d) / (n - 1)
print(f"O={O:.0f}  E={E:.2f}  V={V:.2f}  chi2={(O - E) ** 2 / V:.2f}")

What to look for. The KM column should match the lesson's table: 0.9, 0.9, 0.7875, 0.675, 0.54, 0.54, 0.36, 0.36, so the median is 10 months. The naive means are 5.6 and 6.8 months — both far below the KM median — and the exponential fit gives about 0.074 per month and a median near 9.4 months. The log-rank output should be O = 3, E ≈ 2.16, V ≈ 1.21, χ² ≈ 0.58: no evidence of a difference with ten customers. Diagnose and design

A product manager shares a chart: "Average time to churn, by signup month." It shows customers who signed up in January churning after 7.1 months on average, and those who signed up in September after 1.8 months. They conclude that recent cohorts are much less loyal and want to investigate onboarding. What's wrong, and how would you answer the real question?

Answer

The chart is a censoring artefact. "Average time to churn" can only be computed for customers who have churned. A September signup observed today (say, in early December) can only have churned within about three months, so its average is capped near that; January signups had up to eleven months to churn, so their churners include long-tenure ones. The chart would show recent cohorts as "less loyal" even if every cohort behaved identically.

Compare like with like. Plot Kaplan–Meier curves by signup cohort on a common axis of months since signup. Each cohort's curve extends only as far as it has been observed, and at matching tenures — S(1), S(2), S(3) — the comparison is fair. If the September curve really lies below January's over the first three months, onboarding may be the issue; test the difference with a log-rank test restricted to the first three months, or fit a Cox model with cohort as a covariate.

Check the explanations before blaming onboarding. Did September include a promotion that attracted price-sensitive signups (a cohort-mix effect rather than a product effect)? Did the event definition change (for example, failed payments now counted as churn)? Is anything censored informatively? Then, if a gap remains, an A/B test of onboarding changes with 30- and 90-day retention as the outcome answers the causal question.
