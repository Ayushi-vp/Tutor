A team is building a model to predict delivery times. Their first version, a simple linear model, is off by an average of 14 minutes both on the orders it was trained on and on orders it has never seen. Someone suggests a much more powerful model. They try it and training error drops to 2 minutes — a triumph — but on new orders it is off by 19 minutes, worse than before. A third engineer suggests collecting another year of data; that takes two months and, for the linear model, changes nothing at all. A fourth wants to add weather features. Everyone has an idea and nobody has a way to decide which idea addresses the actual problem.

The bias–variance decomposition is that way. It says a model's error on new data comes from exactly three sources: the model being too rigid to represent the pattern (bias), the model being so sensitive to its particular training data that it learns quirks rather than the pattern (variance), and randomness in the data that no model can remove (noise). Each source has different symptoms and different cures. The linear model's problem was bias, which is why more data did nothing and weather features might help. The powerful model's problem was variance, which is why it looked brilliant on training data and poor elsewhere, and why more data or regularisation would help it.

This is the reasoning behind nearly every "my model isn't good enough, what now?" conversation, and interviewers use it constantly: "train error is 2%, validation is 25%, what do you do?" A candidate who answers from the decomposition sounds like someone who has debugged real models; one who lists every technique they know sounds like someone who hasn't.

This lesson builds the idea from scratch: the intuition, the formula and where it comes from, a worked example with four training sets computed by hand, how to diagnose a real model from its training and validation errors and its learning curve, which levers fix which problem, why averaging models reduces variance (with the formula), the public story of Google Flu Trends — a model that searched 50 million candidate features to fit 1,152 data points — and double descent, the modern wrinkle that explains why enormous neural networks don't behave the way the classical picture predicts.

Darts, thrown by many different players

Picture a dartboard. Now imagine not one throw but many: each throw is a model trained on a different random sample of data from the same world. Where the darts land relative to the bullseye (the truth) tells you everything.

If the darts cluster tightly but away from the bullseye, the thrower is consistently wrong: that is bias. If they are centred on the bullseye on average but spread all over the board, each individual throw is unreliable: that is variance. A good model is both centred and tight. And even a perfect thrower faces a board that wobbles a little on every throw — randomness in the data itself — which is the irreducible noise.

The analogy breaks in one important way: in real life you only throw one dart. You train one model on one dataset, and you cannot see the cloud. That is why the practical tools in this lesson — comparing training and validation error, and learning curves — exist: they infer the shape of the cloud from a single throw.

Underfitting and overfitting

A model with high bias is underfitting: it is too simple for the pattern. Fitting a straight line to data that curves, it is wrong in the same way whichever sample it sees, and it does badly even on its own training data. A model with high variance is overfitting: it is so flexible it bends to fit every point, including the noise. A curve of degree 15 through 16 points passes through every one of them and swings wildly between them; train it on a slightly different 16 points and it swings differently. It does brilliantly on training data and badly on new data.

Capacity: the dial between them

A model's capacity is how wide a range of functions it can represent. A straight line has little capacity; a degree-10 polynomial, a deep decision tree or a large neural network has a lot. Raising capacity typically lowers bias (the model can now represent the true shape) and raises variance (it can now also represent the noise). Somewhere in between is the capacity that makes the total error on new data smallest. You find it by trying several capacities and measuring on a validation set, not by reasoning about it in advance.

How you notice, with numbers

Compare error on the training set with error on a validation set the model never trained on (ml1), and against an estimate of the best achievable error — often how well humans do:

  • Train 30% error, validation 32%, humans 5%: both far above what's achievable, small gap → underfitting (bias).
  • Train 2% error, validation 25%: big gap → overfitting (variance).
  • Train 5%, validation 6%, humans 5%: you are near the best achievable; improvements must come from the data.

1. The setup: a true function, noise, and many possible training sets

To make "bias" and "variance" precise we need a model of how data is produced. Assume each label is a true, unknown function of the features plus random noise:

y = f(x) + ε        where ε has mean 0 and variance σ²

f is the pattern we want to learn — how delivery time really depends on distance, time of day and so on. ε (epsilon) is everything the features can't explain: a driver stopping for fuel, a lift that's out of order. Its average is zero and its variance σ² (sigma squared, the average squared distance from its mean) measures how big it typically is. No model can predict ε from x, by definition.

A training set is a random sample of such (x, y) pairs. Had we collected data a month later we'd have a different sample, and training the same kind of model on it would give a slightly different fitted function, f̂ ("f-hat"). So f̂ is itself random — it depends on which training set we happened to get. The notation E[·] means expected value: the long-run average over all the training sets we might have drawn (and over the noise). E[f̂(x)] is therefore "the average prediction at x, averaged over all the models we could have trained" — the centre of the dart cloud.

2. The decomposition in words, then as a formula

In words: when you predict a new point, your squared error comes from three places. How far the average model is from the truth (bias). How far your particular model is from the average model (variance). And how far the actual observed label is from the truth (noise). For squared error these three add up exactly:

E[(y − f̂(x))²]  =  (E[f̂(x)] − f(x))²   +   E[(f̂(x) − E[f̂(x)])²]   +   σ²
expected error      bias²                   variance                     noise

Where it comes from, in three lines. Write the error as the sum of three pieces: y − f̂ = (f − E[f̂]) + (E[f̂] − f̂) + ε. Square it. The square of a sum of three terms is the three squares plus cross terms. Take the average: the cross terms vanish, because ε has mean zero and doesn't depend on the training set, and E[f̂] − f̂ averages to zero by definition. What remains is the three squared terms — bias², variance and σ². That is the whole derivation; the only assumption that matters is the squared loss, which is why the clean version holds only for squared error.

Bias is squared because it enters through a square: a model that is systematically 2 minutes too high and one that is 2 minutes too low contribute the same.

3. A worked example with four training sets

Take one test point where the true value is f(x₀) = 2.0 and the noise variance is σ² = 0.25. Imagine the world could have given us any of four training sets, equally likely. We train three models of different capacity on each set and record their predictions at x₀:

ModelSet ASet BSet CSet DAverage
Simple (e.g. a constant)1.01.20.81.01.0
Medium (e.g. a cubic)2.01.72.11.81.9
Flexible (e.g. degree 10)2.61.42.81.22.0

Now compute each term by hand.

  1. Simple. Bias² = (1.0 − 2.0)² = 1.00. Variance: deviations from the average 1.0 are 0, +0.2, −0.2, 0; squares 0, 0.04, 0.04, 0; average 0.02. Expected error = 1.00 + 0.02 + 0.25 = 1.27.
  2. Medium. Bias² = (1.9 − 2.0)² = 0.01. Deviations from 1.9: +0.1, −0.2, +0.2, −0.1; squares 0.01, 0.04, 0.04, 0.01; average 0.025. Expected error = 0.01 + 0.025 + 0.25 = 0.285.
  3. Flexible. Bias² = (2.0 − 2.0)² = 0. Deviations from 2.0: +0.6, −0.6, +0.8, −0.8; squares 0.36, 0.36, 0.64, 0.64; average 0.50. Expected error = 0 + 0.50 + 0.25 = 0.75.

Check the flexible model directly, without the formula: its errors against the truth are 0.6, 0.6, 0.8, 0.8 in size, whose squares average 0.50; add the noise the label carries, 0.25, and you get 0.75 — the decomposition is just bookkeeping. The pattern is the whole lesson in miniature. The simple model is consistently wrong (all bias). The flexible model is right on average but unreliable (all variance). The medium model is slightly wrong and slightly unreliable, and has by far the lowest total. And none of them can get below 0.25, the noise floor.

4. The U-curve: sweeping capacity

Repeat that calculation for every capacity from very simple to very flexible and plot the pieces. Bias² falls as capacity rises; variance rises; noise is flat; their sum — expected test error — is U-shaped. Training error, meanwhile, keeps falling, because a more flexible model can always fit the training points at least as well.

The lab reproduces this with real numbers. Fitting polynomials to 25 noisy points from a sine wave, over 500 resampled training sets, gives:

Degree 3 wins. Degrees 5 and 7 have essentially zero bias — they can represent the sine perfectly — but lose because of variance, even though their training error is lower. That is overfitting in one table.

5. Diagnosing a real model from one training run

In practice you have one dataset, so you can't compute bias and variance directly. You infer them from three numbers: training error, validation error, and an estimate of the Bayes error — the lowest error any model could achieve, set by the noise. For tasks humans do well (reading images, transcribing speech), human-level error is the usual proxy; otherwise, disagreement between repeated measurements or between annotators on the same items.

  • Avoidable bias ≈ training error − Bayes estimate. How much better the model could fit even the data it has seen.
  • Variance ≈ validation error − training error. The generalisation gap: how much worse it does on new examples.

Worked: Bayes estimate 1%, training error 8%, validation 10% → avoidable bias 7 points, variance 2 points; work on bias first. Bayes 1%, training 1.5%, validation 10% → avoidable bias 0.5, variance 8.5; work on variance. Always attack the bigger gap first. And note what this reasoning needs: without a floor, "training error 8%" could be terrible or perfect.

A fourth and fifth gap matter in production. If you have tuned many times against the validation set, the test set may come in worse — you have overfitted to validation (ml9). And if production data differs from training data, a further gap opens that bias and variance don't describe at all, because the decomposition assumes the test point comes from the same distribution. That is distribution shift (ml14, ml16), and its fix is data, not capacity.

6. Learning curves: will more data help?

A learning curve plots training and validation error against the number of training examples. It answers the most expensive question in the field — "should we collect more data?" — before you spend the money.

Why the shapes: with 10 examples almost any model can fit them all, so training error starts low and rises as more examples make a perfect fit impossible; validation error starts high (the model has seen too little) and falls. Both head towards the same value — the best error this model class can achieve. If that value is too high, you have a bias problem and the remedy is capacity or features; if the curves are still far apart at your current data size, you have a variance problem and more data will close the gap.

7. The levers, sorted by what they fix

ProblemLeversWhat not to do
High biasMore capacity (bigger model, deeper trees, higher degree), better features, weaker regularisation, train longer, boosting (ml10)Collect more of the same data
High varianceMore data, stronger regularisation (ml3), data augmentation, early stopping, fewer features, simpler model, bagging/ensemblesAdd capacity
At the noise floorBetter labels, new informative features, better measurementKeep tuning the model

Several levers help both: a genuinely informative new feature reduces bias (the model can now explain more) without adding much variance, and more data reduces variance without adding bias. The "trade-off" is what happens when you turn the single capacity dial; good features and more data move you to a better curve altogether.

8. Why averaging models reduces variance

If you could train on many independent training sets and average the models, the average would have the same bias but much less variance — that is the centre of the dart cloud. You can't get independent datasets, but you can approximate them. Bagging (bootstrap aggregating) trains each model on a bootstrap resample — rows drawn from the original data with replacement — and averages them. The models are not independent, though; they share most of their data. For B models each with variance σ² and pairwise correlation ρ, the variance of their average is:

Var(average) = ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ² / B

Plug in the flexible model from section 3 (σ² = 0.50). Ten models with correlation 0.2: 0.2 × 0.5 + 0.8 × 0.5 / 10 = 0.10 + 0.04 = 0.14. Expected error falls from 0.75 to 0 + 0.14 + 0.25 = 0.39. A thousand models: 0.10 + 0.0004 ≈ 0.10, so error 0.35 — and no further, because the first term doesn't shrink with B. The floor ρσ² is why random forests go out of their way to decorrelate their trees (ml10), and why ensembles of near-identical models are disappointing.

9. The modern wrinkle: double descent

Classical theory says: past the sweet spot, bigger models only get worse. Deep learning practice says the opposite — enormous networks that fit their training data perfectly still generalise well. Belkin and colleagues reconciled the two in 2018 with the double descent curve. As capacity grows, test error first follows the U. It peaks at the interpolation threshold: the capacity at which the model can just fit every training point exactly. There, only one contorted function fits the data, so noise is baked into it. Keep growing and many different functions fit the training data exactly; the training procedure — gradient descent starting from small weights — tends to pick among them one that is smooth (small in size), and test error falls again, sometimes below the classical minimum.

Nakkiran and colleagues showed the same shape for deep networks as a function of model size and of training epochs, and found a surprising corollary: near the threshold, more data can hurt, because adding examples moves the threshold to where your model currently sits. For interviews, the takeaway is: the U-curve is the classical, data-limited half of the picture; massively over-parameterised models live on the second descent; and good regularisation largely removes the peak.

Implicit regularisation

Why does the training procedure prefer smooth solutions when many exact fits exist? For linear models trained by gradient descent from zero, the answer is provable: gradient descent converges to the solution with the smallest weights (the minimum-norm solution), which is the smoothest. For neural networks the picture is less exact but similar: small initial weights, small-batch stochastic gradient descent and large learning rates bias training towards "flat" solutions — ones where the loss changes little if the weights move — which tend to generalise. This is called implicit regularisation: the algorithm itself restrains capacity, even though nothing in the loss function asks it to. It is why "number of parameters" is a poor measure of effective capacity for deep networks.

Networks can memorise random labels

Zhang et al. (2016) trained standard image classifiers on data whose labels had been replaced with random ones. The networks still reached zero training error — they memorised pure noise. So their capacity is enormous, and classical bounds that promise good generalisation only for low-capacity models say nothing useful. Yet the same networks generalise on real labels. The resolution is that generalisation depends on the data and the algorithm together: on real data, gradient descent finds simple patterns that explain many examples at once, whereas random labels can only be fitted example by example (the paper found this took longer, though only by a small constant factor), and real labels are explainable by simple patterns.

Classification and the 0–1 loss

The additive decomposition is exact only for squared error. For 0–1 loss (a prediction is either right or wrong), several decompositions exist (Domingos, and James, among others), but bias and variance interact rather than add. The striking consequence: when the average model is wrong at a point, variance can help, because a model that occasionally flips to the right class beats one that is consistently wrong. The practical diagnosis — compare training error, validation error and a floor — carries over unchanged; the clean equation does not.

The ensemble formula, more carefully

The formula ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ²/B assumes every member has the same variance σ² and every pair the same correlation ρ. Its message is robust: adding members helps with diminishing returns, and the floor is set by how correlated they are. Random forests lower ρ by giving each tree a bootstrap sample and a random subset of features at each split, which raises each tree's own variance slightly but lowers the correlation more — a net gain. Stacking diverse model types (trees, linear models, neural nets) lowers ρ further, which is why competition-winning ensembles mix model families (the Netflix Prize is the classic example; see ml10).

Bias, variance and regularisation strength

Regularisation (ml3) is a continuous capacity dial. For ridge regression — least squares plus a penalty λ times the sum of squared weights — increasing λ shrinks the weights towards zero, adding bias and removing variance; the test-error curve against λ is the U-curve mirrored. That continuity is what makes regularisation strength the most common hyperparameter to tune: it moves smoothly along the trade-off without changing the model family. Early stopping in iterative training acts the same way, with the number of training steps as the dial.

Scaling laws reframe the question

For very large language models, the relevant question is no longer "am I overfitting?" — they usually see each training token once or a few times — but "how should a fixed compute budget be split between model size and data?". Empirically, loss falls as a smooth power law in parameters, data and compute; the Chinchilla study found compute-optimal training uses roughly 20 tokens per parameter. In bias–variance language: at that scale the models are data-limited and capacity-limited together, and both are relieved by scale — which is why the classical trade-off is rarely the binding constraint in LLM pretraining, though it very much is when fine-tuning on a few thousand examples.

Variance from the training procedure itself

Two models trained on the same data can differ because of random initialisation, data ordering and non-deterministic GPU arithmetic. This "training variance" adds to the data variance in the decomposition. It matters in practice: a 0.3-point improvement from a change may be smaller than the spread between random seeds. Senior engineers train several seeds before believing a small gain, and report the mean and spread.

The public case: Google Flu Trends

In 2008 Google launched Flu Trends (GFT), which estimated the prevalence of influenza-like illness from search queries, published ahead of the official surveillance reports of the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). It was hailed as a triumph of big data. By February 2013 it was predicting more than double the proportion of doctor visits for influenza-like illness that the CDC reported. Lazer and colleagues' analysis in Science (2014), "The Parable of Google Flu", is a textbook study in variance and overfitting:

  • Too many candidate features for too little data. The original method searched for the best matches among 50 million search terms to fit 1,152 data points. With that many candidates, some terms will match the flu curve by coincidence. The authors describe the original GFT as "part flu detector, part winter detector" — it had picked up seasonal searches (they cite terms such as high-school basketball) that correlated with winter, not with flu.
  • It failed when the pattern changed. The original model completely missed the non-seasonal 2009 H1N1 ("swine flu") pandemic, which peaked outside the usual winter season, exactly what a winter detector would do.
  • Systematic errors afterwards. After updates, GFT overestimated flu prevalence in 100 of the 108 weeks starting August 2011, and its errors were correlated from week to week — a sign of structure the model was missing.
  • A simple model did better. Over the paper's out-of-sample period, mean absolute error was 0.486 for GFT, 0.311 for a simple model using the CDC's own lagged (two-week-old) data, and 0.232 for GFT combined with lagged CDC data.

Two lessons map directly onto this lesson. Selecting features from an enormous candidate pool against a small number of targets is a variance machine: in-sample fit looks superb and generalisation suffers. And the problem was compounded by drift — Google kept changing its search product, which changed how people searched — a production gap that bias and variance don't describe but that monitoring would have caught. The combined model winning is the ensemble lesson: blending two error sources with different errors beat either one.

What overfitting looks like in production systems

Offline wins that vanish online. A model that beats the current one by 0.5% on the validation set, after dozens of tuning rounds against that same set, often shows nothing in an A/B test (ml16). The validation set has been overfitted by repeated selection. Defences: a held-out test set that is truly used once, or a fresh validation set periodically.

Great on the benchmark, worse on the long tail. High-capacity models can memorise frequent patterns and still vary wildly on rare segments with little data. Slice metrics — error per country, per device, per customer tier — reveal variance that the overall average hides.

Instability between retrains. High-variance models produce noticeably different predictions after each retraining even when the data barely changed, which confuses users and downstream systems ("why did my credit limit change?"). Tracking prediction churn between model versions is a practical variance metric.

The debugging routine teams actually use

First, establish a floor (human-level or annotator agreement) and a trivial baseline. Second, overfit a small subset on purpose — if a model cannot reach near-zero error on 100 examples, there is a bug or a bias problem, not a variance problem. Third, train on the full data and compare training error, validation error and the floor, attacking the larger gap first. Fourth, draw a learning curve before deciding to buy data. Fifth, before celebrating a small improvement, repeat the run with several random seeds to see whether the gain exceeds run-to-run variation. This routine answers most "what do I try next?" questions in a few hours.

Goal: measure bias², variance and noise directly by simulation — possible only because we invent the true function. NumPy only; tested with Python 3.12.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
f = lambda x: np.sin(2 * np.pi * x)          # the true function
SIGMA, N, TRIALS = 0.3, 25, 500              # noise sd, points per training set, resamples
x_test = np.linspace(0.1, 0.9, 50)

print("degree   bias^2  variance   noise   expected test MSE   mean train MSE")
for degree in (0, 1, 3, 5, 7):
    preds, train_mse = [], []
    for _ in range(TRIALS):
        x = rng.uniform(0, 1, N)
        y = f(x) + rng.normal(0, SIGMA, N)
        coef = np.polyfit(x, y, degree)
        preds.append(np.polyval(coef, x_test))
        train_mse.append(np.mean((np.polyval(coef, x) - y) ** 2))
    preds = np.array(preds)                          # TRIALS x 50
    bias2 = np.mean((preds.mean(axis=0) - f(x_test)) ** 2)
    var = np.mean(preds.var(axis=0))
    print(f"{degree:>6} {bias2:8.3f} {var:9.3f} {SIGMA**2:7.3f} "
          f"{bias2 + var + SIGMA**2:14.3f} {np.mean(train_mse):16.3f}")

What to look for. Bias² collapses from 0.589 (degree 0) to about zero by degree 5, variance climbs from degree 3 on, and expected test error is lowest at degree 3 (0.106) while training error keeps falling. Then experiment: raise N to 200 and watch variance shrink for the high degrees (more data fixes variance, not bias); set SIGMA to 0 and the floor disappears; add degree 9 and some training sets with a gap in x make the curve explode — variance in its purest form. None of this is possible on real data, where the true function is unknown; that is why section 5's train/validation diagnosis exists.

Work it out

Two models, same data. Diagnose each and say what you would change.

A: train MAE 0.12, validation MAE 0.41.
B: train MAE 0.38, validation MAE 0.40.
You also know repeated measurements of the same input disagree by about 0.10 MAE (MAE is mean absolute error — the average size of the error).

Answer

That 0.10 is the noise floor — no model can beat it, so it is the number both should be read against.

A is variance-limited. A 0.29 gap with training error near the floor means it has memorised. Get more data, regularise harder, simplify, augment, or stop earlier; a learning curve will show how much more data would buy. Note what would not help: more capacity.

B is bias-limited. Almost no gap, but both errors sit 0.28 above the floor — the model class cannot represent the signal. Add capacity, add features, train longer, weaken regularisation. More data will do nothing here, which is the counter-intuitive half.

The reason to state the floor first: without it you cannot tell "B is underfitting" from "B is done and the task is just noisy".
