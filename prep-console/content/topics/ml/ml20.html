A support team receives 40,000 messages a week and wants each one routed automatically to the right queue: billing, delivery, technical, account, or urgent safety issues. An engineer's first instinct in 2026 is to send every message to a large language model with a prompt listing the queues. It works in a demo. Then the numbers come in: thousands of dollars a year in API calls, a second or two of latency per message, occasional outputs that aren't one of the five queue names, and nobody can say why a message about a "charge on my card" went to delivery. Another engineer spends an afternoon training a logistic regression on TF-IDF features from the 60,000 messages the team has already routed by hand. It scores within a couple of points of the LLM on the held-out set, runs in a fraction of a millisecond on the existing server, never returns an invalid label, and its mistakes can be explained by listing the words that pushed each decision.

That story doesn't mean LLMs are wrong for text classification; it means you can't know whether they're worth their cost until you have a strong cheap baseline to compare against. Classical text classification — turning text into word-count features and training a linear model — is that baseline. It's also still the production answer for a large share of real tasks: spam and abuse filters, ticket routing, topic tagging, language identification, intent detection with labelled data. And interviewers ask about it directly: "How would you build a spam classifier?", "TF-IDF plus logistic regression versus fine-tuned BERT?", "Why does naive Bayes work when its assumption is false?".

The ideas also carry forward. TF-IDF is the same weighting that powered search engines before neural retrieval (rec2) and still powers BM25-style lexical search. The trade-offs here — what to throw away when normalising text, how to handle a vocabulary that never stops growing, how to evaluate under imbalance — come back in every NLP system, including those built on transformers.

This lesson builds the pipeline from raw text to a deployed classifier: tokenising and normalising, bag of words and n-grams, TF-IDF computed by hand on a four-document corpus, the hashing trick, naive Bayes worked by hand and compared with logistic regression, imbalance and thresholds, evaluation, and finally a cost-and-latency comparison that shows when a transformer or an LLM has earned its place.

Text has to become numbers

A classifier needs a fixed-length vector of numbers for every example. Text is a variable-length string of characters. Every text classifier therefore starts by deciding how to represent a document as numbers. Modern systems use learned representations — embeddings from a neural network (lm10). The classical approach is simpler and surprisingly strong: describe a document by which words it contains and how often. Each word in the vocabulary is a column; each document is a row; most entries are zero because any document uses only a tiny fraction of the vocabulary.

An analogy: a shopping receipt

Imagine guessing whether a shopper is planning a barbecue or a baby shower from their receipt. You don't need to know the order they picked items up; the items and quantities are enough — charcoal and burgers point one way, nappies and balloons the other. A bag-of-words model reads a document like a receipt: a list of words and counts, order discarded. Some items are uninformative because everyone buys them (milk, bread) — those are like "the" and "please", and TF-IDF is the device that discounts them. The analogy breaks where order matters: "not good" and "good, not bad" contain the same words. Short sequences of words, n-grams, recover some of that.

Why linear models suit this representation

With tens of thousands of columns and a few thousand documents, almost any two classes can be separated by a hyperplane (ml18), so a linear model is usually enough — and it's fast, because scoring a document means adding up the weights of the handful of words it contains. Each class has one weight per word, so you can read off which words push a document towards "billing" and which towards "delivery". Logistic regression (ml6), naive Bayes (mc7) and linear SVMs are the three standard choices; they differ in how they set the weights.

Where this sits in NLP today

Natural language processing (NLP) has three broad generations of text classifiers, and all three are in production somewhere. Sparse features with linear models — this lesson — came first and remain the cheapest. Dense learned representations came next: word embeddings (ml21) averaged or fed to a neural network, then pretrained transformers such as BERT fine-tuned on labelled data, which understand context and paraphrase. Most recently, large language models (LLMs) classify from instructions and a few examples, with no task-specific training at all. Each generation buys more understanding of language with more compute per prediction. Knowing all three lets you pick the cheapest one that meets the accuracy bar, and explain why — which is precisely what interviewers are testing when they ask "TF-IDF or BERT?".

What "strong baseline" means

A baseline is the simplest reasonable model, built properly: sensible tokenisation, TF-IDF with word and maybe character n-grams, a tuned regularisation strength, and honest evaluation. Building it first tells you how hard the task really is, exposes label problems early, and gives a number that any more expensive model must beat by enough to justify its cost. Skipping it is how teams end up paying for an LLM that does no better than a model that would have run for free.

1. Tokenising and normalising

Tokenisation splits text into units. The classical choice is words: split on whitespace and punctuation, perhaps with rules for contractions ("don't" → "do", "n't") and hyphens. Modern neural models use subword tokens learned by byte-pair encoding (lm1, mc19), which never meet an unknown word; for TF-IDF models, words plus character n-grams (overlapping runs of 3–5 characters inside or across words) give some of the same robustness — "refnd" shares most of its character trigrams with "refund".

Normalisation makes variants match. Each step trades information for fewer, denser features:

  • Lowercasing: "Refund" = "refund". Loses "US" vs "us", and shouting in caps, which can matter for abuse detection.
  • Unicode normalisation and accents: "café" = "cafe", full-width characters = ASCII. Cheap and almost always helpful.
  • Placeholders: replace URLs, email addresses, numbers, order IDs and user handles with tokens like <url> and <num>. The fact that a message contains a URL is informative; the specific URL usually isn't, and it creates millions of one-off features.
  • Stop-word removal: drop "the", "and", "of". Less useful with TF-IDF (which down-weights them anyway) and harmful when the list includes negations like "not" and "no".
  • Stemming chops suffixes by rule ("connected", "connection" → "connect"; "studies" → "studi"); lemmatisation maps to the dictionary form using grammar ("was" → "be"). Both reduce sparsity for small datasets and matter more in heavily inflected languages; with lots of English data they often make little difference.

The two rules that matter: treat each step as a hyperparameter checked on validation data, and run exactly the same preprocessing at serving time — the most common production bug in text classifiers is a tokeniser that differs between training and serving (ml14).

2. Bag of words and n-grams

Build a vocabulary of all tokens seen in training (usually dropping those that appear in fewer than, say, 2–5 documents). Each document becomes a vector of counts over that vocabulary: the bag of words. The collection becomes a document-term matrix: one row per document, one column per term, stored sparsely because a 50-word message has at most 50 non-zeros out of perhaps 100,000 columns. A token not in the vocabulary at serving time — out-of-vocabulary — is simply dropped.

Bag of words discards order. n-grams put some back: bigrams are adjacent pairs, so "not good" becomes the features "not", "good" and "not good", and the model can learn a negative weight for the bigram. Wang and Manning (2012) found that adding bigrams gave consistent gains on sentiment tasks. The cost is size: a corpus with 50,000 distinct words may have millions of distinct bigrams, most seen once. Pruning by minimum document frequency, or hashing (section 4), keeps it manageable. Trigrams and beyond rarely pay their way for classification.

3. TF-IDF by hand

Raw counts have two problems. Common words ("my", "please", "the") have high counts in every class and drown out informative ones. And long documents have larger counts overall, so their vectors are longer. TF-IDF fixes both. The weight of term t in document d is

tfidf(t, d) = tf(t, d) × idf(t),     idf(t) = ln(N / df(t))

where tf is the count of t in d, N is the number of documents and df(t) the number containing t. Then each document vector is divided by its length (L2 normalisation), so documents of any length are comparable. Worked, with a four-document corpus:

DocText
d1refund my order
d2where is my order
d3refund please refund
d4love this app

"refund", "my" and "order" each appear in 2 of the 4 documents: idf = ln(4/2) = 0.693. Every other word appears in one document: idf = ln(4/1) = 1.386. For d2, "where is my order": raw weights where 1.386, is 1.386, my 0.693, order 0.693. The length is √(1.386² × 2 + 0.693² × 2) ≈ 2.19, so the normalised vector is where 0.632, is 0.632, my 0.316, order 0.316: the words unique to this document count twice as much as those shared with d1. For d3, "refund please refund": refund has tf 2 so 2 × 0.693 = 1.386, please 1 × 1.386 = 1.386; normalised, both 0.707. The repetition of "refund" exactly compensated for its being more common.

Libraries vary the formula, so state which one you use. scikit-learn's TfidfVectorizer (version 1.9.1 documentation) by default uses a smoothed idf, ln((1 + N)/(1 + df)) + 1, which never reaches zero — terms in every document keep weight 1 — and L2 normalisation. With it, d3 becomes refund 0.844, please 0.536: the "+1" shrinks the gap between common and rare terms. Sublinear tf, 1 + ln(tf), dampens repeated words, which helps when a spammer repeats "free" twenty times. Binarised features (1 if present) are another option and often work well for short texts.

4. The hashing trick

A vocabulary is a dictionary from token to column index. It must be built in a pass over the data, stored, versioned and shipped with the model, and it grows without bound with n-grams, misspellings and new product names. Feature hashing (Weinberger et al., 2009) replaces it with a function: column = hash(token) mod D, for a fixed table size D. No vocabulary, constant memory, and unseen tokens at serving time still get a column.

The price is collisions: two different tokens can land in the same column and share a weight. With D = 2²⁰ ≈ one million (scikit-learn's HashingVectorizer default) and a few hundred thousand active tokens, most important features don't collide; with D = 1,000 many would. A second hash decides each token's sign (+1 or −1), so colliding features partly cancel rather than always adding — scikit-learn enables this by default (alternate_sign=True). The other price is interpretability: you can't look up which word a weight belongs to without keeping a separate sample mapping. Hashing suits streaming data, online learning, very large n-gram spaces and features like user or item IDs; a stored vocabulary suits cases where you need to explain the model.

5. Naive Bayes, worked by hand

Multinomial naive Bayes (mc7) is the generative classic. It models each class as a bag of words drawn from that class's word distribution, assumes words are independent given the class (the "naive" part), and classifies with Bayes' rule: P(class | doc) ∝ P(class) × Π P(wordᵢ | class). Training is counting. Five labelled messages:

Complaint (C)Praise (P)
refund my orderlove this app
order arrived brokengreat app love it
broken app refund

Priors: P(C) = 3/5 = 0.6, P(P) = 0.4. The vocabulary has 10 words; complaints contain 9 word tokens, praise 7. With add-one (Laplace) smoothing — add 1 to every count so unseen words don't get probability zero — P(word | class) = (count + 1)/(class total + 10). Classify "broken app":

C: P(broken|C) = (2+1)/(9+10) = 0.158   P(app|C) = (1+1)/19 = 0.105
   score = 0.6 × 0.158 × 0.105 = 0.0100
P: P(broken|P) = (0+1)/(7+10) = 0.059   P(app|P) = (2+1)/17 = 0.176
   score = 0.4 × 0.059 × 0.176 = 0.0042
P(C | "broken app") = 0.0100 / (0.0100 + 0.0042) ≈ 0.71

"broken" pulls strongly towards complaint, "app" mildly towards praise, and the prior tips it further. In practice you sum log probabilities instead of multiplying, to avoid numerical underflow over hundreds of words.

Why it works despite a false assumption: correlated words ("credit" and "card") are counted as independent evidence, so the probabilities are far too extreme — but the ranking of classes is often still right, and classification only needs the ranking. Its strengths: one pass of counting, no tuning beyond the smoothing constant, robust with tiny datasets, naturally multiclass, and easy to update incrementally. Wang and Manning found it beats SVMs on short sentiment snippets, and that binarised counts work better than raw counts.

6. Logistic regression and linear SVMs on text

Logistic regression (ml6) learns one weight per feature per class, chosen to maximise the probability of the correct labels, with L2 regularisation. It doesn't assume independence: if "credit" and "card" always appear together, it can split the evidence between them rather than double-count it. That's why, given enough data, it usually beats naive Bayes on accuracy and gives much better-calibrated probabilities (ml15). Ng and Jordan (2001) described the classic trade-off: the generative model reaches its best performance with fewer examples, and the discriminative model reaches a lower error with more. Linear SVMs (ml18) on TF-IDF behave very similarly to logistic regression, trading probabilities for hinge loss.

The regularisation strength C (or λ) is the hyperparameter that matters most: with 100,000 sparse features and 5,000 examples, an unregularised model memorises rare words that happened to appear in one class. Tune it on a log grid with cross-validation. A pleasant property of linear text models is that they explain themselves: the top positive and negative weights per class are a readable list of words, and the contribution of each word to a single prediction is its TF-IDF value times its weight. Error analysis then becomes reading misclassified examples alongside their top-contributing words — the fastest way to find label noise, leakage (a signature line "Sent from the billing system" that gives the label away) and missing normalisation.

Wang and Manning's NBSVM combines the two: it scales each binary feature by the naive Bayes log-count ratio, log(P(word | positive)/P(word | negative)), and trains an SVM on those scaled features — a simple model that was strong across all their sentiment and topic datasets.

7. Imbalance, thresholds and evaluation

Text tasks are almost always imbalanced: 1–3% of messages are abusive, a few percent of tickets are urgent, and multiclass routing has a long tail of small categories. The tools of ml7 and ml8 apply directly:

  • Metrics: per-class precision, recall and F1; macro-F1 (average over classes, so small classes count equally) next to micro-F1 (pooled over examples, dominated by large classes); PR curves and PR-AUC for rare positives; a confusion matrix to see which classes are confused with which.
  • Thresholds: choose them for the cost of each error, not at 0.5. For moderation, a common design is two thresholds: auto-action above a high-precision one, human review in a middle band, pass below.
  • Class weights rather than resampling are the usual first step; they shift the decision boundary without discarding or duplicating text.

Text has its own evaluation traps. Near-duplicates: templated messages, retweets and copied spam appear in both train and test unless you deduplicate or group them before splitting, inflating scores (ml9). Time: language drifts — new products, slang, spam tactics — so validate on a later time period than you train on. Label noise: human labels for fuzzy categories often agree only 80–90% of the time; measure inter-annotator agreement on a sample, because it caps the accuracy any model can show.

At an illustrative price of $0.25 per million input tokens (check your provider's current price sheet; prices change often and vary by orders of magnitude between models), the LLM's input alone costs about $190 a day, roughly $70,000 a year, before system-prompt overhead (often larger than the message itself) and output tokens. The baseline costs a few CPU cores. That doesn't make the LLM wrong; it sets the bar. If the LLM is 6 points better in macro-F1 on the classes that drive cost — misrouted urgent tickets — it may pay for itself. If it's 1 point better, it doesn't. Common middle paths: route only the baseline's low-confidence cases (say 10%) to the LLM; or use the LLM to label data and train a fine-tuned small model (distillation), paying the LLM cost once.

Where the ranking flips: with no labels (zero-shot), with categories that change weekly, with tasks needing reasoning over long context or world knowledge, or when output must be structured extraction rather than one label (ae4). Where the baseline usually holds its own: topic-like categories with distinctive vocabulary, large labelled sets, strict latency, and on-device or high-volume settings.

9. Getting labels efficiently

A classical classifier is only as good as its labels, and labelling is usually the most expensive part of the project. Three habits stretch a labelling budget. Start from existing decisions: routed tickets, moderator actions and user reports are labels already, though biased towards what the old process noticed. Use active learning: train on what you have, then send the examples the model is least sure about (probability near the threshold) to labellers, because those teach it the most; a few rounds typically reach a given accuracy with far fewer labels than random sampling. And write a short labelling guide with examples of borderline cases, then measure agreement between two labellers on a few hundred items; if they agree only 85% of the time, a model scoring 84% is already near the ceiling and the next step is clearer category definitions, not a bigger model. Because training a linear model takes seconds, these loops can run many times a day.

Why linear models on sparse text work so well

Text classification problems often have many weakly informative features rather than a few strong ones: hundreds of words each nudge the probability of "billing". Joachims' analysis of SVMs for text pointed out that documents are high-dimensional, sparse, and close to linearly separable, and that most features carry some information — exactly the regime where a regularised linear model with all features beats aggressive feature selection. The margin and L2 penalty spread weight across correlated words rather than relying on one, which is also what makes these models robust to a single misspelt or missing word.

Naive Bayes' calibration problem, quantified

If a 200-word document contains ten words that each double the odds of "spam" under naive Bayes, the model multiplies the odds by 2¹⁰ ≈ 1,000 — even if those ten words always occur together and carry the information of perhaps two. NB's probabilities are therefore pushed towards 0 and 1, and get more extreme for longer documents. That's harmless for argmax classification but damaging for thresholds, ranking across documents of different lengths, and anything that consumes probabilities downstream. Calibrate NB scores (Platt or isotonic, ml15) before using them as probabilities, or prefer logistic regression.

Feature selection and the curse of rare features

Most vocabulary terms occur once or twice. They can't be estimated reliably, yet a weakly regularised model may give them large weights if they happened to appear in one class. Minimum document frequency (drop terms in fewer than k documents), maximum document frequency (drop terms in over 50–90% of documents), and chi-squared or mutual-information selection reduce the problem; L1 regularisation (ml3) selects features as part of training. In practice, strong L2 regularisation plus a minimum-df cut is usually enough, and aggressive selection hurts more than it helps.

Combining word and character features

Word n-grams capture meaning; character n-grams capture morphology, misspellings and obfuscation ("fr33 m0ney"). Concatenating TF-IDF features of word unigrams and bigrams with character 3–5-grams (within word boundaries) is a robust default that often beats either alone, at the cost of a larger feature space. For languages without spaces (Chinese, Japanese, Thai), character n-grams or a dedicated segmenter are required; for agglutinative languages (Turkish, Finnish), character n-grams or subwords recover much of what stemming would.

Multi-label and hierarchical labels

Many text tasks allow several labels per document (a ticket about both billing and login). The simplest approach is one binary classifier per label (binary relevance), each with its own threshold tuned for its own precision/recall needs. Correlated labels can be handled with classifier chains, which feed earlier predictions into later classifiers. Hierarchical taxonomies (department → team → issue type) can be predicted top-down, but errors at the top propagate; a flat classifier over leaves with the hierarchy used only for evaluation is often stronger.

Distilling an LLM into a cheap classifier

When an LLM classifies well but costs too much per message, use it once to label a large unlabelled sample — tens of thousands of messages — and train a TF-IDF or small-transformer model on those labels. The cheap model learns to imitate the LLM at a tiny fraction of the serving cost. Two checks keep this honest: evaluate the distilled model and the LLM against a human-labelled test set (not against LLM labels, which would hide the LLM's own errors), and look at the classes where the student disagrees most with the teacher, which are usually the ones where word counts can't capture what the LLM is using. Prompt and model versions must be recorded with the labels, because changing either changes the training data.

Confidence, abstention and routing

A classifier that must be right on what it decides can decline to decide. Routing every prediction with maximum probability below a threshold to a human or a stronger model ("selective classification") trades coverage for accuracy: a model at 90% accuracy overall might reach 98% on the 70% of messages it is most confident about. This only works if confidence is meaningful, which argues for calibrated logistic regression over raw naive Bayes scores, and for checking accuracy per confidence bucket on validation data. The same mechanism is how a cheap baseline and an expensive model are combined in a cascade.

When the baseline is the production model

Linear text models are easy to operate: training is deterministic and fast, so daily retraining on fresh labels is trivial; the model is a sparse weight matrix of a few megabytes; explanations are lists of words; and the failure modes are understood. That makes them the right production choice where requirements emphasise auditability, low latency, on-device inference or high volume. Their ceiling is set by the representation: anything that depends on word order beyond a couple of words, on world knowledge, or on paraphrase, needs learned representations — the subject of ml21 and the transformer topics.

The public case: Baselines and Bigrams

Wang and Manning's 2012 ACL paper "Baselines and Bigrams: Simple, Good Sentiment and Topic Classification" set out to answer a question many papers glossed over: how good are properly built naive Bayes and SVM baselines? Their findings, in the paper's own summary: adding word bigram features gives consistent gains on sentiment analysis; for short snippet sentiment tasks naive Bayes actually does better than SVMs, while for longer documents the opposite holds; and a simple SVM variant using naive Bayes log-count ratios as feature values performs well across tasks and datasets, matching or beating many published, more complex models of the time. They also confirmed that multinomial NB beats Bernoulli NB and that binarised multinomial NB beats the standard version. The lasting lesson for practitioners is the paper's title: build a strong baseline carefully, because it is often much closer to the state of the art than reported comparisons suggest.

Characteristic failures

Train/serve preprocessing skew. The training pipeline lowercases and strips HTML; the serving path receives raw HTML. Accuracy drops silently. Fix: one shared preprocessing function, versioned with the model, and a test that runs both paths on the same examples.

Leaky tokens. A template footer, an agent's signature, a ticket-system tag or a timestamp format that correlates with the label gives near-perfect offline scores. Fix: inspect top-weighted features for each class; anything that isn't about the content is suspect.

Vocabulary drift. New product names, slang and spam tactics appear; out-of-vocabulary rate rises and precision falls. Fix: monitor OOV rate and score distributions, retrain on recent labels on a schedule, use character n-grams or hashing for robustness.

Prevalence shift. The share of spam doubles during an attack, or falls after a fix; precision at a fixed threshold moves even if the model is unchanged. Fix: monitor predicted positive rate and sampled precision, and re-tune thresholds.

A reference implementation

In scikit-learn terms, the strong baseline is a few lines: a FeatureUnion of TfidfVectorizer on words (ngram_range (1, 2), min_df 2, sublinear_tf True) and TfidfVectorizer on characters (analyzer "char_wb", ngram_range (3, 5)), feeding LogisticRegression with class_weight "balanced", all inside one Pipeline so that cross-validation fits the vectorisers only on training folds and the whole pipeline is saved as one artefact for serving. Grid-search C over a log scale and the minimum document frequency; keep everything else at defaults until error analysis says otherwise. Saving the fitted pipeline, not just the classifier, is what prevents train/serve skew: the vocabulary, idf weights and preprocessing travel with the model.

Monitoring and maintenance

Log the predicted class distribution, mean confidence, OOV rate and input length daily; sample a few hundred predictions a week for human labelling to estimate live precision and recall per class; keep a fixed "golden" test set to detect regressions between model versions; and retrain on a schedule that matches how fast the language drifts — weekly for spam and abuse, monthly or quarterly for ticket routing. Because training takes minutes, these models can be retrained far more often than transformers.

Goal: implement TF-IDF and multinomial naive Bayes from scratch on a tiny corpus, reproduce the lesson's hand calculations, and compare word and bigram features. Standard library plus NumPy; Python 3.12 with the gpt_env NumPy.

import math
from collections import Counter
import numpy as np

docs = ["refund my order", "where is my order", "refund please refund", "love this app"]
toks = [d.split() for d in docs]
N = len(docs)
df = Counter(w for t in toks for w in set(t))
vocab = sorted(df)

def tfidf(t, smooth=False):
    tf = Counter(t)
    idf = lambda w: math.log((1 + N) / (1 + df[w])) + 1 if smooth else math.log(N / df[w])
    v = np.array([tf[w] * idf(w) for w in vocab])
    return v / np.linalg.norm(v)

X = np.array([tfidf(t) for t in toks])
print(dict(zip(vocab, X[1].round(3))))                    # d2: where/is .632, my/order .316
print("smoothed d3:", dict(zip(vocab, tfidf(toks[2], True).round(3))))
print("cosine d1·d2 =", round(X[0] @ X[1], 3))

# Multinomial naive Bayes with add-one smoothing
train = [("refund my order", "C"), ("order arrived broken", "C"), ("broken app refund", "C"),
         ("love this app", "P"), ("great app love it", "P")]
def ngrams(s, n=1):
    w = s.split()
    return w + (["_".join(w[i:i + 2]) for i in range(len(w) - 1)] if n == 2 else [])

def fit_nb(data, n=1):
    V = {g for s, _ in data for g in ngrams(s, n)}
    counts = {c: Counter() for _, c in data}
    for s, c in data:
        counts[c].update(ngrams(s, n))
    prior = Counter(c for _, c in data)
    def predict(s):
        lp = {c: math.log(prior[c] / len(data)) +
                 sum(math.log((counts[c][g] + 1) / (sum(counts[c].values()) + len(V)))
                     for g in ngrams(s, n) if g in V) for c in counts}
        z = max(lp.values())
        p = {c: math.exp(v - z) for c, v in lp.items()}
        return {c: round(v / sum(p.values()), 3) for c, v in p.items()}
    return predict

nb = fit_nb(train)
print(nb("broken app"))                                   # C ≈ 0.71
print(nb("love it"), nb("app arrived broken"))

What to look for. d2's vector should show where and is at 0.632 and my and order at 0.316; the smoothed d3 should be refund 0.844, please 0.536; the cosine between d1 and d2 should be about 0.365. Naive Bayes should give P(C | "broken app") ≈ 0.71, matching the hand calculation. Try fit_nb(train, n=2) and classify "app arrived broken": with five training sentences, bigrams mostly add unseen features (ignored here), which is why n-grams pay off only with more data. Then add "not great app" to the complaints and see how unigram NB treats "great".

Design and decide

You're asked to build a classifier that flags customer messages mentioning a safety issue (injury, fire, allergic reaction) for urgent human review. About 0.5% of 200,000 daily messages are true safety issues. You have 3,000 labelled positives and 50,000 labelled negatives from past reviews. A colleague proposes sending every message to an LLM. What do you build first, how do you evaluate it, and what would change your mind?

Answer

Build the baseline. TF-IDF on word unigrams and bigrams plus character 3–5-grams (misspellings: "alergic"), with placeholders for numbers and URLs and no stop-word removal (negations like "no injury" matter). Train logistic regression with class weights; tune C by stratified cross-validation, with near-duplicates grouped and a time-based final test split.

Evaluate for the real cost. Missing a safety issue is far worse than an extra review. Fix recall as the constraint — say 95% on the test set, with a confidence interval, since 0.5% prevalence means few positives per day — and report the precision and review volume at that threshold: at 1,000 true issues a day, 95% recall with 20% precision means about 4,750 reviews a day. Inspect false negatives one by one; read the top-weighted words for leakage.

Consider the LLM as a second stage. Sending all 200,000 messages to an LLM costs tokens and latency for the 99.5% that are obviously fine. Instead, let the baseline pass everything above a low threshold (high recall) to the LLM or straight to humans, cutting volume by an order of magnitude. Change your mind about the LLM-everywhere design if the baseline can't reach the recall target at a workable review volume — for example, if safety issues are often described indirectly ("my son's lips swelled after the snack") with little distinctive vocabulary — and the LLM measurably can, on the same test set.
