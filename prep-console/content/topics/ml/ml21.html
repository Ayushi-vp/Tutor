A team builds a support-ticket classifier on TF-IDF features (ml20) and it works well, until it meets a message that says "I'd like to be reimbursed". The training data was full of "refund" but had only two examples of "reimburse", so the model has no idea the two words are related. To a bag-of-words model every word is its own column, as unrelated to every other word as "refund" is to "giraffe". The only way to learn that "reimburse" behaves like "refund" is to see it labelled, many times. Labelled data is expensive; unlabelled text is almost free. Can the model learn what words mean from the free text, and bring that knowledge to the small labelled task?

That is what word embeddings did, and the 2013 word2vec papers are the moment the idea took off. Train a tiny model on billions of words of unlabelled text to predict which words appear near which, and the by-product is a vector per word — a few hundred numbers — in which "refund" and "reimburse" land near each other, "Paris" sits near "Berlin", and, famously, the offset from "man" to "woman" roughly matches the offset from "king" to "queen". The vectors could be dropped into any NLP model as features, and they gave gains across almost every task. For several years, "start from pretrained word vectors" was the first line of every NLP system.

Static word vectors have since been overtaken by contextual models — BERT and its successors, and the embedding models behind today's vector search (lm10) — but interviewers still ask "how does word2vec work?" for good reasons. Its training trick, negative sampling, is the ancestor of the contrastive objectives used to train modern embedding and retrieval models. Its failure modes — one vector for every sense of a word, analogies that look better than they are, social biases absorbed from text — are still the failure modes of modern embeddings. And explaining the step from static to contextual vectors is the clearest way to explain why transformers won.

This lesson starts with the distributional hypothesis and count-based vectors, then derives word2vec's skip-gram and CBOW, negative sampling (with one gradient step computed by hand) and subsampling. It covers GloVe as matrix factorisation, fastText's subwords, how to evaluate embeddings and why analogies mislead, bias in embeddings, and finally the path from static vectors through ELMo to BERT.

You shall know a word by the company it keeps

If you read "the patient was given 20 mg of zolpidem before bed" and "zolpidem helped her sleep", you can guess what zolpidem is without a dictionary: it appears where sleep medicines appear. This is the distributional hypothesis — linguists Zellig Harris and J. R. Firth's 1950s idea that words with similar meanings occur in similar contexts. It turns meaning into something measurable: describe each word by the words around it, and words with similar descriptions are similar.

An analogy: describing people by where they spend time

Suppose you describe each person in a city by how often they visit each place: gyms, libraries, bars, offices. Two people who visit the same places in similar proportions are probably similar in some way, even if they've never met. Listing every place makes each description huge and sparse, so you compress: maybe 50 underlying "lifestyle" factors explain most of the visit patterns. Each person becomes a vector of 50 numbers, and similar people have similar vectors. Word embeddings do the same with words and contexts: a word is described by the words it appears near, compressed into a dense vector of 100–300 numbers. The analogy breaks in an instructive way: two people who visit the same places might have opposite opinions about them, and two words that appear in the same contexts — "hot" and "cold", "buy" and "sell" — can be antonyms. Embeddings capture relatedness of usage, not meaning as such.

From sparse to dense

A one-hot vector for a word has a 1 in that word's column of a 100,000-word vocabulary and zeros everywhere else; every pair of words is equally far apart. An embedding is a dense vector, say 300 numbers, in which the geometry carries information: cosine similarity (ml11) between vectors reflects how similar the words' contexts are. Embeddings are learned once from a large unlabelled corpus and then reused, so a classifier trained on a few thousand labelled examples knows from the start that "reimburse" is like "refund".

Two ways to learn the vectors

Count, then compress: count how often each word appears near each other word, reweight the counts so that informative co-occurrences stand out, and compress the huge matrix with a low-rank factorisation — the SVD of ml13 — or, in GloVe's case, a weighted least-squares fit. Predict: train a small model to predict a word's neighbours from the word (or vice versa), and keep the model's internal vectors. Word2vec is the famous predict method. The two families turn out to be closely related; much of the lesson is about why.

Static versus contextual

Everything above gives each word one vector — a static embedding. "Bank" gets one vector whether it's next to a river or a loan. Contextual models compute a fresh vector for each occurrence of a word from the sentence around it; they handle multiple meanings and word order, and they are what modern NLP runs on. Static embeddings are where the ideas started and still the clearest way to understand them.

1. Count-based vectors: co-occurrence, PMI and the SVD

Slide a window of, say, two words on each side over a corpus and count, for every word w, how many times each context word c appears in its window. The result is a co-occurrence matrix X with one row per word and one column per context word. Rows are already vectors: "refund" and "reimburse" both co-occur with "my", "money", "please", "order", "back", so their rows are similar.

Raw counts are dominated by frequent words — everything co-occurs with "the". The fix is pointwise mutual information: PMI(w, c) = log [P(w, c) / (P(w) P(c))], how much more often w and c appear together than they would if independent. If "refund" appears in 0.1% of windows, "money" in 1%, and they appear together in 0.01% of windows, PMI = log(0.0001/(0.001 × 0.01)) = log 10 ≈ 2.3: ten times more often than chance. Pairs that co-occur less than chance get negative PMI, which is noisy for rare words, so practitioners clip negatives to zero: positive PMI (PPMI). The PPMI matrix is huge (vocabulary × vocabulary) and sparse; a truncated SVD (ml13) compresses each row to 300 dimensions, giving dense word vectors. This pipeline was the state of the art before word2vec, and with the right settings it remains competitive.

2. Word2vec: predicting the neighbours

Mikolov and colleagues' 2013 papers replaced counting with a prediction task. Take a sentence and a centre word; its context is the words within a window of, for example, five on each side.

Skip-gram uses the centre word to predict each context word. Every word has two vectors: an input vector v (used when it's the centre) and an output vector u (used when it's a context). The model's probability that c appears near w is a softmax over the whole vocabulary:

P(c | w) = exp(u_c · v_w) / Σ_{c' in vocabulary} exp(u_c' · v_w)

Training maximises the log-probability of the observed (centre, context) pairs. Words that share contexts are pushed to have input vectors with high dot products with the same output vectors, so their input vectors end up similar — the distributional hypothesis, implemented by gradient descent. The input vectors are what's published as "the word vectors".

CBOW (continuous bag of words) goes the other way: average the context words' input vectors and predict the centre word. It makes one prediction per position instead of one per context word, so it's faster, and it does slightly better on frequent words; skip-gram gives rare words more updates and is usually preferred for smaller corpora and rare-word quality.

The model is shallow — no hidden layers, no non-linearity, just dot products — which is exactly why it could be trained on 100 billion words. Google released 300-dimensional skip-gram vectors for 3 million words and phrases trained on part of the Google News corpus, and those became the default pretrained vectors for years.

3. Negative sampling, derived and worked by hand

The softmax's denominator sums over the whole vocabulary — a million dot products per training pair, billions of times. Word2vec's second paper replaced it with negative sampling: instead of "which of a million words is the context?", ask a yes/no question — "is this a real (word, context) pair, or a fake one?" For each real pair, draw k fake contexts (negatives) at random and train a logistic classifier (ml6) to tell them apart, with σ the sigmoid:

L = −log σ(u_c · v_w)  −  Σ_{i=1..k} log σ(−u_i · v_w)

The first term wants the real pair's dot product high; each other term wants a noise pair's dot product low (σ(−x) = 1 − σ(x)). Taking derivatives, using dσ(x)/dx = σ(x)(1 − σ(x)):

∂L/∂v_w = −(1 − σ(u_c·v_w)) · u_c  +  Σᵢ σ(u_i·v_w) · u_i
∂L/∂u_c = −(1 − σ(u_c·v_w)) · v_w
∂L/∂u_i =  σ(u_i·v_w) · v_w

Read it as forces: the centre vector is pulled towards the true context in proportion to how unsure the model was (1 − σ), and pushed away from each negative in proportion to how much the model wrongly believed it (σ). Each step touches k + 1 output vectors instead of the whole vocabulary.

One step by hand, in two dimensions, with one negative (k = 1): centre v = (0.5, 0.1), true context u_c = (0.4, 0.2), negative u_n = (0.3, −0.5).

u_c·v = 0.22   σ = 0.555        u_n·v = 0.10   σ = 0.525
loss  = −ln 0.555 − ln(1 − 0.525) = 0.589 + 0.744 = 1.334
∂L/∂v   = −0.445 × (0.4, 0.2) + 0.525 × (0.3, −0.5) = (−0.021, −0.352)
∂L/∂u_c = −0.445 × (0.5, 0.1) = (−0.223, −0.045)
∂L/∂u_n =  0.525 × (0.5, 0.1) = ( 0.262,  0.052)
learning rate 0.1:
v   → (0.502, 0.135)   u_c → (0.422, 0.204)   u_n → (0.274, −0.505)
new loss = 1.309

The centre vector moved mostly in its second coordinate, away from the negative (whose second coordinate is −0.5), and the true context moved towards the centre. After billions of such nudges, words sharing contexts share directions.

The noise distribution. Negatives are sampled in proportion to unigram frequency raised to the 3/4 power. With two words of frequency 0.9 and 0.1, plain frequency samples them 90/10; the 3/4 power gives 0.924 and 0.178, normalised to about 84/16 — rare words are sampled somewhat more than their frequency. Mikolov et al. chose 3/4 empirically. They recommend k = 5–20 negatives for small datasets and 2–5 for large ones. (The alternative to negative sampling in the same paper, hierarchical softmax, arranges the vocabulary in a binary tree and computes a word's probability as a product of about log₂V binary decisions along its path.)

4. Subsampling frequent words

In any corpus, "the", "of" and "and" make up a large share of tokens, so most training pairs involve them, and pairs like ("the", "refund") teach little. Word2vec discards each occurrence of a word w during training with probability

P(discard w) = 1 − √(t / f(w)),    t ≈ 10⁻⁵, f(w) = frequency of w

A word making up 5% of tokens is kept with probability √(10⁻⁵/0.05) ≈ 0.014 — 98.6% of its occurrences are dropped. Words with frequency below t are never dropped. This speeds training several-fold and improves vectors for rarer words; it also widens the effective window, because dropping frequent words brings content words closer together.

5. GloVe: matrix factorisation with a weighting

Pennington, Socher and Manning's GloVe (2014) went back to counts with an argument about ratios. Consider the context words "solid" and "gas" with target words "ice" and "steam". P(solid | ice) is much larger than P(solid | steam), P(gas | ice) much smaller than P(gas | steam), and for a word like "water" related to both, the ratio is near 1. Ratios of co-occurrence probabilities isolate the meaningful differences, and if dot products of vectors model logs of co-occurrence counts, ratios become differences of dot products — linear structure. GloVe therefore fits:

J = Σ_{i,j} f(X_ij) · (w_i · w̃_j + b_i + b̃_j − log X_ij)²
f(x) = (x / x_max)^α  if x < x_max, else 1     (x_max = 100, α = 3/4)

This is weighted least-squares factorisation of the log co-occurrence matrix. The weighting f is zero for zero counts (log 0 is undefined, and most cells are zero), small for rare noisy pairs, and capped at 1 so that very frequent pairs don't dominate. Each word gets a word vector w and a context vector w̃; the published vectors are their sum. GloVe trains on the non-zero entries of a precomputed matrix, which is efficient for large corpora; in practice its vectors perform similarly to word2vec's, and the choice is usually about available pretrained files.

Levy and Goldberg (2014) showed why the families agree: at its optimum, skip-gram with negative sampling sets u_c · v_w = PMI(w, c) − log k. Word2vec is an implicit factorisation of a shifted PMI matrix — the same object the count-based methods build explicitly — and much of its early advantage came from hyperparameters (subsampling, the 3/4 smoothing, window weighting) that also improve SVD-of-PPMI vectors when transferred.

6. FastText: vectors from subwords

Word2vec and GloVe have no vector for a word they didn't see often enough in training, and they learn "refund", "refunds", "refunded" and "refundable" independently. Bojanowski et al.'s fastText (2017) represents a word as the sum of vectors for its character n-grams, with boundary markers, plus the word itself. With n = 3, "where" becomes <wh, whe, her, ere, re> and the special sequence <where>; by default fastText uses all n-grams of lengths 3 to 6. Training is skip-gram with negative sampling, with the centre word's vector replaced by its n-gram sum.

The payoff: an unseen word like "refundability" still gets a meaningful vector from n-grams shared with "refund" and "ability"; misspellings land near the correct spelling; and morphologically rich languages (Finnish, Turkish, German compounds), where each word form is individually rare, get much better vectors. The cost is many more parameters, bounded by hashing n-grams into a fixed number of buckets (2 million by default) — the hashing trick of ml20 again. Subword thinking is also the bridge to modern tokenisers (lm1), which split rare words into pieces for the same reason.

Using pretrained vectors in a model

Pretrained vectors enter a downstream model in one of three ways. As fixed features: average (or weighted-average) the word vectors of a document and feed the result, perhaps alongside TF-IDF features, to logistic regression or gradient boosting — cheap, and a good way to add "reimburse ≈ refund" knowledge to a small labelled set. As the initial embedding layer of a neural network (an LSTM or CNN over the word sequence, dl7): the network starts from vectors that already encode similarity, and either keeps them frozen (safer with little labelled data) or fine-tunes them (better with more data, at the risk of drifting rare words away from their neighbours). Or for similarity directly: nearest neighbours for synonym lists, query expansion or deduplication. Lample et al.'s named-entity tagger (ml22) is a good example of the second route: initialising with pretrained vectors was the single biggest improvement in their ablation, worth over seven F1 points. Whichever route, the tokenisation used to look words up must match the one used to train the vectors, and the coverage of your vocabulary should be measured before anything else.

7. Analogies, and why they mislead

Word2vec's most famous result: the vector king − man + woman is closest to queen. In general, "a is to b as c is to ?" is answered with the word d whose vector is nearest (by cosine) to b − a + c. Worked with three-dimensional toy vectors:

WordVectorcos with king − man + woman = (0.8, −0.2, 0.2)
king(0.8, 0.3, 0.2)0.833
queen(0.75, −0.15, 0.25)0.996
man(0.2, 0.3, 0.1)0.378
woman(0.2, −0.2, 0.1)0.864
prince(0.7, 0.35, 0.3)0.773

In the toy table everything behaves. Real embeddings are less tidy, and three things make analogies look better than they are. First, the exclusion rule: the standard evaluation picks the nearest word other than a, b and c. Without it the answer is often b itself (king), because the relation offset is small relative to the vectors. Nissim, van Noord and van der Goot (2020) showed that several widely reported analogies — including biased ones such as "man is to doctor as woman is to nurse" — depended on this rule or on cherry-picking from the top few candidates; with the input words allowed, the answer was "doctor". Second, analogy benchmarks are dominated by a few regular relations (capitals, plurals, tenses, gender), which are easy for vector offsets and not representative of meaning generally. Third, analogy accuracy correlates weakly with how useful embeddings are in downstream tasks, which is the only evaluation that really matters.

8. Bias in embeddings

Embeddings learn the statistics of their training text, including its social associations. Bolukbasi et al. (2016) found that word2vec vectors trained on Google News had a "gender direction" (roughly he − she) along which occupation words lined up stereotypically, and that analogy queries produced pairs like man : computer programmer :: woman : homemaker. Their "hard debiasing" projected gender-neutral words off the gender direction. Gonen and Goldberg (2019) then showed that this is largely cosmetic: after debiasing, words that were stereotypically gendered still cluster together, and a classifier can still recover their original gender association from the vectors. The bias is spread across many dimensions, not stored in one.

The practical consequence is that bias must be tested where it causes harm — in the downstream system — with counterfactual tests: swap names, pronouns or demographic terms in inputs and compare outputs (a résumé-screening model that scores résumés mentioning "she" lower is a real, documented kind of risk). Embedding-level fixes are at best partial, and the same issue applies to contextual embeddings and to LLMs.

9. From static to contextual: ELMo and BERT

A static embedding gives "bank" one vector — a blend of its river and finance senses weighted by frequency — and gives "not" and "good" the same vectors whether or not they're adjacent. Using embeddings for a sentence then means averaging them (losing order) or feeding them to a sequence model (dl7) that must learn context from scratch on the labelled task.

ELMo (Peters et al., 2018) trained a two-layer bidirectional LSTM language model — one LSTM predicting the next word left-to-right, another predicting the previous word right-to-left — on a large corpus, with character-based input so it had no vocabulary limit. A word's ELMo representation is a learned weighted sum of the model's layers at that position, so it depends on the whole sentence: "bank" in "river bank" and "bank loan" get different vectors. Adding ELMo vectors to existing task models improved results across a broad set of NLP benchmarks.

BERT (Devlin et al., 2018) replaced the LSTMs with a transformer encoder trained by masked-language modelling (predict randomly masked tokens from both sides at once), on subword tokens. Crucially, BERT isn't used as a frozen feature extractor: the whole network is fine-tuned on each task with a small output layer on top. That combination — deep bidirectional context and end-to-end fine-tuning — gave large gains across classification, tagging (ml22) and question answering, and it's why "pretrain, then fine-tune" replaced "pretrained word vectors plus a task model". Modern sentence-embedding and retrieval models (lm10) are descendants: transformers trained with contrastive objectives that look a lot like negative sampling, applied to sentences instead of words.

Why two vectors per word

Skip-gram keeps separate input and output vectors. With a single vector per word, the model would want u_w · v_w large for a word and itself, but words rarely appear in their own context window, and the shared vector would also have to make "dog" similar to the contexts of "dog" while being one of them. Two vectors decouple the roles. Words with similar contexts end up with similar input vectors (and similar output vectors), while input·output dot products encode association. Publishing input vectors is the convention; GloVe sums both, which slightly helps; some work concatenates them. For similarity queries, input·input cosine finds words used alike ("refund" ~ "reimbursement"), while input·output scores find words that co-occur ("refund" ~ "request").

What the hyperparameters really control

Window size changes what "similar" means. Small windows (2) emphasise syntactic and functional similarity: neighbours of "Hogwarts" are other schools. Large windows (10+) emphasise topical similarity: neighbours are Harry Potter characters. Dimensionality trades expressiveness for data needs; 100–300 is the usual range, with diminishing returns above. Minimum count drops rare words whose vectors would be noise. Levy, Goldberg and Dagan showed that once these settings are matched, count-based and predictive methods perform similarly, which is a useful reminder that in embedding comparisons the hyperparameters often matter more than the algorithm.

Negative sampling as noise-contrastive estimation's practical cousin

Noise-contrastive estimation (NCE) turns density estimation into classification against noise and, with enough samples, recovers the true normalised probabilities. Negative sampling simplifies NCE by dropping the correction term that accounts for the noise distribution, so it no longer estimates P(c | w) — the result is the shifted PMI factorisation instead — but it learns good vectors faster. The same pattern appears in modern contrastive learning (InfoNCE, in-batch negatives in dual-encoder retrieval): score a positive pair against a set of negatives, and the choice of negatives (random, in-batch, hard) shapes what the representation learns. Hard negatives — wrong answers that look plausible — sharpen distinctions, at the risk of false negatives that are actually correct.

Why embeddings have a frequency problem

Vector norms and neighbourhoods are distorted by frequency: frequent words tend to have larger norms and dominate nearest-neighbour lists ("hubs" that are near many words), and rare words have noisy vectors that land in odd places. Subsampling and the 3/4 smoothing partly address this; post-processing tricks such as removing the mean vector and the top few principal components ("all-but-the-top") make similarities more isotropic and often improve downstream results. Contextual embeddings show the same anisotropy — vectors occupy a narrow cone — which is why raw BERT token vectors are poor sentence embeddings without contrastive fine-tuning.

Composition: from words to sentences

Averaging word vectors is a surprisingly strong sentence baseline, and weighting by inverse frequency (down-weighting "the") and removing the first principal component (Arora et al.'s SIF) improves it further. But averages are order-blind and wash out negation — "not good" and "good" are close. Paragraph vectors (doc2vec) learned a document vector jointly with word vectors, with mixed results. The decisive improvement came from models that read sequences — LSTMs and then transformers — and from training sentence encoders directly on sentence-pair objectives such as natural language inference and paraphrase.

Measuring embedding quality without fooling yourself

Intrinsic benchmarks — human-rated word-similarity lists and analogy sets — are cheap and useful for catching broken training runs, but they reward specific properties (synonymy, regular morphology) and can be gamed by hyperparameters. Extrinsic evaluation in the real task is the arbiter. A sound protocol fixes the downstream model, splits and tuning budget, swaps only the embeddings, and repeats over a few seeds, because differences between embedding sets are often within run-to-run noise. Nearest-neighbour inspection of the 50 words that matter most to the product is the quickest qualitative check and catches domain mismatch immediately.

Static vectors in production today

Static embeddings survive where cost and simplicity dominate: features for gradient-boosted models on product data, query expansion in lexical search (find synonyms for a query term), lightweight on-device classifiers, and item embeddings trained with word2vec on sequences of user actions ("item2vec" — treating each session as a sentence and each product as a word), which recommender systems still use because the training trick transfers perfectly to any co-occurrence data.

The public case: word2vec

Mikolov and colleagues released word2vec as open-source C code in 2013 alongside two papers: "Efficient Estimation of Word Representations in Vector Space" introduced skip-gram and CBOW and an analogy test set, and "Distributed Representations of Words and Phrases and their Compositionality" introduced negative sampling, subsampling of frequent words and phrase vectors (learning "New_York" as a single token from collocation statistics). The released vectors, trained on part of the Google News corpus, covered 3 million words and phrases in 300 dimensions. The combination of a simple, fast algorithm, freely available code and good pretrained vectors made it the default starting point for NLP feature engineering for several years, and its training trick spread to recommendation (item2vec), graph embeddings (DeepWalk and node2vec treat random walks as sentences) and beyond.

Characteristic failures

Domain mismatch. General-purpose vectors put "apple" near fruit, "python" near snakes and "cell" near biology. In a phone retailer's or a programming forum's data, those neighbours are wrong. Fix: train or continue training on in-domain text, or use a contextual model that disambiguates from the sentence.

Out-of-vocabulary words. Product names, typos, hashtags and new slang have no vector; averaging over a sentence silently drops them. Fix: fastText-style subwords, monitoring the OOV rate, and a fallback representation.

Preprocessing mismatch. Vectors trained on lowercased, tokenised text looked up with raw tokens ("Refund," with a comma) miss most words. Fix: apply the same tokenisation as the vectors' training, and log lookup hit rates.

Antonyms and negation. "Cancel" and "renew" share contexts and land close together; averaged sentence vectors make "not happy" look like "happy". Fix: use them as features alongside n-grams, or move to a contextual model when this matters.

Bias in downstream decisions. Features inherited from embeddings can make a model treat demographic terms differently. Fix: counterfactual testing in the application, as described in the body.

Item2vec: the same trick on behaviour

Because word2vec only needs sequences of tokens, it applies to any co-occurrence data. Treat each user session as a sentence and each product ID as a word, and skip-gram with negative sampling learns product vectors in which items bought or viewed together are close — useful for "similar items" and as features for ranking models. Music services have applied the same idea to playlists, and graph methods such as DeepWalk and node2vec generate "sentences" by random walks over a graph. The usual adjustments apply: subsample very popular items (the "the" of a catalogue), pick the window to match the relation you want (short for substitutes, long for complements), and handle new items with no history — the cold-start problem — with content features, the analogue of fastText's subwords.

Operating embeddings

A 3-million-word, 300-dimensional float32 table is 3.6 GB; most applications keep only the vocabulary they need (often 100,000–500,000 words) and quantise to float16 or int8. Version embeddings like models: a retrained embedding table changes every downstream feature, so downstream models must be retrained together. Nearest-neighbour queries over embeddings use the approximate indexes of lm10.

Goal: do analogy arithmetic on toy vectors with and without excluding the input words, measure a "gender direction", and reproduce one negative-sampling gradient step from the lesson. NumPy only; Python 3.12 with the gpt_env NumPy.

import numpy as np
E = {w: np.array(v) for w, v in {
    "king": [0.8, 0.3, 0.2], "queen": [0.75, -0.15, 0.25], "man": [0.2, 0.3, 0.1],
    "woman": [0.2, -0.2, 0.1], "prince": [0.7, 0.35, 0.3], "apple": [0.0, 0.0, 0.9],
    "doctor": [0.3, 0.05, 0.6], "nurse": [0.25, -0.25, 0.55]}.items()}
cos = lambda a, b: a @ b / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))

def analogy(a, b, c, exclude=True):
    q = E[b] - E[a] + E[c]
    cands = [w for w in E if not (exclude and w in (a, b, c))]
    return sorted(((round(cos(q, E[w]), 3), w) for w in cands), reverse=True)[:3]

print(analogy("man", "king", "woman"))
print(analogy("man", "doctor", "woman"), analogy("man", "doctor", "woman", exclude=False))

# A crude "gender direction" and each word's projection on it
g = (E["man"] - E["woman"]) / np.linalg.norm(E["man"] - E["woman"])
for w in ("doctor", "nurse", "apple"):
    print(w, round(E[w] @ g, 3))

# One skip-gram negative-sampling step (k = 1), as worked in the lesson
s = lambda x: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-x))
v, uc, un = np.array([0.5, 0.1]), np.array([0.4, 0.2]), np.array([0.3, -0.5])
loss = lambda v, uc, un: -np.log(s(uc @ v)) - np.log(1 - s(un @ v))
gv = -(1 - s(uc @ v)) * uc + s(un @ v) * un
guc, gun = -(1 - s(uc @ v)) * v, s(un @ v) * v
print("loss", round(loss(v, uc, un), 3), "grad v", gv.round(3))
v, uc, un = v - 0.1 * gv, uc - 0.1 * guc, un - 0.1 * gun
print("after step", v.round(3), "loss", round(loss(v, uc, un), 3))

# Subsampling and the 3/4 noise distribution
print("keep 'the' (f=0.05):", round(np.sqrt(1e-5 / 0.05), 4))
f = np.array([0.9, 0.1]) ** 0.75; print("noise probs:", (f / f.sum()).round(3))

What to look for. The king analogy should return queen first (cosine ≈ 0.996). For doctor, both lists put "nurse" first (≈0.98), with "doctor" second (≈0.79) when inputs are allowed — because these toy vectors were built with a gender component for nurse. That is the point: the analogy only reports what the vectors encode. Shrink nurse's second coordinate towards 0.05 and rerun; once the stereotype is gone from the vectors, an input word ("woman", then "doctor") tops the unexcluded list and "nurse" only wins if inputs are excluded — the pattern Nissim et al. found in real embeddings. The projections show "nurse" sitting further along the female side of the gender direction than "doctor" in these hand-made vectors — the geometric form of a stereotype. Explain and decide

An e-commerce search team wants to expand queries with related terms: a search for "sofa" should also match "couch" and "settee". They have 200 million product titles and search queries but few labels. Someone suggests the pretrained Google News word2vec vectors. What do you recommend, and what could go wrong?

Answer

Train in-domain static embeddings, not news vectors. News text uses "sofa" rarely and "couch" in phrases like "couch potato"; product vocabulary (brand names, sizes, materials, "3-seater") is mostly missing. With 200 million titles and queries, train fastText skip-gram vectors on the in-domain text: subwords handle misspellings ("sofaa") and new product names, and phrase detection learns multi-word terms ("corner_sofa"). A small window (2–3) favours substitutes ("sofa" ~ "couch"); a larger one favours related items ("sofa" ~ "cushion"), which is wrong for query expansion — tune it on a few hundred judged query pairs.

Guard against relatedness that isn't substitutability. Embeddings put "sofa" near "armchair" and "sofa bed" near "mattress" because they share contexts. Expansion should only add terms above a similarity threshold, with a lower weight than the original term (ml20's lexical scoring, rec2), and with a block list for known antonym-like pairs ("indoor"/"outdoor"). Measure the result with search metrics on an A/B test (ml16), not with nearest-neighbour lists.

Alternatives to weigh. Co-click data (users who searched X clicked items also found by Y) is a stronger signal of substitutability than text co-occurrence; item2vec on sessions captures it. A contextual or sentence-embedding model (lm10) for semantic retrieval removes the need for word-level expansion altogether, at higher serving cost — which is why many systems combine lexical search with embedding retrieval.
