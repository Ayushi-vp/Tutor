A bank wants to pull structured data out of thousands of free-text emails a day: the counterparty company, the person who signed, the amount, the settlement date. A contract team wants every party, jurisdiction and date in a document highlighted. A healthcare product needs drug names and dosages extracted from clinical notes. A search team wants to know that "flights to new york next friday" contains a destination and a date. All of these are the same problem: find the spans of text that name things of interest and say what kind of thing each one is. That's named-entity recognition (NER), and it's the most common case of a broader task, sequence labelling: assigning a label to every token in a sequence.

It looks like classification, and the first attempt is usually to classify each word on its own: is "York" a location? But words in an entity depend on each other. "New" is a location only because "York" follows it. "Washington" is a person in "George Washington" and a place in "Washington state". An entity has a beginning and an end, and the label of each word constrains its neighbours: you can't be in the middle of a name you never started. Classify words independently and you get fragments — "New" as one location and "York" as another, or an "inside a person's name" label with no beginning. The history of NER is largely the history of models that score whole label sequences instead of single labels: hidden Markov models, conditional random fields, the BiLSTM-CRF, and then BERT.

Interviewers ask about this for three reasons. "How would you build NER?" is a classic design question with clear trade-offs. The Viterbi algorithm is a favourite dynamic-programming exercise. And the current question — "should we fine-tune a tagger or just ask an LLM to extract the entities?" — needs a candidate who understands what the tagger does well (cheap, fast, exact offsets) and what the LLM does well (no training data, flexible schemas), and how to evaluate both on the same footing.

This lesson covers how spans become tags (BIO and BIOES), hidden Markov models with the Viterbi algorithm worked by hand on a three-tag example, conditional random fields and why they beat per-token classifiers and HMMs, the BiLSTM-CRF that set the standard in 2016, BERT token classification and its subword-alignment trap, span-level evaluation with exact and partial matching, and LLM-based extraction versus a fine-tuned tagger.

From spans to tags

An entity is a span: "Charles Babbage" (a person), "London" (a location). Models that label one token at a time need the spans encoded as per-token labels. The standard encoding is BIO: B-type marks the first token of an entity, I-type marks the following tokens of the same entity, and O marks tokens outside any entity.

TokenAdaLovelacemetCharlesBabbageinLondon
BIOB-PERI-PEROB-PERI-PEROB-LOC

The B tag is what lets two adjacent entities of the same type stay separate ("gave Ada Charles's notes" → B-PER, B-PER). BIOES (also called IOBES) adds E-type for the last token of a multi-token entity and S-type for single-token entities, so every boundary is explicit. With T entity types, BIO has 2T + 1 tags and BIOES 4T + 1. Some papers find small gains from BIOES; Lample et al. used it but report no significant improvement over BIO in their setting.

An analogy: reading a bus route

Think of tags as stops along a bus route and words as what you see out of the window. Some sequences of stops are impossible — the bus can't be "inside the city centre" without having entered it — and some are very likely — after "entering the centre" usually comes "still in the centre". What you see out of the window (a cathedral, a motorway) is evidence about where you are, but it's noisy. To reconstruct the route, you combine the evidence at each moment with the rules about which stops can follow which. That's exactly what a sequence model does: emission scores (how well each tag fits each word) combined with transition scores (how plausible each tag is after the previous one). The analogy breaks in that a bus route is fixed in advance; a sentence's tags are what we're trying to infer.

Why not classify each token?

A classifier per token — logistic regression on features of the word and its neighbours, or a softmax on a BERT vector — can be very accurate token by token and still produce invalid output: an I-PER after an O, or "San" and "Francisco" tagged as two separate B-LOC entities. Hugging Face's own token-classification guide shows exactly this from a small model fine-tuned for two epochs: its sample output tags "san" and "francisco" each as B-location. Each error is small at the token level and fatal at the entity level. Sequence models fix this by choosing the best whole sequence of tags, which needs a search over exponentially many sequences — made efficient by dynamic programming.

The four generations

HMMs (1990s): generative, simple counts, Viterbi decoding. CRFs (2001 onward): discriminative, rich hand-engineered features, the standard for a decade. BiLSTM-CRF (2015–2016): learned features from word and character embeddings plus a CRF. Fine-tuned transformers (2018 onward): BERT-style encoders with a token-classification head. Alongside them now sit LLMs prompted to extract entities as structured output. Each generation kept the previous one's core idea — tags depend on context and on each other — and learned more of the features automatically.

1. Hidden Markov models

A hidden Markov model (HMM) is a generative story for how a tagged sentence comes about. Tags are hidden states that follow a Markov chain: the first tag is drawn from start probabilities π, and each next tag depends only on the previous one (the Markov assumption), via transition probabilities A[j → k]. Each tag then emits a word from its own emission distribution P(word | tag). The joint probability of a tag sequence y and words x is:

P(x, y) = π(y₁)·P(x₁ | y₁) · Π_{t=2..n} A[y_{t−1} → y_t] · P(x_t | y_t)

Training with labelled data is counting, like naive Bayes (ml20): transition probabilities are how often tag k follows tag j, emission probabilities how often tag k emits each word, both smoothed. Tagging a new sentence means finding the y that maximises P(x, y) — and since there are Kⁿ possible sequences for K tags and n words (3²⁰ ≈ 3.5 billion for a 20-word sentence with three tags), we need a smarter search.

Also note that "Lovelace" on its own looks slightly more like the start of a name (0.30) than its inside (0.25) — a per-token classifier would tag it B, splitting "Ada Lovelace" into two people.

Viterbi keeps δ_t(k), the probability of the best tag path that ends in tag k at position t, plus a back-pointer to the previous tag on that path. The key insight: the best path ending in k at t must extend the best path ending in some tag j at t − 1, so we only need to remember one path per tag per position.

The best final value is δ₃(O) = 0.00216. Following back-pointers: O at t = 3 came from I, I at t = 2 came from B. The answer is B I O — "Ada Lovelace" as one person. The transition B → I (0.6) outweighed "Lovelace"'s slight preference for B, because B → B is rare (0.1). Summing over all 27 sequences gives P(x) ≈ 0.00257, so the best sequence carries about 84% of the probability; the runner-up, B B O, has 0.000216.

The work per position is K × K (each tag considers every previous tag), so Viterbi costs O(n·K²) instead of Kⁿ. In practice it runs in log space (sums of log probabilities) to avoid underflow. Replacing max with sum gives the forward algorithm, which computes P(x) — the total over all sequences — and, combined with a backward pass, the forward–backward marginals P(y_t = k | x): the probability of each tag at each position, used for per-token confidence and in training CRFs.

HMMs' weakness is the generative emission model. P(word | tag) treats the word as an atomic symbol: it can't easily use "is capitalised", "ends in -son", "previous word is Mr." or "appears in a list of company names", because those overlapping features of the same word aren't independent draws. Unknown words get only smoothed probabilities. That limitation is what CRFs remove.

3. Conditional random fields

A linear-chain conditional random field (Lafferty, McCallum and Pereira, 2001) models P(y | x) directly — discriminatively, like logistic regression (ml6) — but over whole sequences. It scores a tag sequence as a sum of feature weights:

score(x, y) = Σ_t [ emission(y_t, x, t) + transition(y_{t−1}, y_t) ]
P(y | x) = exp(score(x, y)) / Z(x),     Z(x) = Σ_{all y'} exp(score(x, y'))

The emission part can use any features of the whole sentence at position t — the word, its lowercase form, its suffix, its shape ("Xxxx", "dd/dd"), whether it's capitalised, the previous and next words, gazetteer matches — each with a learned weight per tag. The transition part is a K × K table of learned scores, such as a large negative score for O → I-PER. Z(x), the sum over all Kⁿ sequences, is computed by the forward algorithm in O(n·K²), and training maximises the log-likelihood of the gold sequences by gradient methods. Decoding is Viterbi with scores in place of log-probabilities.

Two comparisons explain why CRFs dominated NER for a decade. Against per-token classifiers: the CRF models tag dependencies and decodes globally, so spans are consistent. Against HMMs: the CRF conditions on the words rather than generating them, so it can use rich, overlapping features without pretending they're independent. And against the in-between MEMM (maximum-entropy Markov model, which normalises a classifier per position given the previous tag), the CRF normalises once over whole sequences. Lafferty et al. showed that per-position normalisation causes label bias: a state with few likely successors passes its probability on regardless of what the next word says, so evidence can't override it. Global normalisation lets evidence anywhere lower a path's score.

What a feature-engineered CRF looked like. For the token "Babbage" at position t, a typical NER CRF fired dozens of binary features, each with its own weight per tag: the lowercased word ("babbage"), its shape ("Xxxxxxx"), prefixes and suffixes up to four characters ("Bab", "age"), "is capitalised", "is the first word of the sentence", the previous word ("Charles") and next word ("visited"), the previous word's shape, and "appears in the surname gazetteer". A heavily weighted feature like "previous word is a known first name" can push "Babbage" towards I-PER even if the word itself was never seen in training. Hundreds of thousands of such features, mostly sparse, with L2 or L1 regularisation, were standard — and designing them per language and per domain was the expensive, expert part that neural models removed.

4. The BiLSTM-CRF

Feature-engineered CRFs needed experts to design features per language and domain. Lample et al. (2016) learned them instead. Each word is represented by a pretrained word embedding (ml21) concatenated with a character-level representation — a small bidirectional LSTM over the word's characters, capturing prefixes, suffixes and capitalisation, and giving unseen words a meaningful vector. A bidirectional LSTM (dl7) runs over the sentence, so each position's state summarises its left and right context; a linear layer turns that into an emission score per tag; and a CRF layer with a learned transition matrix ties the tags together and decodes with Viterbi.

On CoNLL-2003 English (the standard benchmark: news text with four types — person, location, organisation, miscellaneous) their LSTM-CRF scored 90.94 F1 without gazetteers or other external labelled resources, beating previous systems including some that used them. Their ablation is the most useful part for interviews: starting from 83.63 F1 for a model with character features and dropout but random word embeddings, adding pretrained word embeddings gave the largest gain (+7.31); the CRF layer added +1.79 over a softmax LSTM tagger (89.15 → 90.94); dropout +1.17; and character-level features +0.74. Knowledge from unlabelled text mattered most, and modelling tag dependencies still mattered.

5. BERT token classification and the subword-alignment trap

With pretrained transformers, NER became fine-tuning: run the sentence through a BERT-family encoder, put a linear layer on each token's final vector to produce tag scores, and train the whole network with cross-entropy on the tags (Devlin et al. reported strong CoNLL-2003 results this way). The contextual vectors already encode much of what the BiLSTM and character features provided, so many systems drop the CRF and take the argmax per token — although a CRF or constrained decoding still helps when data is small or spans must be valid.

The trap is tokenisation (lm1). Labels are per word; BERT sees subword tokens. "Lovelace" may become "love", "##lace"; special tokens [CLS] and [SEP] are added at the ends. Labels must be aligned:

The common bugs: copying the word's label to every subword (so "##lace" gets I-PER and "love" B-PER — or worse, B-PER twice, creating two entities); forgetting that alignment shifts after every multi-piece word; reading predictions from the wrong subword; and truncation — sentences longer than the model's maximum length lose their tail, and entities there vanish silently. Long documents need a sliding window with overlap and a rule for merging predictions. Character offsets (returned by fast tokenizers as offset mappings) are what let you map predicted spans back into the original text exactly.

6. Span-level evaluation

NER is evaluated on entities, as in the CoNLL shared tasks: precision = correct predicted entities / predicted entities, recall = correct predicted entities / gold entities, F1 their harmonic mean (ml7). Under exact match, a predicted entity is correct only if its start, end and type all match a gold entity. Worked on "Ada Lovelace worked with Charles Babbage in London":

GoldPredictedExact match?
PER "Ada Lovelace"PER "Ada Lovelace"yes
PER "Charles Babbage"PER "Babbage"no — boundary error
LOC "London"ORG "London"no — type error

One of three predictions is correct and one of three gold entities is found: precision = recall = F1 = 0.33. Token-level accuracy on the same output is 5/8 = 62.5%, because the five O and first-name tokens are mostly right — which is why token accuracy is never the headline NER metric.

Partial-match schemes are useful diagnostics. A common set, popularised by the SemEval-2013 drug-NER evaluation, reports four views: strict (exact boundaries and type), exact (exact boundaries, any type), partial (overlapping boundaries, half credit) and type (overlap with the right type). In the example, "Babbage" earns partial credit and "London" earns exact-boundary credit. Reporting them side by side tells you whether the model's problem is boundaries or types — boundary errors suggest tokenisation or BIO-consistency issues; type errors suggest ambiguous guidelines or too few examples of a type. Always report per-type results too; micro-F1 is dominated by the most frequent types. In Python, seqeval computes CoNLL-style span metrics from tag sequences (its strict mode also rejects spans that violate the tagging scheme).

7. LLM extraction versus a fine-tuned tagger

An LLM can be prompted with entity-type definitions and a few examples and asked to return entities as structured output — JSON with text and type (ae4). It needs no labelled data, handles new types from a description, copes with long-range context, and can normalise as it extracts ("next Friday" → a date). Its costs mirror ml20's: per-token price, latency of hundreds of milliseconds or more, outputs that may not be in the text at all (hallucinated or paraphrased entities), and no native character offsets — you must locate each returned string in the input, which fails when it's been altered or appears more than once.

Evaluate both the same way: map LLM outputs back to spans, count outputs that can't be found in the text as false positives, and compute span-level F1 per type on the same test set. Common architectures combine them: the LLM pre-labels data for human correction and a small tagger is trained for production; or the tagger handles the bulk and low-confidence documents go to the LLM.

8. The same machinery beyond NER

Everything in this lesson applies to any task that labels each element of a sequence. Part-of-speech tagging (noun, verb, adjective) was the original HMM application and remains the textbook Viterbi example. Chunking finds non-overlapping phrases (noun phrases, verb groups) with BIO tags. Slot filling in voice assistants and search tags "flights to [new york]destination [next friday]date" — NER with product-specific types. Personally identifiable information (PII) redaction tags names, emails, phone numbers and addresses so they can be masked before text is logged or sent to a third party. Outside text, the same HMM and CRF ideas segment speech, label genomic sequences and tag user sessions. The design questions repeat: what are the units, which tag scheme encodes the spans, how much do neighbouring labels constrain each other, and is the evaluation at the level the application consumes?

Training a CRF: the gradient is observed minus expected features

The CRF log-likelihood is score(x, y_gold) − log Z(x). Its gradient with respect to a feature's weight is the feature's count in the gold sequence minus its expected count under the model, Σ_t Σ_{j,k} P(y_{t−1} = j, y_t = k | x) × feature. Those pairwise marginals come from forward–backward. So training pushes weights until the model's expected feature counts match the observed ones — the same "observed minus expected" form as logistic regression, now over structured outputs. The cost per sentence is O(n·K²), which is why CRFs with large tag sets (fine-grained types × BIOES) get expensive, and why some systems use second-order transitions only when necessary.

Constrained decoding without a CRF

A softmax tagger's per-token log-probabilities can be decoded with Viterbi using a fixed transition matrix that is 0 for allowed transitions and −∞ for forbidden ones (O → I-X, B-X → I-Y, start → I-X). This guarantees well-formed BIO output at no training cost and typically recovers part of the gain a trained CRF gives. It's a cheap fix for the "San/Francisco" fragmentation and is worth applying to any token classifier whose output feeds a span-consuming system.

Why pretrained embeddings dominated the ablation

CoNLL-2003 English has about 15,000 training sentences, so most words — and most entity names in the test set — are rare or unseen in training. Pretrained embeddings carry information from billions of words of unlabelled text: that "Babbage" behaves like other surnames, that "Mumbai" is like other cities. That's why Lample et al.'s embedding ablation dwarfs the others, and why BERT-style pretraining improved NER further: contextual pretraining transfers knowledge about which positions in a sentence hold names, not just which words are names. The consistent lesson is that for NER, knowledge from unlabelled data matters more than architectural detail.

Memorisation and unseen entities

Benchmarks overlap heavily between train and test entity names, so reported F1 is partly memorisation. Studies that split test entities into seen and unseen consistently find much lower F1 on unseen names. In production, new product names, people and organisations appear daily, so recall on unseen entities is the number that matters. Character and subword features, context-heavy representations and entity-masking augmentation (replacing names with others of the same type during training) all improve it; evaluating separately on unseen entities is how you know.

Beyond flat NER: nesting, discontinuity and linking

BIO assumes entities are contiguous and non-overlapping. Real annotation schemes break both: nested entities ("University of [London]") and discontinuous ones in biomedical text ("left and right [ventricles]" meaning two entities sharing a word). Span-based models enumerate candidate spans up to a maximum length and classify each, which allows nesting; generative models emit entity lists. After recognition usually comes entity linking: mapping "Babbage" to a specific entry in a knowledge base, which turns extracted strings into usable identifiers and resolves ambiguity ("Paris" the city or the person). In product systems, the extracted span is rarely the final output — normalisation and linking are.

Confidence for spans, not tokens

Applications usually need a confidence per extracted entity — to auto-accept, route to review or discard — but models produce per-token probabilities. Multiplying the marginal probabilities of a span's tags (from forward–backward in a CRF, or the softmax in a token classifier) gives a crude span score; a CRF can compute the exact probability that a span is labelled as a given entity by constrained forward passes. Either way these scores are rarely calibrated, so calibrate them on validation data (ml15) per entity type before setting thresholds, and check precision by confidence bucket. LLM extraction has no native confidence at all; agreement across several sampled runs or a second verification prompt are the usual proxies, at extra cost.

Weak supervision and annotation guidelines

Labelled NER data is expensive because every token must be annotated consistently. Gazetteers (lists of known names), regular expressions for structured types (amounts, dates, IDs) and LLM pre-labelling produce noisy labels that can be combined and corrected cheaply. But the largest source of error is often the guidelines: should "the Bank of England's governor" tag "Bank of England" as ORG? Is "Apple" in "Apple Watch" an organisation or part of a product? Inter-annotator agreement measured in span F1 sets the ceiling for any model, and writing down decisions for ambiguous cases raises it more cheaply than any architecture change.

The public case: Lample et al.'s neural NER

"Neural Architectures for Named Entity Recognition" (NAACL 2016) introduced two models; the LSTM-CRF became the standard neural NER architecture until BERT. On CoNLL-2003 English it reached 90.94 F1 without gazetteers or hand-engineered features, and it achieved state-of-the-art or competitive results in Dutch, German and Spanish from the same architecture — the point being that language-independent learned features could replace per-language feature engineering. The paper's ablation (pretrained embeddings +7.31, CRF +1.79, dropout +1.17, character features +0.74) remains a compact answer to "what matters in a neural tagger?", and its reported preprocessing — no preprocessing apart from replacing every digit with zero in the English data — is a reminder of how much simple normalisation decisions are part of the model.

Characteristic failures

Broken spans. Symptom: downstream systems receive "San" and "Francisco" as two places, or entities starting with I. Cause: per-token argmax without constraints, or subword misalignment. Fix: constrained decoding or a CRF; unit tests on alignment.

Truncated documents. Symptom: recall falls for entities late in long documents. Cause: maximum sequence length. Fix: sliding windows with overlap; monitor recall by position.

Casing and domain shift. Symptom: a model trained on news fails on lower-case chat text or all-caps forms. Cause: capitalisation was the strongest feature. Fix: train on in-domain text, augment with case variations, use cased and uncased evaluation slices.

Unseen entities. Symptom: good offline F1, complaints about missed new names. Fix: evaluate seen versus unseen; add subword, gazetteer and context features; label fresh production samples.

LLM extraction drift. Symptom: output formats or entity boundaries change after a provider model update. Fix: pin model versions, validate structured output against a schema, re-run the span-level test suite on every prompt or model change.

Cost and latency envelope

A BERT-base token classifier processes a few hundred tokens in a few milliseconds on a GPU and tens of milliseconds on a CPU; distilled or smaller encoders cut that further, and a classical CRF runs in well under a millisecond per sentence. At 50,000 documents a day, all of these fit on one machine. An LLM call per document adds both a per-token charge — including the prompt with type definitions and examples, often longer than the document — and seconds of latency per call, which matters for interactive features such as highlighting entities as a user types. Those numbers are why the common production pattern is a small tagger for volume and an LLM for the long tail, the bootstrapping of labels and new entity types.

NER as a privacy control

When NER is used to redact personal data before logging or sending text to an external service, the error asymmetry flips: a missed name or phone number is a privacy incident, while an over-redacted word is a minor annoyance. Thresholds are set for very high recall, rules handle structured types (emails, card numbers with checksum validation), and the system is audited with uniformly sampled documents. Recall on unseen names and unusual formats is the metric to report, and redaction should fail closed — if the model errors, the text isn't sent.

Monitoring

Log entities per document by type, the share of documents with zero entities, the rate of invalid sequences before constrained decoding, and the average confidence. Sample documents uniformly — not only those with predictions — for annotation, to estimate recall honestly. Track per-type F1 over time on that sample, and alert when a type's extraction rate shifts sharply, which often signals an upstream format change (a new email template, a PDF parser update) rather than a model problem.

Goal: implement Viterbi for the lesson's three-tag HMM, confirm the hand calculation, compare with per-token decoding, and compute exact-match span F1. NumPy only; Python 3.12 with the gpt_env NumPy.

import itertools
import numpy as np

tags = ["O", "B-PER", "I-PER"]
start = np.array([0.6, 0.4, 0.0])
A = np.array([[0.7, 0.3, 0.0],      # from O
              [0.3, 0.1, 0.6],      # from B
              [0.6, 0.1, 0.3]])     # from I
emit = {"Ada": [0.01, 0.30, 0.05], "Lovelace": [0.01, 0.30, 0.25], "wrote": [0.20, 0.01, 0.01]}
words = ["Ada", "Lovelace", "wrote"]

def viterbi(words):
    delta = start * np.array(emit[words[0]]); back = []
    for w in words[1:]:
        cand = delta[:, None] * A                    # cand[j, k]: best path into j, then j -> k
        back.append(cand.argmax(0))
        delta = cand.max(0) * np.array(emit[w])
        print(w, delta.round(6))
    path = [int(delta.argmax())]
    for b in reversed(back):
        path.append(int(b[path[-1]]))
    return [tags[i] for i in reversed(path)], delta.max()

print(viterbi(words))
print("per-token:", [tags[int(np.argmax(emit[w]))] for w in words])

# Brute force: all 27 sequences, to check Viterbi and get P(x)
def joint(seq):
    p = start[seq[0]] * emit[words[0]][seq[0]]
    for t in range(1, len(seq)):
        p *= A[seq[t - 1], seq[t]] * emit[words[t]][seq[t]]
    return p
probs = {s: joint(s) for s in itertools.product(range(3), repeat=3)}
Z = sum(probs.values())
best = max(probs, key=probs.get)
print("best", [tags[i] for i in best], "share of P(x):", round(probs[best] / Z, 3))

# Exact-match span F1
def spans(tagseq):
    out, cur = set(), None
    for i, t in enumerate(tagseq + ["O"]):
        if cur and not (t.startswith("I-") and t[2:] == cur[2]):
            out.add((cur[0], i, cur[2])); cur = None
        if t.startswith("B-") or (t.startswith("I-") and cur is None):
            cur = (i, None, t[2:])
    return out
gold = "B-PER I-PER O O B-PER I-PER O B-LOC".split()
pred = "B-PER I-PER O O O B-PER O B-ORG".split()
g, p = spans(gold), spans(pred)
tp = len(g & p); prec, rec = tp / len(p), tp / len(g)
print("spans", sorted(p), "P", prec, "R", rec, "F1", round(2 * prec * rec / (prec + rec), 3))
print("token accuracy", sum(a == b for a, b in zip(gold, pred)) / len(gold))

Design

An insurance company wants to extract policy numbers, claimant names, dates of incident and claim amounts from 50,000 incoming claim emails a day. They have no labelled data. A team proposes calling an LLM on every email. Design the system and its evaluation.

Answer

Split the entity types by difficulty. Policy numbers and amounts follow formats — regular expressions plus validation (check digits, currency parsing) handle most of them exactly and for free. Dates need a date parser on candidate strings plus context ("incident on" vs "email sent"). Names and incident descriptions are where a learned model is needed.

Bootstrap with the LLM, then decide. Write annotation guidelines, then use an LLM with structured output (ae4) on a few thousand emails to pre-label all four types; have annotators correct a sample of 1,000 to create a gold test set and measure the LLM's span-level F1 per type, counting outputs not found in the email text as false positives. Fine-tune a small encoder tagger (BIO tags, first-subword alignment, constrained decoding) on the corrected and LLM-labelled data and evaluate on the same gold set.

Production design. If the tagger is within a point or two of the LLM, serve the tagger plus rules for all 50,000 emails — milliseconds each, exact offsets for highlighting in the claims UI — and route emails where the tagger's confidence is low or required fields are missing (perhaps 5–10%) to the LLM or a human. If the LLM is much better on names in messy emails, keep it for that type only. Either way: validate every extracted field (format, plausibility, policy number exists), report per-type recall on a uniformly sampled monthly audit, and pin model and prompt versions, re-running the test set on every change.
