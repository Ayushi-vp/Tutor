A bank wants to predict which small-business loans will default. It has 800 past loans and, thanks to an enthusiastic data team, 3,000 candidate features: every ratio from every financial statement, several months of account balances, industry codes, postcode statistics. A straightforward linear model fitted to all of it achieves almost perfect accuracy on the 800 training loans and does little better than guessing on the next 200. With more features than examples, the model can find some combination of columns that explains every past default, including all the coincidences. Removing features by hand would take weeks and would be guesswork.

ml2 explained what is happening: this is variance. The model has so much freedom that it fits the noise, and the fitted weights would change completely if a slightly different set of 800 loans had been collected. What ml2 didn't provide was a precise, adjustable way to take some of that freedom away. Regularisation is that tool. The most common form adds a second term to the training objective that charges the model for having large weights. The model can still use any feature it likes, but every unit of weight has to "pay for itself" by reducing the error enough to justify the cost. A single number, λ, sets the price.

Two choices of price behave very differently. Charging for the squares of the weights (L2, ridge) shrinks everything a little. Charging for their absolute values (L1, lasso) sets most of the 3,000 weights to exactly zero, leaving a model the bank can read: these twelve features matter. Understanding why the two differ — algebraically and geometrically — is one of the most common interview questions in machine learning, and the answer reaches all the way to how large language models are trained, where "weight decay" is regularisation under another name.

This lesson builds regularisation from the objective up: what the penalty does to a single weight (worked by hand for both L1 and L2), the diamond-versus-circle geometry with a computed example, choosing λ, why features must be standardised, the Bayesian reading, elastic net, and the many techniques that regularise without looking like a penalty — early stopping, dropout, data augmentation, weight decay in AdamW, label smoothing and LoRA. The public case reproduces a classic published comparison on real prostate-cancer data, where lasso keeps three of eight clinical measurements and still beats unregularised least squares on held-out patients.

A budget for complexity

Imagine giving a contractor a job and paying them only for how well the result matches your specification. They will use every trick available, including fragile ones, to hit every measurement exactly — and the result might fall apart the first time conditions change. Now add a second rule: every material they use costs money, deducted from their fee. They will still use whatever genuinely improves the result, but they will stop adding things that help only marginally. The result is simpler and more robust. That second rule is regularisation.

In machine learning the "materials" are the model's weights. The training objective becomes:

total cost  =  how badly the model fits the training data  +  λ × how large the weights are
                (the loss, e.g. squared error)                (the penalty)

λ (lambda) is the regularisation strength, a hyperparameter chosen by you, not learned. At λ = 0 there is no penalty and you get the ordinary fit. As λ grows, large weights become expensive, and the model uses only the features that reduce error enough to justify their cost. At enormous λ, every weight is driven towards zero and the model predicts roughly the average for everything.

Why small weights mean a simpler model

A weight says how strongly the output reacts to a feature. With large weights, a small change in an input causes a big change in the output, so the model can bend sharply to pass through every training point — including the noisy ones. With small weights, the model's output changes gently as inputs change; it can capture broad patterns but not every wiggle. Limiting weight size therefore limits how contorted the fitted function can be, which is exactly the capacity that ml2 identified as the source of variance. The price is a little bias: the model may now be too gentle to capture a pattern that really is sharp. That is why λ must be tuned rather than set high.

Two ways to measure "how large"

  • L2 penalty (ridge): the sum of squared weights, w₁² + w₂² + …. Large weights are punished heavily (doubling a weight quadruples its cost), small ones hardly at all. Effect: all weights shrink, none reach zero.
  • L1 penalty (lasso): the sum of absolute weights, |w₁| + |w₂| + …. Every unit of weight costs the same, whether the weight is large or tiny. Effect: weights that only help a little are set to exactly zero, so the model selects features.
  • Elastic net: a mix of both.

One practical rule before anything else: standardise the features (rescale each to mean 0 and standard deviation 1) before penalising weights. The penalty sees only the numbers in the weight vector, and those depend on each feature's units. Without standardising, the penalty would favour or punish features according to whether they happened to be recorded in millimetres or kilometres.

1. The penalised objective

For a linear model ŷ = w₁x₁ + … + w_px_p + b with squared-error loss, the three standard objectives are:

ordinary least squares:  minimise  Σᵢ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²
ridge (L2):              minimise  Σᵢ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²  +  λ Σⱼ wⱼ²
lasso (L1):              minimise  Σᵢ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²  +  λ Σⱼ |wⱼ|

The sums over j run over the weights but not the intercept b, which sets the average level of predictions and is not a source of complexity. Libraries scale these differently, which matters when you copy a λ from one to another: scikit-learn's Ridge minimises exactly the ridge line above (calling λ alpha), while its Lasso divides the squared-error term by 2n (twice the number of rows), so the same alpha means a much stronger penalty in lasso than in ridge. LogisticRegression uses C, the inverse of the strength: small C means strong regularisation.

2. One weight, worked by hand

The fastest way to see the difference is a model with a single feature and no intercept (both x and y centred to mean zero). Take three points: x = −1, 0, 1 and y = −2, 0.5, 1.5. Two sums are all we need: Σxy = 2 + 0 + 1.5 = 3.5 and Σx² = 1 + 0 + 1 = 2.

Ordinary least squares. Setting the derivative of Σ(y − wx)² to zero gives w = Σxy / Σx² = 3.5 / 2 = 1.75.

Ridge. The derivative of Σ(y − wx)² + λw² is −2Σxy + 2wΣx² + 2λw. Setting it to zero:

w_ridge = Σxy / (Σx² + λ) = 3.5 / (2 + λ)
λ = 1   → 3.5 / 3   = 1.167
λ = 2   → 3.5 / 4   = 0.875
λ = 10  → 3.5 / 12  = 0.292
λ = 100 → 3.5 / 102 = 0.034     (never exactly zero)

Ridge divides: λ adds to the denominator, so the weight is scaled down by the factor Σx² / (Σx² + λ). It gets smaller and smaller but only reaches zero as λ goes to infinity.

Lasso. The absolute value has a corner at zero, so we handle the positive side (w > 0, where |w| = w and its slope is 1). The derivative of Σ(y − wx)² + λw is −2Σxy + 2wΣx² + λ. Setting it to zero:

w_lasso = (Σxy − λ/2) / Σx² = (3.5 − λ/2) / 2
λ = 1 → 3.0 / 2 = 1.50
λ = 2 → 2.5 / 2 = 1.25
λ = 7 → 0.0 / 2 = 0          and for any λ ≥ 7 the weight stays exactly 0

Lasso subtracts: the evidence for the feature, Σxy = 3.5, is reduced by a fixed λ/2 before dividing. Once λ/2 exceeds the evidence, the formula would give a negative weight, but a negative weight would pay the penalty and fit the data worse, so the best choice is exactly zero. That is soft-thresholding: shrink towards zero by a threshold, and snap to zero anything that was within the threshold. The general rule (for standardised, uncorrelated features) is w = sign(z) · max(|z| − t, 0), where z is the unregularised weight and t the threshold. It is what the diagram in the primer draws.

3. The same thing through gradients

Most models are trained iteratively by gradient descent — repeatedly moving each weight a small step in the direction that reduces the loss (ml4 covers it fully). The gradient is the slope of the objective with respect to each weight. The penalty adds its own slope to the data's:

  • L2 penalty λw² has slope 2λw: proportional to the weight. A weight of 4 is pulled back eight times harder than a weight of 0.5. As the weight shrinks, so does the pull, so it approaches zero ever more slowly and never arrives.
  • L1 penalty λ|w| has slope λ·sign(w): the same size whatever the weight. A weight of 0.01 is pulled towards zero just as hard as a weight of 4, so small weights get pushed all the way.

Because |w| has a corner at zero, plain gradient descent tends to make lasso weights jitter around zero rather than land on it. Lasso solvers therefore use coordinate descent (update one weight at a time, applying soft-thresholding exactly) or proximal gradient methods (take a gradient step on the loss, then soft-threshold), which the lab implements in six lines.

4. The geometry: diamond versus circle

There is an equivalent way to state each problem: minimise the loss subject to the penalty being at most some budget t. Every λ corresponds to some t. With two weights, the ridge budget w₁² + w₂² ≤ t is a disc; the lasso budget |w₁| + |w₂| ≤ t is a diamond with its corners on the axes. The loss has its minimum at the ordinary least-squares solution, and its level sets — the points of equal loss — are rings around that minimum. The regularised solution is the first point where an expanding ring touches the budget region.

Worked example. Suppose the unregularised solution is (w₁, w₂) = (2, 0.5) and, to keep the arithmetic simple, the loss is plain squared distance from that point (circular rings). With a budget of 1:

  • L2 disc of radius 1. The closest point on a disc is along the straight line to the centre: divide (2, 0.5) by its length √(4 + 0.25) = 2.062, giving (0.970, 0.243). Both weights shrink by the same proportion; neither is zero.
  • L1 diamond with |w₁| + |w₂| ≤ 1. The closest point is found by soft-thresholding both coordinates by the same amount τ until they sum to 1. Try τ = 0.75: (1.25, −0.25) — but the second can't go negative, so it clips at 0. With w₂ = 0, w₁ must be 1: τ = 1 gives (2 − 1, max(0.5 − 1, 0)) = (1, 0), the corner. The small weight has been removed entirely.

Real loss rings are ellipses (stretched when features are correlated), which changes where they touch but not the key fact: a region with corners on the axes is touched at a corner far more often than a smooth one is touched on an axis. In high dimensions the effect is stronger, because the L1 ball has corners and edges in every combination of axes.

5. Standardise first — with numbers

Suppose a house-price model finds that each extra metre of garden adds £2,000, so the weight is 2,000 per metre. Record the same length in millimetres and the weight becomes 2 per millimetre. The fit is identical, but the L2 penalty on that weight drops from λ × 4,000,000 to λ × 4 — a factor of a million — so the regulariser would barely touch the millimetre version while crushing the metre version. The choice of units, not usefulness, would decide which features survive. Standardising every feature to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 removes this arbitrariness: a weight then means "the change in prediction per standard deviation of the feature", comparable across features. Compute the means and standard deviations on the training data only, and apply those same numbers to validation and test data, or information leaks from the held-out sets (ml9).

6. Choosing λ

λ is a hyperparameter, so it is chosen on held-out data. The usual procedure is cross-validation (splitting the training data into k parts and rotating which part is held out; ml9 covers it) over a grid on a log scale, such as 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100 — the useful values span orders of magnitude. Plotting each coefficient against λ gives the regularisation path: for ridge, curves that glide smoothly towards zero; for lasso, curves that hit zero one after another, so reading the plot from right to left shows the order in which features enter the model.

A common refinement is the one-standard-error rule. Cross-validation scores are noisy estimates, so instead of the λ with the very best score, take the largest λ whose score is within one standard error of the best. It gives a simpler model whose performance is statistically indistinguishable. The public case below shows the rule at work.

Since you rarely know which world you are in, trying both, or elastic net, under cross-validation is the practical answer.

7. Elastic net: the best of both

Lasso has two weaknesses. When features are strongly correlated — say, three different measurements of company size — it tends to keep one and drop the others, and which one it keeps changes with small changes in the data. And when there are more features than rows, it can select at most as many features as there are rows. Elastic net adds both penalties: λ₁ Σ|wⱼ| + λ₂ Σwⱼ². The L2 part prefers to spread weight across correlated features (for a fixed total, squared weights are smallest when the weight is shared), so correlated groups enter and leave together; the L1 part still produces zeros. In scikit-learn the mix is set by l1_ratio (1 = pure lasso, 0 = pure ridge). With 3,000 correlated financial features and 800 loans, elastic net is the natural first choice.

8. The Bayesian reading

There is a second, probabilistic way to arrive at the same objectives. Suppose labels are the model's prediction plus Gaussian noise of variance σ², and before seeing data you believe each weight is probably small: a prior distribution centred on zero. The MAP estimate (maximum a posteriori) — the weights that are most probable given the data and the prior — minimises the negative log of (likelihood × prior):

Gaussian prior, variance τ²:   Σ(y − ŷ)² / (2σ²) + Σw² / (2τ²)
multiply by 2σ²:               Σ(y − ŷ)² + (σ²/τ²) Σw²      ← ridge with λ = σ²/τ²

A Laplace prior — a distribution with a sharp peak at zero — gives the lasso penalty instead. This reading explains λ: it is the ratio of noise to prior spread. Noisy data (large σ²) or a strong belief that weights are small (small τ²) both mean a larger λ. And the sharp peak of the Laplace prior at zero is the probabilistic counterpart of the diamond's corners: it puts real preference on weights being exactly zero.

Ridge fixes ill-conditioned problems

Ridge regression has a closed-form solution, w = (XᵀX + λI)⁻¹ Xᵀy, where I is the identity matrix. Without the λI, least squares needs to invert XᵀX, which fails or becomes numerically unstable when features are perfectly or nearly collinear, or when there are more features than rows. Adding λ to every diagonal element makes the matrix invertible and well-behaved. That was ridge's original purpose (Hoerl and Kennard, 1970): stabilising regression with correlated predictors. In terms of the data's principal directions (ml13), ridge shrinks the weight along each direction by d²/(d² + λ), where d² measures how much the data varies along it — directions the data barely explores, where estimates are least reliable, are shrunk the most.

Weight decay versus L2 under adaptive optimisers

With plain gradient descent and step size η, the L2 penalty (λ/2)‖w‖² contributes λw to the gradient, so each step multiplies every weight by (1 − ηλ) before the data's update: with η = 0.1 and λ = 0.01, that is 0.999 per step, and after 1,000 steps with no data pressure a weight would be down to 0.999¹⁰⁰⁰ ≈ 0.37 of its value. That multiplicative shrink is weight decay, and for plain gradient descent it is identical to L2. Adaptive optimisers such as Adam divide each parameter's gradient by a running estimate of its typical size. If the L2 term is inside the loss, its gradient is divided too, so parameters that have large gradients receive far less decay than intended. Loshchilov and Hutter's AdamW applies the decay directly to the weights, outside the adaptive scaling, which restores uniform shrinkage and improved generalisation in their experiments. AdamW with weight decay is the default optimiser for transformer training (dl5 covers optimisers).

Dropout

Dropout (Srivastava et al., 2014) sets each hidden unit's output to zero with some probability p (often 0.1–0.5) at every training step, and rescales the rest to keep the expected total unchanged. A unit can't rely on particular other units being present, which discourages fragile co-adaptations. Two readings explain why it regularises: training with dropout approximately trains an ensemble of exponentially many "thinned" networks that share weights, and averaging reduces variance (ml2's ensemble formula); and for linear models, dropout in expectation is equivalent to an L2 penalty weighted by each feature's variance. At inference time dropout is switched off.

Data augmentation and label smoothing

Augmentation creates new training examples by transformations that shouldn't change the label — flipping, cropping or colour-shifting an image; adding noise to audio; paraphrasing text. It encodes knowledge ("a cat flipped left-to-right is still a cat") that rules out many functions which fit the original data but violate the invariance. When the invariance is real, it is often the strongest regulariser available; when it isn't (flipping digits turns some into other digits), it hurts. Label smoothing replaces a one-hot target (1 for the right class, 0 for the others) with, for example, 0.9 and small shares for the others, so the model is never pushed towards infinite confidence; it tends to improve calibration (ml15) and generalisation.

Low-rank constraints in fine-tuning

Fine-tuning all billions of weights of a large model on a few thousand examples invites overfitting. LoRA (low-rank adaptation) freezes the original weights and learns only a small update to each weight matrix, expressed as the product of two thin matrices of rank r (say 8 or 16). That cuts trainable parameters by orders of magnitude and constrains the update to a low-dimensional space — a structural regulariser, analogous to limiting a linear model to a few principal directions.

Sparsity beyond lasso

The L1 penalty is the convex stand-in for what one often really wants: to count non-zero weights (the "L0" penalty) and keep few. Counting is combinatorial and intractable for many features; L1 is its closest convex relaxation, which is why it's used. Group lasso penalises the L2 norm of predefined groups (for example, all the dummy columns of one categorical variable), so whole groups are kept or dropped together. For trees and boosting (ml10), regularisation takes other forms — maximum depth, minimum samples per leaf, shrinkage (learning rate), subsampling — but the principle is the same: limit how closely the model can follow the training data.

The public case: lasso on the prostate-cancer data

The textbook The Elements of Statistical Learning (Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman) uses a real clinical dataset to compare regression methods: 97 men about to undergo prostate surgery, eight clinical measurements (such as log cancer volume, log prostate weight, age and seminal-vesicle invasion), and the target, log prostate-specific antigen (lpsa). The data and its fixed split — 67 men for training, 30 for testing — are published on the book's website. We reproduced the comparison in NumPy with standardised features:

Three lessons carry over to industry. A small dataset with several correlated measurements is exactly where regularisation pays. The simpler, sparser model is not only easier to explain to a clinician but also more accurate on new patients. And the choice between "best CV score" and "one standard error" changed the model from seven features to three with no loss — because with 67 rows, cross-validation differences smaller than one standard error are noise. (With only 30 test patients, the ranking between ridge and lasso here is itself within noise; the robust conclusion is that both beat unregularised least squares.)

Where regularisation goes wrong in production

Scaling mismatch between training and serving. A model trained on standardised features must receive features standardised with the same training means and standard deviations at serving time. Recomputing them on live data, or forgetting to apply them, silently shifts every prediction. Store the scaler with the model as one artefact (ml14).

λ tuned on the wrong split. If the scaler or feature selection was fitted on all data before cross-validation, the chosen λ is optimistic. Put scaling, selection and the model inside one pipeline that cross-validation refits in each fold.

Interpreting lasso's selection causally. Teams report "lasso found that feature X doesn't matter" when X was simply correlated with a feature lasso kept. Check selection stability across bootstrap samples before telling anyone which features matter.

Weight decay on the wrong parameters. In deep-learning recipes, biases and normalisation parameters are usually excluded from weight decay. Decaying them can hurt training, and frameworks don't exclude them automatically — the parameter groups have to be set up explicitly.

What to monitor and tune

Track the regularisation strength alongside the model version, the number of non-zero coefficients for sparse models (a sudden change after retraining often signals a data problem), and the train–validation gap over retrains. When the gap grows as data drifts, increasing λ is a quick mitigation while the underlying shift is investigated.

Goal: watch ridge shrink and lasso select, on data where you know the truth. NumPy only; tested with Python 3.12.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, p = 60, 30                                   # few rows, many features
X = rng.normal(size=(n, p))
w_true = np.zeros(p); w_true[:3] = [3.0, -2.0, 1.5]   # only 3 features matter
y = X @ w_true + rng.normal(0, 1.0, n)
Xtr, ytr, Xva, yva = X[:40], y[:40], X[40:], y[40:]
mse = lambda w, X, y: np.mean((y - X @ w) ** 2)

print("ridge:  lambda   train    val   |w|2")
for lam in [0, 0.1, 1, 10, 100]:
    w = np.linalg.solve(Xtr.T @ Xtr + lam * np.eye(p), Xtr.T @ ytr)
    print(f"{lam:>13} {mse(w, Xtr, ytr):6.2f} {mse(w, Xva, yva):6.2f} {np.linalg.norm(w):6.2f}")

def lasso(X, y, lam, steps=5000):               # ISTA: gradient step, then soft-threshold
    L = np.linalg.norm(X, 2) ** 2               # step size 1/L keeps it stable
    w = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
    for _ in range(steps):
        z = w - X.T @ (X @ w - y) / L
        w = np.sign(z) * np.maximum(np.abs(z) - lam / L, 0)
    return w

print("lasso:  lambda   train    val  nonzero")
for lam in [0.1, 1, 5, 20, 80]:
    w = lasso(Xtr, ytr, lam)
    print(f"{lam:>13} {mse(w, Xtr, ytr):6.2f} {mse(w, Xva, yva):6.2f} {np.sum(w != 0):6d}")
print("lasso weights at lambda=20:", np.round(lasso(Xtr, ytr, 20)[:6], 2))

What to look for. Ridge's weight norm falls from 4.37 to 1.17 but validation error only rises — shrinking all 30 weights doesn't help when the truth is sparse. Lasso's non-zero count falls from 29 to 3, and validation error bottoms out at 1.13 (λ = 5); at λ = 20 the surviving weights are the three true features (2.24, −1.93, 0.97), shrunk towards zero. Try making w_true dense (all 30 non-zero and small) and ridge becomes the winner.

Work it out

In scikit-learn you write LogisticRegression(C=0.01). A colleague says that is barely any regularisation. Who is right, and what happens to the coefficients as you sweep C from 100 down to 0.001?

Answer

Your colleague has it backwards. scikit-learn's LogisticRegression is parameterised by C, the inverse of regularisation strength: smaller C means a larger penalty. C=0.01 is very strong regularisation; C=100 is nearly unregularised.

Sweeping down from C = 100: under the default L2 penalty, coefficients shrink smoothly towards zero, never reaching it. With penalty='l1' (and a solver that supports it, such as liblinear or saga) they hit exactly zero one by one — the weakest features first — and at small enough C every coefficient is zero, so the model predicts the same probability for everyone: the training base rate.

Two follow-ons worth knowing. Scale first — an unscaled feature measured in pounds and one in thousands of pounds get wildly unequal penalties, so the penalty silently decides which features survive based on units. And the intercept is not penalised, which is why the model can still fit the base rate at any C.
