Two engineers train the same model on the same data with the same code. One sets the learning rate to 0.5 and watches the loss climb to infinity within a hundred steps, then print NaN. The other sets it to 0.001 and watches the loss fall so slowly that after a day of GPU time the model is still worse than a straight line. A third engineer uses 0.05 and the model is trained in an hour. Nothing else differs. The learning rate is one number, but it decides whether training fails, crawls or works — and knowing why is the difference between tuning by superstition and tuning by understanding.

Every model in this track after linear regression — logistic regression, boosted trees in their own way, and every neural network including large language models — is trained by some form of gradient descent: compute which direction reduces the loss, take a step that way, repeat. For a linear model with squared error there is a formula for the answer (ml1 used it), but formulas don't exist for most models, and even where they do they become too expensive for millions of rows. So training is a walk, and the learning rate is the length of the stride.

This walk explains a surprising amount of practical ML. Why features should be scaled before training. Why the loss sometimes explodes, and why lowering the learning rate or clipping gradients fixes it. Why large models are trained with mini-batches, why larger batches need larger learning rates, and why the first few thousand steps use a "warmup". Why Adam became the default optimiser, and what momentum actually does. Interviewers ask all of these, often as "your loss is doing this — what's wrong?"

This lesson builds gradient descent from a single slope: a one-dimensional example step by step, one full update of a two-parameter linear regression computed by hand, the stability limit η < 2/c and where it comes from, the condition number and why unscaled features make training crawl, mini-batch and stochastic gradients, momentum and Adam (including why Adam's first step has the same size for every parameter), learning-rate schedules, how to read a loss curve, and the public case of Goyal et al., who trained an ImageNet classifier in one hour by getting exactly these details right.

Walking downhill in fog

Imagine standing on a hillside in thick fog, trying to reach the lowest point of the valley. You can't see the valley, but you can feel the slope under your feet. A sensible strategy: feel which direction goes downhill most steeply, take a step that way, and repeat. When the ground is flat in every direction, stop. That is gradient descent.

In machine learning, your position is the set of parameter values (the model's weights), and the height of the ground is the loss — how badly the model fits the training data with those weights. The gradient is the slope: for every weight, how much the loss would change if you nudged that weight up a little. It points in the direction of steepest increase, so we step the opposite way. The size of the step is set by the learning rate, written η (the Greek letter eta):

new weights = old weights − η × gradient

The analogy holds well, with two refinements. The steps are not a fixed length: the step is η times the slope, so steep ground gives big steps and nearly flat ground gives tiny ones — which is why progress slows naturally as you approach the bottom. And the fog is worse than it sounds: in a real model the "hillside" has millions or billions of dimensions, one per weight, and you can only feel the slope, never see the shape.

One dimension, step by step

Minimise L(w) = (w − 3)², whose lowest point is obviously at w = 3. Its derivative — the slope at any point — is 2(w − 3). Start at w = 0 with η = 0.1:

  • Step 1: slope = 2(0 − 3) = −6. Update: w = 0 − 0.1 × (−6) = 0.6.
  • Step 2: slope = 2(0.6 − 3) = −4.8. Update: w = 0.6 + 0.48 = 1.08.
  • Step 3: slope = 2(1.08 − 3) = −3.84. Update: w = 1.08 + 0.384 = 1.464.

The gap to 3 goes 3 → 2.4 → 1.92 → 1.536: each step removes 20% of what remains, because the update multiplies the gap by (1 − 2η) = 0.8. After 20 steps the gap is 3 × 0.8²⁰ ≈ 0.035.

Now vary η. At η = 0.01 the gap shrinks by only 2% per step: about 230 steps to get within 1%. At η = 0.3 it shrinks to 40% each step: six steps. At η = 0.5 the factor is zero and w lands on 3 in a single step. At η = 1.1 the factor is 1 − 2.2 = −1.2: the gap flips sign and grows 20% every step, so w goes 0 → 6.6 → −1.32 → 8.18 → … and the loss explodes. Picking the learning rate well is the single most important decision in training, and the rest of this lesson explains how the "right" value is determined by the shape of the loss.

How the slopes are computed

For a linear model the gradient has a short formula, derived in the body below. For a neural network with millions of weights, the gradient is computed by backpropagation, which applies the chain rule of calculus backwards through the layers to get every weight's slope in one pass that costs about as much as a couple of forward passes (dl1 covers it). Frameworks like PyTorch and JAX do this automatically; you define the loss and they return the gradient.

1. The gradient, defined

A model with several parameters has a loss that depends on all of them, L(w, b). The partial derivative ∂L/∂w is the slope along w alone, holding b fixed; ∂L/∂b is the slope along b alone. The gradient ∇L is the vector of all partial derivatives, (∂L/∂w, ∂L/∂b). It points in the direction in which the loss increases fastest, and its length says how steep that increase is. Gradient descent updates every parameter at once:

w ← w − η · ∂L/∂w
b ← b − η · ∂L/∂b

For linear regression with mean squared error, L = (1/n) Σᵢ (ŷᵢ − yᵢ)² with ŷᵢ = w·xᵢ + b, the chain rule gives:

∂L/∂w = (2/n) Σᵢ (ŷᵢ − yᵢ) · xᵢ          ← residual times input, averaged
∂L/∂b = (2/n) Σᵢ (ŷᵢ − yᵢ)                ← residual, averaged

Read them in words: if predictions are too low on average (negative residuals), ∂L/∂b is negative and the update raises b. If predictions are too low mostly where x is large, ∂L/∂w is negative and the update raises the slope. The gradient is a precise summary of "which way are we wrong, and where".

2. One full update step, by hand

Three examples: x = 1, 2, 3 with y = 2, 4, 5. Start from w = 0, b = 0 and use η = 0.1.

  1. Predict. ŷ = 0, 0, 0. Residuals ŷ − y = −2, −4, −5. Loss = (4 + 16 + 25)/3 = 15.0.
  2. Gradient for w. Σ(residual × x) = (−2)(1) + (−4)(2) + (−5)(3) = −2 − 8 − 15 = −25. Times 2/3: ∂L/∂w = −16.67.
  3. Gradient for b. Σ residuals = −11. Times 2/3: ∂L/∂b = −7.33.
  4. Update. w = 0 − 0.1 × (−16.67) = 1.667; b = 0 − 0.1 × (−7.33) = 0.733.
  5. Check. New predictions 1.667 × 1 + 0.733 = 2.400; 4.067; 5.733. Residuals 0.400, 0.067, 0.733; squares 0.160, 0.004, 0.538. Loss = 0.702/3 = 0.234.

One step took the loss from 15.0 to 0.234. The best possible line for these points (by the formula of ml1) is w = 1.5, b = 0.667, with loss 0.056, so we have overshot slightly in w and still have work to do — and the next steps, as section 4 shows, will be surprisingly slow. Every training run of every neural network is this loop — predict, measure residuals, compute gradients, update — repeated millions of times, with backpropagation doing step 2–3.

A stochastic version would use a single example per step. Using only (x = 3, y = 5) from the same start: ŷ = 0, residual −5, so ∂L/∂w = 2 × (−5) × 3 = −30 and ∂L/∂b = −10, giving w = 3.0, b = 1.0 — a much bigger, noisier step based on one point, which predicts 4.0 for x = 1 where the truth is 2. Averaged over many such steps the noise cancels, which is why stochastic methods work (section 5).

3. Why η must be below 2/curvature

Every smooth loss looks like a bowl near its minimum, so the one-dimensional quadratic L(w) = (c/2)w² is the model case. Its curvature c measures how sharply the bowl bends (for (w − 3)², c = 2). The gradient is cw, so the update is:

w ← w − η·c·w = (1 − ηc) · w

Every step multiplies the distance to the minimum by the same factor, 1 − ηc. The consequences, which apply to every model:

  • 0 < η < 1/c: factor between 0 and 1. Steady, monotone approach from one side.
  • η = 1/c: factor 0. Lands on the minimum in one step — the ideal step for this curvature.
  • 1/c < η < 2/c: factor between −1 and 0. Overshoots, flips side each step, but still converges.
  • η = 2/c: factor −1. Bounces between two points forever.
  • η > 2/c: factor below −1. Each overshoot is bigger than the last; the loss grows exponentially until it overflows to inf and then NaN.

So the maximum stable learning rate is 2 divided by the curvature. For (w − 3)², c = 2 and the limit is 1, which is why 1.1 diverged above. That single inequality explains most "my loss went to NaN" stories: the learning rate was larger than the loss's sharpest curvature allows.

4. Many parameters: the condition number, and why scaling matters

With several parameters the bowl can be curved differently in different directions. The curvatures along the bowl's natural axes are the eigenvalues of the Hessian — the matrix of second derivatives. The step-size limit is set by the largest curvature (η < 2/c_max, or the steep direction diverges), while progress along the flattest direction proceeds at rate 1 − η·c_min per step. Their ratio, c_max / c_min, is the condition number. When it is large, the loss surface is a long, narrow valley: gradient descent zig-zags across the steep walls while inching along the floor.

Worked example. For the three points above, the Hessian of the MSE with respect to (w, b) is (2/n) XᵀX, where each row of X is (x, 1):

(2/3) · [[1+4+9, 1+2+3], [1+2+3, 3]] = [[9.33, 4.0], [4.0, 2.0]]
eigenvalues ≈ 11.09 and 0.240  →  condition number ≈ 46,  max stable η = 2/11.09 ≈ 0.18

At η = 0.1, the slow direction shrinks by only 1 − 0.1 × 0.240 = 0.976 per step, so reducing the remaining error 100-fold takes about 190 steps — even though the first step removed most of the loss. The cause is that x (averaging 2) and the constant 1 of the intercept point in similar directions, so w and b are tangled together. Now centre the input — use x − 2 = −1, 0, 1 instead. The Hessian becomes (2/3)·[[2, 0], [0, 3]], with eigenvalues 1.33 and 2.0: condition number 1.5. The stable limit rises to η = 1, and at η = 0.5 both directions shrink by at least a factor of 3 per step. Same data, same model, same algorithm: centring alone turns ~190 steps into ~5. This is why features are standardised before gradient-based training, and why normalisation layers inside neural networks (dl4) help optimisation.

5. Mini-batches and stochastic gradients

The exact gradient averages over every training example. With 10 million examples, every step would need 10 million predictions — too slow when training takes hundreds of thousands of steps. Instead, each step uses a random mini-batch of, say, 32 to 4,096 examples (for LLMs, millions of tokens) and computes the gradient on that. Terminology:

  • Batch gradient descent: the whole dataset per step. Exact, slow per step.
  • Stochastic gradient descent (SGD): strictly one example per step; in practice "SGD" usually means mini-batch.
  • Epoch: one full pass over the training data. With 1 million examples and batches of 1,000, an epoch is 1,000 steps.

A mini-batch gradient is an unbiased estimate of the full gradient — right on average — with noise whose variance falls in proportion to 1/B for batch size B. That trade is almost always worth it: 100 noisy steps usually beat one exact step of the same cost. The noise also has a side benefit: it jostles training out of sharp, narrow minima towards flatter ones, which tend to generalise better (see deep).

Batch size and learning rate are linked. A larger batch gives a less noisy gradient, so a bigger step along it is safe. The linear scaling rule says: when you multiply the batch size by k, multiply the learning rate by k. It works up to a problem-dependent critical batch size, beyond which bigger batches stop reducing the number of steps needed and simply waste compute. It also needs warmup — see section 7 and the public case.

6. Momentum and Adam

Momentum fixes zig-zagging. Instead of stepping along the current gradient, keep a velocity that accumulates past gradients:

v ← β·v + g          (β ≈ 0.9)
w ← w − η·v

Across a narrow valley the gradient flips sign every step, so those components cancel in v. Along the valley floor the gradient points the same way every step, so it accumulates: with β = 0.9, a constant gradient builds v up to 10 times its size (1/(1 − β)). The result is damped oscillation and faster progress in exactly the directions plain gradient descent neglects. Worked: with gradients alternating +4, −4, +4 across the valley and a steady −1 along it, after a few steps the velocity's across-valley component hovers around ±2 (partly cancelled) while the along-valley component approaches −10.

Adam adds per-parameter step sizes. It keeps two running averages for each parameter — the mean gradient m (momentum) and the mean squared gradient s — and divides one by the square root of the other:

m ← β₁·m + (1 − β₁)·g              β₁ = 0.9
s ← β₂·s + (1 − β₂)·g²             β₂ = 0.999
m̂ = m / (1 − β₁ᵗ),  ŝ = s / (1 − β₂ᵗ)       ← bias correction at step t
w ← w − η · m̂ / (√ŝ + ε)            ε ≈ 1e-8

Dividing by the root-mean-square gradient means a parameter with consistently tiny gradients still moves at a reasonable pace, and one with huge gradients doesn't take huge steps. The bias correction compensates for m and s starting at zero. A worked consequence: at step 1, m̂ = g and ŝ = g², so the step is η · g/|g| — exactly η for every parameter, whatever its gradient's size. A parameter with gradient 0.0001 and one with gradient 1,000 both move by η on the first step. That is why Adam is robust to badly scaled problems, and why its learning rate is best thought of as "roughly how far each parameter may move per step". AdamW (ml3) adds weight decay outside this scaling and is the default for transformers; dl5 compares optimisers in practice.

7. Learning-rate schedules

A constant learning rate is rarely best. Early in training, a large learning rate makes fast progress; late in training, the gradient noise at that same rate keeps the parameters bouncing around the bottom of the valley, so decaying the rate lets them settle. And at the very start, weights are random, gradients can be large and erratic, and Adam's squared-gradient estimate rests on a handful of samples — so a full-size step can wreck training. Hence the standard schedule:

warmup (step t ≤ T_w):   η_t = η_peak · t / T_w
cosine (after warmup):   η_t = η_min + (η_peak − η_min) · ½ · (1 + cos(π · progress))
                         progress = (t − T_w) / (T_total − T_w)

Worked, with η_peak = 3×10⁻⁴, 2,000 warmup steps, 100,000 total and η_min = 3×10⁻⁵: at step 1,000 the rate is 1.5×10⁻⁴ (halfway up the ramp); at step 51,000, progress is 0.5, cos(π/2) = 0, so η = 3×10⁻⁵ + 2.7×10⁻⁴ × 0.5 = 1.65×10⁻⁴; at step 100,000 it has reached 3×10⁻⁵. Variants include linear decay, step decay (divide by 10 at fixed epochs, common in older vision work), one-cycle, and warmup-stable-decay (hold the peak, then decay quickly at the end), which lets a run be extended without restarting the schedule.

Gradient clipping complements schedules: if the gradient's overall size (its norm) exceeds a threshold such as 1.0, scale it down to that size. One corrupted batch or a rare extreme example then can't throw the parameters far away.

8. Reading loss curves

  • Loss explodes or becomes NaN → learning rate too high (or no warmup, or a bad batch). Lower it, add warmup, clip gradients.
  • Loss falls slowly and smoothly, far from done → learning rate too low.
  • Loss oscillates wildly without trending down → learning rate too high for this batch size.
  • Loss plateaus early at a high value → underfitting, poor initialisation or a bug. The key sanity test: train on a single small batch; a correct pipeline drives its loss near zero. If it can't, the bug is in the model, loss or data, not the learning rate.
  • Training loss falls, validation loss rises → overfitting (ml2): regularise, or stop early.

To find a good learning rate quickly, the learning-rate range test trains for a few hundred steps while increasing η exponentially (say from 10⁻⁷ to 1), plots loss against η, and picks a value somewhat below where the loss starts to rise.

Why saddle points, not local minima

A point with zero gradient is a local minimum only if the loss curves upward in every direction. In a space with millions of dimensions, it is overwhelmingly more likely that some directions curve up and others down — a saddle point. Gradient descent slows near saddles (the gradient is small) but isn't trapped by them: any downhill direction eventually amplifies, and mini-batch noise plus momentum speed the escape. Empirical and theoretical work on large networks suggests that most local minima found in practice have loss close to the global minimum, so the practical problems are plateaus, saddles and ill-conditioning, not bad minima.

The edge of stability

The η < 2/c analysis predicts that training with a fixed learning rate should only work if the loss's sharpest curvature stays below 2/η. Cohen and colleagues observed something subtler in full-batch training of neural networks: the sharpness rises during training until it reaches about 2/η and then hovers there, while the loss keeps decreasing non-monotonically. The network adapts to the learning rate rather than the other way round. This "edge of stability" behaviour is one reason larger learning rates can lead to flatter solutions: the learning rate caps how sharp a region training can settle in.

Noise, batch size and generalisation

The noise in a mini-batch gradient scales roughly with η/B. Holding that ratio fixed is the intuition behind the linear scaling rule — double the batch, double the learning rate, keep the same noise level. Beyond the critical batch size the gradient is already accurate enough that more samples per step don't let you take proportionally fewer steps. Very large batches have also been observed to generalise worse without careful tuning, consistent with less noise leading to sharper minima; layer-wise adaptive methods (LARS, LAMB) were introduced to push batch sizes further. For LLM pretraining, the critical batch size grows as the loss falls, which is why some recipes increase the batch size during training.

Adam's costs and weaknesses

Adam stores two extra numbers per parameter, so optimiser state is twice the size of the weights (in 32-bit precision, 8 bytes per parameter beyond the weights themselves) — significant at billions of parameters, motivating memory-saving variants (8-bit optimiser states, Adafactor) and sharding (gpu9). In some vision tasks, well-tuned SGD with momentum generalises better than Adam. And ε matters in low precision: if √ŝ is tiny, ε sets the maximum step, and too small an ε can make updates explode. Newer optimisers — Lion (sign-based), Sophia (curvature-aware), Muon (orthogonalised updates for matrix parameters) — are active research, each claiming efficiency gains at scale; in an interview it is enough to know they exist and what problem each targets.

Second-order methods

Newton's method divides the gradient by the curvature (multiplies by the inverse Hessian), which on a quadratic reaches the minimum in one step whatever the condition number. For a model with p parameters the Hessian has p² entries — impossible for p in the billions — so full second-order methods are reserved for small problems. L-BFGS, which builds a low-memory approximation from recent gradients, is the standard solver for logistic regression in many libraries. Structured approximations such as K-FAC and Shampoo approximate the curvature layer by layer and have been used in large training runs; Adam's diagonal scaling can itself be seen as a crude, cheap curvature estimate.

Mixed precision and numerical stability

Modern training computes in 16-bit formats for speed. bfloat16 keeps float32's range, so it rarely overflows; float16 has a narrow range (maximum about 65,504), so small gradients underflow to zero and large ones overflow — handled by loss scaling, multiplying the loss by a large factor before backpropagation and dividing the gradients afterwards. A master copy of the weights is usually kept in float32 so that tiny updates (learning rate × gradient) are not rounded away. Divergence that appears only in low precision is a classic debugging case (gpu4).

Learning rate and model size

The best learning rate is not a property of the optimiser alone; it depends on the model's width and depth, because wider layers sum more inputs and so react more strongly to the same per-weight change. Large-model recipes therefore use smaller peak learning rates for bigger models, and parameterisation schemes such as μP (maximal update parameterisation) rescale initialisation and per-layer learning rates so that the optimal learning rate found on a small proxy model transfers to a much larger one. That turns an expensive sweep at full scale into a cheap sweep on a small model — one of the practical ways large training runs are tuned.

The public case: ImageNet in one hour

In 2017 Goyal and colleagues at Facebook AI Research set out to train ResNet-50, a standard 50-layer image classifier, on the 1.28-million-image ImageNet training set as fast as possible by spreading the work across many GPUs. More GPUs means a larger total mini-batch per step, and at the time large batches were known to hurt accuracy. The paper, "Accurate, Large Minibatch SGD: Training ImageNet in 1 Hour", showed that the damage came from optimisation difficulties rather than from large batches as such, and fixed it with two rules this lesson has derived:

  • Linear scaling rule. With the reference setting of learning rate 0.1 for a batch of 256 images, a batch of 8,192 (32× larger) uses a learning rate of 3.2 (32× larger).
  • Gradual warmup. Starting at 3.2 immediately destabilised training, so the learning rate ramped up linearly from the small-batch value to 3.2 over the first five epochs.

With those rules they trained ResNet-50 with 8,192-image mini-batches on 256 GPUs in one hour while matching small-batch accuracy, and reported no loss of accuracy for batch sizes up to 8,192 — beyond that, accuracy degraded, the critical-batch-size limit in action. The implementation achieved about 90% scaling efficiency from 8 to 256 GPUs. The lesson for engineers: when a training recipe breaks as you scale it, the first suspects are the learning rate, its schedule and warmup — not the model.

Failure modes in real training runs

Loss spikes in large-model training. Long LLM training runs often show sudden loss spikes. Teams watch the gradient norm alongside the loss; a spike in the norm usually comes first. Standard mitigations are gradient clipping, a lower peak learning rate or longer warmup, skipping the offending data batches, and rolling back to a checkpoint from before the spike and continuing with the problematic data skipped.

Silent under-training. A learning rate that is too low doesn't crash; it produces a model that is quietly worse. A useful habit is a short learning-rate sweep (for example 1e-4, 3e-4, 1e-3) on a scaled-down run before committing to a large one.

Batch-size changes without re-tuning. Moving a job to more GPUs changes the global batch size. If the learning rate isn't adjusted (and warmup extended), the new run trains differently from the old one — a common source of "the same code gives worse results on the big cluster".

Unscaled inputs. A tabular model trained by gradient descent on raw features (income in pounds next to age in years) converges slowly or not at all, and teams compensate with tiny learning rates. Standardising inputs is cheaper and better.

What to log

Log the learning rate at every step (schedules have bugs), the training loss, the global gradient norm, the fraction of steps where clipping was applied, and validation loss at intervals. For large runs, also log per-layer gradient and weight norms; a single layer whose norm grows steadily is often the source of later instability. When a run is resumed from a checkpoint, check that the optimiser state (Adam's m and s) and the schedule's step counter were restored too — restarting them silently re-runs warmup or takes huge first steps.

Goal: see the stability limit, the effect of centring and the effect of momentum on the three-point example. NumPy only; written for Python 3.12.

import numpy as np

x = np.array([1., 2., 3.]); y = np.array([2., 4., 5.])

def train(x, y, lr, steps=200, beta=0.0):
    w = b = vw = vb = 0.0
    for t in range(steps):
        r = w * x + b - y                       # residuals
        gw, gb = 2 * np.mean(r * x), 2 * np.mean(r)
        vw, vb = beta * vw + gw, beta * vb + gb  # beta=0 → plain gradient descent
        w, b = w - lr * vw, b - lr * vb
        if not np.isfinite(w):
            return t, float("inf")
    return steps, np.mean((w * x + b - y) ** 2)

print("one step at lr=0.1:", train(x, y, 0.1, steps=1))
for lr in [0.01, 0.1, 0.17, 0.19]:
    print(f"lr={lr}: steps, final loss =", train(x, y, lr))
xc = x - x.mean()                                # centre the input
for lr in [0.1, 0.5, 0.9]:
    print(f"centred lr={lr}:", train(xc, y, lr, steps=20))
print("momentum 0.9, lr=0.02:", train(x, y, 0.02, steps=200, beta=0.9))

What to look for. One step gives loss 0.234, as computed by hand. With raw x, η = 0.17 converges but 0.19 diverges — the limit is 2/11.09 ≈ 0.18. η = 0.01 is still far from the best loss (0.056) after 200 steps. With centred x, even η = 0.9 converges within 20 steps. Momentum at a small learning rate reaches the optimum in far fewer steps than plain descent at the same rate.

Work it out

You are minimising f(w) = (c/2)w² with plain gradient descent, and the curvature is c = 50. Find the largest stable learning rate, the fastest one, and say what happens just above and just below the limit.

Answer

The update is w ← w − ηcw = w(1 − ηc). Each step multiplies w by (1 − ηc), so it converges exactly when |1 − ηc| < 1, i.e. 0 < η < 2/c = 0.04.

  • η = 0.02 = 1/c — the factor is 0, so it lands on the optimum in one step. Fastest possible.
  • η = 0.039 — factor −0.95: converges, but oscillating across the minimum, taking about 60 steps to shrink by 20× (0.95⁵⁸ ≈ 0.05).
  • η = 0.04 — factor −1: oscillates forever between +w₀ and −w₀, never converging, never diverging.
  • η = 0.05 — factor −1.5: each step lands 50% further out. After 34 steps w is about a million times its starting value (1.5³⁴ ≈ 10⁶); in float16, whose maximum is about 65,504, it overflows to infinity after roughly 27 steps from w₀ = 1, and the next operations produce NaN.

Real losses have many curvatures at once: stability is set by the largest one while progress along the flattest direction is set by the smallest. The ratio between them is the condition number, and it is why feature scaling, momentum and per-parameter step sizes exist at all.
