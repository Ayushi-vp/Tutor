An online estate agent trains a model to estimate house prices. It performs well on validation data by the team's chosen measure, mean squared error, and ships. Within a week, sellers of ordinary three-bedroom homes complain that the estimates are consistently too high. The cause is a small number of mansions in the training data. Because the model was trained to minimise squared error, a £2 million miss on one mansion counted as much as a £20,000 miss on ten thousand ordinary houses, so the model shifted its predictions upward everywhere to reduce the mansion errors. It did exactly what it was told. It was told the wrong thing.

A loss function is the instruction. It turns "how wrong was this prediction?" into a number, and training (ml4) adjusts the parameters to make the average of that number small. Everything the model learns — whether it predicts averages or typical values, whether it cares more about rare cases or common ones, whether its probabilities can be trusted, how it trades one kind of mistake against another — follows from the choice of loss. Choose it carelessly and the model optimises the wrong objective very precisely.

Interviewers probe this constantly because it separates people who have trained models from people who have called fit(). "Why cross-entropy and not MSE for classification?" "What does MAE estimate?" "Your forecast has asymmetric costs — what do you train on?" "Why does PyTorch want logits, not probabilities?" Each has a crisp answer that comes straight from what the loss punishes.

This lesson works through the losses you will meet, each with numbers: MSE, MAE and Huber on the same data with an outlier (including what a constant model predicts under each); quantile loss and how a cost ratio becomes a percentile; cross-entropy computed through a softmax by hand, with its p − y gradient; why MSE fails for classifiers, with the gradients compared; focal loss and the public case where it made a new kind of object detector work; the idea that every loss is a noise model; and the gap between the loss you train and the metric you report.

A loss is a pricing policy for mistakes

Think of a loss function as a fine schedule. A traffic authority could fine speeding by a fixed amount per kilometre per hour over the limit, or by the square of the excess, or by a flat fee for anything over. Each schedule changes driver behaviour differently: squared fines make drivers terrified of large excesses and relaxed about small ones; flat fees make them indifferent to how much they exceed once they do. A model is a driver who minimises its total fine across the training data. Change the schedule and you change what it learns to avoid.

Three regression losses on the same five numbers

True values 10, 10, 10, 10, 50 (one outlier). A model predicts 10 for all of them, so the errors are 0, 0, 0, 0 and 40.

  • Mean squared error (MSE) = (0 + 0 + 0 + 0 + 40²)/5 = 1,600/5 = 320. The one outlier supplies the entire loss.
  • Mean absolute error (MAE) = (0 + 0 + 0 + 0 + 40)/5 = 8. The outlier counts, but only in proportion.
  • Root mean squared error (RMSE) = √320 ≈ 17.9 — back in the original units, but still outlier-driven.

The deepest way to remember the difference: if a model could only output a single constant for all five examples, the constant that minimises MSE is the mean (18), and the constant that minimises MAE is the median (10). A feature-based model trained with MSE predicts the average outcome for inputs like this one; trained with MAE it predicts the typical outcome. For the estate agent, the median was what sellers expected.

Classification: cross-entropy in one line

A classifier outputs a probability for each class. Cross-entropy (also called log loss) charges −log(probability given to the correct class):

  • Correct class given 0.9 → −ln 0.9 ≈ 0.105.
  • Given 0.5 → 0.693.
  • Given 0.01 → 4.61. Being confidently wrong is punished severely, and the fine grows without limit as the probability approaches zero.

Loss versus metric

The loss is what training optimises; because training follows the slope of the loss (ml4), it must be smooth. The metric is what you report and decide with — accuracy, F1, revenue — and it can be anything. Accuracy can't be trained directly: nudging a weight slightly changes no prediction's class, so accuracy's slope is zero almost everywhere. So models are trained on a smooth loss that tends to improve the metric, and then checked, and tuned, against the metric itself.

Keep one question in mind through the rest of the lesson: what decision will this prediction drive, and what does each kind of mistake cost? Totals call for means, typical cases for medians, stock levels for percentiles, and risk calculations for honest probabilities. The loss is where that answer gets written down.

1. The regression family: what each loss's slope does

Write the residual as r = y − ŷ. What matters for training is the gradient — how hard each example pulls the prediction:

LossPer examplePull (gradient w.r.t. ŷ)A constant model predicts
MSEr²−2r: grows with the errorthe mean
MAE|r|−sign(r): the same for every errorthe median
Huber (δ)½r² if |r| ≤ δ, else δ(|r| − ½δ)−r, capped at ±δbetween mean and median

Under MSE, an example 40 away pulls 40 times harder than one 1 away; under MAE they pull equally; under Huber with δ = 5, the far one pulls 5 times harder and no more. MAE's constant pull has two costs: it is not differentiable at zero, and it doesn't shrink as the prediction gets close, so gradient-based training can oscillate near the optimum. Huber fixes both while keeping robustness, which is why it's a common production choice.

Worked example: a constant model on skewed prices. House prices in £ thousands: 100, 110, 120, 130, 1,000. The best single constant under each loss:

  • MSE → mean = 1,460 / 5 = 292. Above every ordinary house: the model is wrong about 80% of the data to be less wrong about one point.
  • MAE → median = 120. Unmoved by the outlier; it would stay at 120 if the mansion cost £100 million.
  • Huber, δ = 50 → 127.5. Suppose the answer c lies near the four ordinary prices, within 50 of each. Those four pull with force (c − yᵢ) each; the mansion, far beyond δ, pulls with a fixed force of 50. At the optimum the pulls balance: (c − 100) + (c − 110) + (c − 120) + (c − 130) = 50, so 4c − 460 = 50 and c = 127.5. Check: 127.5 is within 50 of all four, as assumed. The outlier nudges the answer up by 7.5 rather than 172.

Before switching losses, ask whether the outlier is an error (fix the data), a real but unwanted influence (use a robust loss), or a real segment the model lacks features for (add features — a "number of bedrooms" or "is detached" feature explains mansions better than any loss). And if the target is multiplicative — prices, incomes, durations — training MSE on log(target) is often the best of all: it treats a 10% error the same at every price level. Note that exponentiating a prediction of the mean log gives the geometric mean, which is closer to a median than a mean; correct for that if totals matter.

2. Quantile loss: predicting a percentile

Sometimes the question isn't "what's the expected demand?" but "how much stock should I hold so I run out only 10% of the time?". That is the 90th percentile of demand, and quantile (pinball) loss trains a model to predict it directly:

pinball_τ(r) = τ · r          if r ≥ 0   (under-prediction: actual above forecast)
             = (τ − 1) · r    if r < 0   (over-prediction)

With τ = 0.9, under-predicting by 10 costs 9 and over-predicting by 10 costs 1. The forecast that balances these is the one exceeded by actual demand only 10% of the time — the 90th percentile. Train one model at τ = 0.1 and another at τ = 0.9 and you get an 80% prediction interval instead of a single point. And the link to business costs is exact: if running out costs 4 per unit and overstocking costs 1, the cost-minimising stock is the percentile τ = 4/(4 + 1) = 0.8 (the "newsvendor" critical ratio). Train at τ = 0.8 and the model's output is the order quantity.

3. Cross-entropy, from probabilities to gradients

A multi-class classifier produces one raw score per class, called a logit. Softmax turns logits into probabilities that are positive and sum to 1: pₖ = exp(zₖ) / Σⱼ exp(zⱼ). Cross-entropy is then −log of the probability of the true class.

Worked, three classes. Logits z = (2, 1, 0), true class 0.

  1. Exponentials: e² = 7.389, e¹ = 2.718, e⁰ = 1. Sum = 11.107.
  2. Probabilities: 7.389/11.107 = 0.665, 2.718/11.107 = 0.245, 1/11.107 = 0.090.
  3. Loss: −ln 0.665 = 0.408.
  4. Gradient with respect to the logits: p − y = (0.665 − 1, 0.245 − 0, 0.090 − 0) = (−0.335, +0.245, +0.090).

That last line is the key fact. Combining softmax and cross-entropy, the derivative of the loss with respect to each logit is just "predicted probability minus target". Gradient descent therefore raises the true class's logit in proportion to how much probability it is missing, and lowers each wrong class's logit in proportion to the probability it wrongly received. The more wrong the model, the stronger the correction — no saturation, no vanishing signal.

For two classes, the same thing with a sigmoid σ(z) = 1/(1 + e^(−z)) is binary cross-entropy: −[y log p + (1 − y) log(1 − p)], with gradient p − y with respect to z. Cross-entropy is also the negative log-likelihood of the labels: minimising it is choosing the parameters under which the observed labels were most probable. (ml6 derives this for logistic regression.)

4. Why not MSE for classifiers? The gradients compared

Take a positive example (y = 1) that the model gets badly wrong, with logit z = −5, so p = σ(−5) = 0.0067.

  • Cross-entropy: gradient with respect to z is p − y = −0.993. Strong push in the right direction.
  • MSE on the probability, (p − y)²: by the chain rule the gradient is 2(p − y) · σ′(z), and σ′(z) = p(1 − p) = 0.0066. Gradient = 2 × (−0.993) × 0.0066 = −0.013.

The MSE gradient is about 75 times weaker exactly when the model is most wrong, because the sigmoid is flat (saturated) out there and MSE multiplies by its slope. Cross-entropy's logarithm cancels that slope. So MSE-trained classifiers learn slowly from their worst mistakes, and cross-entropy is the standard.

5. Focal loss: when easy examples drown the signal

Suppose 99.9% of examples are easy negatives the model already gets right with probability 0.9. Each contributes a small cross-entropy (0.105), but there are so many that together they dominate the total loss and gradient, and the rare hard examples barely register. Focal loss multiplies cross-entropy by (1 − p_t)^γ, where p_t is the probability given to the true class:

  • Easy example, p_t = 0.9, γ = 2: factor (0.1)² = 0.01 → loss 0.105 × 0.01 = 0.00105, cut 100-fold.
  • Hard example, p_t = 0.1: factor (0.9)² = 0.81 → loss 2.303 × 0.81 = 1.87, barely changed.

The easy example's weight relative to the hard one drops from about 1:22 to about 1:1,800. Training attention moves to the examples that still have something to teach. This is the public case below. Focal loss reweights by difficulty, while class weights reweight by class (every negative counts, say, one-tenth as much); both change the model's optimum and therefore its probabilities, which is the subject of ml8 and ml15.

6. Other losses you will meet

  • Hinge loss, max(0, 1 − y·f(x)) with labels ±1: zero once an example is on the right side with a margin; used by support vector machines. It doesn't produce probabilities.
  • Poisson and Tweedie deviance: for counts (orders per day) and non-negative skewed targets with many zeros (insurance claims). They penalise errors relative to the expected variance, so a miss of 5 on an expected count of 2 matters more than a miss of 5 on 2,000.
  • KL divergence: compares two probability distributions; used in knowledge distillation (a small "student" model matches a large "teacher's" probabilities) and in variational autoencoders.
  • Contrastive losses (InfoNCE): a cross-entropy over "which of these candidates is the true match?", used to train embedding models such as CLIP (ml1).
  • Ranking losses: pairwise (score the relevant item above the irrelevant one) and listwise losses for search and recommendation, where the metric is order, not value.

7. Weights, and losses that combine several goals

Every loss above is an average over examples, and averages can be weighted. A sample weight multiplies an example's loss: weight 2 means it counts as if it appeared twice. Weights are how you encode that some examples matter more (high-value customers), correct for sampling (if you kept only 1 in 10 negatives to save compute, give each kept negative weight 10 to restore the original balance), or emphasise recent data when the world changes. Worked: 900 negatives and 100 positives, and you want each class to contribute equally. Give positives weight 900/100 = 9. The weighted loss now treats the data as if it were balanced — which also means the model's predicted probabilities will be inflated for positives (it believes positives are 50% of the world, not 10%), a trap covered in ml8.

Many real models optimise several things at once. A recommendation model may predict click, purchase and return from shared features, with a total loss L = L_click + 0.5·L_purchase + 2·L_return. The weights decide how much each head influences the shared layers, and they interact with the losses' scales: a cross-entropy of about 0.7 and an MSE of about 10,000 on prices can't be added naively, or the MSE term will take over. Normalise targets, or choose weights so each term contributes a comparable share of the gradient, and track each component separately in training logs, never just the sum.

Regularisation (ml3) is another added term: the full objective is data loss plus λ × penalty, and everything said here about scales applies — a penalty that is tiny next to a loss measured in millions of pounds does nothing.

8. How to evaluate the choice

Loss choice is itself a hyperparameter, judged on validation data against the metric that matters. A practical routine: train the candidates (say MSE, Huber and MSE on log-target), then compare them on the business metric, on the median error, and on error by slice — cheap versus expensive items, frequent versus rare customers. MSE-trained models usually win on the mean error and lose on the median; robust losses the reverse. If the slice that matters most to the business is the tail, a robust loss may be exactly wrong. Plotting predictions against actuals, or residuals against predictions, often shows immediately whether a model is being dragged by a few extremes.

9. Numerical stability: never compute log(softmax) in two steps

Softmax of large logits overflows: exp(1,000) is infinity in floating point. Subtracting the largest logit first changes nothing mathematically (it cancels in the ratio) but keeps every exponent ≤ 0. Separately, the log of a tiny probability can underflow to log(0) = −∞. Fused implementations compute log_softmax(z) = z − max(z) − log Σ exp(z − max(z)) directly — the log-sum-exp trick — so both problems disappear. That is why PyTorch's CrossEntropyLoss takes raw logits, and why BCEWithLogitsLoss is preferred over applying a sigmoid and then binary cross-entropy. Passing probabilities into a loss that expects logits is a classic silent bug: training runs, just badly.

Every loss is a noise model

Suppose labels are the model's prediction plus Gaussian noise of fixed variance. The likelihood of the data is a product of Gaussian densities, and its negative log is, up to constants, the sum of squared residuals. So minimising MSE is maximum-likelihood estimation under Gaussian noise. Laplace noise (sharper peak, heavier tails) gives MAE; a Bernoulli or categorical distribution over labels gives cross-entropy; Poisson counts give Poisson deviance. This reading turns loss choice into a modelling question: how do the observations scatter around the truth? Heavy tails suggest Laplace or Student-t style losses; variance growing with the mean suggests Poisson, gamma or Tweedie; multiplicative noise suggests modelling the logarithm.

Proper scoring rules

A loss on probability forecasts is proper if, when the true probability of an event is q, the expected loss is minimised by forecasting exactly q. Log loss: expected loss is −[q log p + (1 − q) log(1 − p)], whose minimum over p is at p = q. The Brier score (squared error on probabilities) is also proper. Accuracy is not — any forecast on the right side of 0.5 scores the same — and neither is hinge loss. Training on a proper loss aligns the model with producing honest probabilities, which is what expected-cost decisions, risk ranking and calibration need. Reweighting classes or focusing on hard examples deliberately breaks this, so the outputs must be recalibrated before being read as probabilities (ml15).

Surrogates and consistency

The losses we actually want to minimise — 0–1 error, F1, NDCG — are discontinuous. We train on smooth surrogate losses instead. Theory on "classification-calibrated" or consistent surrogates says when minimising the surrogate also minimises the true error in the limit of infinite data: cross-entropy, hinge and exponential losses all are for 0–1 error. For set-based metrics like F1 there is no simple consistent per-example surrogate, and the reliable approach is to train with cross-entropy and then choose the decision threshold that maximises F1 on validation data (ml7).

Label smoothing and what it trades

Label smoothing replaces the one-hot target (1 for the true class) with 1 − ε and ε/(K − 1) for the other K − 1 classes, for example 0.9 and small shares. The optimal logits under smoothed targets are finite, so the model never tries to push logits to infinity; this regularises (ml3) and often improves calibration. The cost: the model's outputs cluster tightly by class, discarding information about which wrong classes are similar, which can make a smoothed model a worse teacher for distillation.

Asymmetric and business-derived losses

When errors have different costs, put the costs in the loss or in the decision rule. For regression, quantile loss at the critical ratio implements asymmetric linear costs exactly. For classification, it is usually better to train an honest probability model with cross-entropy and apply the cost-sensitive threshold afterwards (predict positive when p × cost of a miss exceeds (1 − p) × cost of a false alarm), because then one model serves many cost settings and its probabilities stay interpretable. Changing the loss for each cost setting bakes the costs into the model and makes it harder to reuse.

Robust losses and the influence of a single point

Statisticians describe robustness through the influence of one observation: how much the fitted model moves if that point is changed. Under MSE the influence is unbounded — push one label to infinity and the fit follows it. Under Huber and MAE it is bounded by δ or by 1, so no single point can move the fit arbitrarily far. The robust version has a cost: when the noise really is Gaussian and there are no outliers, MSE is the most efficient estimator, and Huber loses a little accuracy (with the conventional δ ≈ 1.35 standard deviations, about 5%). That's the trade: a small loss of efficiency on clean data for protection against contaminated data.

The loss of language models

LLM pretraining minimises average next-token cross-entropy, usually reported in nats (natural log) or bits per token, and as perplexity = exp(mean cross-entropy). A mean of 2.0 nats per token gives perplexity e² ≈ 7.39. Because the loss is per token, perplexities are comparable only between models with the same tokeniser; across tokenisers, compare bits per byte of text. Preference optimisation such as DPO uses a logistic loss on the difference between the model's log-probability ratios (relative to a frozen reference model) for a preferred and a rejected response — still a cross-entropy, applied to pairs.

The public case: focal loss and RetinaNet

Object detectors find and label every object in an image. In 2017 the most accurate ones were "two-stage": a first stage proposed a few thousand candidate regions, and a second stage classified them. "One-stage" detectors, which classify a dense grid of candidate boxes directly, were faster and simpler but consistently less accurate. Lin and colleagues at Facebook AI Research asked why, and concluded in "Focal Loss for Dense Object Detection" that the central cause was extreme foreground–background class imbalance: a dense detector evaluates on the order of 100,000 candidate locations per image, almost all of them easy background. Under ordinary cross-entropy, those easy negatives each contribute a small loss but together overwhelm the gradient, so the detector learns little from the rare, hard examples.

Their fix was purely a change of loss: reshape cross-entropy so that it "down-weights the loss assigned to well-classified examples" — focal loss, with γ = 2 and a class weight α = 0.25 working best in their experiments. With it, their simple one-stage detector, RetinaNet, matched the speed of previous one-stage detectors while surpassing the accuracy of all existing two-stage detectors, in the paper's words. The architecture was deliberately ordinary; the loss made the difference. The general lesson for engineers: when the data's composition is badly skewed, look at what the loss is actually spending its gradient on before reaching for a bigger model.

Failure modes in production

Mean versus median surprises. An MSE model reports the expected value, which for skewed targets is above what most users experience (delivery times, prices, waiting times). If the product displays the prediction as "typical", use a median (MAE, or quantile 0.5) or show a range.

Metric–loss mismatch. Teams optimise log loss offline and are judged online on revenue or clicks. A model can improve log loss mostly on examples that don't affect the decision (very low-probability items that are never shown). Evaluate offline with a metric close to the decision, such as ranking metrics for recommendation, and keep an eye on the online metric (ml16).

Reweighted losses and broken probabilities. Class weights, focal loss and negative subsampling all shift predicted probabilities away from true frequencies. Downstream systems that multiply probabilities by values (expected revenue from an ad click, expected loss from fraud) are then systematically wrong unless the outputs are recalibrated (ml15).

Numerical bugs. Passing probabilities to a loss that expects logits, applying softmax twice, or computing log(softmax) separately in low precision produces NaNs or quietly slower learning. Unit-test the loss on a hand-computed example like section 3's.

Choosing a loss in practice

Start from the decision: is it about totals (mean, MSE or a deviance matching the target's noise), typical cases (median, MAE/Huber), thresholds and stock levels (quantiles), or probabilities feeding expected-value calculations (cross-entropy, kept proper)? Then check the target's distribution — skew, zeros, counts, outliers — and whether a transformation such as the logarithm makes it well behaved. Finally, evaluate with the business metric, not just the training loss, and log both.

Goal: compute each loss on the same data, find the best constant under each, and compare cross-entropy and MSE gradients. NumPy only; written for Python 3.12.

import numpy as np

y = np.array([100., 110., 120., 130., 1000.])
grid = np.linspace(50, 1100, 10501)
def best(loss):                                  # brute-force the best constant
    return grid[np.argmin([loss(y - c).mean() for c in grid])]
mse   = lambda r: r ** 2
mae   = lambda r: np.abs(r)
huber = lambda r, d=50: np.where(np.abs(r) <= d, 0.5 * r**2, d * (np.abs(r) - 0.5 * d))
pinball = lambda r, t=0.9: np.maximum(t * r, (t - 1) * r)
for name, f in [("MSE", mse), ("MAE", mae), ("Huber", huber), ("pinball 0.9", pinball)]:
    print(f"{name:12s} best constant = {best(f):.1f}")

z = np.array([2., 1., 0.])                        # softmax + cross-entropy, true class 0
p = np.exp(z - z.max()); p /= p.sum()
print("probs", p.round(3), "CE", round(-np.log(p[0]), 3), "grad", (p - [1, 0, 0]).round(3))

zc = -5.0; pc = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-zc))             # badly wrong positive example
print("CE grad", round(pc - 1, 3), " MSE grad", round(2 * (pc - 1) * pc * (1 - pc), 4))

What to look for. The best constants: MSE 292 (mean), MAE 120 (median), Huber 127.5, and pinball at τ = 0.9 lands on 1,000: with five points, anything below the mansion is exceeded by 20% of the data, more than the 10% a 90th percentile allows (moving up past 130 saves 0.9 per unit on the mansion and costs only 4 × 0.1 on the others). The softmax line should print probabilities 0.665, 0.245, 0.090, loss 0.408, gradient −0.335, 0.245, 0.090. The last line shows cross-entropy's gradient (−0.993) about 75 times larger than MSE's (−0.0132).

Work it out

Five house prices, in thousands: 100, 110, 120, 130, 1000. You fit a model with no features — it can only output one constant. What does it predict under MSE, under MAE, and under Huber with δ = 50?

Answer

MSE → the mean = 292. Sum 1,460 ÷ 5. The single mansion drags the prediction above every ordinary house, so the model is wrong about 80% of the data to be less wrong about one point.

MAE → the median = 120. Completely unmoved by the outlier; it would give 120 whether the fifth house cost £1M or £100M.

Huber, δ = 50 → 127.5. Guess that the answer c sits within 50 of the four ordinary prices. Each of them pulls with force (c − yᵢ); the mansion is far beyond δ, so it pulls with a constant force of δ = 50. Balance: (c − 100) + (c − 110) + (c − 120) + (c − 130) = 50 → 4c = 510 → c = 127.5, which is indeed within 50 of all four. The outlier nudges the answer by 7.5 instead of dragging it by 172.

The interview line: MSE estimates the conditional mean, MAE the conditional median, Huber something in between controlled by δ. Then the real question — is the mansion an error to be robust against, or a real segment you are failing to model? If it is real, the fix is a feature, not a loss function.
