A subscription business wants to know which customers are likely to cancel next month, so its retention team can call them. It needs more than a yes/no answer. Calling costs money, and the team can make only 2,000 calls, so it needs a ranking; the finance team wants to forecast revenue, so it needs a probability — "this customer has a 31% chance of leaving" — that can be multiplied by the customer's value; and a regulator may ask why a particular customer was treated differently, so the model's reasoning must be explainable in a sentence.

Logistic regression does all three, and it's why almost every classification interview starts with it. It is linear regression's sibling for yes/no outcomes: compute a weighted sum of the features, as linear regression does, then squash that sum into a probability. Each weight has a precise meaning ("each support ticket in the last month doubles the odds of cancelling"), the probabilities are well calibrated when the model fits, and training has a single best answer that every solver finds. It trains in seconds on millions of rows, and it is the last layer of nearly every neural-network classifier.

It also carries a set of subtleties that interviewers love, because they reveal whether a candidate has actually used it: odds versus probabilities (the most common misreading of any model in industry), why training uses cross-entropy, what happens when classes are perfectly separable, how to correct probabilities after downsampling negatives, and when coefficients mean what people think they mean.

This lesson builds the model from a score to a probability: the sigmoid, odds and log-odds with worked conversions, maximum-likelihood training with one gradient step computed by hand, the linear decision boundary and how to bend it with features, separation (with the loss going to zero as weights grow), multi-class softmax regression, honest interpretation, and the public case of Facebook's 2014 ad-click system — a logistic regression fed by boosted trees, retrained daily, trained on downsampled data and recalibrated with a one-line formula.

From a score to a probability

Logistic regression answers yes/no questions with a probability, in two steps:

  1. Compute a score exactly as linear regression would: z = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + … + b. The score can be any number from −∞ to +∞.
  2. Squash it into the range 0 to 1 with the sigmoid (logistic) function: p = 1 / (1 + e^(−z)).

A few sigmoid values to remember: z = 0 → 0.5; z = 2 → 0.88; z = −2 → 0.12; z = 5 → 0.993. Large positive scores mean "very likely yes", large negative ones "very likely no".

A worked example

A churn model has learned z = −3 + 0.8 × (support tickets last month) − 0.05 × (months as customer). A customer with 4 tickets and 20 months of tenure: z = −3 + 3.2 − 1.0 = −0.8, so p = 1/(1 + e^0.8) = 1/(1 + 2.23) ≈ 0.31 — a 31% chance of cancelling. To act, the business chooses a threshold, say "call everyone above 0.25"; choosing it well is the subject of ml7.

Odds and log-odds: the key to reading the model

The odds of an event are p / (1 − p): a probability of 0.8 is odds of 4 ("4 to 1"); 0.5 is odds of 1; 0.2 is odds of 0.25. The logarithm of the odds, log(p / (1 − p)), is the log-odds or logit. Rearranging the sigmoid shows the central fact:

log( p / (1 − p) )  =  w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + … + b

Logistic regression is a linear model of the log-odds. Each weight is the change in log-odds per unit of its feature. In the churn model, each extra support ticket adds 0.8 to the log-odds, which multiplies the odds by e^0.8 ≈ 2.23 — the odds ratio. Our customer's odds are 0.31/0.69 = 0.45; one more ticket makes them 0.45 × 2.23 = 1.0, a probability of 0.50. Evidence adds on the log-odds scale; the sigmoid only converts the total into a probability at the end.

This additivity is what makes the model easy to explain. For our customer, the story is: start from the baseline log-odds of −3 (a customer with no tickets and no tenure), add 0.8 for each of the 4 tickets (+3.2), subtract 0.05 for each of the 20 months (−1.0), and land at −0.8. Each feature's contribution can be shown as a bar in a chart, and the explanation for any individual prediction is just that list of contributions — something much harder to produce for a large tree ensemble or neural network.

How it is trained, in one sentence

The weights are chosen to make the observed outcomes as probable as possible — maximum likelihood — which is the same as minimising the cross-entropy loss of ml5. There is no formula for the answer, so it is found iteratively (ml4), but the loss is a single smooth bowl (convex), so every solver finds the same best weights.

1. The likelihood and why it becomes cross-entropy

For one example with label y ∈ {0, 1} and predicted probability p, the probability the model assigns to what actually happened is p if y = 1 and 1 − p if y = 0 — compactly, p^y (1 − p)^(1 − y). Assuming examples are independent (ml1's i.i.d.), the probability of the whole training set — the likelihood — is the product over examples. Products of many small numbers underflow, and logarithms turn products into sums, so we maximise the log-likelihood instead, or equivalently minimise its negative average:

L(w, b) = −(1/n) Σᵢ [ yᵢ log pᵢ + (1 − yᵢ) log(1 − pᵢ) ]      pᵢ = σ(w·xᵢ + b)

That is binary cross-entropy exactly (ml5). So "maximum likelihood" and "minimise log loss" are the same instruction, and the loss has a statistical justification rather than being an arbitrary choice.

The simplest case, by hand. With no features, the model is just p = σ(b). If 30 of 100 customers churned, the likelihood is maximised at p = 0.30 — the observed rate — so b = log(0.3/0.7) = −0.847. With features, the model distributes that 30% across customers according to their features, but a version of this property survives: at the optimum, the average predicted probability equals the observed positive rate (section 6).

2. The gradient, and one training step by hand

Differentiating the loss for one example with respect to the score z, using the sigmoid's derivative σ′(z) = σ(z)(1 − σ(z)), everything cancels to:

∂L/∂z = p − y      →      ∂L/∂w = (p − y)·x      ∂L/∂b = p − y

Prediction error times input — the same shape as linear regression's gradient in ml4. A positive example predicted at 0.2 pushes its weights up by 0.8 × x; one predicted at 0.95 barely pushes at all.

Worked step. Four students; the feature is hours studied, centred at its mean (ml4 showed why centring helps): x = −1.5, −0.5, +0.5, +1.5. Passed the exam: y = 0, 0, 1, 1. Start at w = 0, b = 0 with learning rate η = 1.

  1. Predict. z = 0 for everyone, so p = 0.5 for everyone. Loss = −log 0.5 = 0.693 each, mean 0.693.
  2. Errors p − y: +0.5, +0.5, −0.5, −0.5.
  3. Gradient for w: mean of (p − y)·x = (0.5 × −1.5 + 0.5 × −0.5 − 0.5 × 0.5 − 0.5 × 1.5)/4 = (−0.75 − 0.25 − 0.25 − 0.75)/4 = −0.5. Gradient for b: mean of (p − y) = 0.
  4. Update: w = 0 − 1 × (−0.5) = 0.5; b stays 0.
  5. Check: scores −0.75, −0.25, 0.25, 0.75 give p = 0.321, 0.438, 0.562, 0.679. The losses are −log(1 − 0.321) = 0.387, −log(0.562) = 0.576, and the same two again for the passing students: mean 0.481. One step cut the loss from 0.693 to 0.481.

3. The decision boundary

With a threshold of 0.5, the model predicts "yes" when p > 0.5, which is when z > 0. So the decision boundary is the set of points where w·x + b = 0: a straight line with two features, a flat plane (hyperplane) with more. Logistic regression is therefore a linear classifier. Changing the threshold moves the boundary parallel to itself — a threshold of 0.2 puts it where z = log(0.2/0.8) = −1.39 — but never bends it.

To learn a curved boundary, you give the model curved features. Adding x₁², x₂² and x₁x₂ lets the boundary be an ellipse or parabola in the original inputs; binning a continuous feature (age 18–25, 26–35, …) lets its effect be any step shape; an interaction feature (tickets × is_new_customer) lets tickets matter more for new customers. That manual feature work is exactly what decision trees and neural networks automate, and it's why "logistic regression on good features" can be remarkably strong.

4. Perfect separation: when the best weights are infinite

The four students above are perfectly separable: every non-passer studied less than every passer. Make w larger and every prediction moves further in the right direction, so the loss keeps falling: at w = 1 (b = 0) it is 0.338, at w = 2 it is 0.181, at w = 5 it is 0.040, at w = 10 it is 0.0034 and at w = 20 it is 0.00002. There is no finite minimum; the weights run towards infinity, and a solver either stops at an iteration limit with enormous weights or reports it failed to converge. Predicted probabilities of exactly 0 or 1 are the symptom.

Separation is common with many features and few examples, or when one rare category occurs only among positives. Regularisation (ml3) fixes it: the penalty makes large weights costly, so there is a finite best trade-off. This is why scikit-learn's LogisticRegression applies an L2 penalty by default (C = 1.0) — it isn't only about overfitting; without it the problem may have no answer.

5. More than two classes

Softmax (multinomial) regression gives each of K classes its own weight vector, computes K scores and converts them to probabilities with softmax (ml5), trained with categorical cross-entropy. The probabilities sum to one and the gradient for each class's score is again p − y. One-vs-rest instead trains K separate binary models ("class k versus everything else") and picks the highest score; it's simple and parallelisable, but the K scores aren't coordinated and don't sum to one. Modern libraries default to softmax. The final layer of a neural classifier is exactly softmax regression on the features the network has learned.

6. Calibration by construction

At the optimum, the gradient with respect to b is zero, and that gradient is mean(p − y). So the average predicted probability equals the observed positive rate on the training data — automatically. The same holds for every binary feature in the model: among customers with a support ticket, predicted and actual churn rates agree. That's why logistic regression's probabilities are usually trustworthy when the model is correctly specified. They go wrong when the true log-odds aren't linear in the features (a missing interaction makes some groups over- and others under-predicted), when regularisation is strong (predictions are shrunk towards the base rate), or when training data was reweighted or resampled.

7. Downsampling and the intercept

With a click-through rate of 0.1%, a dataset of a billion ad impressions contains a million clicks and 999 million non-clicks. Keeping only a fraction w of the negatives cuts training cost dramatically. Because logistic regression is linear in log-odds, discarding negatives at random multiplies the odds of a positive by 1/w and so adds log(1/w) to every score — only the intercept changes. Predictions made in the downsampled world, p, are converted back with:

q = p / ( p + (1 − p) / w )

Worked: after keeping 1% of negatives (w = 0.01), the model predicts p = 0.10 for a typical ad. Then q = 0.10 / (0.10 + 0.90/0.01) = 0.10 / 90.1 ≈ 0.0011 — back to the true 0.1% base rate. Forget this correction and every predicted click probability is 90 times too high, which in an ads auction means bidding wildly wrong. This exact formula appears in the Facebook paper that is this lesson's public case.

8. Regularised logistic regression and choosing C

In practice logistic regression is almost always fitted with a penalty: minimise the log loss plus λ times an L2 penalty (the sum of squared weights) or an L1 penalty (the sum of absolute weights), as in ml3. scikit-learn expresses the strength as C = 1/λ, so small C means strong regularisation. With L2 the weights shrink smoothly and predicted probabilities are pulled towards the base rate; with L1 many weights become exactly zero, which is useful with thousands of sparse features such as words or hashed identifiers. C is chosen by cross-validation on a log-scale grid (0.001, 0.01, …, 100), judged by log loss if you need probabilities or by the metric you care about otherwise. Two practical notes: the intercept is never penalised, so the base rate is always fitted; and because the penalty depends on units, features must be standardised first.

9. Class weights and imbalanced data

With 5% positives, a common reflex is to weight each positive more heavily in the loss (scikit-learn's class_weight="balanced" gives positives weight 0.95/0.05 = 19 here). For logistic regression this has an effect very like downsampling: the model behaves as if positives were 19 times more common, so the intercept rises by about log 19 ≈ 2.9 and every predicted probability is inflated — a customer with a true 5% risk gets a prediction around 50%. The ranking of customers barely changes, which is all a threshold decision needs, but the probabilities are no longer probabilities. If you weight classes, correct the outputs the same way as in section 7 (with w = 1/19) or recalibrate (ml15). Often it is better not to reweight at all and instead choose the decision threshold from costs, as ml7 and ml8 explain.

10. Evaluating the fit

Log loss on held-out data is the natural measure, but its raw value depends on the base rate: predicting the base rate itself already scores well when positives are rare. So compare against that trivial baseline. With a 30% positive rate, the baseline log loss is −(0.3 ln 0.3 + 0.7 ln 0.7) = 0.361 + 0.250 = 0.611. A model scoring 0.45 has a normalised entropy of 0.45/0.611 = 0.74 — it removes 26% of the uncertainty — which is exactly the metric Facebook used in the public case below. Alongside it, check ranking — how well the scores put positives above negatives, measured with ROC (receiver operating characteristic) and precision–recall curves in ml7 — and calibration (ml15).

Finally, always compare with the two baselines of ml1: predicting the base rate for everyone, and a logistic regression on the obvious features. If a complex model can't beat the second by a margin that justifies its cost, the complex model shouldn't ship. Logistic regression is so often the model that survives this comparison in production that many teams treat "beats a well-regularised logistic regression on the same features" as the entry ticket for anything fancier.

11. Reading a fitted model honestly

  • Report odds ratios, e^w, with confidence intervals, and translate into probabilities at realistic baselines (the drill below).
  • Standardise before comparing sizes. A weight of 0.8 per support ticket and 0.05 per month of tenure aren't comparable until both are per standard deviation.
  • Coefficients are conditional. Each is the effect "holding the other features fixed". With correlated features (multicollinearity), individual coefficients are unstable and can flip sign while predictions barely change.
  • Association is not causation. Support tickets predict churn, but calling customers so they file fewer tickets won't prevent it; tickets may be a symptom of a product problem. Causal claims need experiments (ml16).

How solvers find the optimum

The log loss is convex and smooth, so any sensible method converges to the same answer, but they differ in speed and in which penalties they support. Newton's method uses the Hessian — for logistic regression, XᵀSX with S a diagonal matrix of p(1 − p) — and converges in a handful of steps; statisticians call it IRLS (iteratively reweighted least squares) because each step is a weighted linear regression. L-BFGS approximates the Newton step from recent gradients and is scikit-learn's default (L2 only). liblinear handles L1 on small data; saga, a stochastic method, handles L1 and elastic net on large data. At web scale, models are trained online with stochastic methods and per-coordinate learning rates — FTRL-Proximal (Google) and per-coordinate SGD (Facebook) — because each sparse feature appears at a very different frequency, and a single global learning rate would under-train rare features or overshoot common ones.

A generalised linear model

Logistic regression belongs to the family of generalised linear models (GLMs): a linear score, a link function connecting it to the mean of the outcome, and a noise distribution. Linear regression is the identity link with Gaussian noise; logistic regression is the logit link with Bernoulli outcomes; Poisson regression is the log link with count outcomes (ml5's noise-model view). The shared machinery — maximum likelihood, IRLS, standard errors, deviance — is why statistics packages report p-values and confidence intervals for logistic coefficients, which rely on the model being correctly specified and on there being no separation.

Generative versus discriminative: Naive Bayes

Naive Bayes models how features are generated within each class and uses Bayes' rule to get P(class | features); with its independence assumption, its log-odds are also linear in the features. Logistic regression models P(class | features) directly. Ng and Jordan's classic analysis found Naive Bayes can reach its (higher) asymptotic error with fewer examples, while logistic regression reaches a lower error given enough data — the general trade between a generative model's strong assumptions and a discriminative model's flexibility.

Firth's correction and rare events

Maximum-likelihood estimates of logistic coefficients are biased away from zero in small samples, and badly so with rare outcomes or near-separation. Firth's penalised likelihood adds a penalty (based on the Jeffreys prior) that removes the first-order bias and guarantees finite estimates even under separation. In ML practice the ordinary L2 penalty plays the same stabilising role; in clinical or epidemiological work with small samples, Firth's method is the standard answer.

The scorecard tradition

Credit scoring is a long-standing production use. Continuous variables are binned, each bin is encoded by its weight of evidence — log of the ratio of good to bad rates in the bin — and a logistic regression is fitted on those encodings. The result is turned into points (for example, 20 points double the odds), so a loan officer can see exactly which attributes contributed how many points. The appeal is regulatory: monotone, explainable effects and a transparent reason for every decision, at a small cost in accuracy compared with boosted trees.

Standard errors and what "significant" means here

Statistics packages report a standard error for each coefficient, derived from the curvature (Hessian) of the log-likelihood at the optimum: a sharply curved likelihood means the data pins the coefficient down tightly. The ratio of coefficient to standard error gives a Wald test and a p-value. Three cautions matter in ML settings: with millions of rows almost every coefficient is "significant" while being practically tiny; with correlated features standard errors inflate and individual tests become meaningless; and after any feature selection or tuning on the same data, the reported p-values are optimistic. Use confidence intervals on odds ratios to communicate uncertainty, and bootstrap resampling when the assumptions are doubtful.

Where it lives inside modern systems

The final layer of a neural classifier is softmax regression on learned features; a "linear probe" — logistic regression on frozen embeddings — is the standard way to measure how much information an embedding contains; Platt scaling calibrates another model's scores with a one-feature logistic regression; and in LLM work, simple logistic classifiers on embeddings are used for routing, safety filtering and data quality scoring because they are fast and easy to audit. Even the reward models used to align LLMs are trained with a logistic loss: the probability that response A is preferred to response B is modelled as the sigmoid of the difference in their scores (the Bradley–Terry model).

The public case: Facebook's ad-click model (2014)

In "Practical Lessons from Predicting Clicks on Ads at Facebook" (He et al., 2014), Facebook's ads team described the model that predicted click probabilities for an audience of over 750 million daily active users and over 1 million active advertisers. Calibrated probabilities mattered because the ads auction multiplies the predicted click probability by the bid: a model that over-predicts by 2× distorts the whole marketplace. The core was logistic regression, and several of this lesson's ideas appear directly:

  • Features are what matter. The paper's first lesson is that the right features — especially historical ones, such as the ad's or user's past click-through rate — dominate everything else. Removing historical features cost 4.5% in prediction accuracy; removing contextual features cost less than 1%.
  • Bending the boundary with trees. Instead of hand-crafting non-linear features (section 3), they trained boosted decision trees and fed the index of the leaf each example lands in, as one-hot features, into logistic regression. The hybrid improved normalised entropy — their log-loss metric, normalised by the loss of always predicting the average click rate — by over 3% relative to either model alone (NE 96.58% of the trees-only model, versus 99.43% for logistic regression alone), where typical feature-engineering wins were a fraction of a percent.
  • Freshness. Accuracy degraded as the gap between training and serving grew; moving from weekly to daily retraining reduced normalised entropy by about 1%. The logistic-regression layer was trained online, with per-coordinate learning rates performing best of the schemes they compared.
  • Downsampling and recalibration. They trained on data with most negatives removed (a negative sampling rate of 0.025 performed best) and corrected predictions with q = p / (p + (1 − p)/w) — the formula of section 7 — giving the example of a 0.1% true rate that becomes about 10% after 1% negative downsampling.

The lessons generalise: in production, a simple, well-calibrated model on strong features, retrained frequently and corrected for sampling, beats a sophisticated model trained once on stale data.

Failure modes in production

Forgetting to recalibrate after sampling or reweighting. Predictions come out inflated by the sampling factor; anything multiplying them by a value (bids, expected loss) is wrong by the same factor.

Misreading coefficients. "A coefficient of 0.7 means +70% churn" leads to wrong business cases. Always convert through odds at a realistic baseline.

Silent separation. A new categorical value that appears only among positives in a retrain gets a huge weight; predictions for that group jump to near-certainty. Regularisation limits the damage; monitoring coefficient magnitudes between retrains catches it.

Stale scalers and encodings. Standardisation parameters and category encodings must travel with the model; recomputing them on serving data shifts every score (ml14).

What to monitor

Track calibration (predicted versus observed rate, overall and by segment), log loss or normalised entropy over time, the distribution of scores, and coefficient drift between retrains. A sudden change in the average predicted probability with no change in the observed rate usually means a feature pipeline broke. And keep the logistic-regression baseline running in shadow next to any more complex replacement: if the complex model's advantage shrinks over time, that is an early sign it is decaying faster.

Goal: implement logistic regression from scratch, check its coefficients against the truth, watch separation make weights diverge, and apply the downsampling correction. NumPy only; written for Python 3.12.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def fit(X, y, lr=0.5, steps=3000, lam=0.0):
    w, b = np.zeros(X.shape[1]), 0.0
    for _ in range(steps):
        p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(X @ w + b)))
        w -= lr * (X.T @ (p - y) / len(y) + lam * w)   # intercept not penalised
        b -= lr * np.mean(p - y)
    return w, b

# 1. Recover known coefficients
n = 20000; X = rng.normal(size=(n, 2))
w_true, b_true = np.array([1.5, -0.7]), -1.0
y = (rng.random(n) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(X @ w_true + b_true)))).astype(float)
w, b = fit(X, y)
p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(X @ w + b)))
print("fitted", w.round(2), round(b, 2), "| mean p", p.mean().round(3), "rate", y.mean().round(3))

# 2. Separation: weights grow without a penalty, stay finite with one
xs = np.array([[-1.5], [-0.5], [0.5], [1.5]]); ys = np.array([0., 0., 1., 1.])
for steps in (100, 1000, 10000):
    print(steps, "no penalty w =", fit(xs, ys, steps=steps)[0].round(2),
          " L2 w =", fit(xs, ys, steps=steps, lam=0.1)[0].round(2))

# 3. Downsampling correction
keep = (y == 1) | (rng.random(n) < 0.1)            # keep 10% of negatives
w2, b2 = fit(X[keep], y[keep])
p2 = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(X @ w2 + b2)))
q = p2 / (p2 + (1 - p2) / 0.1)
print("raw mean", p2.mean().round(3), "corrected", q.mean().round(3), "true", y.mean().round(3))

What to look for. Part 1 recovers weights near (1.5, −0.7) and intercept near −1.0, and the mean predicted probability equals the observed rate. Part 2's unpenalised weight keeps growing with more steps while the L2 version settles. Part 3's raw predictions are far too high; the corrected mean matches the true rate, and the fitted slopes (unlike the intercept) are nearly unchanged by downsampling.

Work it out

A churn model has coefficient 0.693 on had_support_ticket (binary). A customer without a ticket has a 10% churn probability. What is it with a ticket? A stakeholder guesses "about 20%, since it doubles".

Answer

What doubles is the odds, not the probability: e^0.693 = 2.0.

Baseline odds = 0.10 / 0.90 = 0.111. With a ticket: 0.111 × 2 = 0.222. Convert back: p = 0.222 / 1.222 = 18.2%.

So the stakeholder is close — but only by accident of starting at 10%. Start at 50% instead: odds 1.0 → 2.0 → 66.7%, not 100%. Start at 80%: odds 4 → 8 → 88.9%. The same coefficient moves probability by 8, 17 or 9 percentage points depending where you start, because the effect is linear in log-odds and probability is squashed at both ends.

This is the single most common misreading of a logistic regression, and being able to correct it politely is worth more than deriving the loss.
