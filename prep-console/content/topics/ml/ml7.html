A hospital buys a software model that watches every inpatient's records and raises an alert when it thinks the patient is developing sepsis, a life-threatening reaction to infection where hours matter. The vendor's documentation reports an excellent score on a standard metric. Nurses start receiving alerts. After a few months, two complaints dominate: most alerts are about patients who turn out not to have sepsis, so staff learn to ignore them; and many patients who do develop sepsis were never flagged at all. Nothing is wrong with the software in the sense of a bug. The metric that looked excellent simply measured something different from what the nurses experience.

This is not hypothetical — it is roughly what an external validation of the widely deployed Epic Sepsis Model found in 2021, and it is this lesson's public case. It illustrates the central lesson of evaluation: a model does not have a single "accuracy". It produces scores; someone chooses a threshold; the combination produces four kinds of outcome — correct alarms, false alarms, misses and correct silences — whose counts depend on the threshold, on how common the condition is, and on the population. Different metrics summarise those counts in different ways, and each hides something.

Evaluation questions fill interviews for AI-engineering roles because they separate people who have deployed models from people who have only trained them. "Your model has 0.97 AUC and the business says it's useless — why?" "How do you pick the threshold?" "When is the precision–recall curve better than ROC?" "What fraction of positive tests are true if prevalence is 1%?" Each has a precise answer that follows from one small table.

This lesson builds everything from that table, the confusion matrix: accuracy, precision, recall, F1 and the false positive rate with a worked example; why changing the threshold trades one error for another; the ROC curve and precision–recall curve, both built by hand from ten scored examples (including AUC computed by counting pairs); the base-rate formula that explains why rare-event models produce mostly false alarms; choosing the operating point from costs, capacity or a required recall; multi-class averaging; uncertainty and slices; and the sepsis model's numbers.

Why accuracy is not enough

If 1% of transactions are fraud, a "model" that says "not fraud" every time is 99% accurate and completely useless. Accuracy counts all mistakes equally and is dominated by the common class. To judge a classifier we need to look at each kind of mistake separately.

The confusion matrix

For a yes/no problem, every prediction falls in one of four cells, comparing the prediction with the truth:

  • True positive (TP): predicted fraud, was fraud — a correct alarm.
  • False positive (FP): predicted fraud, was fine — a false alarm.
  • False negative (FN): predicted fine, was fraud — a miss.
  • True negative (TN): predicted fine, was fine — a correct silence.

A worked example

1,000 transactions, of which 20 are fraud. The model flags 30; of those, 15 are real fraud. So TP = 15, FP = 15, FN = 20 − 15 = 5, and TN = 980 − 15 = 965.

  • Accuracy = (15 + 965) / 1,000 = 98% — exactly what "never flag" would score.
  • Precision = TP / (TP + FP) = 15 / 30 = 50%: half of the alarms are real.
  • Recall (also called sensitivity or true positive rate) = TP / (TP + FN) = 15 / 20 = 75%: three quarters of the fraud was caught.
  • F1, the harmonic mean of precision and recall = 2 × 0.5 × 0.75 / (0.5 + 0.75) = 0.60.
  • False positive rate = FP / (FP + TN) = 15 / 980 ≈ 1.5%. Its complement, specificity, is ≈ 98.5%.

Thresholds

Most classifiers output a score or probability (ml6), and you choose a threshold above which you say "positive". Lower the threshold and you flag more: recall rises, but more false alarms come with the extra flags, so precision usually falls. Raise it and the reverse happens. There is no single "precision of the model" — only a precision at a threshold. The curves in the body show the whole range of trade-offs at once.

Which error hurts depends on who pays

False positives and false negatives are paid for by different people. In fraud detection, a false positive costs a legitimate customer a declined card and the bank a support call; a false negative costs the bank the stolen money. In cancer screening, a false positive costs an anxious patient a follow-up test; a false negative can cost a life. In a spam filter, a false positive hides a real email — often worse than letting a spam through. So the "best" balance between precision and recall is never a property of the model; it's a property of the use. That's why the first question to ask about any metric is "at what threshold, and chosen how?"

Two families of metric

It helps to sort metrics into two families. Threshold metrics — accuracy, precision, recall, F1, false positive rate — describe the decisions made at one particular threshold. Threshold-free metrics — the area under the ROC curve (AUC) and average precision — summarise how well the scores rank positives above negatives across all thresholds. The first family tells you what operations will experience; the second tells you how good the scores are before anyone chooses a threshold. You need both, and confusing them is the root of most evaluation mistakes.

1. What each metric answers

Each metric is a ratio from the confusion matrix, and each answers a question someone actually asks:

MetricFormulaQuestion it answers
Precision (PPV)TP / (TP + FP)When it alarms, how often is it right?
Recall (sensitivity, TPR)TP / (TP + FN)Of the real cases, how many does it catch?
False positive rateFP / (FP + TN)Of the negatives, how many get bothered?
SpecificityTN / (TN + FP)Of the negatives, how many are left alone?
Negative predictive valueTN / (TN + FN)When it's silent, how often is that right?
Accuracy(TP + TN) / allHow often is it right overall? (misleading when imbalanced)

Medicine uses sensitivity, specificity and positive predictive value (PPV) for recall, 1 − FPR and precision; information retrieval uses precision and recall; the words differ but the cells are the same.

F1 combines precision and recall with a harmonic mean, 2PR / (P + R), which is dominated by the smaller of the two: precision 1.0 with recall 0.1 has an arithmetic mean of 0.55 but an F1 of 0.18. That's the point — a model can't look good by maximising one while neglecting the other. When the two errors have different importance, F-beta = (1 + β²)PR / (β²P + R) weights recall β times as much as precision: F2 for screening, where misses are worse; F0.5 when false alarms are worse. Even better, when you can put money on each error, use cost directly (section 5).

3. The ROC curve and AUC

The ROC curve (receiver operating characteristic, a name from radar engineering) plots the true positive rate against the false positive rate for every possible threshold. Walk down the sorted list: each positive moves the curve up by 1/4 (one of four positives), each negative moves it right by 1/6 (one of six negatives). The points are (0, 0) → (0, 0.25) → (0, 0.5) → (0.17, 0.5) → (0.17, 0.75) → (0.33, 0.75) → (0.33, 1.0) → then right along the top to (1, 1).

The area under the ROC curve, AUC, has a beautiful meaning: the probability that a randomly chosen positive scores higher than a randomly chosen negative. That gives a way to compute it without drawing anything. There are 4 × 6 = 24 positive–negative pairs. Count, for each positive, how many negatives score below it: 0.90 beats all 6; 0.80 beats all 6; 0.55 beats 5 (all but 0.70); 0.40 beats 4 (0.35, 0.30, 0.20, 0.10). Total 21 of 24, so AUC = 0.875 — the same as the shaded area (1 − the three missing squares of size 1/4 × 1/6 = 1 − 3/24). A random ranking scores 0.5 (the diagonal); a perfect one 1.0; a score well below 0.5 means real signal with the labels or sign flipped.

AUC depends only on the order of the scores, not their values, so it says nothing about calibration (ml15), and it averages over every threshold, including ones you would never use. And because both of its axes are rates within a class — TPR within the positives, FPR within the negatives — it doesn't change when the class balance changes. That is its virtue and, for rare events, its trap.

4. The precision–recall curve, and why rare events need it

The precision–recall (PR) curve plots precision against recall across thresholds. For our ten examples the points, flagging one more case at a time, are (0.25, 1.00), (0.50, 1.00), (0.50, 0.67), (0.75, 0.75), (0.75, 0.60), (1.00, 0.67), then precision falls to 0.40 as the remaining negatives are flagged. Its summary is average precision (AP): the precision at each point where recall increases, weighted by the size of the increase. Recall rises by 0.25 at ranks 1, 2, 4 and 6, where precision is 1, 1, 3/4 and 4/6, so AP = 0.25 × (1 + 1 + 0.75 + 0.667) = 0.854. The PR curve's baseline — what a random ranking achieves — is the prevalence, here 0.4; with 1% positives it is 0.01.

Now the key effect. Keep the same model — the same TPR and FPR at each threshold — and make positives rare. Precision can be written in terms of the rates and the prevalence π:

precision = TPR·π / ( TPR·π + FPR·(1 − π) )

At TPR 0.8 and FPR 0.05, with π = 0.5 precision is 0.4 / (0.4 + 0.025) = 94%. With π = 0.01 it is 0.008 / (0.008 + 0.0495) = 13.9%. The ROC point is identical in both cases; the alert queue went from almost all real to six false alarms for every real one. With rare positives, ROC-AUC can be 0.95 while the operational experience is dismal, and only the PR view (or precision at the deployed threshold) shows it. Saito and Rehmsmeier's simulations make exactly this point.

5. Choosing the operating point

The threshold should come from the decision the model supports. Three common ways:

From costs. If probabilities are calibrated and a false negative costs C_FN while a false positive costs C_FP, flagging a case with probability p has expected cost (1 − p)·C_FP, and not flagging it has expected cost p·C_FN. Flag when p·C_FN > (1 − p)·C_FP, i.e. when

p > C_FP / (C_FP + C_FN)

With a £4 review and a £400 missed fraud: 4 / 404 ≈ 0.0099. A 1% fraud probability is worth reviewing — which is why fraud systems flag far more than half-likely cases. With equal costs the formula gives 0.5, the only situation where 0.5 is special.

From capacity. If the team can review 500 cases a day, the operating point is simply the top 500 scores, and the metric that matters is precision@500 (how many of the 500 are real) and the value caught.

From a required rate. A safety process may require recall of at least 95%; choose the highest threshold that achieves it on validation data and report the precision and alert volume it implies — the cost of that requirement.

Whichever way, choose on validation data, never on the test set, and report precision, recall and volume at the chosen threshold, with the date range and prevalence they were measured at.

Worked: costs on the ten examples. Suppose a miss costs 5 and a false alarm costs 1. Total cost at each threshold from section 2's table: at 0.80, 0 false alarms and 2 misses = 10; at 0.55, 1 + 1 × 5 = 6; at 0.40, 2 false alarms and no misses = 2; at 0.10, 6 false alarms = 6. The best threshold is 0.40. With equal costs (1 and 1), thresholds 0.80, 0.55 and 0.40 all cost 2, and flagging everything costs 6 — changing the cost ratio moved the best threshold without changing the model at all. On real data you do exactly this on a validation set, over hundreds of candidate thresholds.

6. More than two classes

With K classes, compute precision and recall per class (treating each class in turn as "positive" and the rest as "negative"), then average. Macro averaging takes the plain mean over classes, so a rare class counts as much as a common one. Micro averaging pools all the TP, FP and FN counts first, so common classes dominate; for single-label problems micro-F1 equals accuracy. Weighted averaging weights each class's metric by its frequency. Worked: a three-class model with per-class F1 of 0.95 (80% of data), 0.80 (15%) and 0.30 (5%) has macro-F1 (0.95 + 0.80 + 0.30)/3 = 0.68 and weighted-F1 0.95 × 0.8 + 0.80 × 0.15 + 0.30 × 0.05 = 0.895. Same model; the choice of average moved the headline by 21 points. Say which one you report, and use macro when rare classes matter.

7. Metrics for probabilities and rankings

Threshold metrics judge decisions. Two other families judge the scores themselves. Probability metrics — log loss and the Brier score (ml5) — reward calibrated probabilities and are the right choice when the probability itself is used (pricing, expected-value calculations). Ranking metrics — precision@k, recall@k, mean reciprocal rank, NDCG — judge only the top of a ranked list and fit search, recommendation and review queues. Regression has its own set: MAE, RMSE, MAPE (percentage error, which breaks when actual values are near zero) and R² (the fraction of variance explained, which can be negative on test data for a model worse than predicting the mean).

8. Evaluating classifiers built on LLMs

AI-engineering work increasingly uses a large language model (a text-generating model pretrained on next-token prediction, ml1) as a classifier: "Is this support ticket about billing? Answer yes or no." Everything above still applies, with two twists. First, a prompt that returns only a label gives a single point on the ROC and PR plots — one threshold, fixed by the prompt — so you can't trade precision for recall. To get a curve, obtain a score: the model's probability for the "yes" token (from token log-probabilities, where the provider's programming interface exposes them), an averaged vote over several samples, or a self-reported confidence (least reliable). Second, prompt changes move the operating point unpredictably: rewording "flag anything that might be billing" versus "only clear billing issues" is a threshold change in disguise, so compare prompts at matched recall or precision, not by accuracy alone. Build a labelled evaluation set of a few hundred examples, report the confusion matrix per prompt version, and treat the prompt like any other model version.

9. A checklist before reporting a number

  • Is the evaluation data drawn like production — same period, population and prevalence — and never used for training or tuning?
  • Is the threshold stated, and was it chosen on validation data?
  • Is there a baseline (majority class, existing rules, the current model) at the same threshold or volume?
  • Is there an uncertainty estimate (section deep), and are key slices reported?
  • Does the metric match the decision — precision@capacity for review queues, recall at a minimum for safety, calibration for probabilities that feed prices?

AUC as a statistic, and its limits

AUC equals the Mann–Whitney U statistic divided by the number of positive–negative pairs, which is why it can be computed by sorting scores (rank sums) in O(n log n) without drawing a curve, and why ties count as half. Because it integrates over all thresholds, two models can have the same AUC and very different performance in the region that matters — one better at low false-positive rates, the other at high recall. Partial AUC restricts the area to a region such as FPR < 1%, and "recall at a fixed precision" or "precision at a fixed recall" summarise the operating region directly. For large-scale ranking systems the relevant slice is usually the very top of the list.

Prevalence shift and re-estimating precision

Recall and FPR are conditional on the true class, so if the model and the class definitions don't change, they transfer between populations; precision doesn't, because it depends on prevalence. When the evaluation set was enriched with positives (common in medical and fraud datasets), recompute the precision you'd see in production with the formula in section 4 and the production prevalence. The same logic warns against comparing precision across time periods or regions without accounting for base rates.

Uncertainty and comparing models

Metrics computed on a finite test set are estimates. With 50 positives, recall is measured in steps of 2%, and its 95% interval can easily span ±12 points. Bootstrap the test set to get intervals. To compare two models on the same test set, use paired methods, which exploit the correlation between their errors: DeLong's test for AUC, McNemar's test for accuracy (it only uses the examples where the two models disagree), or a paired bootstrap of the difference. A 0.003 AUC gain on a few thousand examples is almost never meaningful.

Slices and fairness metrics

Aggregate metrics hide failures in subgroups: a model with 90% recall overall may have 60% recall for new customers, a region or a device type. Report metrics per important slice, especially slices defined by how the model will be used or by protected attributes. Common fairness criteria are themselves confusion-matrix comparisons across groups: equal opportunity asks for equal recall, equalised odds for equal recall and equal FPR, predictive parity for equal precision. When base rates differ between groups, these criteria generally can't all hold at once — a well-known impossibility result — so choosing among them is a policy decision, not a technical one.

Label quality limits every metric

A metric is only as good as the labels it is computed against. If 5% of "legitimate" transactions are actually undetected fraud, the model gets penalised for catching them (they count as false positives), and measured precision underestimates the truth. Label definitions also shift: the Epic Sepsis Model's developers and the external validators used different definitions of sepsis onset, which is part of why the measured numbers differ. Senior answers ask how the labels were produced before trusting the metric.

Discrimination is not calibration

AUC and average precision measure discrimination: whether positives score higher than negatives. They are unchanged by any order-preserving transformation of the scores — squaring every score, or dividing all by ten, leaves AUC identical. Calibration asks whether a predicted 30% actually happens 30% of the time. A model can rank perfectly and be badly calibrated (every probability ten times too high after downsampling, ml6), or be perfectly calibrated and rank poorly (predicting the base rate for everyone). The cost-based threshold formula needs calibrated probabilities; a capacity-based threshold needs only good ranking. Report both kinds of metric, and see ml15 for measuring and fixing calibration. A useful habit is to state every evaluation as a pair — "AP 0.41, expected calibration error 0.02" — so nobody mistakes good ranking for trustworthy probabilities, or the reverse.

Goodhart's law

"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Teams optimising a single offline metric find ways to raise it that don't help users: tuning to the test set, exploiting label artefacts, improving the metric on easy cases. The defences are held-out data used rarely, several complementary metrics (a precision, a recall, a calibration measure, a business number), slice analysis, and ultimately online experiments that measure the outcome itself (ml16).

The public case: the Epic Sepsis Model

The Epic Sepsis Model is a proprietary score built into Epic's electronic health record software and deployed at hundreds of US hospitals. Its developer reported areas under the ROC curve of 0.73 to 0.83. In 2021, Wong and colleagues at Michigan Medicine published an independent external validation in JAMA Internal Medicine, covering 27,697 patients across 38,455 hospitalisations, of which 2,552 (6.6%) involved sepsis. Their findings, in this lesson's vocabulary:

  • AUC 0.63 (95% CI 0.62–0.64) — well below the reported range, and much closer to chance than the product's description suggested.
  • At the score threshold of 6 used in practice, sensitivity (recall) was 33%, specificity 83% and positive predictive value (precision) 12%. The model missed 1,709 of the 2,552 sepsis cases — 67%.
  • The alerts fired for 18% of all hospitalisations (6,971 of 38,455), most of them patients without sepsis — an alert burden that invites the "alert fatigue" of the opening story.
  • It identified only 183 sepsis patients (7%) who had not already received timely antibiotics from clinicians — the cases where an alert could actually change care.

From the reported numbers you can rebuild the whole confusion matrix: TP ≈ 843 (33% of 2,552), FN = 1,709, FP ≈ 6,971 − 843 = 6,128, TN ≈ 35,903 − 6,128 = 29,775. Check: precision 843 / 6,971 ≈ 12%; specificity 29,775 / 35,903 ≈ 83%. Every lesson of this topic is visible: a single AUC from the developer said little about performance at the operating threshold; prevalence of 6.6% plus an FPR of 17% produced mostly false alarms; recall at the threshold was low; the metric that mattered clinically — catching cases clinicians hadn't already caught — was different again; and the population, label definition and data at an external site changed the numbers entirely.

What goes wrong in production evaluation

Metrics measured on the wrong population. A model evaluated on a balanced or enriched sample reports precision that production will never see. Evaluate on data drawn like production traffic, or correct for prevalence.

Invisible recall. If only flagged cases are investigated, misses are never labelled, and recall can't be measured. Audit a random sample of unflagged cases regularly.

Threshold drift. When the score distribution shifts — seasonality, a new customer segment, an upstream feature change — a fixed threshold changes the alert volume. Monitor volume and re-tune thresholds on recent labelled data.

The easy-positive trap. A model can score well by ranking highly the positives that existing rules already catch. Measure performance on the cases that matter: those the current process misses, as the sepsis study did with "patients not already given antibiotics".

A reporting template

For any deployed classifier, report: the date range and population of the evaluation data and its prevalence; the operating threshold and how it was chosen; precision, recall, FPR and daily flagged volume at that threshold, with bootstrap intervals; the PR curve (and ROC curve) with AP and AUC; calibration (ml15); the same numbers for key slices; and the business outcome the metric is supposed to predict. That is what separates an evaluation that survives contact with operations from one that doesn't.

Goal: build the confusion matrix, ROC points, AUC and average precision by hand for the ten examples, then see how prevalence changes precision. NumPy only; written for Python 3.12.

import numpy as np

s = np.array([.9, .8, .7, .55, .5, .4, .35, .3, .2, .1])
y = np.array([1, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0])

def confusion(t):
    pred = s >= t
    tp = np.sum(pred & (y == 1)); fp = np.sum(pred & (y == 0))
    fn = np.sum(~pred & (y == 1)); tn = np.sum(~pred & (y == 0))
    return tp, fp, fn, tn

for t in (.8, .55, .4, .1):
    tp, fp, fn, tn = confusion(t)
    print(f"t={t}: TP={tp} FP={fp} FN={fn} TN={tn} "
          f"precision={tp/(tp+fp):.2f} recall={tp/(tp+fn):.2f} FPR={fp/(fp+tn):.2f}")

pos, neg = s[y == 1], s[y == 0]                 # AUC by counting pairs
auc = np.mean([(p > n) + 0.5 * (p == n) for p in pos for n in neg])
order = np.argsort(-s); hits = y[order]         # average precision
prec_at_k = np.cumsum(hits) / np.arange(1, len(y) + 1)
ap = np.sum(prec_at_k[hits == 1]) / hits.sum()
print("AUC", auc, "AP", round(ap, 3))

for pi in (0.5, 0.1, 0.01):                     # same TPR/FPR, different prevalence
    tpr, fpr = 0.8, 0.05
    print(f"prevalence {pi}: precision = {tpr*pi / (tpr*pi + fpr*(1-pi)):.3f}")

What to look for. The four threshold rows should match section 2's table; AUC prints 0.875 and AP 0.854, as computed by hand. The last loop shows precision collapsing from 0.94 to 0.14 as prevalence falls from 50% to 1% with an unchanged model. Try adding a tie (set two scores equal) to see ties count as half in AUC.

Work it out

Fraud detector on 10,000 transactions. Prevalence 1%. The model catches 80% of fraud at a 5% false-positive rate. Your manager calls that "80% accurate with only 5% errors". Compute the confusion matrix, the precision and the accuracy, and suggest what to report instead.

Answer

100 fraudulent, 9,900 legitimate.

TP = 80% × 100 = 80 · FN = 20 · FP = 5% × 9,900 = 495 · TN = 9,405.

Precision = 80 / (80 + 495) = 13.9%. Six out of every seven flagged transactions are legitimate customers being blocked.

That 5% false-positive rate sounds small only because it is measured against the huge negative class — 5% of 9,900 swamps 80% of 100. This is the base-rate effect, and it is why ROC-AUC flatters rare-event models: FPR barely moves while the review queue fills with noise.

Accuracy, incidentally, is (80 + 9,405)/10,000 = 94.9% — lower than a model that flags nothing (99.0%), even though this model is far more useful. Report precision and recall at the deployed threshold, the daily volume the team must review (575 per 10,000 transactions), and the fraud value caught, and choose the threshold from the cost of a review versus the cost of a missed fraud.
