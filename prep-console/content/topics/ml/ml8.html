A bank builds a model to predict which small-business loans will default. Two percent of past loans defaulted. The first model scores 98% accuracy, and the team is briefly delighted until someone notices it predicts "no default" for every single application. Someone suggests the textbook remedy: balance the classes. They throw away most of the good loans until defaults make up half the training set, retrain, and now the model flags plenty of applications. It goes to production, where the risk team uses its predicted probabilities to price loans — and every applicant looks about 25 times riskier than they are, because the model learned a world where half of all loans default.

That story contains most of what goes wrong with class imbalance, the situation where one class is much rarer than the other. The rare class is almost always the one that matters: fraud, disease, equipment failure, churn, a click on an ad. The folklore says imbalance is a modelling problem to be fixed by rebalancing the data. The evidence says something more nuanced. Most of the trouble comes from measuring the wrong thing (accuracy) and deciding with the wrong threshold (0.5). Standard training on the natural distribution often ranks the rare class perfectly well. And the popular fixes — oversampling, undersampling, SMOTE, class weights — all distort the model's probabilities in a predictable way that has to be corrected.

Interviewers ask about imbalance constantly, and the strongest answer is not a list of techniques but an order of operations with reasons: fix the metric, move the threshold, then consider weights or resampling if the model genuinely underweights positives — and correct the probabilities if anything uses them.

This lesson builds that answer: what imbalance actually breaks, with a threshold sweep on loan data; why cross-entropy self-balances once the model learns the base rate (with the gradient arithmetic); class weights, undersampling and oversampling and exactly how each shifts probabilities, with the correction formula worked; how SMOTE creates points and why it must stay inside cross-validation folds; evaluating with few positives; prevalence shift in production; and the public case — a 2022 simulation and ovarian-cancer study finding that imbalance corrections made calibration much worse without improving discrimination.

What "imbalanced" means

A dataset is imbalanced when one class is much rarer than the others: card fraud (around 0.1–0.2% of transactions in public datasets), disease screening (1%), equipment failures, ad clicks (around 1–2%), loan defaults (2%). The rare class is called the minority class and the common one the majority class. It's usually the minority we care about.

An analogy: a smoke alarm in a kitchen

A smoke alarm hears thousands of harmless events (toast, steam) for every real fire. If you judged it by accuracy — "fraction of moments it was right" — an alarm that never sounds would score 99.99%. What you actually care about is two things: does it go off when there's a fire (recall), and when it goes off, is there usually a fire (precision)? And how sensitive to set it is a choice you make based on costs: a kitchen that burns toast daily wants a less sensitive setting than a server room. Imbalance doesn't make the alarm's sensor worse; it makes the naive way of scoring the alarm useless and makes the sensitivity setting crucial. The analogy breaks in one respect: a machine-learning model's sensor is trained from examples, and with very few fires to learn from, the sensor itself can be undertrained — which is a data-quantity problem, not a proportion problem.

Why it causes trouble

  • Accuracy lies. Always predicting the majority class scores 98% on 2% defaults.
  • The default threshold is wrong. A well-calibrated model gives a rare-class example a probability above 0.5 only when it is more likely positive than not. With a 2% base rate that's uncommon, so at 0.5 the model flags almost nothing — not because it hasn't learned, but because 0.5 is the wrong cut.
  • Few examples to learn from. 200 defaults among 10,000 loans is only 200 examples of what default looks like.
  • Noisy evaluation. With few positives, metrics like recall jump around between splits.

A worked threshold sweep

10,000 loan applications, 200 defaults (2%). At threshold 0.5 the model flags 20 applications, 18 of them defaults: precision 90%, recall 18/200 = 9% — it misses 91% of defaults. At threshold 0.1 it flags 400, 140 of them defaults: precision 35%, recall 70%. At 0.03 it flags 1,500, 180 of them defaults: precision 12%, recall 90%. Nothing about the model changed; only the threshold. Which row is right depends on what a missed default costs compared with a rejected good customer — the cost-based threshold of ml7.

The tools, in the order to try them

  1. Measure properly: precision, recall, PR curves and expected cost, not accuracy.
  2. Move the threshold to match the costs or capacity.
  3. Class weights in the loss, if the model genuinely under-weights positives.
  4. Resampling: undersample the majority (especially when there's far too much of it) or oversample the minority, including synthetic methods such as SMOTE.
  5. Change the data or the problem: collect more positives, add features, or use a cascade or anomaly detection.

And one rule that cuts across all of them: steps 3 and 4 change the model's probabilities, so if anything downstream uses those probabilities, correct them.

1. What training does with rare positives

It's worth seeing why standard training copes better than the folklore suggests. Take logistic regression (ml6) trained with cross-entropy on data with 1% positives. For each example the gradient with respect to its score is p − y (ml5), so the size of its push is |p − y|.

  • At the very start, weights are zero and every prediction is p = 0.5. Every example pushes with size 0.5, so the 1% of positives supply 1% of the total push. This is the phase people worry about.
  • Once the intercept learns the base rate — which takes a few steps, because the intercept's gradient is mean(p − y) — typical predictions are p ≈ 0.01. A negative now pushes with |0.01 − 0| = 0.01; a positive with |0.01 − 1| = 0.99. Per 100 examples: 99 negatives × 0.01 = 0.99 and 1 positive × 0.99 = 0.99. The two classes supply equal total push.

So after the first phase, cross-entropy is self-balancing: each rare example is worth about 99 common ones, exactly compensating for their frequency. The model isn't ignoring the minority. What remains is the fact that there are only so many positive examples to learn from, and the threshold. This is why strong, well-regularised models trained on natural data often rank rare positives very well — and why rebalancing tends to change probabilities much more than it changes ranking.

2. Measuring: metric, split and uncertainty

Evaluation comes first because most "imbalance problems" are measurement problems (ml7). Use precision and recall at a meaningful threshold, the precision–recall curve and average precision, or expected cost, and always include the trivial baseline. Two further habits matter specifically for rare classes:

Stratified splits. A random 20% test split of 10,000 loans with 200 defaults might contain 32 defaults or 48. Stratification keeps the class proportions equal in every split (exactly 40 here), and in cross-validation (ml9) it keeps each fold representative.

Uncertainty. With 50 positives in the test set, recall moves in steps of 2%, and at a measured recall of 0.80 its standard error is √(0.8 × 0.2 / 50) ≈ 0.057, so a 95% interval runs from about 0.69 to 0.91. Two models whose recalls differ by 5 points on this test set are statistically indistinguishable. Use repeated stratified cross-validation and bootstrap intervals, and choose between models only on differences larger than the noise.

3. Moving the threshold

If probabilities are calibrated, the cost-minimising threshold is C_FP / (C_FP + C_FN) (ml7). Suppose rejecting a good applicant loses £200 of profit and approving a defaulter loses £5,000. The threshold is 200 / 5,200 ≈ 0.038: reject any application with more than a 3.8% chance of default. In the sweep above that is close to the 0.03 row — 90% recall at 12% precision — which looks alarming by the precision number alone but is the cheapest policy given these costs. If instead the team can only review 400 applications a week, the threshold is whatever flags 400 (0.1 in the sweep). Neither of these involves retraining, and both can be changed tomorrow if costs or capacity change. This is why threshold tuning should come before any change to the training data.

4. Class weights

A class weight multiplies the loss of every example of that class. scikit-learn's class_weight="balanced" sets each class's weight inversely proportional to its frequency; with 2% positives, positives get weight 0.98/0.02 = 49 relative to negatives. In expectation, weighting positives by w is the same as showing the model each positive w times. The model's optimum moves: it now behaves as if positives were 49 times more common, so its predicted odds for every case are multiplied by about 49.

Worked. A borrower with a true 2% default risk has odds 0.02/0.98 = 0.0204. Multiplied by 49, the weighted model's odds are about 1.0 — a predicted probability of 50%. For ranking and a tuned threshold this is harmless: the ordering of borrowers is essentially unchanged, and you simply use a higher threshold. For pricing, it is a disaster: every borrower looks 25 times riskier. The correction divides the odds back by w:

q = p / ( p + (1 − p) · w )        weighted positives by w; p = model output, q = corrected

With p = 0.5 and w = 49: q = 0.5 / (0.5 + 24.5) = 0.02. For non-linear models the shift isn't exactly a constant on the log-odds scale, so recalibration on held-out data at the true prevalence (ml15) is the safer general fix.

When do weights genuinely help? When the model has limited capacity and must trade errors between classes — a weak or heavily regularised model will spend its capacity on the majority — and in neural networks trained on extreme imbalance, where weighting can speed up learning of the rare class. With large weights, each rare example produces a big gradient, which can make stochastic training noisier; moderate weights (square root of the ratio, for example) are a common compromise.

5. Undersampling and oversampling

Random undersampling keeps all positives and a random fraction w of negatives. Its main legitimate use is cost: with 500 million negatives, keeping 2.5% of them loses little information (most negatives are easy and redundant) and cuts training cost by 40 times. The probability shift is exact for a well-specified model — keeping a fraction w of negatives multiplies the odds of positive by 1/w — and is corrected with q = p / (p + (1 − p)/w) (ml6). With fraud at 1% and 1 in 99 negatives kept to reach 1:1, a model output of 0.5 corrects to q = 0.5 / (0.5 + 0.5 × 99) = 1%.

Random oversampling duplicates positives until the classes balance. It throws nothing away, but exact duplicates let flexible models memorise the rare examples, increasing variance (ml2), and — like weights — it inflates probabilities.

6. SMOTE, and why it must stay inside the fold

SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique, Chawla et al., 2002) oversamples without exact duplicates. For a minority example x, pick one of its k nearest minority neighbours, and create a new point a random fraction λ of the way along the line between them: new = x + λ·(neighbour − x). Worked: x = (2, 10), neighbour = (4, 14), λ = 0.25 gives new = (2 + 0.25 × 2, 10 + 0.25 × 4) = (2.5, 11).

The idea is that a wider cloud of minority points encourages a smoother, more generous boundary around the minority class, which helps weak learners such as a single decision tree or a nearest-neighbour classifier. Its limits: it assumes the space between two minority points is also minority territory, which is false when classes interleave; interpolating between categorical values or in very high dimensions creates unrealistic points; and it inflates probabilities like any oversampling. Elor and Averbuch-Elor (2022) compared balancing methods across many datasets and found that balancing helped weak learners but did not improve strong modern classifiers such as gradient-boosted trees.

The leakage trap. If SMOTE (or any oversampling) is applied to the whole dataset before splitting into training and validation, synthetic points built from validation examples end up in training. The model is then evaluated on points it has effectively seen, and scores look better than they will in production. Resampling must be part of the training pipeline, fitted separately inside each cross-validation fold (ml9), and validation and test data must never be resampled — they should reflect the real class proportions.

7. What rebalancing does to probabilities

Every technique in sections 4–6 moves the model towards predicting positives, and so every one of them breaks the link between output and true probability. This is the part of the folklore that causes real damage. If the scores feed only a threshold or a ranking, the damage is contained: choose the threshold on validation data drawn at the true prevalence. If the scores are used as probabilities — expected loss (probability × amount), pricing, capacity planning ("we expect 300 defaults"), combining with other models — they must be corrected with the formula (weights and random undersampling) or recalibrated on held-out data at the true prevalence (anything else).

8. Changing the data or the problem

When the limiting factor is the number of positives, the best investments are on the data side: collecting more history, pooling related positive types, targeted labelling of likely positives (active learning, ml1), and better features. Hard-negative mining keeps the negatives the model confuses with positives and drops the easy ones, focusing capacity where it matters. When positives are too rare or too varied to learn — novel fraud patterns, never-seen machine faults — anomaly detection models normal behaviour and flags departures from it, trading precision for the ability to catch the unseen. And at very large scale, a cascade of a cheap high-recall filter followed by an expensive precise model (or human reviewers) is the standard architecture.

9. The loan example, three ways

Put the pieces together on the bank's problem — 2% defaults, £200 lost per wrongly rejected good applicant, £5,000 lost per approved defaulter — comparing three teams.

Team A trains on the natural data and tunes the threshold. Its model's probabilities are calibrated, so the cost formula applies directly: reject above 200 / 5,200 ≈ 0.038. It validates that threshold on held-out data at the real 2% prevalence, reports precision and recall there, and hands the risk team probabilities they can use for pricing as-is.

Team B balances the classes with weights of 49 and keeps a 0.5 threshold. Its ranking is about as good as Team A's. Its threshold of 0.5 on inflated probabilities corresponds to a true risk of 0.5 / (0.5 + 0.5 × 49) = 2% — so it rejects everyone above a 2% true risk, a stricter policy than the costs justify, rejecting many profitable customers. Its pricing uses probabilities 25 times too high. Nothing in its offline AUC warned of either problem.

Team C balances the classes, then corrects. It applies the weighting correction to every output (or recalibrates on a held-out set at 2%), then applies the cost threshold of 0.038 to the corrected probabilities. Its decisions and prices end up essentially the same as Team A's — after extra work, and with the risk that the correction isn't exact for its non-linear model.

The comparison is the whole lesson: rebalancing didn't buy Team C anything that threshold tuning didn't already give Team A, and without correction it made Team B's model wrong in two different ways. Rebalancing earns its place only when it improves the model itself — a limited-capacity learner, a neural network that learns the rare class too slowly, or a training set too big to use in full.

Why rebalancing seldom improves ranking

For a model that can represent the true relationship, reweighting or resampling classes changes the optimal scores only by a monotone transformation — for logistic regression, a constant shift in log-odds. Monotone transformations don't change which case scores higher, so AUC and average precision are unaffected in the limit of plenty of data. Where rebalancing does change ranking, it's through finite-sample effects on flexible models (duplicated points increase variance, discarded negatives lose information) or through capacity limits, where the model must trade errors between classes and the weights change the trade. This is the theoretical backbone of the empirical finding that corrections often don't improve discrimination.

Prior (label) shift in production

Class proportions change: fraud spikes during an attack, defaults rise in a recession, disease prevalence varies by clinic. If only the proportions change — each class still looks the same — this is label shift. A model trained at one prevalence is then systematically miscalibrated at another, and a fixed threshold produces a different volume of flags. Given the new prevalence, the same odds correction applies. When the new prevalence is unknown, it can be estimated from unlabelled production data using the model's own predictions — for example, with an expectation–maximisation procedure that alternates between re-estimating the prior and re-adjusting the predicted probabilities, or with black-box shift estimation using the model's confusion matrix.

Example-dependent costs

Not all positives are equally important: a £10,000 fraud matters 1,000 times more than a £10 one. Class weights treat them the same. Cost-sensitive approaches weight each example by its cost in training, or — usually better — keep the model probabilistic and apply the cost at decision time: flag when p × amount at risk exceeds the cost of review. The second approach keeps probabilities honest and lets the cost logic change without retraining.

Long-tailed multi-class problems

With thousands of classes — product categories, species, rare diagnoses — frequency varies across orders of magnitude, and models favour frequent classes. Logit adjustment (Menon et al.) corrects this by subtracting τ·log(prior) from each class's score at prediction time, or adding it to the loss during training, which targets balanced error (equal weight on every class) rather than overall error. Related methods include balanced softmax and training the representation on natural data but the final classifier on class-balanced samples. Evaluate with macro-averaged metrics and by frequency bucket.

Focal loss and hard examples

Focal loss (ml5) addresses a different aspect: not the class proportion but the dominance of easy examples. In dense object detection, most of the ~100,000 candidate boxes per image are trivially background; down-weighting well-classified examples let the detector learn from the hard ones. It shares the probability distortion of all reweighting schemes, and its benefit is largest when the majority class is overwhelmingly easy.

Negative subsampling as an efficiency tool

In systems with billions of examples, the question isn't balance but compute. Most negatives are easy and carry little information once the model has learned them; keeping a random fraction w of negatives reduces training cost roughly by 1/w with little loss in accuracy, and the exact log-odds correction restores calibration. The variance cost is concentrated in the negatives' contribution, so the optimal w depends on how informative typical negatives are. Refinements keep negatives with probability depending on how hard they are (importance sampling), then weight each kept negative by the inverse of its keep probability so the loss stays unbiased — a principled version of hard-negative mining.

Imbalance in LLM-era pipelines

The same issues appear in modern AI systems in new places. Safety and content classifiers face extreme imbalance (harmful requests are rare), so thresholds are tuned per category from the cost of a missed harmful request versus an unnecessary refusal. Retrieval-augmented systems train embedding models with contrastive losses where each query has one positive passage and many negatives, and the choice of hard negatives matters more than their number. And when fine-tuning a language model on labelled examples, a skewed label distribution teaches the model the prior as well as the task, so evaluation must look at per-class recall, not just overall accuracy.

Selective labels

Imbalance often comes with a biased label process. A lender observes defaults only on approved loans; a fraud team confirms fraud only on investigated transactions. The observed positive rate then reflects past policy, not the population, and training on it teaches the model the old policy's blind spots. Randomised exploration (approving a small random share below the cut-off), reject inference and auditing random samples of unflagged cases are the corrections (ml1).

The public case: "The harm of class imbalance corrections"

In 2022, van den Goorbergh, van Smeden, Timmerman and Van Calster tested the folklore directly for clinical risk prediction, where predicted probabilities are shown to doctors and patients. They trained standard and ridge-penalised logistic regression models with and without random undersampling, random oversampling and SMOTE, in Monte Carlo simulations and in a real case study on ovarian cancer diagnosis, and measured discrimination (AUC), calibration and classification. Their results, in their own summary:

  • All imbalance correction methods led to poor calibration — strong overestimation of the probability of belonging to the minority class.
  • They did not lead to better discrimination in terms of AUC.
  • They improved classification in terms of sensitivity and specificity — but similar results were obtained by shifting the probability threshold instead.

Their conclusion: outcome imbalance is not a problem in itself, and imbalance correction may even worsen model performance. For a doctor told "this patient has a 60% chance of a malignant tumour" when the true risk is 15%, that worsening is not academic. A separate large benchmark by Elor and Averbuch-Elor reached a compatible conclusion from the machine-learning side: balancing helps weak classifiers but not state-of-the-art ones. Together they justify the order of operations in this lesson: metric, then threshold, then — only if needed — weights or resampling, followed by probability correction.

Where rebalancing is the right call

Undersampling negatives is standard practice in very large-scale systems such as ad-click prediction, where there are billions of negatives and training cost matters; it is paired with an exact probability correction (Facebook's ad-click paper, ml6's case, found a negative sampling rate of 0.025 performed best and gives the correction formula). Weights or oversampling can help when a model's capacity is limited, or in deep-learning settings where the rare class is learned too slowly. The mistake is not using these tools; it's using them by reflex and forgetting what they do to probabilities.

Failure modes in production

Inflated probabilities reaching users. Risk scores shown to people, used in pricing or summed for forecasts, from a model trained on rebalanced data. Symptom: predicted counts far above observed counts. Fix: correct or recalibrate, and monitor the mean predicted probability against the observed rate.

Resampled validation sets. Tuning thresholds or reporting precision on a balanced validation set gives numbers production will never see. Validation and test data should have the real prevalence.

Leaky oversampling. SMOTE applied before cross-validation, producing optimistic offline scores and a disappointing launch.

Prevalence drift. The positive rate changes and alert volume swings. Monitor flagged volume, precision on audited samples and the score distribution; re-tune thresholds on recent labelled data.

A practical recipe

  1. Establish metrics at the true prevalence: PR curve, AP, precision and recall at candidate thresholds, with bootstrap intervals.
  2. Use stratified (and, for rare events, repeated) cross-validation.
  3. Train a strong model on the natural distribution; tune the threshold from costs or capacity on validation data.
  4. Only if the model underweights positives, or training cost demands it, try class weights or undersampling — inside the pipeline.
  5. Correct or recalibrate probabilities if anything consumes them, and verify calibration on held-out data.

Goal: show that threshold tuning matches rebalancing on ranking and decisions, while rebalancing inflates probabilities until corrected. NumPy only; written for Python 3.12.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)

n = 50000; X = rng.normal(size=(n, 3))
z = X @ np.array([1.2, -0.8, 0.5]) - 4.2            # about 2% positives
y = (rng.random(n) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))).astype(float)
tr, te = slice(0, 40000), slice(40000, None)

def fit(X, y, wpos=1.0, lr=0.5, steps=3000):
    w, b = np.zeros(X.shape[1]), 0.0
    sw = np.where(y == 1, wpos, 1.0); sw = sw / sw.mean()
    for _ in range(steps):
        p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(X @ w + b)))
        g = sw * (p - y)
        w -= lr * X.T @ g / len(y); b -= lr * g.mean()
    return lambda X: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(X @ w + b)))

def auc(s, y):
    r = np.argsort(np.argsort(s)) + 1; npos = y.sum()
    return (r[y == 1].sum() - npos * (npos + 1) / 2) / (npos * (len(y) - npos))

wpos = (1 - y[tr].mean()) / y[tr].mean()             # "balanced" weight ≈ 49
plain, weighted = fit(X[tr], y[tr]), fit(X[tr], y[tr], wpos)
for name, m in [("plain", plain), ("weighted", weighted)]:
    p = m(X[te])
    print(f"{name:8s} AUC={auc(p, y[te]):.3f}  mean p={p.mean():.3f}  true rate={y[te].mean():.3f}")
pw = weighted(X[te]); q = pw / (pw + (1 - pw) * wpos)
print("weighted, corrected mean p =", q.mean().round(3))
for t in (0.5, 0.1, 0.05):
    f = plain(X[te]) >= t
    print(f"plain t={t}: flagged={f.sum()} recall={(f & (y[te]==1)).sum()/y[te].sum():.2f}")

What to look for. Both models have almost the same AUC — weighting didn't improve ranking. The weighted model's mean predicted probability is many times the true rate; the correction brings it back. The plain model flags few cases at 0.5, and lowering its threshold recovers recall with no retraining.

Work it out

Fraud runs at 1%. You undersample negatives to train on a balanced 1:1 set. The model now outputs 0.5 for a transaction. What is the actual probability that it is fraud? And what else changes?

Answer

About 1%, not 50%.

In the original data there are 99 negatives per positive. To reach 1:1 you kept a fraction w = 1/99 of negatives, which multiplied the odds of fraud by 99 in the data the model saw. Undo it on the odds scale: model odds at p = 0.5 are 1.0; true odds = 1.0 / 99 = 0.0101; p = 0.0101 / 1.0101 ≈ 1.0%. Equivalently, q = p / (p + (1 − p)/w) = 0.5 / (0.5 + 0.5 × 99) = 0.01.

Why it matters: the ranking is unaffected, so if you only need a top-N review queue, undersampling costs you little. But the moment anything computes expected value — p × loss for a decline decision, an alert budget, a forecast of fraud volume — a 50× inflated probability produces 50× wrong decisions. Either apply the correction, or recalibrate on a held-out set at the true prevalence.

What else changes: the validation set used to choose the threshold must be at the true 1% prevalence, not the balanced one, or precision will look far better than production. The same probability warning applies to class weights and to SMOTE, and SMOTE must be applied inside each cross-validation fold or synthetic neighbours of validation points leak into training.
