A team builds a model to predict which hospital patients will be readmitted within 30 days. Under cross-validation it scores an AUC of 0.94 — far better than anything in the published literature. Leadership is thrilled; the model is deployed at a partner hospital, where it scores 0.71. No code changed. What changed is that, at the partner hospital, the model was finally being evaluated the way it would be used: on new patients, with only the information available at the moment of discharge. The 0.94 was never real. Some of each patient's visits sat in the training folds while others sat in the validation folds, so the model had learned to recognise patients; and one feature, a rolling count of visits, quietly included visits that happened after the prediction date.

This is data leakage: information that won't exist at prediction time finding its way into training or evaluation. It is the single most common reason a model looks excellent offline and disappoints in production, and it isn't limited to industry. In 2022, Kapoor and Narayanan surveyed the scientific literature and found leakage-driven errors in 329 papers across 17 fields — this lesson's public case — including an entire research area where the headline claim, that complex machine-learning models beat logistic regression, vanished once the leakage was fixed.

The defence is a discipline of evaluation: holding data back and treating it exactly like future data; choosing a split that matches how the model will be used (random, stratified, by group, by time); putting every data-dependent step inside the split; and knowing how noisy the resulting number is. Interviewers probe this hard because it separates engineers whose models survive contact with production from those whose don't. "Validation 0.94, production 0.71 — find the bug" is one of the most common ML interview questions there is.

This lesson builds that discipline: train/validation/test and why each exists; k-fold cross-validation with fold scores worked through; stratified, grouped, time-based and nested variants with diagrams; how big a test set must be; leakage in its common forms, each with a concrete example (including target encoding computed by hand); pipelines as the structural fix; validation-set overfitting and benchmark contamination in LLMs; and the reproducibility study that found leakage across science.

The golden rule

A model's score on the data it trained on says little about how it will do on new data — it may simply have memorised (ml2). So we hold back data the model never sees during training and measure on that. The rule that everything in this lesson follows from: evaluation data must be treated exactly like future, unseen data. Not only must the model not train on it; no decision, no preprocessing statistic and no feature may be influenced by it either.

An analogy: exam papers

A teacher who wants to know whether students understand the material sets an exam with questions the students haven't seen. If the students have seen the exact questions — or if the teacher adjusts the course after seeing which questions they got wrong, then re-sets the same exam — the marks go up without understanding going up. Leakage is any way that exam questions reach the students; validation-set overfitting is re-setting the same exam after adjusting the course. The analogy breaks in one instructive place: machine-learning leakage is usually accidental and invisible, like a clue printed faintly on the back of the exam paper, which is why it needs systematic checks rather than good intentions.

Train, validation, test

  • Training set (for example 70%): fit the model's parameters.
  • Validation set (15%): compare models and choose hyperparameters such as tree depth, regularisation strength or the decision threshold.
  • Test set (15%): used once, at the very end, for an honest estimate. If you keep tweaking after looking at the test score, it stops being honest.

Cross-validation

With limited data, one validation split is noisy — you might get a lucky or unlucky split. k-fold cross-validation divides the data into k equal parts called folds, trains on k − 1 of them, validates on the remaining one, and repeats k times so each fold is the validation set once. Average the k scores and look at their spread.

What leakage looks like

Leakage is harder to spot than overfitting because nothing looks wrong: the code runs, the training curve is healthy, the validation score is high. The problem is in what the model was allowed to see. Three classic examples, each of which makes offline scores look better than reality:

  • A feature from the future. A hospital readmission model uses "length of the discharge summary" — written after the stay and correlated with how sick the patient was.
  • Preprocessing that saw the test data. Normalising features with the mean and standard deviation of the whole dataset before splitting: the training process has "seen" test statistics.
  • The same entity on both sides. One customer's transactions in both training and test, so the model recognises the customer rather than learning general patterns.

In each case the fix is the same question asked in advance: when this model is used for real, what will it know, and about whom? The split and the features must reproduce exactly that situation.

1. Reading cross-validation results

Take the five fold scores above: AUC 0.81, 0.84, 0.79, 0.86, 0.80. The mean is 4.10 / 5 = 0.82. The deviations from the mean are −0.01, +0.02, −0.03, +0.04, −0.02; their squares sum to 0.0034, and dividing by k − 1 = 4 gives a variance of 0.00085, so the standard deviation across folds is 0.029. Two readings follow. First, any single split could have reported anything from 0.79 to 0.86 — a team that happened to get fold 4 as its only validation set would have overstated performance by 0.04. Second, a competing model that scores 0.83 is not clearly better; differences smaller than the fold-to-fold spread need more careful comparison (repeated CV with different shuffles, or a paired comparison on the same folds, where each model is evaluated on exactly the same splits and you look at the per-fold differences).

After cross-validation has chosen a model and its settings, the usual final step is to retrain on all the training data with those settings — the k fold models were only for estimation. The test set, untouched throughout, then gives one final number.

Choosing k is a trade-off. Each fold's model trains on (k − 1)/k of the data, so small k (say 2) trains on half the data and underestimates what a full-data model would achieve. Large k trains on almost all the data but costs k model fits, and the fold models are nearly identical, so their scores are highly correlated and the average's variance doesn't shrink as much as you'd hope. Five or ten folds are the standard compromise; leave-one-out (k = n) is reserved for tiny datasets.

2. Choosing the split to match deployment

The most important question in evaluation is: what will the model see when it's used, and does my split imitate that?

Stratified k-fold keeps each class's proportion the same in every fold. With 2% positives and 1,000 rows, a random fold of 200 might contain 1 positive or 8; stratification guarantees 4. Use it whenever classes are imbalanced (ml8).

Group k-fold keeps all rows belonging to one entity — a patient, user, device, document, or all augmented crops of one image — in the same fold. If a model will be applied to new patients, a random split that puts the same patient's visits on both sides measures something easier: predicting a patient it has already seen. In the opening story, 40,000 rows covered 12,000 patients (about 3.3 visits each), so most validation patients had other visits in training. The model learned patient-specific quirks, and the random split rewarded it.

Time-based splits train on the past and validate on the future. If a model will forecast next month, a shuffled split trains on the future to predict the past: trends, seasonality and the similarity of neighbouring days let it interpolate rather than forecast. The standard scheme is an expanding window — train on January to June, validate on July; train on January to July, validate on August; and so on — or a sliding window of fixed length when old data is less relevant.

Leave a gap between the end of training and the start of validation at least as long as the label delay or the longest feature window. If a label ("churned within 30 days") takes 30 days to become known, training rows from the last 30 days before the cutoff have labels that overlap the validation period. Real problems often need several properties at once — grouped by user, ordered in time, stratified for rare labels — and the split then has to be designed explicitly rather than taken from a library default.

3. Nested cross-validation

If you use cross-validation to choose hyperparameters — say, the best of 50 regularisation strengths — the best CV score is optimistic, because you picked the configuration that happened to do best on those particular folds, noise included. Nested cross-validation puts the whole tuning procedure inside an outer loop: for each outer fold, run an inner cross-validation on the outer training part to pick the hyperparameters, train with them, and score on the outer validation fold, which played no part in the choice. The outer scores estimate the performance of the procedure "tune then train". It costs (outer × inner × configurations) fits, so it's used when data is small and an honest estimate matters — typical in medical and scientific studies. With plenty of data, a separate untouched test set does the same job more cheaply.

4. How big must the test set be?

A metric computed on n examples is an estimate with sampling error. For a proportion p — accuracy, recall, precision — the standard error is √(p(1 − p)/n), and a 95% confidence interval is roughly ±1.96 standard errors.

  • 95% accuracy on 1,000 examples: √(0.95 × 0.05 / 1,000) = 0.0069 → about ±1.4 points.
  • The same on 10,000 examples: 0.0022 → about ±0.4 points.
  • Recall of 0.8 measured on 50 positives: √(0.8 × 0.2 / 50) = 0.057 → about ±11 points.

So "the new model improves accuracy from 94.8% to 95.3%" on 1,000 test examples means nothing, and for rare classes the number of positives, not the total, sets the precision of the estimate. Size the test set so its interval is narrower than the smallest difference you'd act on.

5. Leakage, ranked by how often it bites

(a) Preprocessing before splitting. Any step that learns from data — scaling (means and standard deviations), imputation (fill values), encoding (category vocabularies, target encodings), feature selection, dimensionality reduction (ml13), resampling (ml8) — must be fitted on training data only. Fitted on everything, it carries information from validation and test data into training. Sometimes the effect is small (a mean shifted slightly); sometimes it is catastrophic. The textbook demonstration comes from The Elements of Statistical Learning (section 7.10.2): with far more features than rows and labels that are pure noise, selecting the features most correlated with the label on the whole dataset and then cross-validating a classifier on those features reports low error, although no model can do better than chance. Selecting inside each fold gives the honest answer: about 50%.

(b) Target leakage. A feature that exists or changes only because the outcome happened. "Refund amount" in a churn model; "ICU admission date" in a severity model; "account closed flag" in a default model; "number of support calls about cancellation". The test is to ask, for each feature: at the moment we'd make this prediction, would this value already be known, and would it have this value?

(c) Temporal leakage. Aggregates computed over windows that include the prediction moment or later: a "30-day rolling average of visits" computed on the whole table, or "customer's average order value" using all orders ever, including future ones. Fix: compute every feature point-in-time — as of the prediction timestamp — which is what feature stores' point-in-time joins provide (ml14).

(d) Duplicates and entities across the split. Exact duplicates (the same row collected twice), near-duplicates (the same document with a different footer, the same image resized) and shared entities (group k-fold above). For text and images, near-duplicate detection before splitting is standard; for LLMs this becomes benchmark contamination (deep).

(e) Target encoding computed in-sample. Replacing a category with the mean label of its rows is powerful, but computed on the rows being trained on, it leaks each row's own label. Worked: the city "Leeds" appears in two training rows, both churners. Its in-sample encoding is 1.0, so the model learns "encoding 1.0 → churn" — which is just the label reflected back. A category that appears once always gets exactly its own label. The fix is out-of-fold encoding: each row's encoding is computed from the other folds only, plus smoothing towards the global rate for rare categories, e.g. (sum of labels + m × global rate) / (count + m) with m = 10. For Leeds with 2 churners, a global rate of 0.2 and m = 10: (2 + 10 × 0.2) / (2 + 10) = 0.33 instead of 1.0.

(f) Test-set reuse. Covered in section 7 — the slowest, most invisible leak.

6. Pipelines: the structural fix

The reliable way to avoid preprocessing leakage is structural: bundle every data-dependent step with the model into a single pipeline object, and cross-validate the pipeline. Each fold then refits the scaler, encoder, selector and model on its own training part and only applies them to its validation part. In scikit-learn this is make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SelectKBest(k=20), LogisticRegression()) passed to cross_val_score with the appropriate splitter (StratifiedKFold, GroupKFold with a groups array, or TimeSeriesSplit). The same pipeline object is what gets deployed, which also prevents the training/serving mismatch of ml14.

7. Validation-set overfitting

Every time you look at a validation or test score and change something in response, some information about that set flows into your choices. One look is harmless. Two hundred experiments, each kept or discarded based on the same set, add up to an optimisation procedure against that set — including its noise. Picture 200 candidate models that are all truly identical at 80% accuracy, evaluated on 1,000 examples: their measured scores scatter with a standard error of about 1.3 points, so the best of 200 will typically measure around 83–84% — a 3–4 point "improvement" that is pure selection. Defences: a final holdout touched once; refreshing test sets periodically; confidence intervals and paired comparisons for decisions; and, in competitions, the public/private leaderboard split, whose gap is exactly this effect made visible.

8. Putting it together: an evaluation plan

For the readmission model, an honest plan reads like this. Deployment: at discharge, predict whether a new patient returns within 30 days, using only data recorded up to discharge. Features: every aggregate computed as of the discharge timestamp from an event log, never from current-state tables. Split: by time (train on discharges up to a cutoff, validate on later ones, with a 30-day gap so labels mature) and by patient (no patient in both). Preprocessing: imputation, scaling and encodings in a pipeline, fitted per fold. Tuning: hyperparameters chosen on the validation periods; the last three months reserved as a test set, used once. Reporting: AUC and precision at the operating threshold with bootstrap intervals, plus a separate score on a held-out hospital to measure shift. Each line corresponds to one of the failure modes above, and writing the plan down before training is the cheapest leakage defence there is.

Point-in-time correctness in real data systems

Production data warehouses store the current state of things: today's customer tier, today's account status, the latest address. A training table built by joining events to these tables gives every past event today's values — a customer who became "premium" last week appears premium in all their historical rows, including those that preceded (and perhaps caused) the upgrade. Point-in-time joins look up the value as of each event's timestamp, which requires keeping history (slowly changing dimensions, event logs). Feature stores exist largely to make this automatic. Getting it wrong is the most common form of temporal leakage in industry, and it is invisible in the code, because the join looks correct.

Leakage through proxies and artefacts

Leakage doesn't need an obviously forbidden feature. Row identifiers assigned in order of collection can correlate with the label if positives were collected in a batch; file names or folder structure can encode the class; images of one class may all come from one hospital's scanner, so the model learns the scanner. A useful diagnostic is adversarial validation: train a classifier to distinguish training rows from test rows. If it succeeds well above chance, the sets differ systematically, and its most important features point at the difference — sometimes a leak, sometimes genuine shift that the evaluation should reflect.

Distribution shift is not leakage, but CV can't see it

Cross-validation estimates performance on the distribution the data came from. Deployment often differs: new regions, later time periods, new products, a different hospital. That gap is not leakage — nothing illegitimate reached training — but it's another reason offline scores exceed production ones. Estimate it deliberately with shifted holdouts: hold out a whole region, the latest months, or an entire hospital, and compare with in-distribution CV. The difference is a robustness measurement you can report and track.

Benchmark contamination in LLMs

Large language models are trained on web-scale corpora that may contain the questions and answers of public benchmarks, so a high benchmark score can reflect memorisation — leakage at internet scale, and hard to rule out because training data is often undisclosed. Mitigations include searching training data for n-gram overlaps with test items, building evaluation sets privately or after the model's training cutoff, comparing performance on original items with paraphrased or perturbed versions (memorisation shows as a drop on rewrites), and tracking performance on genuinely new material. For teams fine-tuning models, the same discipline applies locally: deduplicate evaluation examples against fine-tuning data, and keep a private, versioned evaluation set that never enters any training mix.

Adaptive data analysis and the reusable holdout

The statistics of validation-set overfitting has been studied formally as adaptive data analysis: when each analysis depends on previous results from the same data, classical confidence intervals no longer hold. One proposed defence, the reusable holdout (Dwork et al.), answers queries against the holdout only when they differ noticeably from the training estimate, and adds noise to its answers, limiting how much information each query reveals. In practice, most teams approximate it with discipline: pre-registered comparisons, few looks at the final test set, and fresh data for final decisions.

Leakage in feature engineering at scale

In large feature pipelines, leakage hides in shared infrastructure. A feature computed by another team may have been backfilled — recomputed historically with a later version of the logic or with data that arrived late — so the historical values used in training differ from what the live system would have produced at the time. Label-derived features ("number of past fraud reports for this merchant") must exclude the current event and anything reported after it. And features built from model outputs (another model's score) can leak if that model was trained on data that includes your validation period. Lineage tracking — recording for each feature which data and which time window produced it — is the systematic defence.

When cross-validation estimates are biased in other ways

Each fold model trains on less data than the final model, so CV slightly underestimates final performance (the learning-curve effect of ml2). Fold scores are correlated, so the naive standard error of the mean understates uncertainty; repeated CV with different shuffles and corrected tests help. And for very small datasets, the variance of the CV estimate can be as large as the differences being compared, which argues for simpler models and wider intervals rather than finer tuning.

The public case: leakage across science

In 2022, Sayash Kapoor and Arvind Narayanan at Princeton examined how machine learning was being used in scientific research and asked how often reported results were inflated by leakage. Surveying published reviews and their own analysis, they found 17 fields where leakage-driven errors had been identified, collectively affecting 329 papers, in some cases invalidating the papers' main conclusions. They proposed a taxonomy of 8 types of leakage, ranging from textbook errors — no separate test set, preprocessing on train and test together, feature selection before splitting, duplicates — to subtle ones: features that are illegitimate proxies for the outcome, and test sets drawn from a different distribution than the claim concerns, including temporal leakage.

Their case study is striking. In civil-war prediction, a line of papers had reported that complex machine-learning models (random forests, boosting) dramatically outperformed the logistic-regression models political scientists had used for decades. Kapoor and Narayanan reproduced them and found that all the papers claiming superior performance of complex models failed to reproduce because of data leakage — one imputed missing values on the training and test data together, two reused a dataset that had been imputed that way, and one used proxies for the outcome as features — and that once the leakage was fixed, the complex models did not perform substantively better than logistic regression. The headline claim of a whole research strand was an artefact of evaluation. They propose "model info sheets" — structured reporting of how the data was split and what each feature means at prediction time — so reviewers can catch leakage before publication.

For engineers the lesson is the same as the readmission story: an evaluation is only as good as its imitation of deployment. The civil-war models would have been "deployed" on future years; the only honest evaluation trained on the past and tested on later years with features available at the time.

How leakage shows up in production

A dominant feature. One feature carries most of the model's importance and its relationship with the label is almost deterministic. Ask how it's generated and when it's recorded before celebrating.

Offline–online gap. The model's online performance (or its first A/B test) is far below offline. Compare the features the model received in production with the same features recomputed offline for the same requests: differences point to temporal leakage or training/serving skew (ml14).

Performance that decays immediately. A model that is best on the first day and falls quickly may have been evaluated on a period too similar to training — a random split of time-ordered data.

An evaluation checklist

  1. Write down what the model will know at prediction time and what it will be asked to predict; build every feature point-in-time.
  2. Choose the split that imitates deployment: by time for forecasting, by group for new entities, stratified for rare labels.
  3. Put all data-dependent preprocessing inside a pipeline, fitted per fold.
  4. Deduplicate across splits, including near-duplicates.
  5. Tune on validation or inner CV only; touch the test set once; report intervals.
  6. Run a shifted holdout (latest period, new region) to estimate robustness.
  7. Investigate any score that seems too good, starting with the most important features.

Goal: reproduce the classic leak — feature selection before cross-validation on pure noise — and see the honest answer when selection happens inside each fold. NumPy only; written for Python 3.12.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

n, p, k = 50, 5000, 20                      # 50 rows, 5,000 noise features, keep 20
X = rng.normal(size=(n, p)); y = rng.integers(0, 2, n)   # labels are pure noise

def top_k(X, y, k):                          # features most correlated with the label
    Xc = X - X.mean(0); yc = y - y.mean()
    corr = np.abs(Xc.T @ yc) / (np.linalg.norm(Xc, axis=0) * np.linalg.norm(yc) + 1e-12)
    return np.argsort(corr)[-k:]

def centroid_acc(Xtr, ytr, Xva, yva):        # nearest class-mean classifier
    m0, m1 = Xtr[ytr == 0].mean(0), Xtr[ytr == 1].mean(0)
    pred = (np.linalg.norm(Xva - m1, axis=1) < np.linalg.norm(Xva - m0, axis=1)).astype(int)
    return np.mean(pred == yva)

folds = np.arange(n) % 5; rng.shuffle(folds)
wrong_feats = top_k(X, y, k)                 # WRONG: selected using all rows
wrong, right = [], []
for f in range(5):
    tr, va = folds != f, folds == f
    wrong.append(centroid_acc(X[tr][:, wrong_feats], y[tr], X[va][:, wrong_feats], y[va]))
    feats = top_k(X[tr], y[tr], k)           # RIGHT: selected inside the fold
    right.append(centroid_acc(X[tr][:, feats], y[tr], X[va][:, feats], y[va]))
print(f"selection before CV: {np.mean(wrong):.2f}   selection inside CV: {np.mean(right):.2f}")

What to look for. The labels are coin flips, so no model can beat about 50%. Selecting features on all rows reports far higher accuracy, because the 20 features were chosen for correlating with the validation labels too; selecting inside each fold gives roughly chance. Try k = 200 or n = 500 and watch how the gap changes.

Work it out

A readmission model scores 0.94 AUC under 5-fold CV and 0.71 on a held-out hospital. The dataset has 40,000 rows covering 12,000 patients, and features include a 30-day rolling average of prior visits. Find the bugs, and say what you'd expect after fixing them.

Answer

At least two, and both are structural rather than statistical.

1. Group leakage. 40,000 rows over 12,000 patients means about 3.3 visits each. A random 5-fold split puts the same patient in training and validation, so the model learns the patient, not the condition. Fix: GroupKFold on patient id. Expect CV to drop sharply — that drop is the measurement becoming honest, not a regression.

2. Temporal leakage. A "30-day rolling average of prior visits" computed over the whole table includes visits that happen after the prediction point. Fix: recompute as a point-in-time aggregate using only data available at discharge, and split by time as well as by group, with a gap of at least 30 days for the readmission label to mature.

Then check the remaining gap. If CV drops to, say, 0.76 and the other hospital still scores 0.71, the remaining difference is distribution shift between hospitals — different coding practices, case mix, equipment — which is a generalisation problem, not a leak. Measure it by holding out whole hospitals in validation, and address it with more diverse training data or per-site recalibration. Fix the leaks first, or you will misattribute the cause.
