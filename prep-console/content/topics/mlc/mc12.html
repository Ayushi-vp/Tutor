Backpropagation (mc11) tells you, for every one of a model's parameters, which way is downhill. It does not tell you how far to step. That decision, made billions of times during a training run, belongs to the optimiser, and it is the difference between a model that trains in a day and one that never trains at all. Plain gradient descent takes a step proportional to the gradient with one global learning rate. Real loss surfaces punish that: in some directions the surface is steep and a large step overshoots, in others it is nearly flat and a small step goes nowhere, and different layers produce gradients that differ in size by orders of magnitude. Momentum, Adam and AdamW are the standard answers, and AdamW with a warmup-and-cosine learning-rate schedule trains almost every large language model you have heard of.

Interviewers ask candidates to implement them because the update rules are short and every line encodes a decision that matters. Can you explain why momentum speeds progress along a valley floor while damping oscillation across it? Can you say why Adam divides by the square root of a running average of squared gradients, and why it needs a "bias correction" in its first steps (without which its first step is more than three times too large)? Do you know why "weight decay" in Adam is not the same as L2 regularisation, and that the difference was large enough to earn its own paper and optimiser, AdamW? Can you write the schedule, the gradient clipping and the state handling that wrap the optimiser in a real training loop?

There are practical stakes too. The optimiser's state, two numbers per parameter for Adam, is twice the size of the model itself; for a 7-billion-parameter model that is 56 GB in float32, which is why sharding optimizer state is a core technique in large-scale training. Keep that state in float16 to save memory and the default ε of 10⁻⁸ becomes exactly zero, small squared gradients vanish, and the update divides by zero.

The lesson works both momentum and Adam by hand for two steps on a simple quadratic, shows bias correction and the AdamW-versus-L2 difference with real numbers, then implements the optimisers element by element and vectorised, checks them against PyTorch's torch.optim.SGD, Adam and AdamW to twelve decimal places, compares them on an ill-conditioned quadratic, and builds the warmup-cosine schedule and global-norm clipping. It closes with memory, mixed precision, β₂ choices for large models and how to read a loss spike.

Imagine walking down a long, narrow, slightly tilted valley in fog, able to feel only the slope under your feet. The steepest direction points mostly toward the valley wall, not along the valley, so if you always step straight downhill you zig-zag from wall to wall and make slow progress toward the bottom. That is plain gradient descent on an ill-conditioned problem, one where the curvature differs a lot between directions (mc1 showed it for linear regression).

Momentum gives the walker inertia. Instead of stepping in the direction of the current slope, you keep a running velocity that adds each new slope to a fraction (say 90%) of the previous velocity. The across-valley slopes alternate left and right and mostly cancel in the velocity; the along-valley slopes always point the same way and add up, so you build speed along the floor. With a momentum of 0.9 the velocity can grow to about ten times a single step in a consistent direction.

Adam adds a second idea: give every parameter its own step size. It keeps, for each parameter, a running average of its gradient (the first moment, like momentum) and a running average of its squared gradient (the second moment, a measure of how large that parameter's gradients typically are). The update divides the first by the square root of the second. A parameter whose gradients are always huge gets them divided by a huge number; one whose gradients are tiny gets them divided by a tiny number. The result is that every parameter moves by roughly the learning rate per step, whatever the scale of its gradients, so one learning rate suits embeddings, attention weights and layer norms alike.

Both averages start at zero, which makes their early values too small, especially the second moment, which averages over roughly the last thousand steps and so starts out mostly zero. Adam's bias correction divides each average by the fraction of its weight that has actually been filled in, which exactly undoes the problem. And AdamW fixes how regularisation interacts with all this: shrinking weights toward zero ("weight decay") should be applied to the weights directly, not mixed into the gradient where Adam's per-parameter scaling would distort it.

In code all of this is small. An optimiser is an object that owns some per-parameter memory (a velocity, or two moment estimates, plus a step counter) and has one method, step, that reads the gradients and updates the parameters in place. Everything interesting is in the few arithmetic lines inside step, which is exactly why interviewers ask for them.

Around the optimiser sits a schedule that changes the learning rate over training: a short warmup from small to full size, so early steps do not blow up while statistics are unreliable, then a slow decay (often a half-cosine curve) so the final steps settle into a minimum instead of rattling around it. Where the walking picture breaks: real loss surfaces have millions of dimensions, the slope you feel is noisy (each step sees only one mini-batch), and the valley itself moves as the model learns. The update rules were designed for exactly that noise.

1. The problem as it is usually asked

"Implement SGD with momentum, Adam and AdamW as classes with a step(grads) method that updates parameters in place, plus a learning-rate schedule with linear warmup and cosine decay. Show they work." Clarify: the momentum convention (PyTorch's v = μv + g or the "classical" v = μv − lr·g; they differ when the learning rate changes); whether Nesterov is wanted; the exact AdamW ordering (decay before or after the Adam step); and how state is stored. Then outline: per-parameter state dictionaries, in-place updates, the schedule as a pure function of the step, tests by hand and against torch.optim.

2. The maths in plain words

Momentum. Keep a velocity per parameter, initially zero. Each step, multiply the velocity by μ (0.9 is typical) and add the new gradient; then move the parameter by minus the learning rate times the velocity. Unrolled, the velocity is g_t + μg_{t−1} + μ²g_{t−2} + …, a weighted sum of all past gradients with geometrically fading weights. If the gradient is constant, the velocity approaches g/(1 − μ), ten times g for μ = 0.9. Nesterov momentum uses g + μv as the update instead of v, which amounts to looking one momentum step ahead and damps overshoot.

Adam. Two running averages per parameter. The first, m, is an exponential moving average of the gradient with decay β₁ = 0.9 (roughly the last ten gradients). The second, v, is an exponential moving average of the squared gradient with β₂ = 0.999 (roughly the last thousand). The step is lr × m / (√v + ε): the average direction divided by the typical magnitude, so its size is about lr when gradients are consistent and smaller when they are noisy (noisy gradients have a large √v relative to |m|). Because both averages start at zero, after t steps they hold only a fraction 1 − βᵗ of the weight they should; dividing by that fraction is the bias correction. ε, 10⁻⁸, keeps the division safe if v is zero.

Weight decay. Regularisation pulls weights toward zero. With plain SGD, adding ½λ‖w‖² to the loss (L2) adds λw to the gradient, which is identical to multiplying the weights by (1 − lr·λ) each step. In Adam it is not identical, because the λw term passes through m and v and gets divided by √v: weights with large gradients are barely decayed. AdamW instead applies w ← w − lr·λ·w directly, outside the adaptive part.

3. The equations

SGD + momentum (PyTorch convention)      v ← μ·v + (g + λw)        w ← w − lr·v
  Nesterov variant                       update = (g + λw) + μ·v   w ← w − lr·update

Adam (t counts from 1)                   m ← β₁m + (1 − β₁)g
                                         v ← β₂v + (1 − β₂)g²
                                         m̂ = m/(1 − β₁ᵗ),   v̂ = v/(1 − β₂ᵗ)
                                         w ← w − lr · m̂ / (√v̂ + ε)
  Adam + L2                              g ← g + λw   (before the moments)
  AdamW (as in torch.optim.AdamW)        w ← w − lr·λ·w   then the Adam step

warmup + cosine                          lr(s) = base·(s + 1)/W                         s < W
                                         lr(s) = min + ½(base − min)(1 + cos(π·(s − W)/(T − W)))   s ≥ W

global-norm clipping                     n = √(Σ_tensors ‖g‖²);   if n > c:  g ← g · c/n

Why does bias correction divide by exactly 1 − βᵗ? If every gradient were the same value g, then after t steps m = (1 − β₁)(g + β₁g + … + β₁^(t−1)g) = (1 − β₁ᵗ)g, a geometric series. Dividing by 1 − β₁ᵗ recovers g exactly. The same holds for v with β₂, and because β₂ is so close to 1, 1 − β₂ᵗ is tiny early on (0.001 at t = 1), which is why the uncorrected v is so badly biased.

4. A worked example by hand

Minimise f(w) = 0.25w², whose gradient is g = 0.5w, starting from w = 1 with learning rate 0.1.

Momentum, μ = 0.9. Step 1: g = 0.5, v = 0.9·0 + 0.5 = 0.5, w = 1 − 0.1·0.5 = 0.95. Step 2: g = 0.475, v = 0.9·0.5 + 0.475 = 0.925, w = 0.95 − 0.0925 = 0.8575. The second step is almost twice the first even though the gradient shrank: the velocity remembers.

Adam, β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999. Step 1: g = 0.5; m = 0.1·0.5 = 0.05; v = 0.001·0.25 = 0.00025. Bias-corrected: m̂ = 0.05/0.1 = 0.5, v̂ = 0.00025/0.001 = 0.25. Step size 0.1 × 0.5/√0.25 = 0.1 × 0.5/0.5 = 0.1, so w = 0.9. The first step is exactly lr in size, whatever the gradient's magnitude: m̂/√v̂ is g/|g|. Without bias correction the step would have been 0.1 × 0.05/√0.00025 = 0.1 × 0.05/0.0158 = 0.316, more than three times too large, because 1 − β₂ = 0.001 shrank v ten times more than 1 − β₁ = 0.1 shrank m, and √ turns that into a factor of 3.16.

Step 2: g = 0.45; m = 0.9·0.05 + 0.1·0.45 = 0.09; v = 0.999·0.00025 + 0.001·0.2025 = 0.00045225. Bias-corrected with t = 2: m̂ = 0.09/(1 − 0.81) = 0.4737, v̂ = 0.00045225/(1 − 0.998001) = 0.2262, √v̂ = 0.4756. Step 0.1 × 0.4737/0.4756 = 0.0996, w = 0.80041.

Weight decay λ = 0.1, first step only. AdamW first shrinks the weight, w = 1 − 0.1·0.1·1 = 0.99, then takes the unit-size Adam step: 0.99 − 0.1 = 0.89. Adam + L2 instead uses the gradient 0.5 + 0.1·1 = 0.6; but the first Adam step has size lr whatever the gradient, so w = 0.9, exactly as if there were no decay at all. The adaptive normalisation swallowed the regulariser. Over many steps Adam + L2 does decay the weights a little, but by an amount that depends on each parameter's gradient history, which is the inconsistency AdamW removes. The first block asserts every number.

import math
import time
import numpy as np

# The hand-worked example: minimise f(w) = 0.25·w², so the gradient is g = 0.5·w, from w = 1, lr = 0.1.
grad = lambda w: 0.5 * w

# SGD with momentum 0.9 (PyTorch form: v = μv + g, w -= lr·v).
w, v = 1.0, 0.0
g = grad(w); v = 0.9 * v + g; w -= 0.1 * v                 # g 0.5, v 0.5, w 0.95
assert (g, v) == (0.5, 0.5) and math.isclose(w, 0.95)
g = grad(w); v = 0.9 * v + g; w -= 0.1 * v                 # g 0.475, v 0.925, w 0.8575
assert math.isclose(g, 0.475) and math.isclose(v, 0.925) and math.isclose(w, 0.8575)

# Adam (β1 0.9, β2 0.999, ε 1e-8), two steps.
w, m, s = 1.0, 0.0, 0.0
b1, b2, eps, lr = 0.9, 0.999, 1e-8, 0.1
g = grad(w)                                                  # 0.5
m = b1 * m + (1 - b1) * g; s = b2 * s + (1 - b2) * g * g     # m 0.05, s 0.00025
m_hat, s_hat = m / (1 - b1), s / (1 - b2)                    # 0.5 and 0.25: bias corrected
assert math.isclose(m, 0.05) and math.isclose(s, 0.00025)
assert math.isclose(m_hat, 0.5) and math.isclose(s_hat, 0.25)
w -= lr * m_hat / (math.sqrt(s_hat) + eps)                   # step of exactly lr: 0.1
assert math.isclose(w, 0.9, rel_tol=1e-7)
# Without bias correction the first step would be lr·0.05/sqrt(0.00025) = 0.316, 3.16 times too big.
assert math.isclose(lr * m / math.sqrt(s), 0.1 / math.sqrt(0.1), rel_tol=1e-9)
g = grad(w)                                                  # 0.45
m = b1 * m + (1 - b1) * g; s = b2 * s + (1 - b2) * g * g     # m 0.09, s 0.00045225
m_hat, s_hat = m / (1 - b1 ** 2), s / (1 - b2 ** 2)          # 0.47368, 0.22624
assert math.isclose(m, 0.09, rel_tol=1e-7) and math.isclose(s, 0.00045225, rel_tol=1e-7)
w -= lr * m_hat / (math.sqrt(s_hat) + eps)
assert math.isclose(w, 0.80041, abs_tol=1e-5)

# Weight decay 0.1 on the first step: AdamW shrinks w directly; Adam + L2 adds 0.1·w to g,
# and Adam's normalisation then cancels it completely on step one.
w_adamw = (1.0 - lr * 0.1 * 1.0) - lr * 1.0                  # decay, then the unit-size Adam step
assert math.isclose(w_adamw, 0.89)
g_l2 = grad(1.0) + 0.1 * 1.0                                 # 0.6
m1, s1 = (1 - b1) * g_l2, (1 - b2) * g_l2 ** 2
w_l2 = 1.0 - lr * (m1 / (1 - b1)) / (math.sqrt(s1 / (1 - b2)) + eps)
assert math.isclose(w_l2, 0.9, rel_tol=1e-7)                 # identical to no decay at all

Plain SGD shrinks w by a constant 5% per step, because the gradient shrinks with w: steady and slow. Momentum accelerates (its steps grow from 0.05 to 0.16), overshoots zero at step 8 and swings to −0.31; on this one-dimensional bowl it will oscillate around the minimum with slowly decaying amplitude, which is the cost of inertia. Choosing μ is choosing how much overshoot to accept in exchange for speed. Adam moves almost exactly 0.1 per step regardless of the gradient's size, the sign-descent behaviour from the deep section, and so marches straight to the minimum, then rattles around it with steps of about lr (after 40 steps it sits at 0.10). That last point is why Adam needs a decaying learning rate to settle: the step size does not shrink on its own as the gradient vanishes, because the normalisation cancels the gradient's magnitude.

5. The naive implementation

Adam written element by element over Python lists, one parameter at a time:

def adam_naive(w, grads_seq, lr=0.1, b1=0.9, b2=0.999, eps=1e-8):
    """Adam written element by element with Python lists, one step per gradient in grads_seq."""
    w = list(w); m = [0.0] * len(w); s = [0.0] * len(w)
    for t, g in enumerate(grads_seq, start=1):
        for i in range(len(w)):
            m[i] = b1 * m[i] + (1 - b1) * g[i]
            s[i] = b2 * s[i] + (1 - b2) * g[i] * g[i]
            m_hat = m[i] / (1 - b1 ** t)
            s_hat = s[i] / (1 - b2 ** t)
            w[i] -= lr * m_hat / (math.sqrt(s_hat) + eps)
    return w

It makes the per-parameter nature of Adam explicit: nothing couples one parameter's update to another's. That is also why the vectorised version is trivial, every operation is element-wise. On 200,000 parameters one step of the loop version took 330 ms against 5.8 ms vectorised; a real model has billions of parameters and fused GPU kernels apply the update in one pass over memory.

6. The vectorised optimisers and the schedule

class SGD:
    def __init__(self, params, lr=0.01, momentum=0.0, nesterov=False, weight_decay=0.0):
        self.params, self.lr, self.mu, self.nesterov, self.wd = params, lr, momentum, nesterov, weight_decay
        self.v = {k: np.zeros_like(p) for k, p in params.items()}

    def step(self, grads):
        for k, p in self.params.items():
            g = grads[k] + self.wd * p                       # L2 through the gradient
            self.v[k] = self.mu * self.v[k] + g
            update = g + self.mu * self.v[k] if self.nesterov else self.v[k]
            p -= self.lr * update                            # in place: callers keep their references

class Adam:
    def __init__(self, params, lr=1e-3, betas=(0.9, 0.999), eps=1e-8, weight_decay=0.0, decoupled=True):
        self.params, self.lr, (self.b1, self.b2), self.eps = params, lr, betas, eps
        self.wd, self.decoupled = weight_decay, decoupled           # decoupled=True is AdamW
        self.m = {k: np.zeros_like(p) for k, p in params.items()}
        self.v = {k: np.zeros_like(p) for k, p in params.items()}
        self.t = 0

    def step(self, grads):
        self.t += 1
        for k, p in self.params.items():
            g = grads[k]
            if self.wd and not self.decoupled:
                g = g + self.wd * p                          # Adam + L2: decay later divided by sqrt(v)
            self.m[k] = self.b1 * self.m[k] + (1 - self.b1) * g
            self.v[k] = self.b2 * self.v[k] + (1 - self.b2) * g * g
            m_hat = self.m[k] / (1 - self.b1 ** self.t)      # bias correction: both start at zero
            v_hat = self.v[k] / (1 - self.b2 ** self.t)
            if self.wd and self.decoupled:
                p -= self.lr * self.wd * p                   # AdamW: shrink the weights directly
            p -= self.lr * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + self.eps)

def lr_at(step, base_lr, warmup, total, min_lr=0.0):
    """Linear warmup to base_lr, then cosine decay to min_lr at step `total`."""
    if step < warmup:
        return base_lr * (step + 1) / warmup
    progress = min(1.0, (step - warmup) / max(1, total - warmup))
    return min_lr + 0.5 * (base_lr - min_lr) * (1 + np.cos(np.pi * progress))

Parameters as a dictionary of arrays, updated in place. p -= ... modifies the array that the model also holds, so the model sees the update without any copying. Writing p = p - ... would rebind a local name and silently leave the model unchanged, the classic NumPy training-loop bug from mc11.

State per parameter. SGD keeps one velocity array per parameter; Adam keeps two moment arrays and a shared step counter t, incremented once per step call (not once per parameter, which would make the bias correction wrong for every parameter after the first).

Weight decay variants. With decoupled=False, λw is added to the gradient before the moments: Adam + L2, which is what torch.optim.Adam(weight_decay=...) does. With decoupled=True, the weights are shrunk by lr·λ before the Adam step: AdamW, matching torch.optim.AdamW's documented algorithm, which applies θ ← θ − γλθ before computing the moments.

Nesterov. update = g + μv after updating v, which is PyTorch's formulation of Nesterov momentum.

The schedule is a pure function of the step number, which is the right design: it can be plotted, tested and resumed from a checkpoint without replaying training. Warmup rises linearly from base/W at step 0 to base at step W − 1; the cosine then falls from base to min_lr at step T.

7. Testing it

The test block checks: the classes reproduce the hand-worked trajectories (momentum 0.8575, Adam 0.80041, AdamW 0.89 versus Adam + L2 0.90); the element-by-element loop equals the vectorised Adam over 20 random gradients; and, most convincingly, five configurations (SGD with momentum, Nesterov, Adam, AdamW with λ = 0.1, Adam with L2 λ = 0.1) produce trajectories identical to PyTorch's torch.optim classes to 10⁻¹² over 25 steps of the same random gradients. Then behaviour: on a quadratic with curvatures 1 and 100, after 150 steps at lr = 0.01, plain SGD ends at norm 0.22, heavy-ball momentum at 4.2 × 10⁻⁴ and Nesterov at 1.9 × 10⁻⁴; Nesterov at lr = 0.015 diverges because its stability limit is tighter; Adam's first step has size lr for gradients scaled by 10⁻⁴, 1 or 10⁴; the schedule hits its four checkpoints and never increases after warmup; and the float16 trap below.

H = np.array([1.0, 100.0])
def run_opt(make, steps=150):

Matching torch.optim to twelve decimals pins down every convention that varies between descriptions of these algorithms: where the learning rate multiplies momentum, how Nesterov is formulated, when bias correction applies, whether ε is added inside or outside the square root (outside, after bias correction), and where AdamW applies the decay. A test against your own hand calculation alone would not catch a convention you misremember.

8. Numerical stability traps

Trap 1: ε inside or outside the root, and its size. Adam adds ε to √v̂, after bias correction. Variants that put ε inside the root, or before bias correction, behave differently early in training. ε also bounds the step: when v̂ is tiny, the step approaches lr·m̂/ε, which can be enormous. Some large-model recipes raise ε (to 10⁻⁶ or more) for that reason.

Trap 2: float16 state. np.float16(1e-8) is exactly 0, the smallest positive float16 being about 6 × 10⁻⁸. A gradient of 10⁻⁴ squares to 10⁻⁸, which also underflows to 0. With v = 0 and ε = 0 the update divides by zero and the test gets an infinite step. Keep optimizer state, and master weights, in float32.

Trap 3: the step counter. Bias correction uses t, the number of updates so far. Resetting it when resuming from a checkpoint (or incrementing it per parameter) makes early-step corrections reappear mid-training, producing a sudden large update. Save and restore t with the moments.

Trap 4: momentum with a changing learning rate. In PyTorch's convention the velocity accumulates raw gradients and the learning rate multiplies the velocity at each step, so a learning-rate drop takes effect immediately. In the "classical" convention, the velocity accumulates lr·g, so old, larger-lr contributions linger. Neither is wrong, but mixing them when porting code changes training.

Trap 5: clipping after scaling. With mixed-precision loss scaling, gradients are multiplied by a large factor during the backward pass; clipping them before unscaling clips the wrong quantity. Unscale, then clip, then step.

Trap 6: weight decay on everything. Decaying biases and normalisation gains pulls them toward zero for no benefit and can hurt; most recipes exclude them (and embeddings, sometimes) from weight decay by putting them in a separate parameter group.

Gradient accumulation and the optimiser

When a batch is too large for memory, training splits it into micro-batches, runs forward and backward on each, adds up the gradients, and calls the optimiser once. The optimiser must see the gradient of the mean loss over the whole batch, so each micro-batch's loss is divided by the number of micro-batches (or, for token-level losses, by the total token count across all of them, as mc10 discussed). Clipping happens once, after accumulation, on the summed gradient. The optimiser's step counter advances once per optimiser step, not per micro-batch, and the schedule is indexed by optimiser steps too. Getting any of these wrong makes the effective learning rate depend on the accumulation setting, so a run that is "the same" with two different memory budgets trains differently.

9. Edge cases worth naming

A parameter that receives no gradient on some step (an embedding row for a token not in the batch, or a frozen layer) should not be stepped at all in most implementations; PyTorch skips parameters whose grad is None, but a zero gradient still decays Adam's moments and applies weight decay. A zero gradient everywhere gives an Adam step of lr·m̂/(√v̂ + ε), which keeps moving by momentum for a while, as it should. The very first step with ε = 0 and an exactly zero gradient for some parameter divides 0 by 0; ε exists for that case. And a learning rate of exactly zero during warmup's first step (if warmup starts at 0 instead of base/W) wastes a step but is harmless, except that Adam's moments still update and t still advances.

10. Complexity

Every optimiser here is element-wise: O(P) time per step for P parameters, a handful of reads and writes per parameter, so in practice it is memory-bandwidth bound rather than compute bound. Memory is the real cost: momentum SGD stores one extra value per parameter, Adam two. In float32 that is 4 or 8 bytes per parameter on top of the parameters and gradients. For a 7-billion-parameter model: weights 28 GB (float32 master copy), gradients 28 GB, Adam state 56 GB, before activations. Sharding the state across data-parallel workers (ZeRO stage 1) divides the 56 GB by the number of workers, which is often the first optimisation large-model training applies.

Volunteer memory and precision: two states per parameter, float32. Finish with the schedule and clipping, because a correct optimiser with a bad schedule still trains badly.

Why momentum accelerates, quantitatively

On a quadratic with curvatures between λ_min and λ_max (condition number κ = λ_max/λ_min), plain gradient descent with the best fixed learning rate shrinks the error by a factor of about (κ − 1)/(κ + 1) per step, so it needs on the order of κ steps per factor of e. Heavy-ball momentum with tuned learning rate and μ achieves (√κ − 1)/(√κ + 1), so on the order of √κ steps. For κ = 100 (this lesson's quadratic) that is the difference between roughly 100 and 10 steps per factor of e, which matches what the test shows: 150 steps leave plain SGD at 0.22 and momentum near 4 × 10⁻⁴. Goh's interactive Distill article shows the same analysis visually, including why too much momentum oscillates and why the learning rate must shrink as μ grows.

Adam as approximate sign descent with noise awareness

When gradients are consistent, m̂ ≈ g and √v̂ ≈ |g|, so the step is about lr·sign(g): each parameter moves by lr per step. When gradients are noisy, with mean μ_g and standard deviation σ, m̂ ≈ μ_g but √v̂ ≈ √(μ_g² + σ²), so the step shrinks by the signal-to-noise ratio. Adam is therefore a per-parameter trust region: confident directions move at full speed, noisy ones cautiously. That view explains its strengths (robust to gradient scale, fast early progress) and a known weakness: v̂ adapts to recent gradient sizes, so a parameter whose gradients were small for a long time (a rare token's embedding) can take a huge step when a large gradient finally arrives. Lower β₂ (0.95 is common in LLM training) makes v̂ react faster and reduces such spikes.

Decoupled weight decay, and why it generalises better

Loshchilov and Hutter's analysis: with L2 inside Adam, the effective decay of each weight is λ/√v̂, so weights with historically large gradients are regularised less, exactly the weights that may most need it, and the best λ becomes entangled with the learning rate. Decoupling makes each weight shrink by the same relative amount, lr·λ, per step. Their experiments on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet32×32 showed AdamW's test error about 15% lower in relative terms than Adam with L2, closing the gap to SGD with momentum on image classification. Note one subtlety in PyTorch: the decay is lr·λ, so it follows the learning-rate schedule; the paper also discusses a schedule multiplier applied to both.

Learning-rate warmup, mechanistically

Warmup addresses several early-training hazards at once. Adam's v̂ is estimated from a few gradients and is noisy, so the per-parameter scaling is unreliable. Activations and their gradients change scale rapidly as normalisation layers and residual streams settle. And with large batches, the gradient is accurate but the curvature is unknown, so a full-size step can overshoot badly. RAdam showed much of the benefit comes from the variance of v̂ in the first steps, and proposed an automatic correction; in practice, a few hundred to a few thousand warmup steps of linear increase remain standard.

Learning rate and batch size

Changing the batch size changes the right learning rate. For SGD, the "linear scaling rule" (multiply the learning rate by k when the batch grows by k, with warmup) worked well up to large batches in image classification, because averaging k times more examples reduces gradient noise and allows proportionally larger steps. For Adam the relationship is weaker, closer to a square-root scaling in some studies, because Adam's step size is already normalised. Beyond a "critical batch size" that depends on the task and on training progress, larger batches stop reducing the number of steps needed and simply waste compute. When reproducing a recipe with a different number of GPUs, keep the global batch size and learning rate the same if you can, and if you cannot, retune the learning rate rather than assuming a rule.

Memory-light optimisers

Adam's two states double or triple training memory. Adafactor factors the second moment of a matrix-shaped parameter into row and column statistics, storing O(n + m) numbers for an n × m matrix instead of n·m, and drops the first moment. 8-bit Adam stores both moments in 8-bit integers with per-block float32 scales, cutting state memory by about four. Newer methods such as Lion use only a momentum buffer and the sign of an interpolated update. Each trades a little stability or tuning effort for memory, and each is a modification of the update rules written in this lesson.

Gradient clipping and loss spikes

Global-norm clipping rescales the whole gradient when its norm exceeds a threshold, which bounds the size of any single update without changing its direction. With Adam the effect is subtler than with SGD, because m and v are themselves averages: clipping one step's gradient prevents it from inflating m and v for many subsequent steps. Large-model reports routinely log the pre-clip gradient norm, the fraction of steps clipped, and loss spikes; a rising clip rate is often the first sign of trouble.

Where these optimisers run

AdamW with linear warmup and cosine or linear decay, β₁ = 0.9, β₂ around 0.95 to 0.999, weight decay around 0.1 and gradient clipping at a global norm of 1.0 is the default recipe for transformer pre-training and fine-tuning. SGD with momentum (often Nesterov) and a step or cosine schedule remains common for convolutional vision models. In large runs the optimiser is a fused kernel operating on sharded state, but the arithmetic is exactly this lesson's.

Failure modes and symptoms

Early divergence. Loss explodes in the first hundred steps. Causes: no warmup, peak learning rate too high, uncorrected moments in a hand-written Adam, or a step counter that starts at 0 instead of 1 (then 1 − βᵗ = 0 and bias correction divides by zero). Fix: warmup, lower peak, check t.

Loss spikes mid-training. Sudden jumps that sometimes recover. Causes: outlier batches, stale v̂ for rarely updated parameters, numerical issues. Fixes: global-norm clipping, lower β₂, higher ε, skipping or down-weighting outlier batches, and in the worst case rewinding to a checkpoint and skipping the data that caused it.

Weights that never decay, or decay too much. Using Adam(weight_decay=...) where AdamW was intended gives inconsistent regularisation; applying decay to biases and norms shrinks parameters that should not be regularised. Check which optimiser class and which parameter groups are actually configured.

Resumed runs that misbehave. After restarting from a checkpoint, loss jumps. Causes: optimizer state not saved (moments restart at zero, bias correction restarts), schedule step not restored, or a different data order. Save model, optimizer state including t, scheduler step and data position together.

A frozen layer that still changes. In fine-tuning, a layer meant to be frozen keeps drifting. Cause: its parameters were excluded from gradient computation but left in the optimiser with weight decay switched on, so AdamW shrinks them every step even with zero gradient. Fix: leave frozen parameters out of the optimiser entirely, and assert after a step that their values are unchanged.

Out of memory from the optimiser. A model fits for inference but not training because Adam's state and the gradients triple the memory. Fixes: state sharding (ZeRO/FSDP), 8-bit optimizer states, Adafactor, or offloading state to CPU memory.

A schedule that does not match the run length. The cosine schedule is defined over a planned number of steps. If training is extended past that number, the learning rate sits at the floor; if it is stopped early, it never decayed. Both change results noticeably. Constant-then-decay schedules (warmup, long constant phase, short final decay) make it easier to extend or branch runs, which is one reason some recent recipes prefer them.

What to monitor

Learning rate per step (plotted, to confirm the schedule), pre-clip global gradient norm and clip fraction, the update-to-weight ratio per layer (around 10⁻³ per step is a common healthy range), loss and its spikes, and for Adam the distribution of √v̂ (very small values flag parameters that can take huge steps).

A cost estimate

For a 70-billion-parameter model trained with AdamW in mixed precision, the persistent memory per parameter is roughly 2 bytes (bfloat16 weights) + 4 (float32 master weights) + 4 + 4 (two float32 moments) + 2 or 4 (gradients) ≈ 16 bytes, so about 1.1 TB before activations. Spread over 64 GPUs with fully sharded state, that is about 17.5 GB per GPU, which is why sharding is not an optimisation but a requirement at this scale. The optimizer step itself reads and writes all of it once per step, a few terabytes of memory traffic, typically a few per cent of step time when fused.

The reference for this problem

Kingma and Ba's "Adam: A Method for Stochastic Optimization" (arXiv 1412.6980) is the reference for the core algorithm. Its Algorithm 1 gives the update implemented here, its section 3 derives the "initialization bias correction" terms 1 − β₁ᵗ and 1 − β₂ᵗ exactly as in section 3 above, and it recommends the default settings lr = 0.001, β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999 and ε = 10⁻⁸ that PyTorch still uses. Loshchilov and Hutter's "Decoupled Weight Decay Regularization" (arXiv 1711.05101) states the central claim of section 4's weight-decay example: "L2 regularization and weight decay regularization are equivalent for standard stochastic gradient descent (when rescaled by the learning rate), but … this is not the case for adaptive gradient algorithms, such as Adam", and reports a 15% relative improvement in test error from decoupling. PyTorch's AdamW documentation specifies the algorithm order (shrink θ by γλθ, then update the moments and take the bias-corrected step) and defaults (lr 10⁻³, betas (0.9, 0.999), ε 10⁻⁸, weight decay 10⁻²) that this lesson's implementation matches to twelve decimal places.

Work it out

Add gradient clipping by global norm. Where in the training step does it go, why the global norm rather than per-tensor, and what should you log?

Answer

Clip after the backward pass and before optimizer.step: compute the L2 norm over all gradient tensors together, and if it exceeds max_norm, scale every gradient by max_norm / norm. Scaling all tensors by the same factor preserves the direction of the full update; clipping each tensor separately changes the direction.

def clip_grad_norm(grads, max_norm):
    total = np.sqrt(sum((g ** 2).sum() for g in grads.values()))
    if total > max_norm:
        for g in grads.values():
            g *= max_norm / (total + 1e-12)
    return total                        # log the pre-clip norm: spikes are an early warning

grads = {"a": np.array([3.0, 0.0]), "b": np.array([0.0, 4.0])}      # global norm 5
direction = np.concatenate(list(grads.values())) / 5
pre = clip_grad_norm(grads, 1.0)
after = np.concatenate(list(grads.values()))
assert pre == 5.0 and np.isclose(np.linalg.norm(after), 1.0)
assert np.allclose(after / np.linalg.norm(after), direction)          # direction preserved

Log the pre-clip norm every step. A slowly rising norm or sudden spikes are the earliest sign of a training instability, often before the loss moves, and the fraction of steps that get clipped tells you whether max_norm is doing anything. With mixed precision, unscale the gradients first (scaler.unscale_) or you clip the scaled values.
