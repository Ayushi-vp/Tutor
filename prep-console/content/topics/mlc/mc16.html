Every large language model is a stack of transformer blocks, and every block is built around one operation: attention. When a model reads "The trophy did not fit in the suitcase because it was too big", attention is the mechanism that lets the word "it" look back at "trophy" and "suitcase", weigh them, and pull in the information that resolves what "it" refers to. Attention is computed at every layer, for every head and every pair of tokens, which also makes it the main cost of long-context models. If you are interviewing for any role that touches LLMs, you should expect to be asked to write it.

"Implement multi-head self-attention in NumPy, with a causal mask" is probably the single most common LLM coding question. It is short enough to finish in twenty minutes, but each line hides a decision that interviewers probe. Why is there a 1/√d_k? (Without it, in this lesson's test, the softmax put an average of 79% of its weight on a single key out of 64, against 11% with it, and saturated softmaxes barely learn.) Where does the mask go, before or after the softmax, and with what value? (A mask of −∞ turns a fully masked row into NaN.) How do you run eight heads without a Python loop? (A reshape and a transpose.) How do you prove the causal mask works? (Change a future token and check that no earlier output moves.) And, at a more senior level: what does the backward pass look like, what does it cost, and why does FlashAttention exist? Each of those follow-ups has a short, concrete answer once you have built the operation yourself, and this lesson builds every piece.

The stakes are real outside interviews too. Mask bugs are among the most damaging bugs in LLM training: a decoder whose mask leaks one position of the future learns to copy the next token and reports a wonderful training loss, then generates nonsense. Padding bugs make a model's outputs depend on what else is in the batch. And getting the shapes and memory right is the difference between a model that fits 32,000 tokens of context and one that runs out of memory at 4,000.

This lesson builds attention from three tokens worked by hand, with and without the causal mask, then writes a loop version, the batched multi-head version, the combination of causal and padding masks, and a hand-derived backward pass. The tests check every hand number, compare against loops, check causality, padding and permutation equivariance, verify the backward pass with finite differences, and match PyTorch's scaled_dot_product_attention forward and backward to twelve decimal places. The deeper sections cover the variance argument for the scale, the T² cost, FlashAttention's tiling, and multi-query and grouped-query attention.

Imagine each word in a sentence carrying three cards. The query card says what the word is looking for ("I am a pronoun; I need a noun I could refer to"). The key card says what the word offers to others ("I am a concrete noun, singular, an object"). The value card holds the information the word will hand over if someone attends to it. To update one word, compare its query card with every word's key card; good matches get high scores. Turn the scores into weights that add up to one, and take the weighted average of everyone's value cards. That average becomes the word's new representation, now mixed with information from the words it found relevant.

In numbers, each card is a vector, produced from the word's current representation by a learned matrix: q = x·W_Q, k = x·W_K, v = x·W_V. A match score is a dot product q·k, divided by √d_k (the square root of the vectors' length) to keep the scores in a sensible range. Softmax over each query's scores gives the attention weights, and the output is the weighted sum of value vectors. Stacking all the queries as rows of Q, all keys as K and all values as V, the whole computation for every word at once is softmax(QKᵀ/√d_k)·V, two matrix products and a softmax.

Two refinements make it a transformer's attention. A mask controls who may look at whom. In a decoder that generates text left to right, a word must not see the words after it during training (otherwise it could just copy the next word), so a causal mask forbids every position from attending to later ones. Batches of sentences of different lengths are padded to a common length, and a padding mask stops real words from attending to padding. Masks act on the scores before the softmax, so forbidden positions get exactly zero weight and the remaining weights still sum to one.

The weights are easy to read once you have them. A weight of 0.67 from token 2 to itself and 0.33 to token 1 means token 2's new vector is two-thirds its own value and one-third token 1's. Because the weights are a probability distribution, the output is always inside the "convex hull" of the visible value vectors: attention can blend and select information, but on its own it cannot create a vector larger than any it is averaging. The surrounding layers (the output projection, the feed-forward network) do the transforming.

Multi-head attention runs several attention computations side by side, each on a slice of the vector dimensions with its own projections, so different heads can track different relationships (one head may follow syntax, another coreference). The heads' outputs are concatenated and mixed by a final output matrix. Splitting into heads costs nothing extra: it is just a reshape of the projected vectors. Where the card picture breaks: real queries, keys and values are learned vectors with hundreds of dimensions, and what a head "looks for" is rarely as interpretable as the story suggests, but the arithmetic is exactly this.

1. The problem as it is usually asked

"Implement multi-head self-attention in NumPy for input of shape (B, T, D) with h heads, supporting a causal mask and a padding mask." Clarify: is it self-attention (Q, K, V all from x) or cross-attention (K, V from another sequence)? Do they want the output projection? Are masks booleans (True means allowed) or additive (0 and −∞)? Should fully masked rows be handled? Then outline: the attention function with shapes, the mask applied to scores, head splitting by reshape, the output projection, and tests (hand example, causality, padding, framework comparison).

2. The maths in plain words

For each query position i and key position j, the score is the dot product of query i and key j divided by √d_k. Masked pairs get a score so negative that their softmax weight is zero. For each query, a softmax over its row of scores gives weights that are positive and sum to one. The output for query i is the weighted sum of the value vectors with those weights.

Why the dot product? It is large when two vectors point the same way, which is what "the query matches this key" means, and it is a matrix product when done for all pairs at once. Why √d_k? If the components of q and k are independent with mean 0 and variance 1, the dot product is a sum of d_k terms each with variance 1, so its variance is d_k: for d_k = 64 the scores have a standard deviation of 8, and a softmax over scores spread that widely is nearly one-hot. Dividing by √d_k brings the standard deviation back to 1 for any head size.

For multiple heads, the D-dimensional query, key and value vectors are each cut into h consecutive slices of length d_k = D/h. Head n uses slice n of each. Because the slices are consecutive columns, the cut is a reshape from (B, T, D) to (B, T, h, d_k), and moving the head axis forward gives (B, h, T, d_k), which matrix products treat as a batch of B·h independent attention computations.

3. The equations

Q = X·W_Q,  K = X·W_K,  V = X·W_V                     X: (B, T, D), each W: (D, D)
split heads:  (B, T, D) → (B, T, h, d_k) → (B, h, T, d_k),   d_k = D/h

S = Q·Kᵀ / √d_k                                       (B, h, T, T)
S[mask == False] = most negative finite value          mask broadcast to (B, h, T, T)
P = softmax(S, over the last axis)                     rows sum to 1
O = P·V                                                (B, h, T, d_k)
merge heads:  (B, h, T, d_k) → (B, T, h, d_k) → (B, T, D);   Y = O_merged·W_O

causal mask:   M[i, j] = (j ≤ i)                       np.tril(np.ones((T, T), bool))
padding mask:  M[b, j] = (j < length[b]),  reshaped to (B, 1, 1, T)
combined:      causal[None, None] & padding[:, None, None, :]   → (B, 1, T, T)

backward (per head):  dV = Pᵀ·dO,   dP = dO·Vᵀ,   dS = P ⊙ (dP − rowsum(dP ⊙ P)),
                      dQ = dS·K/√d_k,   dK = dSᵀ·Q/√d_k

The backward pass reuses two rules you already know: matrix-product gradients (mc11) for O = PV and S = QKᵀ, and the softmax Jacobian applied row by row (mc10), which gives the dP − rowsum(dP ⊙ P) term. Masked positions have P = 0, so dS is zero there automatically and no gradient flows to keys a query was not allowed to see.

4. A worked example by hand

Three tokens with two-dimensional vectors, x₁ = [1, 0], x₂ = [0, 1], x₃ = [1, 1], and identity projections, so Q = K = V = X and d_k = 2.

  • Scores. XXᵀ has rows [1, 0, 1], [0, 1, 1], [1, 1, 2]; dividing by √2 = 1.414 gives rows [0.707, 0, 0.707], [0, 0.707, 0.707], [0.707, 0.707, 1.414].
  • Causal mask. Row 1 keeps column 1 only; row 2 keeps columns 1 and 2; row 3 keeps all three.
  • Row 1. One allowed score, so its weight is 1: output [1, 0], the token's own value.
  • Row 2. Scores [0, 0.707]: e⁰ = 1, e^0.707 = 2.028, sum 3.028, weights [0.330, 0.670]. Output 0.330·[1, 0] + 0.670·[0, 1] = [0.330, 0.670].
  • Row 3. Scores [0.707, 0.707, 1.414]: exponentials 2.028, 2.028, 4.113, sum 8.169, weights [0.248, 0.248, 0.504]. Output 0.248·[1, 0] + 0.248·[0, 1] + 0.504·[1, 1] = [0.752, 0.752].
  • Without the mask, row 1 sees scores [0.707, 0, 0.707]: exponentials 2.028, 1, 2.028, sum 5.056, weights [0.401, 0.198, 0.401], output [0.802, 0.599]. The first token now mixes in information from tokens that come after it, which is exactly what a decoder must not do during training.

Two things to notice. Token 3's own key matches its query best (score 1.414), so it gives itself half the weight; that self-preference is common in real models too. And the weights depend only on score differences: adding the same number to every score in a row would change nothing, which is why the stable softmax may subtract the row maximum.

import math
import time
import numpy as np

# The hand-worked example: three tokens, d_k = 2, Q = K = V = X (identity projections).
X3 = np.array([[1.0, 0.0], [0.0, 1.0], [1.0, 1.0]])
scores = X3 @ X3.T / math.sqrt(2)                         # (3, 3)
assert np.allclose(scores, np.array([[1, 0, 1], [0, 1, 1], [1, 1, 2]]) / math.sqrt(2))
# Causal attention: token i may look at tokens 0..i only.
w1 = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0])                            # row 0 sees only itself
e = np.exp([0.0, 1 / math.sqrt(2)]); w2 = np.r_[e / e.sum(), 0.0]
e = np.exp(np.array([1, 1, 2]) / math.sqrt(2)); w3 = e / e.sum()
assert np.allclose(w2, [0.3302, 0.6698, 0], atol=1e-4)
assert np.allclose(w3, [0.2483, 0.2483, 0.5035], atol=1e-4)
out_causal = np.stack([w1, w2, w3]) @ X3
assert np.allclose(out_causal, [[1, 0], [0.3302, 0.6698], [0.7517, 0.7517]], atol=1e-4)
# Without the mask, token 0 also attends to tokens 1 and 2.
e = np.exp(np.array([1, 0, 1]) / math.sqrt(2)); w1_full = e / e.sum()
assert np.allclose(w1_full, [0.4011, 0.1978, 0.4011], atol=1e-4)
assert np.allclose(w1_full @ X3, [0.8022, 0.5989], atol=1e-4)

The backward pass for one row, by hand

Take row 2 of the causal example and suppose the loss cares only about the first component of its output, so dO₂ = [1, 0]. Its weights were P₂ = [0.330, 0.670, 0]. First, dP₂ = dO₂·Vᵀ: the dot product of [1, 0] with each value row [1, 0], [0, 1], [1, 1] gives [1, 0, 1], how much increasing each weight would raise the loss. The softmax backward subtracts the weighted average of those, Σ dP·P = 0.330, and multiplies by P: dS₂ = [0.330 × 0.670, 0.670 × (0 − 0.330), 0 × (1 − 0.330)] = [0.221, −0.221, 0]. The masked key gets exactly zero, as it must. Dividing by √2 gives [0.156, −0.156, 0], and dQ₂ = dS₂·K = 0.156·[1, 0] − 0.156·[0, 1] = [0.156, −0.156]. Read it as an instruction: to make output 2's first component larger, move query 2 toward key 1 and away from key 2, so more weight goes to value 1, whose first component is 1. The gradient check in the tests confirms exactly these mechanics on random inputs.

5. The naive implementation

A version that loops over queries, then over the keys each query may see, computing every dot product, the softmax and the weighted sum explicitly:

def attention_loops(Q, K, V, causal=False):
    """Q, K, V of shape (T, d): one query at a time, one key at a time."""
    T, d = Q.shape
    out = np.zeros((T, V.shape[1]))
    for i in range(T):
        keys = range(i + 1) if causal else range(T)
        s = [sum(Q[i, c] * K[j, c] for c in range(d)) / math.sqrt(d) for j in keys]
        m = max(s); ex = [math.exp(v - m) for v in s]; tot = sum(ex)
        for j, wj in zip(keys, ex):
            out[i] += (wj / tot) * V[j]
    return out

It encodes the causal mask by simply not visiting future keys, which makes its correctness obvious and makes it an excellent reference. It is slow: T²·d interpreted multiply-adds; for 128 tokens with d = 64 it took 258 ms, against under 3 ms for the matrix version.

6. The vectorised multi-head version

def softmax(z, axis=-1):
    z = z - z.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True)
    e = np.exp(z)
    return e / e.sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True)

def attention(Q, K, V, mask=None):
    """Q (..., Tq, dk), K (..., Tk, dk), V (..., Tk, dv); mask broadcastable to (..., Tq, Tk), True = may attend."""
    scores = Q @ np.swapaxes(K, -1, -2) / np.sqrt(Q.shape[-1])      # (..., Tq, Tk)
    if mask is not None:
        # a large finite negative, not -inf: a fully masked row then gives uniform weights, not NaN
        scores = np.where(mask, scores, np.finfo(scores.dtype).min)
    weights = softmax(scores)
    return weights @ V, weights

def attention_backward(Q, K, V, P, dout):
    """Gradients of sum(dout * (P @ V)) where P = softmax(QKᵀ/√d + mask)."""
    dV = np.swapaxes(P, -1, -2) @ dout                              # (..., Tk, dv)
    dP = dout @ np.swapaxes(V, -1, -2)                              # (..., Tq, Tk)
    dS = P * (dP - (dP * P).sum(-1, keepdims=True))                 # softmax backward, row by row
    dS = dS / np.sqrt(Q.shape[-1])
    return dS @ K, np.swapaxes(dS, -1, -2) @ Q, dV                  # dQ, dK, dV

def causal_mask(T):
    return np.tril(np.ones((T, T), dtype=bool))                        # row i may see columns 0..i

class MultiHeadAttention:
    def __init__(self, d_model, n_heads, seed=0):
        assert d_model % n_heads == 0
        rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
        self.h, self.dk = n_heads, d_model // n_heads
        std = 1 / np.sqrt(d_model)
        self.Wq, self.Wk, self.Wv, self.Wo = (rng.normal(0, std, (d_model, d_model)) for _ in range(4))

    def split(self, x):                                   # (B, T, D) -> (B, h, T, dk)
        B, T, _ = x.shape
        return x.reshape(B, T, self.h, self.dk).transpose(0, 2, 1, 3)

    def __call__(self, x, mask=None):
        B, T, D = x.shape
        q, k, v = self.split(x @ self.Wq), self.split(x @ self.Wk), self.split(x @ self.Wv)
        out, w = attention(q, k, v, mask)                  # all heads at once: (B, h, T, dk)
        out = out.transpose(0, 2, 1, 3).reshape(B, T, D)   # concatenate the heads
        return out @ self.Wo, w

attention works on any leading batch dimensions: np.swapaxes(K, -1, -2) transposes only the last two axes, so Q @ Kᵀ batches over (B, h). The mask is a boolean array broadcastable to the score shape; np.where keeps allowed scores and replaces the rest with np.finfo(dtype).min, the most negative finite number. The softmax subtracts each row's maximum before exponentiating.

attention_backward implements the four backward lines from section 3, given the forward's weights P. It is used in the tests, where finite differences and PyTorch's autograd confirm it.

MultiHeadAttention holds four D × D matrices. split reshapes (B, T, D) to (B, T, h, d_k) and transposes to (B, h, T, d_k). The call projects, splits, attends with all heads at once, transposes back and reshapes to (B, T, D), which concatenates the heads in order, then applies W_O. The weights are returned too, useful for testing and visualisation.

7. Masks in practice

The causal mask is lower-triangular: row i allows columns 0 to i. A padding mask marks real tokens per sequence; with lengths 6 and 3 padded to 6, the second sequence's mask is [T, T, T, F, F, F]. Reshaped to (B, 1, 1, T), it broadcasts over heads and over query positions, hiding the padded keys from every query. Combining the two with a logical AND gives a (B, 1, T, T) mask that the drill uses. Padded query positions still produce outputs (averages over whatever keys they may see), but those outputs are never used: the loss ignores them with ignore_index (mc10), and real positions never attend to them.

Two details deserve care. With right padding (real tokens first), the causal mask alone already stops real tokens from seeing the padding after them, so a padding mask matters mainly for left padding, which batched generation uses so that all sequences end at the same position, and for bidirectional encoders. And with left padding, a pad position's row in the combined mask can be entirely False, which is exactly the case where a −∞ mask value produces NaN; the finite value gives a uniform row instead.

Cross-attention with the same function

Nothing in attention assumes that queries, keys and values come from the same sequence. For a decoder attending to an encoder (translation, speech recognition) or a language model attending to image features, compute Q from the decoder's states and K, V from the other sequence: the score matrix is then T_q × T_k, rectangular, the causal mask does not apply, and a key-padding mask for the encoder's padded positions has shape (B, 1, 1, T_k). Because the encoder's keys and values do not change while the decoder generates, they are computed once per input and reused at every step. The function's shape conventions (any leading batch axes, mask broadcastable to the score shape) were chosen so that this case needs no new code.

8. Testing it

The tests check: the vectorised function reproduces the hand example's weights and outputs with and without the mask, and so does the loop version; loops and matrices agree on random inputs; the multi-head output has the right shapes, each row of weights sums to one and the future gets zero weight; perturbing the last two tokens leaves the first four outputs unchanged (causality); concatenating per-head results computed in an explicit loop equals the reshape version; shuffling tokens without a mask shuffles the outputs (permutation equivariance); the backward pass matches central differences for Q, K and V; PyTorch's scaled_dot_product_attention with is_causal=True gives the same output and, through autograd, the same three gradients to 10⁻¹², and the same multi-head result when fed this lesson's projections; the score variance divided by d_k is 1.001 for d_k = 64; unscaled scores put an average peak weight of 0.79 on one of 64 keys against 0.11 when scaled; a fully masked row gives NaN with −∞ and uniform weights with the finite value; and the timing.

Qs, Ks, Vs = rng.normal(size=(3, 4, 3)); dout = rng.normal(size=(4, 3)); mk = causal_mask(4)
_, P = attention(Qs, Ks, Vs, mk)
dQ, dK, dV = attention_backward(Qs, Ks, Vs, P, dout)
f = lambda q, k, v: (attention(q, k, v, mk)[0] * dout).sum()
eps = 1e-6
for arr, grad, which in ((Qs, dQ, 0), (Ks, dK, 1), (Vs, dV, 2)):
    num = np.zeros_like(arr)
    for i in np.ndindex(arr.shape):

The causality test is the one to remember. It does not inspect the mask; it checks the property the mask exists to guarantee, that outputs at position t depend only on inputs up to t. It catches off-by-one masks (using k=1 in tril), masks applied after the softmax, and masks broadcast along the wrong axis, all of which can pass a test that only checks the mask's shape.

9. Numerical and practical traps

Trap 1: forgetting the scale. Training still runs, but attention is nearly one-hot from the start, gradients through the softmax are tiny and learning is slow or unstable, worse for larger heads.

Trap 2: masking after the softmax. Zeroing weights after normalising leaves rows that no longer sum to one, and the masked positions still shaped the normalisation. Mask the scores. A quick check: print one row of weights for a masked query; the masked entries must be exactly 0 and the remaining entries must sum to 1.

Trap 3: −∞ and fully masked rows. NaN. Use the most negative finite value, and in float16 make sure the value is representable (−65,504), or mask with a large negative number like −10⁴ after upcasting scores to float32.

Trap 4: softmax in low precision. Scores in float16 can overflow once multiplied out of the matrix product, and the softmax's sum loses precision. Fused kernels compute scores and softmax in float32; hand-written code should upcast.

Trap 5: reshaping without transposing. Reshaping (B, T, D) straight to (B, h, T, d_k) is legal but wrong: it interleaves tokens and dimensions. The reshape must split D first, then the transpose moves the head axis.

Trap 6: the wrong softmax axis. Softmax over queries (axis −2) instead of keys (axis −1) produces columns summing to one; outputs look plausible. The "rows sum to one" assertion catches it.

Trap 7: memory. The (B, h, T, T) weight array is the largest tensor in the layer. For long sequences, compute attention in query chunks, or use a fused kernel.

Edge cases worth naming

A sequence of length one attends only to itself, so its output is its own value vector, whatever the scores; a good quick test. Queries and keys of different lengths (cross-attention, or decoding one new token against a cache of earlier ones, mc18) give a rectangular score matrix, and the causal mask must then be offset: the new token at position t may see cache positions 0 to t, so the mask row is all True rather than the last row of a square triangle. Identical keys produce identical scores and split weight evenly, which is correct. A head dimension that does not divide the model dimension cannot be split by reshape and should be rejected at construction, as the class's assertion does. And very long sequences with a fixed scale make each weight small: with 100,000 visible keys of similar score, no single key can receive much weight, one motivation for the length-dependent scaling some long-context methods add.

10. Complexity

Per layer, the four projections cost 4·B·T·D² multiply-adds and the attention itself 2·B·T²·D (scores plus weighted sum, summed over heads). Memory is O(B·h·T²) for the weights if materialised, plus O(B·T·D) for activations. The quadratic terms dominate when T is larger than about 2D. For D = 4,096 and T = 8,192, attention scores for one layer are 8,192² × 32 heads × 2 bytes = 4.3 GB per sequence in float16, which is why materialising them is avoided. The backward pass costs about twice the forward and, naively, needs the weights P from the forward pass; fused implementations recompute them instead.

11. Talking through the design

In the interview: write the formula with shapes; explain the scale with the variance argument; build the causal mask and apply it to the scores with a finite negative value; split heads with reshape plus transpose and merge them back; add W_O; then test causality and equality with a head loop. Volunteer the costs (T² time and memory), FlashAttention as the fix, and GQA for the KV cache. If asked for the backward pass, reuse the matrix-product and softmax rules. If time remains, sketch how the same function serves cross-attention and how grouped-query attention changes only the shapes of K and V; both show you understand the operation rather than one memorised form of it.

The variance argument, precisely

The original paper justifies the scale in a footnote: assume the components of q and k are independent random variables with mean 0 and variance 1; then their dot product Σ qᵢkᵢ has mean 0 and variance d_k. Each product qᵢkᵢ has variance E[qᵢ²]E[kᵢ²] = 1, and variances of independent terms add. The paper's text explains the consequence: for large d_k the dot products grow large in magnitude, "pushing the softmax function into regions where it has extremely small gradients". The test measured the variance ratio at 1.001 for d_k = 64, and the saturation directly: the mean largest weight among 64 keys was 0.79 for unscaled scores and 0.11 for scaled ones. In practice, learned queries and keys are not unit-variance, which is why some models also normalise q and k (QK-norm) or learn a temperature.

FlashAttention: exact attention without the T × T matrix

The standard computation writes S, then P, to main GPU memory and reads them back, so for long sequences memory traffic, not arithmetic, sets the speed. FlashAttention splits queries into tiles and streams over key/value tiles, keeping a running maximum, a running sum of exponentials and a running output for each query row, rescaling them whenever a new tile raises the maximum: the online softmax that mc10's drill used for the vocabulary. The result is exactly attention, computed with O(T) extra memory and far fewer trips to main memory. The backward pass stores only each row's log-sum-exp and recomputes P tile by tile, which costs extra arithmetic but less time, because arithmetic is cheap compared with memory traffic on modern GPUs.

Cross-attention, and why only Q differs

In an encoder-decoder model, or a vision-language model attending to image features, queries come from one sequence and keys and values from another. The code is unchanged except that K and V are projected from the other sequence, so the score matrix is T_q × T_k, not square, and the causal mask does not apply (padding masks on the keys still do). Cross-attention's keys and values can be computed once per input and reused for every generated token, a cache like the KV cache of mc18.

Multi-query and grouped-query attention

During generation, the keys and values of all previous tokens are cached and read at every step, so their size sets memory and bandwidth. Multi-query attention keeps h query heads but only one key head and one value head, shared by all queries; grouped-query attention keeps h_kv key/value heads, each shared by h/h_kv query heads. The attention arithmetic is unchanged: broadcast each K/V head to its group of query heads, which in NumPy is a reshape of the queries to (B, h_kv, h/h_kv, T, d_k) against K and V of shape (B, h_kv, 1, T, d_k). The KV cache shrinks by h/h_kv: 8 times for Llama 2 70B's 64 query heads and 8 KV heads. Ainslie and colleagues showed GQA recovers almost all of multi-head quality at close to MQA's speed, and it is now standard.

Why attention is permutation-equivariant, and what breaks the symmetry

Each output is a function of its own query and the set of (key, value) pairs; reorder the tokens and the same sets appear in a different order, so the outputs are reordered and otherwise unchanged. A bag of words, in other words. Two things break the symmetry: positional encodings added to or rotated into the vectors (mc17), which make a token's representation depend on where it is, and the causal mask, which makes the set of visible keys depend on position. Decoder-only models without any positional encoding can in fact learn some position information from the causal mask alone, a known curiosity.

Attention as a soft dictionary lookup

A Python dictionary returns the value whose key exactly equals the query. Attention returns a weighted blend of all values, weighted by how well each key matches the query, a differentiable version of a lookup, which is why it can be trained by gradient descent. As the scale grows (or the temperature falls), the softmax approaches a hard argmax and attention approaches an exact lookup; this view underlies interpretations of attention heads as copying, retrieval and induction mechanisms.

Attention sinks

Because softmax weights must sum to one, a head that has nothing useful to attend to still has to put its weight somewhere. Trained models often learn to dump that weight on the first token of the sequence (or on special tokens), which then acts as a sink whose value vector contributes little. This matters in practice: streaming-generation methods that keep only a window of recent tokens in the cache break badly if they evict the first token, and keeping a handful of initial sink tokens alongside the window restores quality. Some recent architectures add an explicit learnable sink, or allow a softmax that can sum to less than one, to give heads a cleaner way to attend to nothing.

Where attention runs

In every layer of every transformer: decoder self-attention in LLMs, bidirectional self-attention in encoders such as BERT and embedding models, cross-attention in translation, speech recognition and vision-language models, and in vision transformers over image patches. In production it is almost always a fused kernel (FlashAttention, cuDNN's attention, xFormers, PyTorch's scaled_dot_product_attention which dispatches to them), and the engineer's job is to call it with the right shapes, masks and dtypes.

Failure modes and symptoms

Future leakage. Training loss far lower than expected, validation generation incoherent. Cause: a causal mask that is off by one, transposed, or not applied in one attention path (for example in a custom packed-sequence implementation). Detection: the causality test on the real model: perturb token t+1 and check outputs up to t are bit-identical.

Batch-dependent outputs. The same prompt gives different outputs depending on what else is in the batch. Cause: a missing padding mask (especially with left padding in batched generation), or padding attended in bidirectional models. Detection: compare a padded batch's outputs with unpadded single runs, as the drill does.

NaN losses with packed or left-padded batches. Cause: fully masked rows with −∞. Fix: finite mask values, or skip such rows.

Out-of-memory at long context. The T² weight matrix. Fix: a fused kernel, chunked attention, or for inference a paged KV cache.

Silent slow paths. Fused kernels have requirements (head dimension sizes, dtypes, mask types); if a call does not meet them, frameworks fall back to the materialising implementation and memory use jumps. Check which backend was selected when profiling.

Inference and training disagree. A model's outputs at generation time differ from a full forward pass over the same tokens. Causes: the cached keys and values were computed with a different mask or positional offset than the training pass used, or a fused inference kernel handles padding differently. The fix is the same comparison this lesson's tests make: run the full sequence through the training path and the incremental path, and require agreement to within the precision's tolerance at every position (mc18 builds exactly that test).

Debugging with attention maps

Returning the weights, as this implementation does, pays off when something is wrong. Plotting a head's (T, T) weight matrix for a sample input shows at a glance whether the causal triangle is respected, whether padded columns receive weight, and whether a head has collapsed (all weight on the first token, a common "attention sink" pattern, or uniform over everything). Do not over-read individual heads as explanations of model behaviour, since information also flows through residual connections and other heads, but as a diagnostic for mask and padding bugs, an attention map is the fastest tool there is.

What to monitor

Attention entropy per head (heads collapsing to near one-hot or near uniform can signal training trouble), the maximum attention logit (growing logits precede instabilities, which QK-norm or logit capping address), memory per layer against sequence length, and which kernel backend is in use.

A cost estimate

For a 7-billion-parameter model with D = 4,096, 32 heads and 32 layers, the projections cost about 4 × 4,096² ≈ 67 million multiply-adds per token per layer. At a 4,096-token context, the score and weighted-sum terms add 2 × 4,096 × 4,096 ≈ 34 million per token per layer, about half the projections; at 32,768 tokens they are 268 million, four times the projections. That crossover is why long-context models care so much about attention's efficiency.

The reference for this problem

Vaswani and colleagues' "Attention Is All You Need" (arXiv 1706.03762) defines everything implemented here. Scaled dot-product attention is softmax(QKᵀ/√d_k)V. Its footnote explains the scale: assuming the components of q and k are independent random variables with mean 0 and variance 1, their dot product has mean 0 and variance d_k, and large dot products push "the softmax function into regions where it has extremely small gradients". The decoder's self-attention prevents "leftward information flow" by "masking out (setting to −∞) all values in the input of the softmax which correspond to illegal connections", the causal mask (this lesson uses a large finite value instead, for the fully-masked-row reason above). Multi-head attention uses "h = 8 parallel attention layers, or heads", with "d_k = d_v = d_model/h = 64", and because of the reduced dimension per head "the total computational cost is similar to that of single-head attention with full dimensionality". This lesson's implementation matches PyTorch's scaled_dot_product_attention in forward and backward to 10⁻¹².

Goal: run the solution, then vary it, break it and time it. Python 3.12 with NumPy; PyTorch only for the optional cross-checks.

  1. Run the four blocks as one script. All assertions pass, including the PyTorch forward and backward comparisons, and it prints the hand output, the scaling measurements and the timing.
  2. Vary the inputs. Change x₃ to [2, 0] and recompute the causal weights and outputs by hand before running the code. Which token does token 3 now attend to most, and why?
  3. Break the mask. Use np.tril(..., k=1), then apply the mask after the softmax, then use −∞ with a fully masked row. Run the causality test and the NaN test after each change.
  4. Break the heads. Replace the reshape-then-transpose in split with a direct reshape to (B, h, T, d_k). Which test fails?
  5. Measure the scale. Plot the mean peak weight against d_k = 8, 64 and 512, with and without the √d_k division.
  6. Time and scale it. Time the vectorised attention for T = 256, 1,024 and 4,096 with d = 64 and record the size of the weight array; then rewrite it to process queries in chunks of 256 and compare peak memory.

What to look for: every mask bug breaks either causality or row sums, the direct reshape breaks the head-loop equality, saturation grows with d_k only without the scale, time grows roughly four-fold per doubling of T, and chunking caps memory without changing the result. Check each variant's output against the unchunked one to many decimals before trusting a timing.

Work it out

A batch holds sequences of lengths 6 and 3, padded to 6. Build a mask combining the causal mask and a key-padding mask, and show that the outputs for the real tokens of the short sequence equal what you get by running it alone, unpadded.

Answer

The key-padding mask has shape (B, 1, 1, T), broadcast over heads and query positions, and the causal mask (T, T). Their logical AND broadcasts to (B, 1, T, T). Pad queries produce garbage outputs, which is fine: the loss and every later layer ignore them.

lengths = np.array([6, 3])
xp = rng.normal(size=(2, T, D))
key_ok = np.arange(T)[None, :] < lengths[:, None]                  # (B, T): real tokens
mask = causal_mask(T)[None, None] & key_ok[:, None, None, :]        # (B, 1, T, T)
outp, wp = mha(xp, mask)

alone, _ = mha(xp[1:2, :3], causal_mask(3))                          # the short sequence, unpadded
assert np.allclose(outp[1, :3], alone[0])
assert np.all(wp[1][..., 3:] == 0)                                   # no weight on pad keys

With right padding, the causal mask already prevents real tokens from seeing the pads that come after them, so the padding mask matters most for left padding (used for batched generation) and for bidirectional encoders. Say that, and note that pad positions must also be excluded from the loss with ignore_index.
