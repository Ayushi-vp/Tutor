The sentences "the dog chased the cat" and "the cat chased the dog" contain the same words. Attention, the operation at the heart of a transformer, cannot tell them apart: as mc16 showed, without extra information it treats its input as a bag of tokens, and shuffling the tokens just shuffles the outputs. Language is all about order, so every transformer injects position somehow. The original 2017 transformer added a fixed pattern of sine and cosine waves to each token's embedding. Almost every large language model since 2022, Llama, Mistral, Qwen, Gemma and their relatives, uses rotary position embedding (RoPE) instead, which rotates query and key vectors inside every attention layer by angles proportional to their positions.

RoPE is popular in coding interviews for LLM roles because it is short to implement, easy to get subtly wrong, and has a crisp mathematical property you can prove and test: the attention score between a query at position m and a key at position n depends only on the distance m − n. Interviewers ask you to implement it, to prove the property, and then to explain what happens when you try to run a model on longer text than it was trained on, which is a live engineering problem: extending a model's context from 4,000 to 32,000 or 128,000 tokens is done by rescaling exactly the RoPE angles this lesson computes.

The traps are concrete. Two incompatible conventions exist for which dimensions are paired (adjacent dimensions, or dimension i with i + d/2), and loading weights trained with one into code using the other produces a model that runs, generates fluent-looking text and is wrong. Angles at large positions computed in float32 are off by a few thousandths of a radian at position 123,457, enough to blur the fine-grained order information. And feeding positions beyond the trained length makes the slowly rotating dimensions reach angles the model has never seen.

This lesson builds the sinusoidal encoding and RoPE from scratch. It works a four-dimensional example by hand: the frequencies, a rotated query, and the scores at offsets 0, 1 and 2. It implements RoPE three ways (explicit rotation matrices, the fast pairwise form, and complex multiplication as in Meta's Llama code), relates the pairwise form to the rotate-half convention, proves and tests the relative-position property, demonstrates the float32 trap, and in the drill implements position interpolation and NTK-aware scaling for context extension.

Positional encoding is also one of the clearest places in a transformer where a small piece of mathematics has large engineering consequences. The choice of rotation over addition decides how the KV cache is stored, how context length can be extended after training, which checkpoints are compatible with which code, and how precision must be handled at long range. Understanding the forty lines of code in this lesson is enough to reason about all of those.

Imagine giving every word in a sentence a set of clock hands. Each word has many hands, all turning at different speeds: the first hand goes round once every six positions, the next a little slower, and the last takes tens of thousands of positions to complete a turn. A word's position is recorded in the angles of its hands. Two words that are close together have hands at similar angles on the slow clocks and possibly different angles on the fast ones; two words far apart differ even on the slow clocks. Fast hands give fine resolution for nearby words, slow hands tell you about long distances, and together they encode position at every scale. That is the idea behind both encodings in this lesson; they differ in how the clock angles are combined with the word.

The sinusoidal encoding writes down each hand's position as a (sine, cosine) pair, collects all pairs into a vector the same size as the word embedding, and adds that vector to the embedding before the first layer. Pair i turns at frequency ω_i = 10000^(−2i/d), so with d = 128 the fastest pair completes a turn every 6.3 positions and the slowest every 54,000 or so.

RoPE uses the same clocks differently. Instead of adding a position vector, it takes each query and key vector inside attention, splits it into pairs of numbers, treats each pair as a little arrow in a plane, and rotates the arrow by that pair's clock angle for the token's position. The word's content decides the arrows' lengths and starting directions; the position decides how far they are turned. When a query at position m is compared with a key at position n by a dot product, each pair contributes the product of the arrows' lengths times the cosine of the angle between them, and that angle includes the difference of the two rotations, (m − n)·θ_i. The absolute positions cancel; only the distance survives.

Rotations have three properties that make RoPE attractive. They never change a vector's length, so they do not disturb the scale of attention scores. They compose, so rotating a token's key when it enters the cache and its query later gives the right relative angle without recomputation. And the relative-distance property is exact, not learned: the model does not have to discover that "three tokens back" means the same thing at position 10 as at position 1,000.

Where the clock picture breaks: the model is trained only up to some maximum length, so the slowest hands never complete even one turn during training. Beyond that length, they reach angles the model has never seen, and quality collapses. Rescaling the clocks so long texts fit into the familiar range of angles is how context is extended, and the drill does exactly that.

One more intuition helps with RoPE's design: rotation is the only way to change a two-dimensional vector's direction while keeping its length, and a dot product between two vectors depends only on their lengths and the angle between them. So if positions are encoded as rotations, the dot product automatically sees the difference of the rotations, the relative position, and nothing else about position. That one geometric fact is the whole reason RoPE works, and it is worth being able to say in a sentence.

1. The problem as it is usually asked

"Implement sinusoidal positional encoding and rotary position embedding in NumPy, apply RoPE to queries and keys of shape (B, h, T, d_k), and show that the attention score depends only on relative position." Clarify: which RoPE convention (interleaved pairs or rotate-half); the base (10,000 unless told otherwise); whether to apply RoPE to all of d_k (usually); and whether positions can start at an offset (yes, for cached decoding). Then outline: an angle table, the pairwise rotation, a test of the relative property, equivalence with explicit matrices, and the offset behaviour.

2. The maths in plain words

Both encodings use the same set of frequencies: pair i (out of d/2) gets θ_i = base^(−2i/d). With base 10,000, θ₀ = 1 radian per position and the frequencies shrink geometrically, down to about 10⁻⁴ for the last pair when d = 128.

The sinusoidal encoding for position p is a d-dimensional vector whose pair i is (sin(p·θ_i), cos(p·θ_i)). It is added to the token embedding at the input. One property makes it work: moving from position p to p + k changes each pair by a fixed rotation (by angle k·θ_i) that does not depend on p, so a linear layer can, in principle, learn to look a fixed distance back. The test checks this shift-as-rotation property explicitly.

RoPE applies the rotation to the vectors themselves. For a query or key vector x at position p, pair i, (x_{2i}, x_{2i+1}), is replaced by its rotation by angle p·θ_i: the new first component is x_{2i}·cos − x_{2i+1}·sin and the new second is x_{2i}·sin + x_{2i+1}·cos. Do it to every query and key in every attention layer; leave values alone.

Why does the score depend only on the offset? Write the rotation for position p as a block-diagonal matrix R_p, one 2 × 2 rotation per pair. Rotating by a then b is rotating by a + b, and the transpose of a rotation is the rotation in the opposite direction. So the score between a rotated query at m and a rotated key at n is (R_m q)ᵀ(R_n k) = qᵀ R_mᵀ R_n k = qᵀ R_(n−m) k: the original vectors with a single rotation by the offset between them.

3. The equations

frequencies:     θ_i = base^(−2i/d),   i = 0 … d/2 − 1,   base = 10,000

sinusoidal:      PE[p, 2i]   = sin(p·θ_i)
                 PE[p, 2i+1] = cos(p·θ_i)          added to the embedding at the input

RoPE (pair i of x at position p, angle a = p·θ_i):
                 x′_{2i}   = x_{2i}·cos a − x_{2i+1}·sin a
                 x′_{2i+1} = x_{2i}·sin a + x_{2i+1}·cos a     applied to q and k in every layer

as matrices:     x′ = R_p x,   R_p = blockdiag( [[cos pθ_i, −sin pθ_i], [sin pθ_i, cos pθ_i]] )
key identity:    R_mᵀ R_n = R_(n−m)   ⟹   (R_m q)·(R_n k) = q·(R_(n−m) k)

as complex numbers:  z_i = x_{2i} + i·x_{2i+1};   z′_i = z_i · e^(i·p·θ_i)
rotate-half:     pair dimension i with i + d/2 instead of 2i with 2i + 1

The complex form is how Meta's original Llama code implements RoPE: view consecutive pairs as complex numbers, multiply by unit complex numbers e^(i·angle), view back as real pairs. Multiplying complex numbers adds their angles and multiplies their lengths, which is the rotation. The test checks it gives the same result as the pairwise form.

4. A worked example by hand

Take d = 4. The two frequencies are θ₀ = 10000^0 = 1 and θ₁ = 10000^(−2/4) = 10000^(−0.5) = 0.01.

  • Sinusoidal encoding of position 1. [sin 1, cos 1, sin 0.01, cos 0.01] = [0.8415, 0.5403, 0.0100, 0.99995]. Position 0 would be [0, 1, 0, 1].
  • RoPE on q = [1, 0, 1, 0] at position 1. Pair 0 is (1, 0), rotated by 1 radian: (cos 1, sin 1) = (0.5403, 0.8415). Pair 1 is (1, 0), rotated by 0.01: (0.99995, 0.0100). So the rotated query is [0.5403, 0.8415, 0.99995, 0.0100]. Its length is still √2, the length of the original.
  • Scores with q = k = [1, 0, 1, 0]. Each pair is a unit vector, so its contribution to the dot product is the cosine of the angle between the rotated query and key pairs, which is (offset)·θ_i. The score at offset o is cos(o·1) + cos(o·0.01).
    • Offset 0: 1 + 1 = 2.
    • Offset 1: cos 1 + cos 0.01 = 0.5403 + 0.99995 = 1.5403.
    • Offset 2: cos 2 + cos 0.02 = −0.4161 + 0.9998 = 0.5837.

Two lessons hide in these three numbers. The score depends only on the offset, so the pair (position 2, position 1) gives 1.5403 exactly like (position 101, position 100). And the two pairs play different roles: the fast pair drops from 1 to −0.42 within two tokens, so it separates neighbours sharply, while the slow pair stays above 0.999 and only matters over long distances. With many pairs at geometrically spaced speeds, the model gets a fine-to-coarse description of distance. The first solution block asserts every number above.

import math
import time
import numpy as np

# The hand-worked example: d = 4, base 10000, so the two pair frequencies are 1 and 10000^(-2/4) = 0.01.
theta = [10000 ** (-0 / 4), 10000 ** (-2 / 4)]
assert theta == [1.0, 0.01]

# Sinusoidal encoding of position 1: [sin(1), cos(1), sin(0.01), cos(0.01)].
pe1 = [math.sin(1), math.cos(1), math.sin(0.01), math.cos(0.01)]
assert np.allclose(pe1, [0.8415, 0.5403, 0.0100, 0.99995], atol=1e-4)

# RoPE rotates each pair of q by (position × frequency). q = [1, 0, 1, 0] at position 1:
def rot(x, y, a):
    return (x * math.cos(a) - y * math.sin(a), x * math.sin(a) + y * math.cos(a))
q_rot = [*rot(1, 0, 1 * 1.0), *rot(1, 0, 1 * 0.01)]
assert np.allclose(q_rot, [0.5403, 0.8415, 0.99995, 0.0100], atol=1e-4)

# With q = k = [1, 0, 1, 0], the score depends only on the offset m - n:
# score = cos((m-n)·1) + cos((m-n)·0.01)
def hand_score(off):
    return math.cos(off * 1.0) + math.cos(off * 0.01)
assert math.isclose(hand_score(0), 2.0)
assert math.isclose(hand_score(1), 1.5403, abs_tol=1e-4)
assert math.isclose(hand_score(2), 0.5837, abs_tol=1e-4)

5. The naive implementation: explicit rotation matrices

The most literal implementation builds, for each position, the full d × d block-diagonal rotation matrix and multiplies it by that position's vector:

def rope_matrix(pos, dk, base=10000.0):
    """The full dk × dk block-diagonal rotation for one position (the 'naive' form)."""
    R = np.zeros((dk, dk))
    for i in range(dk // 2):
        a = pos * base ** (-2 * i / dk)
        c, s = math.cos(a), math.sin(a)
        R[2 * i:2 * i + 2, 2 * i:2 * i + 2] = [[c, -s], [s, c]]
    return R

def apply_rope_naive(x, base=10000.0):
    """x (T, dk): build a rotation matrix per position and multiply. O(T · dk²)."""
    return np.stack([rope_matrix(t, x.shape[1], base) @ x[t] for t in range(len(x))])

It makes the mathematics visible (the identity R_mᵀ R_n = R_(n−m) can be checked directly on these matrices, and the tests do), but it wastes almost all of its work multiplying zeros: a d × d matrix has d² entries and only 2d are non-zero. For 512 positions with d = 128 it took 61 ms, against about 1 ms for the pairwise form.

6. The vectorised implementation

def sinusoidal(T, d, base=10000.0):
    pos = np.arange(T)[:, None]
    freq = base ** (-np.arange(0, d, 2) / d)          # (d/2,) geometric frequencies
    pe = np.zeros((T, d))
    pe[:, 0::2], pe[:, 1::2] = np.sin(pos * freq), np.cos(pos * freq)
    return pe

def rope_angles(T, dk, base=10000.0, offset=0, scale=1.0):
    """Angle for every (position, pair): (T, dk/2). `scale` < 1 is position interpolation."""
    inv_freq = base ** (-np.arange(0, dk, 2) / dk)
    return np.outer((np.arange(offset, offset + T)) * scale, inv_freq)

def apply_rope(x, angles):
    """Rotate each adjacent pair (x[2i], x[2i+1]) of the last axis by its angle. x (..., T, dk)."""
    x1, x2 = x[..., 0::2], x[..., 1::2]
    cos, sin = np.cos(angles), np.sin(angles)
    out = np.empty_like(x)
    out[..., 0::2] = x1 * cos - x2 * sin
    out[..., 1::2] = x1 * sin + x2 * cos
    return out

sinusoidal builds the frequency vector once, multiplies by a column of positions (broadcasting to a T × d/2 table of angles), and writes sines to even dimensions and cosines to odd ones.

rope_angles returns the T × d/2 table of position × θ_i, with an offset for the first position (needed when rotating new tokens during cached generation) and a scale multiplier on positions (used for position interpolation in the drill). In production this table, or its cos and sin, is computed once and cached.

apply_rope takes the even and odd slices of the last axis (x[..., 0::2] and x[..., 1::2]), applies the 2 × 2 rotation formula element-wise with the cos and sin tables, and writes the results back to even and odd positions. Because the angle table is T × d/2 and the slices are (..., T, d/2), broadcasting applies the same rotation to every batch element and head. The cost is a few element-wise operations per number, O(T·d).

7. Conventions: interleaved, complex and rotate-half

The pairing of dimensions is a convention, and there are two in common use. The RoFormer paper and Meta's original Llama code pair adjacent dimensions (0, 1), (2, 3), …, the convention implemented here, often written with complex numbers. GPT-NeoX and Hugging Face's Llama implementation pair dimension i with i + d/2, computing x·cos + rotate_half(x)·sin where rotate_half swaps and negates the two halves. The two are the same operation applied to a permuted vector: if you reorder the dimensions as (0, 2, 4, …, 1, 3, 5, …), interleaved pairs become half-split pairs. The test checks exactly that identity, and also checks that applying one convention to vectors laid out for the other gives a different, wrong answer.

That last point is why the conventions matter in practice. A model's W_Q and W_K are trained to produce vectors in a particular layout. Hugging Face's conversion script for Llama checkpoints permutes the rows of those weight matrices to switch conventions; forget the permutation and the model loads, runs, and produces garbage that can look superficially plausible. When porting or debugging a model, checking the RoPE convention is near the top of the list.

RoPE inside multi-head attention

In a real attention layer, queries have shape (B, h, T, d_k) and keys (B, h_kv, T, d_k), possibly with fewer key heads under grouped-query attention. RoPE is applied after the projections and head split, before the scores. The angle table depends only on position and pair, (T, d_k/2), so apply_rope broadcasts it across batch and heads without copying: every head uses the same frequencies, and the rotation is applied to the even and odd slices of the last axis. Keys are rotated once, when they are computed, and stored rotated in the KV cache; at each decoding step only the new token's query and key are rotated, with the angle row for its position. Values pass through untouched. Two practical details: the rotation must use each sequence's own positions (which differ under left padding), and if the model uses partial rotary embeddings, only the first fraction of each head's dimensions is rotated and the rest are concatenated back unchanged.

Context extension in numbers

Make the drill's scenario concrete with d = 128. The slowest pair's frequency is θ₆₃ = 10000^(−126/128) ≈ 1.15 × 10⁻⁴. At the end of a 4,096-token training context its angle is 4,096 × 1.15 × 10⁻⁴ ≈ 0.47 radians, the largest it ever reached in training. At position 16,383 it would be about 1.89 radians, four times further round than anything the model has seen. Position interpolation divides the position by 4, bringing that angle back to 0.47, but it also divides the fastest pair's angle, from 16,383 to about 4,096 radians, so neighbouring tokens now differ by a quarter of a radian on that pair instead of a full radian. NTK-aware scaling raises the base to 10,000 × 4^(128/126) ≈ 40,900, which leaves θ₀ = 1 exactly and divides θ₆₃ by exactly 4: the slow pair is interpolated as with PI, the fast pair keeps full resolution. The drill's assertions check each of these statements numerically.

8. Testing it

The tests check: the vectorised sinusoidal and RoPE functions reproduce the hand-worked vectors and scores; the pairwise form equals multiplying by explicit rotation matrices for 32 positions in 64 dimensions; the score at (5, 2) equals the score at (13, 10), the score at (7, 7) equals q·k, and (5, 2) differs from (2, 5); the matrix identity R₅ᵀR₂ = R₋₃ holds; rotation preserves every vector's length; rotating the token at position 20 alone (with an offset) equals the corresponding row of the full result; the complex-number formulation equals the pairwise one; the rotate-half form equals the interleaved one after permutation and not without; the sinusoidal shift-as-rotation property holds for every pair; and the float32 angle trap.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 1. The vectorised functions reproduce the hand example.
assert np.allclose(sinusoidal(2, 4)[1], pe1)
A4 = rope_angles(3, 4)
q4 = np.array([1.0, 0, 1, 0])
assert np.allclose(apply_rope(q4[None], A4[1:2])[0], q_rot)
s4 = lambda m, n: apply_rope(q4[None], A4[m:m + 1])[0] @ apply_rope(q4[None], A4[n:n + 1])[0]
assert np.isclose(s4(2, 1), hand_score(1)) and np.isclose(s4(2, 0), hand_score(2))

# 2. Pairwise rotation equals multiplying by explicit block-diagonal matrices.
dk, T = 64, 32
X = rng.normal(size=(T, dk))
A = rope_angles(T, dk)
assert np.allclose(apply_rope_naive(X), apply_rope(X, A))

# 3. The defining property: the q·k score depends only on the offset m - n.
q, k = rng.normal(size=dk), rng.normal(size=dk)
def score(m, n):
    return apply_rope(q[None], A[m:m + 1])[0] @ apply_rope(k[None], A[n:n + 1])[0]
assert np.isclose(score(5, 2), score(13, 10)) and np.isclose(score(7, 7), score(0, 0))
assert np.isclose(score(0, 0), q @ k)                  # position 0 is no rotation
assert not np.isclose(score(5, 2), score(2, 5))        # but direction matters
# ...which is the matrix identity R_mᵀ R_n = R_(n-m)
assert np.allclose(rope_matrix(5, 8).T @ rope_matrix(2, 8), rope_matrix(-3, 8))

# 4. Rotations preserve length; incremental decoding equals slicing the full result.
assert np.allclose(np.linalg.norm(apply_rope(X, A), axis=-1), np.linalg.norm(X, axis=-1))
assert np.allclose(apply_rope(X[20:21], rope_angles(1, dk, offset=20)), apply_rope(X, A)[20:21])

# 5. Reference formulation: pairs as complex numbers multiplied by e^(i·angle) (the Llama code's form).
z = (X[:, 0::2] + 1j * X[:, 1::2]) * np.exp(1j * A)
assert np.allclose(np.stack([z.real, z.imag], -1).reshape(T, dk), apply_rope(X, A))

# 6. The "rotate half" convention (GPT-NeoX / Hugging Face Llama) pairs dimension i with i + dk/2.
def apply_rope_half(x, angles):
    h = x.shape[-1] // 2
    x1, x2 = x[..., :h], x[..., h:]
    c, s = np.cos(angles), np.sin(angles)
    return np.concatenate([x1 * c - x2 * s, x1 * s + x2 * c], -1)
perm = np.r_[np.arange(0, dk, 2), np.arange(1, dk, 2)]      # interleaved -> halves
assert np.allclose(apply_rope_half(X[:, perm], A), apply_rope(X, A)[:, perm])
assert not np.allclose(apply_rope_half(X, A), apply_rope(X, A))   # mixing conventions is a bug

# 7. Sinusoidal: shifting by kk positions is a fixed rotation of each (sin, cos) pair.
pe = sinusoidal(50, 16)
freq = 10000.0 ** (-np.arange(0, 16, 2) / 16)
kk = 7
for i, w in enumerate(freq):
    R = np.array([[np.cos(kk * w), -np.sin(kk * w)], [np.sin(kk * w), np.cos(kk * w)]])
    assert np.allclose(pe[:40, 2 * i:2 * i + 2] @ R, pe[kk:40 + kk, 2 * i:2 * i + 2])

# 8. Precision trap: angles for large positions computed in float32 are wrong by a lot.
inv32 = (10000.0 ** (-np.arange(0, 128, 2) / 128)).astype(np.float32)
ang32 = np.float32(123_457) * inv32
ang64 = 123_457 * (10000.0 ** (-np.arange(0, 128, 2) / 128))
err = np.abs(np.cos(ang32) - np.cos(ang64)).max()
assert err > 1e-3                                          # about 0.003 at position 123,457
small = np.abs(np.cos(np.float32(4096) * inv32) - np.cos(4096 * (10000.0 ** (-np.arange(0, 128, 2) / 128)))).max()
assert small < 1e-4                                        # fine at short context

# 9. Timing: explicit matrices vs pairwise rotation for T = 512, dk = 128.
Xb = rng.normal(size=(512, 128))

Ab = rope_angles(512, 128)
t0 = time.perf_counter(); apply_rope(Xb, Ab); t_vec = time.perf_counter() - t0
if __name__ == "__main__":

    print(f"float32 cos error: position 4,096 {small:.1e}, position 123,457 {err:.4f}")

The offset test matters for generation. In KV-cached decoding (mc18), each new token is processed alone, at position t, and its key is stored already rotated. If the code rotated new tokens as if they were at position 0, or recomputed angles with an off-by-one offset, every generated token would see slightly wrong distances to everything before it, a bug that produces degraded but not obviously broken text.

9. Numerical and practical traps

Trap 1: float32 angles at long context. The fastest pair's angle is the position itself. Float32 has a 24-bit mantissa, so near 100,000 it can only represent multiples of about 0.008. In the test, the cosines computed from float32 angles at position 123,457 were off by up to 0.003, against 2 × 10⁻⁵ at position 4,096. bfloat16, with an 8-bit mantissa, cannot even represent consecutive integer positions above 256 exactly. Compute angles from integer positions in float32 (or float64 for very long contexts), then take cos and sin and cast down.

Trap 2: mixed conventions. Described above; test with a known checkpoint against its reference implementation.

Trap 3: rotating values. RoPE applies to queries and keys only. Rotating values changes the outputs in a position-dependent way that the model was not trained for.

Trap 4: wrong base or partial rotation. Some models use base 500,000 or 1,000,000 for long context, and some rotate only a fraction of each head's dimensions (partial rotary, used in GPT-NeoX and others). Read the configuration rather than assuming 10,000 and full rotation.

Trap 5: offsets in cached decoding. New tokens must be rotated at their true positions, which with left padding or sliding windows is not simply the index in the current tensor. Track positions explicitly.

Trap 6: odd head dimensions. RoPE needs pairs, so d_k must be even. Assert it.

10. Edge cases worth naming

Position 0 is the identity rotation, so the score at (0, 0) equals the raw q·k, a good sanity check. Negative offsets (a key after the query, possible in encoders without a causal mask) are handled naturally: the rotation by a negative angle. Very large positions wrap around for fast pairs (angles are periodic), which is fine; the slow pairs carry the long-range information. A batch where different sequences start at different positions (left padding) needs per-sequence position arrays and therefore per-sequence angle tables, which rope_angles supports through its offset or by indexing a precomputed table with a position array.

11. Complexity

Both encodings cost O(T·d): the sinusoidal table once per sequence, RoPE once per query and key per layer, a few multiply-adds per number. The angle and cos/sin tables are O(T·d/2) and are usually precomputed up to the maximum length and cached. Compared with attention's O(T²·d) and the projections' O(T·d²), RoPE is negligible in arithmetic, but as an element-wise operation it costs memory bandwidth, which is why production implementations fuse it into the attention or projection kernel.

12. Talking through the design

In the interview: explain why position is needed (permutation equivariance); write the frequencies; write RoPE as a pairwise rotation and as a block-diagonal matrix (one 2 × 2 rotation per pair, zeros elsewhere); prove R_mᵀR_n = R_(n−m) and therefore the relative property; implement with even and odd slices; test with shifted position pairs; then volunteer the two conventions, cached decoding offsets, and how context extension rescales positions or frequencies.

If the interviewer pushes on extrapolation, give the numbers from the context-extension section: the slowest pair's angle at the training edge, how far beyond it a four-fold longer input goes, and what position interpolation and NTK scaling each do to the fastest and slowest pairs. Concrete angles are far more convincing than naming the methods.

Why rotations, and the long-term decay

The RoFormer paper motivates RoPE as the solution to a design question: find a function of a vector and its position whose inner products depend only on the vectors and the relative position. In two dimensions the solution is multiplication by e^(i·mθ), a rotation; in d dimensions, independent rotations of d/2 planes. The paper also argues that with the geometric frequencies, the expected magnitude of the score contribution decays as the relative distance grows, "decaying inter-token dependency with increasing relative distances", a mild built-in recency bias. The hand example shows its flavour: with aligned unit pairs the score starts at 2 and the fast pair's contribution oscillates and averages out over distance, leaving only the slow pairs.

Context extension, quantitatively

Suppose a model with d = 128 was trained on 4,096 tokens and must run on 16,384. At position 16,383 the slowest pair's angle is about four times larger than any it saw in training (the drill checks it is more than three times beyond the trained edge). Position interpolation (Chen and colleagues) multiplies positions by 1/4, so every angle is back in range, but neighbouring tokens are now only a quarter-step apart on the fast pairs, which the model must re-learn to resolve; a short fine-tune (on the order of a thousand steps in their experiments) suffices. NTK-aware scaling instead multiplies the base by s^(d/(d−2)): the fastest pair's frequency, base⁰ = 1, is unchanged, and the slowest pair's frequency is divided by exactly s. YaRN interpolates only the low-frequency bands, keeps high-frequency bands untouched, ramps between them, and adds a temperature to the attention logits. Many recent long-context models ship with such a scaling configuration in their settings.

Sinusoidal encoding's linear-shift property

The original paper chose sines and cosines because "for any fixed offset k, PE(pos+k) can be represented as a linear function of PE(pos)": each pair at position p + k is the pair at p rotated by kθ_i, a fixed 2 × 2 matrix. The test verifies this pair by pair. In principle, attention could use it to look a fixed distance back; in practice, because the encoding is added to the content embedding at the input, position and content are entangled by the time they reach deep layers, which is one argument for RoPE's injection of position directly into every layer's q·k.

ALiBi and learned encodings

Learned absolute embeddings (BERT, GPT-2) give each position a trainable vector up to a fixed maximum length; they work well within that length and not at all beyond it. ALiBi (Press and colleagues) uses no embeddings: it adds −slope_h·|m − n| to each attention score, with a different slope per head, so attention prefers recent tokens by a fixed amount per token of distance; models trained this way extrapolate gracefully to longer inputs. RoPE sits between: relative by construction, more expressive than a fixed bias, but in need of explicit scaling to extrapolate. That trade-off, plus strong empirical results, made it the default.

Beyond one dimension: RoPE for images and video

Text positions are integers on a line; image patches have a row and a column, and video adds time. Multi-dimensional RoPE splits each head's pairs into groups and rotates one group by the row position, another by the column position (and a third by time), so the score between two patches depends on their relative offset in each direction. Vision-language models use variants of this so that image tokens keep their 2-D geometry while text tokens keep their 1-D order in the same sequence. The implementation is the same apply_rope with a different angle table: per pair, the relevant coordinate times the pair's frequency.

RoPE and linear attention, and why it is applied per layer

Because RoPE is a multiplication of q and k by position-dependent rotations, it fits into attention variants that never form the full score matrix, such as linear attention, which additive relative-position schemes do not; the RoFormer paper highlights this compatibility. It is applied in every layer because position information added at the input fades through many layers of mixing; rotating q and k in each layer gives every attention computation an exact, fresh view of relative distance.

Choosing the base

The base sets the slowest wavelength: with base 10,000 and d = 128 it is about 54,000 tokens. If a model will be trained on long documents, a larger base keeps the slowest pairs from completing many turns within the context, so distant positions remain distinguishable; several recent models train with bases of 500,000 or more for exactly this reason, and NTK-aware scaling is in effect a post-hoc increase of the base. The cost is resolution: a larger base slows every pair except the first, so mid-range distances are encoded by fewer fast-moving pairs. The base is therefore a design choice tied to the intended context length, not a universal constant, and it must match between training and inference.

Where these encodings run

RoPE is in nearly every open LLM released since 2023 and in many multimodal models (with 2-D or 3-D variants for image patches and video). Sinusoidal and learned encodings survive in older encoders, speech models and some vision transformers. In production, RoPE is applied inside fused attention or projection kernels with precomputed cos/sin caches, and context extension is a configuration choice (a scaling type and factor) shipped with the model.

Failure modes and symptoms

Broken ports. A converted checkpoint generates fluent but off-topic or repetitive text. Cause: the RoPE convention changed without permuting W_Q and W_K, a different base, or partial rotary ignored. Detection: compare attention scores layer by layer with the reference implementation on one input.

Collapse beyond the trained length. Perplexity rises sharply past the training context. Cause: unseen angles on slow pairs. Fix: a scaling method (position interpolation, NTK, YaRN) with or without brief fine-tuning, and evaluation at the target length on long-document tasks, not just perplexity.

Retrieval that fails only in the middle. A long-context model answers questions about the start and end of a document but misses facts placed in the middle. Positional encoding is one contributor (along with training data that rarely requires mid-document retrieval), and scaling choices change the pattern. Evaluate at several insertion depths, not one, before trusting a context-length claim.

Gradual degradation at very long context. Quality drifts down over hundreds of thousands of tokens even within the supported range. Possible causes: angle precision (Trap 1), or too-coarse cos/sin tables stored in bfloat16. Fix: compute angles in float32 or higher from integer positions.

Cached generation differs from a full forward pass. Cause: wrong position offsets for new tokens, especially with left padding, sliding windows or speculative decoding. Fix: the incremental-equals-full test from section 8 and mc18, run in the real serving code path.

Position mismatches in packed training. When several short documents are packed into one training sequence, positions should usually restart at zero for each document (and attention should not cross document boundaries). If positions run continuously across documents, the model learns that a document's first token can sit at position 3,000, and its behaviour at real position 0 at inference differs. Check how the data loader assigns position IDs. A tiny packed batch printed with its positions settles it.

Inconsistent scaling between training and serving. A model fine-tuned with one context-extension setting is served with another (or with none), and long-input quality drops while short inputs look fine. The scaling type and factor are part of the model, like its weights; ship them in the configuration and assert them at load time.

What to monitor

Perplexity as a function of position on long documents (it should not rise near the end of the supported range), retrieval accuracy on "needle in a haystack" style probes at several depths and lengths, and agreement between cached and uncached logits in serving.

A cost estimate

For a model with 32 layers, 32 heads of dimension 128 and 8 key/value heads, applying RoPE to one token costs 32 × (32 + 8) × 128 ≈ 164,000 rotated numbers, about 650,000 floating-point operations: under 0.01% of a 7-billion-parameter model's 14 billion per token. The cos/sin cache for 128,000 positions × 64 pairs is 8.2 million entries per table, 33 MB each in float32. Neither is a bottleneck; correctness, not cost, is what makes RoPE an interview topic.

The reference for this problem

Su and colleagues' "RoFormer: Enhanced Transformer with Rotary Position Embedding" (arXiv 2104.09864) introduced the method. Its abstract states that RoPE "encodes the absolute position with a rotation matrix and meanwhile incorporates the explicit relative position dependency in self-attention formulation", and lists its properties: "the flexibility of sequence length, decaying inter-token dependency with increasing relative distances, and the capability of equipping the linear self-attention with relative position encoding". The paper defines the frequencies as θ_i = 10000^(−2(i−1)/d) for pairs i = 1 … d/2, the same geometric schedule as the sinusoidal encoding it reviews, p_(i,2t) = sin(k/10000^(2t/d)) and p_(i,2t+1) = cos(k/10000^(2t/d)), from Vaswani and colleagues. This lesson implements the paper's adjacent-pair form, tests its relative-position property and its equivalence with the complex-number and rotate-half implementations used in practice.

Goal: run the solution, then vary it, break it and time it. Python 3.12 with NumPy only.

  1. Run the four blocks as one script. All assertions pass and it prints the hand scores, the float32 errors and the timing.
  2. Vary the inputs. With d = 4, compute by hand the scores at offsets 0, 1 and 2 for q = [1, 0, 0, 1] and k = [1, 0, 1, 0]. (Hint: the second pair starts 90 degrees apart.) Check with apply_rope.
  3. Break the property. Rotate queries with base 10,000 and keys with base 20,000, or keys at position n + 1; rerun the shifted-pairs test and explain which assertion fails.
  4. Break the convention. Apply apply_rope_half to queries and apply_rope to keys. Is the score still a function of the offset alone?
  5. Measure precision. Plot the maximum cosine error from float32 angles for positions 10³ to 10⁶, then repeat with angles built from bfloat16-rounded positions (round positions to 8 significant bits).
  6. Time it. Compare the matrix and pairwise implementations for T = 512, 2,048 and 8,192 with d = 128.

What to look for: the mismatched base or position breaks the shifted-pairs equality, mixed conventions break it too, float32 error grows roughly in proportion to position while bfloat16 is unusable beyond a few hundred positions, and the matrix form's cost grows with d² per token. For each experiment, write down the prediction first; the point of the lab is to confirm the reasoning, not just the numbers. Keep the scripts.

Work it out

A model was trained with RoPE to 4,096 tokens and you need 16,384. Implement position interpolation and NTK-aware base scaling, and check how each changes the angles of the fastest and slowest frequency pairs at position 16,383.

Answer 
L, L_new, dk, base = 4096, 16384, 128, 10000.0
s = L_new / L
edge = rope_angles(1, dk, offset=L)[0]                         # training covered angles below this, per pair

pi = rope_angles(1, dk, offset=L_new - 1, scale=1 / s)[0]     # squeeze positions into [0, L)
ntk = rope_angles(1, dk, base=base * s ** (dk / (dk - 2)), offset=L_new - 1)[0]
plain = rope_angles(1, dk, offset=L_new - 1)[0]

assert np.all(pi < edge)                                        # PI: every angle is in the trained range
assert plain[-1] > 3 * edge[-1]                                 # unscaled: slowest pair far out of range
assert np.isclose(ntk[-1], edge[-1], rtol=0.01)                 # NTK: slowest pair lands at the trained edge
assert np.isclose(ntk[0], plain[0]) and not np.isclose(pi[0], plain[0])   # NTK leaves the fastest pair alone

PI shrinks every frequency by 4×, so neighbouring tokens become 4× harder to tell apart, hence the fine-tuning it needs. NTK scaling changes the base so the slowest pair is exactly interpolated while the fastest pair is untouched, preserving local resolution; intermediate frequencies get intermediate treatment. YaRN makes that blend explicit per band and adds an attention temperature.
