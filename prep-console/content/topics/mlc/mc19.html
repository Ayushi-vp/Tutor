A language model cannot read text. It reads integers. Before a prompt reaches the network, a tokenizer turns it into a sequence of IDs, and after the model produces IDs, the tokenizer turns them back into text. That step decides how many tokens a prompt costs, how much text fits in the context window, why a model can write fluent English but stumbles when asked how many r's are in "strawberry", and why the same paragraph can cost three times as much in Hindi as in English. Almost every modern LLM tokenizer is a variant of byte-pair encoding (BPE), introduced to neural machine translation by Sennrich, Haddow and Birch in 2016 and made byte-level by GPT-2.

"Implement BPE" is a favourite LLM coding question because the algorithm fits in a page but has several precise details that interviewers can probe. Training merges the most frequent adjacent pair, but how are ties broken (different rules give different vocabularies)? Encoding must replay merges in the order they were learned, not greedily match the longest token. Decoding must assemble bytes before turning them into characters, or multi-byte characters are corrupted. Pre-tokenisation decides whether tokens can span words. And naive training recounts every pair after every merge, which is far too slow for a real corpus.

Tokenizer bugs are also expensive in practice. A tokenizer that does not exactly match the one a model was trained with produces token IDs the model has never seen in those combinations, and quality drops silently. A tokenizer that treats a user's literal "<|endoftext|>" as a control token is a prompt-injection hole. And a vocabulary trained mostly on English quietly makes the product several times more expensive for other languages.

Interviewers like the problem for one more reason: it rewards careful, testable engineering over cleverness. Every step has a definite right answer that can be checked against a reference implementation, so a candidate who writes small functions, states the tie-breaking rule and tests round trips on awkward inputs stands out quickly.

This lesson starts with the exact example from Sennrich et al.'s paper, four words with frequencies, counts the pairs by hand and reproduces the ten merges their script prints. It implements their regex-based Algorithm 1 as the naive trainer, then a byte-level trainer with a GPT-2-style pre-tokeniser, encoding by merge rank and decoding through the byte table, and checks it learns exactly the same tokens and ranks as the educational trainer inside OpenAI's tiktoken. It then adds an incremental trainer that is ten times faster with identical output, shows the multi-byte decoding trap, and in the drill measures how unevenly an English-trained tokenizer treats Hindi.

Think of compressing a long text by inventing abbreviations. Find the pair of letters that appears next to each other most often, say "t" followed by "h", invent a new symbol "th" for it, and rewrite the text with that symbol. Now look again: perhaps "th" followed by "e" is the most common pair, so invent "the". Keep going. After a few thousand rounds you have a vocabulary of fragments, from single letters up to whole common words, and any text can be written with them. That is byte-pair encoding: originally a 1994 data-compression trick, adapted for language models because it produces a vocabulary of frequent fragments that balances two goals: common words become single tokens, rare words split into a few reusable pieces, and nothing is ever unrepresentable.

The list of invented abbreviations, in the order they were invented, is the tokenizer. To tokenise new text, start from single letters (or bytes) and apply the abbreviations in the same order they were invented: wherever the first-invented pair occurs, merge it; then the second; and so on. Applying them in that order, rather than looking for the longest known fragment, guarantees that a word is split the same way every time, the same way it was split during training.

Two refinements turn this into a GPT-style tokenizer. First, the base symbols are bytes, not letters. Every text, in any language, with any emoji, is a sequence of UTF-8 bytes, and there are only 256 byte values, so the starting vocabulary covers everything and there is no such thing as an unknown character. Second, the text is first cut into word-like chunks by a regular expression (a word with its leading space, a number, a run of punctuation), and pairs are only counted and merged within chunks. That keeps tokens from spanning words, so "dog" followed by a full stop does not become a single token "dog.".

The resulting numbers are concrete. GPT-2's tokenizer has 50,257 tokens: the 256 bytes, 50,000 learned merges and one end-of-text marker. A common English word such as " the" is one token; a rare word such as " quixotic" is a few; a long German compound or an unusual name may be many. On ordinary English text the average is roughly four characters per token, which is where the familiar rule of thumb comes from, and it is a property of this particular training process, not of language.

Where the abbreviation picture breaks: BPE never asks whether a fragment means anything. It is purely a frequency count, and the pieces it produces are useful because frequent strings in language tend to be meaningful (suffixes, stems, common words), not because the algorithm knows any linguistics. It also depends on the training text: fragments common in the training language become tokens, and other languages are left as many small pieces.

1. The problem as it is usually asked

"Implement a byte-level BPE tokenizer: train(text, vocab_size), encode(text) and decode(ids). Encoding then decoding must return the original text for any input." Clarify: byte-level or character-level (byte-level, GPT-style, unless told otherwise); whether to pre-tokenise with a regex (yes); how to break ties (first pair encountered, to be reproducible); whether special tokens are needed; and corpus size (it decides whether the naive trainer is acceptable). Then outline: pre-tokenise and count chunks, byte sequences per chunk, repeated pair counting and merging, encode by merge rank, decode by bytes, tests.

2. The maths in plain words

Training. Split the corpus into chunks and count how many times each distinct chunk occurs. Represent each chunk as a list of symbols, initially its bytes. Count every adjacent pair of symbols across all chunks, adding each chunk's frequency for each occurrence of the pair inside it. The pair with the highest count becomes a new symbol with the next unused ID; replace every non-overlapping occurrence of the pair, left to right, in every chunk. Record the merge. Repeat until the vocabulary has the requested size, or until no pair occurs at least twice (a pair seen once saves nothing).

Encoding. For each chunk of new text, start from its bytes. Look at every adjacent pair; among those that appear in the merge table, find the one learned earliest (lowest rank) and merge it, wherever it occurs. Repeat until no adjacent pair is in the table. The result is a list of token IDs.

Decoding. Each base ID 0-255 is one byte; each merged ID's bytes are the concatenation of its two parts' bytes. Map every ID to its bytes, join them, and decode the whole byte string as UTF-8.

3. The equations, as pseudocode

train(text, n_merges):
  chunks = Counter(pretokenise(text))                      # distinct chunk → frequency
  seq[c] = list(c.encode("utf-8"))                          # base ids 0..255
  for new_id in 256 .. 256 + n_merges − 1:
      count[(a, b)] = Σ_chunks freq[c] · #occurrences of (a, b) in seq[c]
      (a, b) = argmax count       (ties: the pair counted first)
      stop if count[(a, b)] < 2
      merges[(a, b)] = new_id;  replace (a, b) by new_id in every seq, left to right

encode(text):   for each chunk: ids = bytes; while some adjacent pair is in merges:
                   merge the pair with the smallest merges[pair] (earliest learned)
decode(ids):    bytes(vocab[i] for i in ids).decode("utf-8")
                vocab[i] = bytes([i]) for i < 256;  vocab[new_id] = vocab[a] + vocab[b]
vocabulary size = 256 + number of merges (+ special tokens)

Why does encoding by rank reproduce training? During training, the merge with rank r was applied to all chunks after merges 0 to r − 1 and before r + 1 onwards. Applying the lowest-rank available merge first at encoding time replays exactly that sequence for each chunk, so a chunk seen in training is segmented exactly as it was then; the tests verify this for every training chunk. Greedy longest-match does not have this property.

4. A worked example by hand

Sennrich, Haddow and Birch illustrate BPE with a four-word dictionary, each word split into characters with an end-of-word marker </w>, weighted by frequency: low 5, lower 2, newest 6, widest 3.

  • Count pairs. In "n e w e s t </w>" (frequency 6) and "w i d e s t </w>" (3), the pairs e s, s t and t </w> each occur once per word: 6 + 3 = 9. w e occurs in "newest" (6) and "lower" (2): 8. l o and o w occur in "low" and "lower": 5 + 2 = 7. Everything else is lower.
  • Merge 1. Three pairs tie at 9. The paper's script uses Python's max over a dictionary, which returns the first maximum in insertion order; counting words in order, e s is seen first. New symbol es. "newest" becomes "n e w es t </w>".
  • Merges 2 and 3. Now es t has 9 (it replaced s t), so est; then est </w> has 9, so est</w>. Both superlatives now end in one symbol.
  • Merges 4 and 5. Recount. w e has dropped to 2, because in "n e w est</w>" the w is now followed by the merged symbol est</w>, so only "lower" still contributes a w e. The highest counts are now l o and o w at 7: l o (counted first) merges to lo, and then lo w has 7 and merges to low.
  • Merges 6 to 10. n e (6) → ne, ne w (6) → new, new est</w> (6) → newest</w>, low </w> (5) → low</w>, and w i (3) → wi.

Those are exactly the ten merges the paper's Algorithm 1 prints, and the first block asserts them along with the initial counts. The paper's Figure 1 uses a second dictionary, {low, lowest, newer, wider}, and shows that the unseen word "lower" is segmented as low er</w>. With every word at frequency 1 there are many ties; this lesson's run learns lo, low, er, er</w> where the figure lists r</w>, lo, low, er</w>, a different tie order, but both produce the same segmentation of "lower", which the tests check. Ties matter; outcomes often do not.

import collections
import re
import time

# The hand-worked example: Sennrich et al.'s Algorithm 1 vocabulary (word: frequency).
vocab0 = {"l o w </w>": 5, "l o w e r </w>": 2, "n e w e s t </w>": 6, "w i d e s t </w>": 3}

def get_stats(vocab):
    pairs = collections.defaultdict(int)
    for word, freq in vocab.items():
        symbols = word.split()
        for i in range(len(symbols) - 1):
            pairs[symbols[i], symbols[i + 1]] += freq
    return pairs

stats = get_stats(vocab0)
assert stats["e", "s"] == stats["s", "t"] == stats["t", "</w>"] == 9      # newest 6 + widest 3
assert stats["w", "e"] == 8 and stats["l", "o"] == stats["o", "w"] == 7
assert max(stats, key=stats.get) == ("e", "s")                            # ties: first counted wins

5. The naive implementation: the paper's Algorithm 1

Sennrich et al. give a minimal Python implementation, reproduced here: words are strings of space-separated symbols, get_stats counts pairs weighted by frequency, and merge_vocab rewrites every word with a regular expression that replaces the chosen pair (as whole symbols) with their concatenation.

def merge_vocab(pair, v_in):
    """Sennrich et al. (2016), Algorithm 1: rewrite every word with a regex, once per merge."""
    v_out = {}
    bigram = re.escape(" ".join(pair))
    p = re.compile(r"(?<!\S)" + bigram + r"(?!\S)")
    for word in v_in:
        v_out[p.sub("".join(pair), word)] = v_in[word]
    return v_out

def learn_bpe_naive(vocab, num_merges):
    merges = []
    for _ in range(num_merges):
        pairs = get_stats(vocab)
        if not pairs:
            break
        best = max(pairs, key=pairs.get)
        vocab = merge_vocab(best, vocab)
        merges.append(best)
    return merges, vocab

It is short and correct, and it is the version to sketch first in an interview. Its cost: every merge recounts every pair of every word and rewrites every word with a regex, so training is O(merges × total symbols) with a large constant. The paper notes that "in practice, we increase efficiency by indexing all pairs, and updating data structures incrementally", which is what section 7 does.

6. The byte-level tokenizer

# a simplified GPT-2 pre-tokeniser: words with their leading space, numbers, punctuation, whitespace
SPLIT_PAT = r" ?[^\W\d_]+| ?\d+| ?[^\s\w]+|_+|\s+(?!\S)|\s+"
SPLIT = re.compile(SPLIT_PAT)

def chunks(text, pretokenize=True):
    return SPLIT.findall(text) if pretokenize else [text]

def merge(ids, pair, new_id):
    """Replace every non-overlapping occurrence of `pair`, left to right."""
    out, i = [], 0
    while i < len(ids):
        if i + 1 < len(ids) and ids[i] == pair[0] and ids[i + 1] == pair[1]:
            out.append(new_id); i += 2
        else:
            out.append(ids[i]); i += 1
    return out

def train_bpe(text, n_merges, pretokenize=True):
    words = collections.Counter(chunks(text, pretokenize))   # each distinct chunk once, with its count
    seqs = {w: list(w.encode("utf-8")) for w in words}      # 256 byte tokens: nothing is ever unknown
    merges = {}                                             # (a, b) -> new id, in the order learned
    for new_id in range(256, 256 + n_merges):
        counts = collections.Counter()
        for w, freq in words.items():                       # pairs never cross a chunk boundary
            for pair in zip(seqs[w], seqs[w][1:]):
                counts[pair] += freq
        if not counts:
            break
        pair = max(counts, key=counts.get)                  # ties: first pair counted wins
        if counts[pair] < 2:
            break                                           # a pair seen once compresses nothing
        merges[pair] = new_id
        seqs = {w: merge(ids, pair, new_id) for w, ids in seqs.items()}
    return merges, seqs

def train_bpe_incremental(text, n_merges, pretokenize=True):
    """Same merges as train_bpe, but after each merge only the words containing the merged pair are
    re-counted. An index from pair to words makes that lookup cheap."""
    words = list(collections.Counter(chunks(text, pretokenize)).items())   # first-occurrence order
    seqs = [list(w.encode("utf-8")) for w, _ in words]
    freq = [f for _, f in words]
    counts = collections.Counter()
    where = collections.defaultdict(set)                   # pair -> indices of words containing it
    for wi, ids in enumerate(seqs):
        for pair in zip(ids, ids[1:]):
            counts[pair] += freq[wi]; where[pair].add(wi)
    def first_seen(pair):                                  # tie-break exactly like a full recount
        wi = min(where[pair])
        ids = seqs[wi]
        return wi, next(i for i in range(len(ids) - 1) if (ids[i], ids[i + 1]) == pair)
    merges = {}
    for new_id in range(256, 256 + n_merges):
        if not counts:
            break
        top = max(counts.values())
        if top < 2:
            break
        pair = min((p for p, c in counts.items() if c == top), key=first_seen)
        merges[pair] = new_id
        for wi in list(where[pair]):                       # only the affected words
            old = seqs[wi]
            for p in zip(old, old[1:]):
                counts[p] -= freq[wi]
                if counts[p] == 0:
                    del counts[p]
                where[p].discard(wi)
            seqs[wi] = new = merge(old, pair, new_id)
            for p in zip(new, new[1:]):
                counts[p] += freq[wi]; where[p].add(wi)
    return merges

def encode_chunk(chunk, merges):
    ids = list(chunk.encode("utf-8"))
    while len(ids) >= 2:
        pair = min(zip(ids, ids[1:]), key=lambda p: merges.get(p, float("inf")))   # earliest-learned merge
        if pair not in merges:
            break
        ids = merge(ids, pair, merges[pair])
    return ids

def encode(text, merges, pretokenize=True):
    return [t for c in chunks(text, pretokenize) for t in encode_chunk(c, merges)]

def build_vocab(merges):
    vocab = {i: bytes([i]) for i in range(256)}
    for (a, b), new_id in sorted(merges.items(), key=lambda kv: kv[1]):
        vocab[new_id] = vocab[a] + vocab[b]
    return vocab

def decode(ids, merges):
    vocab = build_vocab(merges)
    return b"".join(vocab[i] for i in ids).decode("utf-8", errors="replace")

Pre-tokeniser. SPLIT is a simplified GPT-2 pattern: an optional space followed by letters, an optional space followed by digits, an optional space followed by punctuation, underscores, and whitespace (with a lookahead that leaves a single space attached to the following word). findall covers every character, so joining the chunks gives back the original text exactly, which the tests check.

train_bpe counts distinct chunks, stores each as a list of byte IDs, and in each round counts pairs weighted by chunk frequency, takes the most frequent (Python's max over a Counter returns the first maximum in insertion order, the same tie rule as the paper), stops if it occurs fewer than twice, records the merge and rewrites every chunk with merge, which replaces non-overlapping occurrences left to right.

train_bpe_incremental learns identical merges faster: it keeps the pair counts and an index from each pair to the chunks that contain it, and after a merge it updates only those chunks, subtracting their old pairs and adding their new ones. To reproduce the full recount's tie-breaking exactly, it resolves ties by the position where each tied pair would be met first in a full recount.

encode_chunk repeatedly finds the adjacent pair with the lowest merge rank (missing pairs rank as infinity) and merges it; build_vocab reconstructs every ID's bytes; decode joins bytes and decodes UTF-8 once, with errors="replace" so that a sequence of IDs that does not form valid UTF-8 (possible if a model emits a broken sequence) still decodes.

Counting tokens for cost and limits

Because providers bill and limit by tokens, counting them correctly is a production task in its own right. The count depends on the exact tokenizer: the same English paragraph can be 10-20% fewer tokens under a newer, larger vocabulary, and much more different for code, numbers and other languages. Character-based estimates ("about four characters per token") are fine for rough budgeting in English and badly wrong elsewhere; the drill shows an English-trained tokenizer spending one token per byte on Hindi. Chat formats add their own tokens for roles and separators, so a request's true count is the encoded conversation plus that template overhead. The reliable approach is to count with the model's own tokenizer, on the fully formatted request, and to measure tokens per character per language on real traffic.

7. Testing it

The tests check: Algorithm 1 on the paper's vocabulary produces the ten merges the paper's script prints; on the Figure 1 dictionary, the unseen "lower" is segmented as low er</w>; the textbook string "aaabdaaabac" merges "aa" first; on a test paragraph, the naive regex trainer (with a visible symbol substituted for spaces, GPT-2's trick) and the byte-level trainer learn the same 60 merges, and the incremental trainer learns them too; OpenAI's tiktoken._educational.bpe_train, given the same text and the same regex, learns exactly the same tokens with the same ranks, and its bpe_encode produces the same IDs; encoding each training chunk reproduces its training segmentation; five strings, including unseen words, an emoji, Devanagari, underscores, double spaces and the empty string, survive a round trip; the text compresses to fewer than a third as many tokens as bytes (393 tokens for 1,568 bytes); " the" is a token and no learned token contains a space after its first byte; without pre-tokenisation, a token spanning "fox jumps over" appears; and the euro sign decodes correctly from joined bytes but as three replacement characters when decoded token by token. Finally, the timing.

Matching tiktoken's educational trainer is a strong test because the two implementations were written independently and agree only if every detail matches: chunking, byte-level start, frequency weighting, the non-overlapping left-to-right merge, and above all the tie-breaking rule. A single difference in tie-breaking changes some merge, and every later merge can then differ.

8. Practical traps

Trap 1: tie-breaking. Equal counts are common, especially early on small corpora. "First counted wins" depends on iteration order, which depends on how the corpus was read; reproducing someone else's tokenizer requires their exact rule, and a sort by count alone, or a set, makes training non-deterministic.

Trap 2: overlapping pairs. In "aaa", the pair (a, a) occurs twice when counting but can be merged only once, left to right, giving [aa, a]. Counting overlapping occurrences while merging non-overlapping ones is the convention everyone follows; deviating changes the merges.

Trap 3: decoding multi-byte characters. In the test, "€" is three bytes and three tokens; decoding each token separately gives three U+FFFD replacement characters. When streaming generated tokens to a user, keep incomplete UTF-8 bytes buffered until the character completes.

Trap 4: encoding speed. The encode_chunk here scans all pairs per merge, quadratic in chunk length. Fine for words; for very long chunks (a megabyte of text with no spaces) production encoders cap chunk length or use a priority queue over pairs with a linked list of symbols, making encoding O(n log n).

Trap 5: whitespace and normalisation. Different handling of leading spaces, Unicode normalisation (NFC versus NFD forms of accented characters) or newline conventions produce different byte sequences and therefore different tokens. A tokenizer is defined by its regex, normalisation and merges together; change any one and the model sees unfamiliar input.

Trap 6: special tokens in user text. If the encoder turns the literal text of a control token into its special ID, user input can forge structure. Encode user text with special tokens disabled.

Adding special tokens

Real tokenizers reserve IDs beyond the merges for control tokens: end of text, start and end of chat turns, tool-call delimiters, padding. They are added after training, with IDs above the last merge, and handled outside BPE: the encoder first splits the input on the literal special-token strings (only when explicitly allowed), encodes the ordinary text between them with BPE, and inserts the reserved IDs; the decoder maps those IDs back to their strings. Because special tokens never come from merges, ordinary text can never accidentally produce one, which is exactly the property that keeps users from forging conversation structure. Adding new special tokens to an existing model means adding rows to its embedding table and fine-tuning so the model learns what they mean.

9. Edge cases worth naming

The empty string encodes to no tokens and decodes back to the empty string. A chunk of one byte cannot merge. A text with no repeated pairs stops training immediately because no pair occurs twice. Invalid UTF-8 cannot come from str.encode, but a model can emit token sequences whose bytes are invalid, which is why decode uses errors="replace". Very large vocabularies can reach a point where the most frequent pair occurs only a handful of times, producing tokens that appear almost nowhere else, an argument for stopping at a minimum count. And case matters: "The" and "the" are different byte sequences and different tokens unless the training text makes them share merges.

10. Complexity

Pre-tokenising and counting the corpus is O(corpus length) once. The full-recount trainer then costs O(M × S) for M merges and S total symbols across distinct chunks, recounting and rewriting everything each round. The incremental trainer costs, per merge, time proportional to the chunks containing the merged pair, usually a small fraction; on the test's 3,718 distinct random words, 60 merges took 56 ms incrementally against 594 ms for a full recount and 455 ms for the regex version, with identical results. Encoding a chunk of length n with the simple scan is O(n²) in the worst case, O(n log n) with a priority queue. Memory is the merge table and vocabulary, a few megabytes for 100,000 tokens.

11. Talking through the design

In the interview: explain BPE as compression by repeated pair merging; work a few merges by hand with counts and the tie rule; write the trainer over chunk frequencies with byte-level base symbols; write encode by merge rank and explain why not longest match; write decode by bytes; test round trips including emoji and the empty string. Then volunteer the senior points: the pre-tokeniser regex and why it matters, incremental training, streaming decode of multi-byte characters, special-token injection, and multilingual fairness. State your tie rule out loud before writing the training loop, since it is the detail most often probed.

Subword regularisation and BPE-dropout

Deterministic BPE always splits a word the same way, so a model never sees alternative segmentations and can be brittle to small spelling variations. Kudo's subword regularisation samples segmentations from a Unigram model during training; BPE-dropout achieves something similar for BPE by randomly skipping merges while encoding training data, so the same word sometimes appears in smaller pieces. Both act as data augmentation and improve robustness, especially for low-resource translation. At inference, encoding is deterministic again. These methods show that the segmentation is a modelling choice, not a fixed fact about the text.

Encoding efficiently at scale

The simple encoder rescans every adjacent pair after each merge, which is quadratic in chunk length. Production encoders keep the chunk as a doubly linked list of symbols and a priority queue of adjacent pairs keyed by merge rank; merging a pair removes two nodes, inserts one, and updates only the two neighbouring pairs in the queue, so a chunk of n bytes encodes in O(n log n). They also cache the encodings of frequent chunks (most words recur), so the BPE loop runs only for chunks not seen before. tiktoken combines both with a compiled regex, which is why it can encode text at many megabytes per second while this lesson's pure-Python version manages far less.

What the tokenizer is trained on

A tokenizer is trained once, usually on a sample of the model's pre-training mix, and then frozen for the model's lifetime: changing it later invalidates the embedding table. The sample's composition decides which languages, programming languages and formats get efficient merges. Teams balance the sample deliberately (upweighting code or under-represented languages), deduplicate it (a few repeated boilerplate strings can otherwise claim many merges), and check fertility by language before committing, because a tokenizer mistake is one of the few training decisions that cannot be fixed by more training.

Why frequency-based merging works

BPE greedily maximises compression: each merge reduces the total number of symbols in the corpus by the merged pair's count, the largest reduction available at that step. Frequent strings in natural language are disproportionately meaningful (common words, inflectional endings, frequent stems), so a compression objective yields a linguistically plausible vocabulary without any linguistic input. Sennrich et al.'s motivation was open-vocabulary translation: rare words and names can be represented as sequences of subword units instead of a single unknown token, and their translation experiments showed better handling of rare words than back-off dictionaries.

Byte-level BPE and the GPT-2 regex

GPT-2 made the base vocabulary the 256 bytes, so any string is encodable, and added a pre-tokeniser regex so merges stay within categories: contractions ('s, 't, 're), letters with an optional leading space, digits, other symbols, and whitespace. GPT-2's vocabulary has 50,257 tokens: 256 bytes, 50,000 merges and one end-of-text token. Later OpenAI tokenisers refined the regex (for example limiting digit runs to three characters, which makes numbers split more regularly) and grew the vocabulary to about 100,000 (cl100k) and about 200,000 (o200k) tokens. The pattern this lesson uses is a simplified version; matching a real tokenizer requires its exact regex, which is why the test passes the same pattern to tiktoken's trainer.

WordPiece, Unigram and SentencePiece

WordPiece (BERT) builds a vocabulary similarly but chooses merges by a likelihood-based score rather than raw frequency, and encodes with greedy longest-match from the left. The Unigram language-model tokenizer (Kudo) starts with a large candidate vocabulary and prunes it, keeping the pieces that best explain the corpus under a unigram model; it can produce several segmentations with probabilities, which enables subword regularisation (sampling segmentations during training). SentencePiece is a library that implements BPE and Unigram directly on raw text, treating spaces as an ordinary symbol, which makes it language-independent and lossless; many multilingual models use it.

Tokenisation and model behaviour

Because the model sees tokens, anything about characters inside a token must be learned indirectly: spelling, rhyming, counting letters, reversing strings. Numbers split into irregular chunks make arithmetic harder, which is why some tokenisers split digits individually or into fixed groups. Leading-space conventions mean "dog" at the start of a line and " dog" after a space are different tokens, a source of odd prompt sensitivity. "Glitch tokens" are tokens that occurred in the tokenizer's training data but almost never in the model's, so their embeddings are barely trained and the model behaves strangely when it sees them. All of these are tokenizer facts with model consequences.

Multilingual fairness and fertility

Fertility, tokens per word or per character, varies widely across languages for a tokenizer trained mostly on English. Petrov and colleagues measured that the same content can require several times more tokens in some languages, which means proportionally higher API cost, shorter effective context and slower generation for those users. The drill reproduces the effect in miniature: an English-trained tokenizer leaves Hindi at the byte level, one token per byte, three per Devanagari character. Larger vocabularies trained on balanced multilingual data reduce the gap, part of why recent vocabularies grew to 200,000 tokens and more.

Where tokenizers run

At both ends of every LLM call: on the client or server to count tokens for billing and context limits, before the model to encode prompts, and after it to stream decoded text. Production tokenizers are written in Rust or C++ (tiktoken, Hugging Face tokenizers, SentencePiece) because encoding is on the latency path and training on terabytes must be fast, but they implement exactly the algorithm in this lesson.

Failure modes and symptoms

Tokenizer-model mismatch. A model served with a slightly different tokenizer (another version's regex, a different normalisation, a missing merge) produces coherent but noticeably worse output. Detection: encode a reference text and compare IDs with the tokenizer shipped alongside the checkpoint; any difference is a bug.

Broken characters in streamed output. Users see replacement characters (�) mid-word in non-English or emoji-heavy responses. Cause: decoding each streamed token separately. Fix: buffer incomplete UTF-8 sequences. Python's incremental UTF-8 decoder (codecs.getincrementaldecoder) does exactly this: feed it each token's bytes and it emits only complete characters.

Context limits hit early for some users. Token counts per message are far higher for some languages or for code with unusual formatting. Measure tokens per character by language and content type; budget context accordingly; consider a model with a more efficient tokenizer for those users. Report the numbers per language to product teams, since a context limit that is generous in English can be tight in Hindi, Thai or Amharic, and pricing per token passes that difference straight to users.

Prompt injection through special tokens. User text containing a literal control-token string is encoded as the control token. Encode untrusted text with special tokens disallowed; tiktoken raises an error by default when it sees special-token text unless told otherwise.

Counting mistakes in billing and limits. Client-side estimates that use a different tokenizer from the server (or count characters) disagree with the server's count and cause rejected requests or wrong bills. Use the model's own tokenizer for counting.

Silent tokenizer upgrades. A library update changes a default (a regex, a normalisation step, how whitespace is stripped) and token IDs shift for some inputs. The model still runs, but behaves slightly differently, and cached prompts or stored token counts become wrong. Pin the tokenizer version with the model, store a hash of the merge table and regex, and keep an exact-ID regression test on a reference corpus that includes code, emoji, numbers and several scripts.

Mismatched chat templates. The tokenizer is correct but the conversation is formatted with the wrong role markers or separators, so the model sees an unfamiliar structure and follows instructions poorly. The template that turns messages into text (and therefore tokens) ships with the model just like the merges; apply the model's own template and compare its token IDs with the reference implementation for a sample conversation.

What to monitor

Tokens per request and per character by language and product surface, the rate of replacement characters in outputs, encoding latency at the tail, and, after any tokenizer or model update, an exact-match check of token IDs on a fixed reference corpus.

A cost estimate

A tokenizer with 100,000 tokens and a model dimension of 4,096 has an embedding table of 410 million parameters, and the output layer the same unless tied; doubling the vocabulary adds that much again. On the other side, a better vocabulary that cuts tokens per text by 15% cuts the cost of every forward pass, attention's quadratic term by about 28%, and every API bill by 15%. That trade-off, a bigger table against shorter sequences, is the main lever tokenizer designers pull.

The reference for this problem

Sennrich, Haddow and Birch's "Neural Machine Translation of Rare Words with Subword Units" (ACL 2016, arXiv 1508.07909) adapted byte pair encoding, "a simple data compression technique that iteratively replaces the most frequent pair of bytes in a sequence with a single, unused byte", to word segmentation: initialise the symbol vocabulary with characters plus an end-of-word symbol, "iteratively count all symbol pairs and replace each occurrence of the most frequent pair ('A', 'B') with a new symbol 'AB'", with "the final symbol vocabulary size … equal to the size of the initial vocabulary, plus the number of merge operations". Their Algorithm 1 is a minimal Python implementation over the vocabulary {'l o w </w>': 5, 'l o w e r </w>': 2, 'n e w e s t </w>': 6, 'w i d e s t </w>': 3}, which this lesson reproduces exactly; their Figure 1 shows merges learned from {low, lowest, newer, wider} and the segmentation of the unseen word "lower" into "low er</w>". They note that "for efficiency, we do not consider pairs that cross word boundaries", the role of the pre-tokeniser here, and that "in practice, we increase efficiency by indexing all pairs, and updating data structures incrementally", the incremental trainer here.

Goal: run the solution, then vary it, break it and time it. Python 3.12 with NumPy-free standard library (tiktoken only for the optional cross-check).

  1. Run the four blocks as one script. All assertions pass, including the tiktoken comparison, and it prints the paper's merges, the first byte-level merges and the trainer timings.
  2. Vary the inputs. Add "lowest: 4" to the paper's vocabulary and work the first four merges by hand (counts first), then check with learn_bpe_naive.
  3. Break the tie rule. Replace max(counts, key=counts.get) with a choice that breaks ties by the larger pair, and rerun the tiktoken comparison. How many of the 60 merges still match?
  4. Break encoding. Write a greedy longest-match encoder over the learned vocabulary and find a training chunk it segments differently from encode_chunk.
  5. Break decoding. Stream-decode a sentence containing "€", "नमस्ते" and an emoji token by token, then add a buffer that holds incomplete UTF-8 bytes and confirm the output is clean.
  6. Time it. Compare the three trainers on 4,000, 16,000 and 64,000 random words with 60 and 200 merges.

What to look for: changed tie-breaking diverges early and every later merge can follow; longest-match disagrees on words whose pieces were merged in a different order than their length suggests; buffering removes every replacement character; and the incremental trainer's advantage grows with the number of distinct words. Record the merge lists, not just the timings, and the encoded IDs of a fixed sentence, so you can see exactly where two variants diverge.

Work it out

Train your tokenizer on English only, then measure characters per token on an English sentence and on a Hindi sentence of similar meaning. What does the difference mean for cost and context length, and what would fix it?

Answer 
english = "the weather is very nice today and we are going to the market together. "
hindi = "आज मौसम बहुत अच्छा है और हम साथ में बाज़ार जा रहे हैं। "
merges_en, _ = train_bpe(english * 30, 80)

def chars_per_token(s, m):
    return len(s) / len(encode(s, m))

en_rate, hi_rate = chars_per_token(english, merges_en), chars_per_token(hindi, merges_en)
assert en_rate > 3 * hi_rate                       # English packs several characters per token
assert len(encode(hindi, merges_en)) == len(hindi.encode("utf-8"))   # Hindi falls back to raw bytes

No merge ever involved Devanagari bytes, so every Hindi character costs its full three UTF-8 bytes as three tokens, while English packs several characters into each token. The same sentence is several times more tokens in Hindi: higher API cost, less text per context window, slower generation. The fix is in the tokenizer's training mix: include the languages you serve, or use a larger multilingual vocabulary, which is why newer models with 200k-token vocabularies show big efficiency gains for Indic languages.
