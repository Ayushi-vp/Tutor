A company wants its own version of an open 7-billion-parameter model: one that writes in the house style, follows the company's JSON formats and knows its product names. Full fine-tuning would update all 7 billion weights. With the Adam optimizer that means storing the weights, their gradients and two optimizer values per weight, roughly 16 bytes per parameter in mixed precision, over 100 GB of GPU memory before activations, and a 14 GB checkpoint for every variant. Ten customer-specific variants would be ten full copies of the model. Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) changes the arithmetic: freeze the pretrained weights, and learn each layer's change as the product of two thin matrices. The trainable part becomes a fraction of a percent of the model, the checkpoint shrinks to tens of megabytes, and many variants can share one copy of the base model.

LoRA's paper put numbers on it for GPT-3 175B: 10,000 times fewer trainable parameters and 3 times less GPU memory than fine-tuning with Adam, training memory down from 1.2 TB to 350 GB, checkpoints from 350 GB to 35 MB, and no extra inference latency once the update is merged. QLoRA then quantised the frozen base to 4 bits, putting a 65-billion-parameter fine-tune on a single 48 GB GPU. Today LoRA and its variants are the default way most teams customise open models.

The storage side matters as much as the training side. A product team that keeps a fine-tuned variant per customer, per language or per feature would otherwise keep a full copy of the model for each one, and load and unload whole models as traffic shifts. With LoRA, every variant is a file of tens of megabytes that can be loaded next to a shared base model in a fraction of a second.

As an interview question, "implement a LoRA layer" checks whether you understand both the linear algebra and the engineering. Why must B start at zero and A random (and what happens if both are zero)? Why compute (x·A)·B rather than x·(A·B)? What are the gradients, and why does A receive none on the first step? What does the α/r scale do? How is the adapter merged for serving, and why can merging in float16 quietly lose the update? And how much memory does a QLoRA run need?

This lesson works one training step by hand on a 2 × 2 example, then writes a naive layer that materialises the full update and the factored layer used in practice, derives the gradients and checks them against finite differences and PyTorch, shows that rank-4 adapters recover a rank-4 change exactly while rank-2 ones cannot, merges and unmerges, demonstrates the float16 merge trap, times the factored path (62 times cheaper than materialising here), and in the drill computes QLoRA memory for a 65B model.

Imagine a large, finished painting that you are not allowed to touch, and a sheet of transparent film laid over it on which you may draw. The film lets you change what the viewer sees without altering the original, and you can lift it off, swap in a different film for a different client, or eventually press it permanently onto the canvas. LoRA is the film for neural networks: the pretrained weights stay frozen, and a small, trainable correction is added on top of each chosen layer.

The clever part is the shape of the correction. A layer's weight is a large matrix, say 4,096 by 4,096, nearly 17 million numbers. A full correction of the same size would be just as large. LoRA instead writes the correction as the product of two thin matrices: A, 4,096 by r, and B, r by 4,096, with r small (8, say). Their product is a full 4,096 × 4,096 matrix, but a special one: every column is a combination of only r directions, so it is a low-rank matrix. A and B together hold 8 × 8,192 = 65,536 numbers, 0.39% of the full matrix. The bet behind LoRA is that the change fine-tuning needs is mostly low-rank: adapting a model to a task mainly turns up or down a few directions it already represents, rather than rewriting everything.

What does "rank" mean concretely? Take a tiny 3 × 3 example. The matrix with rows [1, 2, 3], [2, 4, 6] and [3, 6, 9] looks like nine independent numbers, but every row is a multiple of [1, 2, 3]: it is the column [1, 2, 3] times the row [1, 2, 3]. It has rank 1 and can be stored as six numbers instead of nine. A rank-2 matrix is a sum of two such outer products, and so on. For a 3 × 3 matrix the saving is small; for a 4,096 × 4,096 matrix of rank 8 it is the difference between 16.8 million numbers and 65,536. The singular value decomposition from mc8 tells you how close any matrix is to a low-rank one: if a few singular values dominate, a low-rank approximation captures most of it.

Two design choices make it work smoothly. The correction starts at exactly zero: B is initialised to zero, so at the first step the adapted model behaves identically to the original, and training only adds what the task needs. A, by contrast, starts random, because a correction of the form A·B needs A to be non-zero for B to learn anything. And the correction is scaled by α/r, a constant that keeps its effective size similar when you try different ranks.

The payoffs are practical. Only A and B need gradients and optimizer state, so training memory drops sharply. The saved adapter is tiny, so one base model can serve many tasks by swapping adapters. And when you want maximum speed, you can merge the correction into the weight, W + (α/r)·A·B, leaving a single matrix with no extra cost at inference. Where the film analogy breaks: a LoRA update is not limited to small cosmetic changes. With a large enough learning rate it can change behaviour substantially, but only along r directions per layer, which is both its efficiency and its limit.

1. The problem as it is usually asked

"Implement a LoRA linear layer in NumPy: forward, gradients for the adapter parameters, and merging. Show it can learn a low-rank change and that merging does not change outputs." Clarify: the convention for shapes (here W is d_in × d_out and y = x·W, as in NumPy-style code; PyTorch's nn.Linear stores the transpose); the scaling (α/r); whether biases are adapted (usually not); the initialisation (A random, B zero). Outline: the class with a factored forward, hand-derived gradients, merge and unmerge, and tests including a gradient check and a rank-capacity experiment.

2. The maths in plain words

The adapted layer's output is the frozen layer's output plus a correction: multiply the input by A to get a short vector of length r, multiply that by B to get back to the output size, scale by α/r, and add. Because the frozen weight is never updated, its gradient is never computed or stored.

The gradients follow the linear-layer rule from mc11 twice. Call the bottleneck h = x·A. B is multiplied by h, so B's gradient is the bottleneck's transpose times the output gradient, scaled by α/r. The bottleneck receives the output gradient times B's transpose, scaled; A is multiplied by x, so A's gradient is x's transpose times that. Notice the consequence of initialising B to zero: on the first step the bottleneck receives zero gradient, so A's gradient is zero and only B moves; from the second step on, B is non-zero and both train.

Merging is ordinary matrix addition: the product A·B is a matrix of W's shape; scale it and add it to W, and the layer becomes a plain linear layer again with exactly the same outputs. Subtracting the same product undoes it, which is how one base model can switch between adapters.

3. The equations

forward:     h = x·A                      (N, r)          A: (d_in, r), random
             y = x·W + s·h·B              (N, d_out)      B: (r, d_out), zero at init,  s = α/r
backward:    dB = s·hᵀ·dy                 (r, d_out)
             dh = s·dy·Bᵀ                 (N, r)
             dA = xᵀ·dh                   (d_in, r)
             dW = none (frozen);   dx = dy·Wᵀ + dh·Aᵀ   (if earlier layers need it)

merge:       W ← W + s·A·B                one d_in × d_out matrix, no inference overhead
unmerge:     W ← W − s·A·B

trainable parameters:  r·(d_in + d_out)   versus d_in·d_out
cost of the side path: N·r·(d_in + d_out) multiply-adds  versus  d_in·r·d_out + N·d_in·d_out if ΔW is built

Because the update A·B has rank at most r, LoRA can represent any change of rank up to r exactly and nothing beyond it. That gives a crisp test: generate a target produced by a rank-4 change, and rank-4 adapters should fit it to numerical precision while rank-2 adapters cannot; the tests do exactly this.

4. A worked example by hand

A 2 × 2 layer with W = the identity, rank r = 1 and α = 1, so the scale α/r is 1. Take A = [1, 0]ᵀ (random in general, fixed here for easy numbers) and B = [0, 0]. One input x = [1, 2] with target t = [1, 3], loss half the squared error.

  • Forward. x·W = [1, 2]; h = x·A = 1·1 + 2·0 = 1; h·B = [0, 0]. Output [1, 2], exactly the frozen model's. Error e = [0, −1], loss 0.5.
  • Gradients. dB = hᵀ·e = 1 × [0, −1] = [0, −1]. dh = e·Bᵀ = 0 because B is zero, so dA = xᵀ·0 = [0, 0]. On the first step only B can move.
  • Step with learning rate 0.5: B = [0, 0] − 0.5·[0, −1] = [0, 0.5].
  • New forward. h·B = 1·[0, 0.5] = [0, 0.5]; output [1, 2.5]; error [0, −0.5]; loss 0.125, a quarter of the start.
  • Second step's A gradient. Now dh = e·Bᵀ = 0·0 + (−0.5)·0.5 = −0.25, so dA = xᵀ·dh = [1, 2]ᵀ·(−0.25) = [−0.25, −0.5]. A begins to move, rotating the bottleneck direction toward what helps.

Two facts are visible in these few numbers. The zero B made the first step safe (the model started exactly at the pretrained function) without blocking learning (B's gradient was immediately non-zero because A was not zero). And the update stayed inside its low-rank form: the change to the output, [0, 0.5], is the bottleneck value times B, a rank-1 correction. Scaled up, a 4,096 × 4,096 layer has 16,777,216 weights and a rank-8 adapter 65,536. The first solution block asserts every number above.

import time
import numpy as np

# The hand-worked example: W = I (2×2), rank r = 1, alpha = 1 so the scale alpha/r = 1.
W2 = np.eye(2)
A2 = np.array([[1.0], [0.0]])          # random in general; fixed here so the numbers are easy
B2 = np.zeros((1, 2))                  # zero: the adapter starts as an exact no-op
x2 = np.array([[1.0, 2.0]]); t2 = np.array([[1.0, 3.0]])
y = x2 @ W2 + (x2 @ A2) @ B2
assert y.tolist() == [[1.0, 2.0]]                       # same as the frozen model
e = y - t2                                              # [0, -1]; loss = 0.5 * 1 = 0.5
dB = (x2 @ A2).T @ e                                    # (xA = 1) * e = [0, -1]
dA = x2.T @ (e @ B2.T)                                  # B = 0, so no gradient reaches A yet
assert dB.tolist() == [[0.0, -1.0]] and np.all(dA == 0)
B2 = B2 - 0.5 * dB                                      # lr 0.5 -> B = [0, 0.5]
y = x2 @ W2 + (x2 @ A2) @ B2
assert y.tolist() == [[1.0, 2.5]] and 0.5 * ((y - t2) ** 2).sum() == 0.125
e = y - t2                                              # [0, -0.5]
dA = x2.T @ (e @ B2.T)                                  # now B != 0, so A receives gradient
assert dA.ravel().tolist() == [-0.25, -0.5]
# Parameter counts: a 4096 × 4096 layer has 16,777,216 weights; rank 8 adds 8 × (4096 + 4096) = 65,536.
assert 4096 * 4096 == 16_777_216 and 8 * (4096 + 4096) == 65_536

5. The naive implementation: materialising the update

The most literal implementation builds the full update matrix ΔW = s·A·B (here with explicit loops over its outer products) and multiplies the input by W + ΔW:

def lora_forward_naive(x, W, A, B, scale):
    """Materialise the full update ΔW = scale · A B every call: d_in × d_out extra work and memory."""
    dW = np.zeros_like(W)
    for i in range(A.shape[0]):                          # explicit outer-product accumulation
        for k in range(A.shape[1]):
            dW[i] += scale * A[i, k] * B[k]
    return x @ (W + dW)

It computes the right answer and is a useful reference, but it throws away LoRA's main advantage: it builds and stores a d_in × d_out matrix every call, costing d_in·r·d_out operations to form plus a full-size multiply to apply. For the adapter path alone, at d = 1,024 and r = 8 with 64 rows, materialising took 19.2 ms in the test against 0.31 ms for the factored path, a factor of 62, and for a real model the d × d update per layer would also double activation memory.

6. The factored layer

class LoRALinear:
    """y = x W + (alpha / r) · (x A) B, with W frozen; A (d_in, r), B (r, d_out)."""

    def __init__(self, W, r=8, alpha=16, seed=0):
        rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
        self.W = W                                           # pretrained weight, never updated
        d_in, d_out = W.shape
        self.A = rng.normal(0, 1 / np.sqrt(d_in), (d_in, r))  # random
        self.B = np.zeros((r, d_out))                        # zero: the adapter starts as a no-op
        self.scale = alpha / r
        self.merged = False

    def __call__(self, x):
        y = x @ self.W
        if not self.merged:
            y = y + self.scale * (x @ self.A) @ self.B       # (N, r) first: never build d_in × d_out
        return y

    def grads(self, x, dy):
        """Gradients of the loss for A and B given dL/dy. W gets none."""
        xa = x @ self.A                                      # (N, r)
        dB = self.scale * xa.T @ dy                          # (r, d_out)
        dA = self.scale * x.T @ (dy @ self.B.T)              # (d_in, r)
        return dA, dB

    def merge(self):
        self.W = self.W + self.scale * self.A @ self.B       # one matrix again: zero inference overhead
        self.merged = True

    def unmerge(self):
        self.W = self.W - self.scale * self.A @ self.B
        self.merged = False

def trainable_fraction(d_in, d_out, r):
    return r * (d_in + d_out) / (d_in * d_out)

Initialisation. A is drawn with standard deviation 1/√d_in, a common choice that keeps x·A of order one; B is zero; the scale is α/r.

Forward. x @ W, plus, if not merged, scale · (x @ A) @ B: the parentheses force the thin product first, so the largest intermediate is N × r.

grads returns the two adapter gradients from the output gradient, exactly the equations above. A real framework would get these from autograd with W marked as not requiring gradients; writing them by hand makes clear which tensors the backward pass needs (x and x·A) and confirms W's gradient is never formed.

merge and unmerge add and subtract the scaled product. After merging, the forward pass skips the side path.

7. Testing it

The tests check: the class reproduces the hand-worked step; with B = 0 the layer equals the frozen layer exactly, and with a non-zero B the factored forward equals the materialised one; finite differences confirm both adapter gradients; PyTorch autograd, with W left frozen, produces the same gradients for A and B to 10⁻¹² and no gradient for W; rank-4 adapters trained for 3,000 steps fit a target generated by a rank-4 change to a loss 7 × 10⁻³¹ of the starting loss, while rank-2 adapters stall at 29% of it; merging leaves outputs unchanged and unmerging restores the original weights; the float16 merge trap; and the factored-versus-materialised timing.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 1. The class reproduces the hand-worked step.
h = LoRALinear(W2, r=1, alpha=1); h.A = np.array([[1.0], [0.0]])
dA_h, dB_h = h.grads(x2, h(x2) - t2)
assert np.all(dA_h == 0) and dB_h.tolist() == [[0.0, -1.0]]
h.B -= 0.5 * dB_h
assert h(x2).tolist() == [[1.0, 2.5]]

# 2. Zero-initialised B is an exact no-op; factored and materialised forwards agree.
d_in, d_out, N = 32, 24, 256
W = rng.normal(size=(d_in, d_out))
x = rng.normal(size=(N, d_in))
layer = LoRALinear(W, r=4, alpha=8)
assert np.allclose(layer(x), x @ W)
layer.B = rng.normal(size=layer.B.shape) * 0.1
assert np.allclose(layer(x), lora_forward_naive(x, W, layer.A, layer.B, layer.scale))

# 3. Gradient check on 0.5 * ||y - t||² / N.
t = rng.normal(size=(N, d_out))
loss = lambda: 0.5 * ((layer(x) - t) ** 2).sum() / N
dA, dB = layer.grads(x, (layer(x) - t) / N)
for P, G in ((layer.A, dA), (layer.B, dB)):
    for i in [(0, 0), (3, 2), (P.shape[0] - 1, P.shape[1] - 1)]:
        old = P[i]; P[i] = old + 1e-6; lp = loss(); P[i] = old - 1e-6; lm = loss(); P[i] = old
        assert abs((lp - lm) / 2e-6 - G[i]) < 1e-6

# 4. Reference: PyTorch autograd through the same computation, with W frozen.
try:
    import torch
    Wt = torch.tensor(W)                                       # requires_grad False: frozen
    At = torch.tensor(layer.A, requires_grad=True); Bt = torch.tensor(layer.B, requires_grad=True)
    yt = torch.tensor(x) @ Wt + layer.scale * (torch.tensor(x) @ At) @ Bt
    (0.5 * ((yt - torch.tensor(t)) ** 2).sum() / N).backward()
    assert np.allclose(At.grad.numpy(), dA, atol=1e-12) and np.allclose(Bt.grad.numpy(), dB, atol=1e-12)
    assert Wt.grad is None
except ImportError:
    pass

# 5. Rank-4 adapters learn a genuine rank-4 change; rank-2 adapters cannot.
delta = rng.normal(size=(d_in, 4)) @ rng.normal(size=(4, d_out)) * 0.3
target = x @ (W + delta)
def fit(r, steps=3000, lr=0.01):
    m = LoRALinear(W, r=r, alpha=2 * r, seed=1)
    for _ in range(steps):
        err = m(x) - target
        gA, gB = m.grads(x, err / N)
        m.A -= lr * gA; m.B -= lr * gB
    return m, 0.5 * ((m(x) - target) ** 2).sum() / N
first = 0.5 * ((x @ W - target) ** 2).sum() / N
lora, final4 = fit(4)
_, final2 = fit(2)
assert final4 < 1e-3 * first and final2 > 0.1 * first      # rank 2 cannot represent a rank-4 change

# 6. Merging changes nothing but speed; unmerging restores W.
before = lora(x); W_orig = lora.W.copy()
lora.merge()
assert np.allclose(lora(x), before)
lora.unmerge()
assert np.allclose(lora.W, W_orig)
assert round(100 * trainable_fraction(4096, 4096, 8), 2) == 0.39

# 7. Precision trap: merging a small update into float16 weights rounds it away.
w16 = np.full(4, 1.0, dtype=np.float16)
upd = np.full(4, 2e-4)
assert np.all((w16 + upd.astype(np.float16)) == w16)       # 1 + 0.0002 rounds back to 1 in float16
assert np.all((w16.astype(np.float64) + upd) != 1.0)       # merged in float64 it survives

# 8. Timing: materialising ΔW (d × d) versus the rank-r path, d = 1024, r = 8, 64 rows.
dbig, r8 = 1024, 8
Wb, Ab, Bb = rng.normal(size=(dbig, dbig)), rng.normal(size=(dbig, r8)), rng.normal(size=(r8, dbig))
xb = rng.normal(size=(64, dbig))
xb @ Ab @ Bb; Ab @ Bb                                         # warm up BLAS before timing
t0 = time.perf_counter()
for _ in range(20):
    extra_naive = xb @ (2.0 * Ab @ Bb)                       # build the d × d update, then multiply
t_naive = (time.perf_counter() - t0) / 20
t0 = time.perf_counter()

    extra_fact = 2.0 * (xb @ Ab) @ Bb                        # two thin products, never d × d
t_fact = (time.perf_counter() - t0) / 20
assert np.allclose(extra_naive, extra_fact) and t_fact < t_naive
if __name__ == "__main__":
    print(f"rank-4 fit loss {final4/first:.2e} of start, rank-2 {final2/first:.2f}")
    print(f"adapter path, 64 x 1024, r = 8: materialised ΔW {t_naive*1e3:.2f} ms, factored {t_fact*1e3:.3f} ms ({t_naive/t_fact:.0f}x)")

The rank experiment is worth dwelling on, because it tests LoRA's defining property rather than its code. A rank-r adapter can represent any update of rank up to r. If the "true" change is rank 4, rank-4 adapters can match it exactly, and gradient descent finds it; rank-2 adapters can at best match its best rank-2 approximation (the top two singular directions, mc8), so their loss cannot fall below what the remaining two directions contribute. Real fine-tuning updates are not exactly low-rank, but the LoRA paper's analysis suggests their important part is, which is why small ranks work.

8. Numerical and practical traps

Trap 1: both factors zero. No gradient ever flows (the drill shows it). One factor must be random.

Trap 2: materialising the update. Computing x @ (A @ B) instead of (x @ A) @ B silently costs a d_in × d_out matrix per layer per step. The parentheses matter.

Trap 3: merging in low precision. In float16, 1 + 0.0002 is exactly 1: the spacing between float16 numbers near 1 is about 0.001. LoRA updates are often small relative to the weights, so merging into float16 or bfloat16 can round much of the update away. In the test, the same update survives when merged in float64. Merge in float32, then cast once, and evaluate the merged model before shipping it.

Trap 4: a wrong scale at merge time. Training used α/r; merging with α alone, or with a different rank, scales the update wrongly. Store α and r with the adapter.

Trap 5: transposed conventions. PyTorch's nn.Linear stores W as (d_out, d_in) and computes x·Wᵀ, so its LoRA matrices are A (r, d_in) and B (d_out, r) with the update B·A. Mixing conventions when loading adapters trained elsewhere produces shape errors or, for square layers, silently transposed updates.

Trap 6: learning rate. Because so few parameters train, LoRA usually needs a higher learning rate than full fine-tuning (often around 10⁻⁴ to 2 × 10⁻⁴ with Adam, versus 10⁻⁵ for full fine-tuning). Copying a full fine-tuning learning rate makes LoRA appear not to learn.

9. Edge cases worth naming

Rank equal to min(d_in, d_out) makes LoRA able to represent any update, with more parameters than the layer itself only if r exceeds d_in·d_out/(d_in + d_out); there is no point going that high. Rank 1 is legal and occasionally enough. Applying LoRA to embeddings is possible (A selects rows like an embedding lookup) and is sometimes done when adding new tokens. Biases and normalisation gains are usually left frozen, but some recipes train them too, which is cheap. Dropout on the adapter input (LoRA dropout) is a common regulariser. And multiple adapters can be summed on one layer, each with its own scale, which is how adapter merging and composition experiments work.

Training the adapter with Adam

The tests train with plain gradient descent to keep the arithmetic visible, but real LoRA training uses Adam or AdamW (mc12), and the only change is which tensors the optimiser sees: the list of parameters handed to it contains every A and every B and nothing else. Adam keeps its two moment estimates for those tensors only, which is where most of the memory saving comes from. Weight decay, if used, applies to the adapters (pulling the update toward zero, which means toward the pretrained model) rather than to the frozen weights. Learning-rate warm-up and cosine decay are used as usual. Because B starts at zero, the first few hundred steps are spent growing B while A slowly rotates; the training loss typically drops quickly once B has become non-trivial.

Choosing rank, alpha and targets in practice

A reasonable starting point for an instruction-tuning or formatting task is rank 8 or 16 on all linear layers, α equal to twice the rank, LoRA dropout of 0.05 and a learning rate around 2 × 10⁻⁴. Raise the rank if the training loss plateaus well above what full fine-tuning would reach and the task genuinely needs new behaviour; lower it if the adapter overfits a small dataset. Changing the rank while keeping α/r fixed keeps the update's effective size comparable, so the learning rate usually transfers. The LoRA paper's own budget comparison is a useful anchor: with a fixed 18 million trainable parameters on GPT-3, spreading them across both the query and value projections at rank 4 worked better than putting them all into one projection at rank 8.

Where the gradient flows, and where it does not

Even though W is frozen, gradients still pass through it to earlier layers: the input gradient of the layer is dy·Wᵀ plus the adapter's contribution dh·Aᵀ. That is why LoRA training still needs a full backward pass through the network and still stores activations for every layer. What disappears is the weight-gradient product xᵀ·dy for W, a d_in × d_out matrix per layer per step, and the optimiser state for W. Keeping these two facts apart (gradients still flow through frozen weights; frozen weights simply receive none) answers a common follow-up about why LoRA saves memory but not all compute.

10. Complexity

Per layer and step, the side path costs N·r·(d_in + d_out) multiply-adds forward and about twice that backward, small next to the frozen matrix multiply N·d_in·d_out when r ≪ d. The frozen path still needs its forward and the input-gradient part of its backward (dy·Wᵀ, to propagate gradients to earlier layers), but not the weight-gradient product, which saves roughly a third of the backward compute; the paper reports a 25% training speed-up on GPT-3 175B. Memory: adapter parameters, gradients and Adam state at r·(d_in + d_out) each, instead of d_in·d_out; activations are unchanged, so gradient checkpointing remains important for long sequences.

Wrapping an existing model

In a real codebase you rarely write a model with LoRA built in; you wrap the linear layers of an existing one. The procedure is mechanical: walk the model's modules, pick those whose names match the target list (for example the query and value projections), replace each with a LoRA wrapper that holds a reference to the original weight, mark every original parameter as not requiring gradients, and hand only the adapter parameters to the optimiser. Saving writes only A, B, the rank, α and the list of target names; loading reverses the walk on a freshly loaded base model. That is essentially what the PEFT library does, and implementing it once by hand makes its configuration options (target modules, rank, alpha, dropout, which modules to keep fully trainable) self-explanatory.

A rank-capacity intuition

Why does the rank-2 fit stall at 29% rather than at some other number? The target change has four singular values. A rank-2 product can capture at most the two largest; the error that remains is set by the energy in the other two, measured through the inputs. With randomly drawn factors the four singular values are of similar size, so the two missing ones carry a large share, and the loss settles at that floor however long you train. If the target's singular values fell off quickly, as the LoRA paper argues real fine-tuning updates do, rank 2 would come much closer. That is the whole argument for LoRA in one experiment: its accuracy depends on how fast the update's singular values decay.

11. Talking through the design

In the interview: write y = xW + (α/r)(xA)B with shapes; explain B = 0 and A random; insist on the parentheses; derive dB and dA and note dA = 0 on the first step; add merge and unmerge; test with a no-op check, a gradient check and a low-rank target. Then the system view: parameter and memory counts, QLoRA's 4-bit base, multi-adapter serving, which modules to target, and the precision caveat for merging.

Why low rank is enough: the paper's evidence

Hu and colleagues hypothesise that the change in weights during adaptation has low "intrinsic rank", and test it on GPT-3. Adapting the query and value projections at r = 4 or even r = 1 performs close to much larger ranks; the top singular directions learned with r = 8 and r = 64 overlap strongly; and across random seeds the top directions agree while the rest look like noise. Comparing the update with the pretrained weight, they find it amplifies directions that are present in the pretrained weight but not among its top singular directions, by a factor of about 20 at r = 4. Their interpretation: adaptation amplifies task-relevant features the pretrained model learned but did not emphasise. That is why a tiny adapter can change behaviour substantially.

LoRA and the SVD

There is a close connection to mc8. Given any full fine-tuning update ΔW, the best rank-r approximation in the least-squares sense is its truncated SVD: keep the r largest singular values and their singular vectors. LoRA does not compute an SVD; it learns A and B directly by gradient descent. But the rank experiment in the tests is the same idea seen from the other side: when the target change has rank 4, a rank-4 product can represent it exactly and gradient descent finds an equivalent factorisation (not unique: A·M and M⁻¹·B give the same product for any invertible r × r matrix M), while a rank-2 product can at best reach the truncated SVD's error. One practical consequence is that you can compress a fully fine-tuned model into a LoRA-style adapter after the fact by taking a truncated SVD of each layer's difference from the base, accepting whatever error the discarded singular values carry.

Initialisation variants

The zero-B initialisation guarantees a no-op start but means the first steps are spent growing B from nothing. Some variants initialise from the pretrained weight itself instead: PiSSA, for example, sets A and B from the top singular directions of W and freezes the remainder, so training adapts the most important directions first; LoftQ chooses the initial adapters jointly with the quantisation of a QLoRA base so that base plus adapters approximates the original full-precision weight more closely. These change where training starts, not the forward pass, so the implementation in this lesson needs only a different constructor.

QLoRA: 4-bit base weights

LoRA removes gradients and optimizer state for the frozen weights, but the frozen weights themselves still occupy 2 bytes per parameter in 16-bit. QLoRA stores them in a 4-bit format designed for normally distributed weights (NF4), quantises the per-block scaling constants themselves (double quantisation) and pages optimizer state to CPU memory during spikes. During the forward and backward passes, each block is dequantised to bf16 on the fly, the adapters stay in higher precision, and gradients flow through the frozen dequantised weights into the adapters. Dettmers and colleagues report fine-tuning a 65B model on a single 48 GB GPU, matching 16-bit LoRA quality. The drill's arithmetic, 32.5 GB of base plus about 4 GB of adapters and optimizer state, shows why it fits.

Variants: DoRA, rsLoRA, LoRA+ and others

Several refinements adjust the parameterisation. rsLoRA scales the update by α/√r instead of α/r, arguing that the original scaling makes large ranks learn too slowly. LoRA+ uses a larger learning rate for B than for A, motivated by their different roles. DoRA decomposes each weight into a magnitude and a direction and applies LoRA to the direction while training the magnitude separately, reporting gains closer to full fine-tuning. VeRA shares frozen random A and B across layers and trains only small scaling vectors. All keep the core idea: a frozen base plus a cheap, structured update.

Serving many adapters

Because adapters are small, one GPU can hold a base model and thousands of adapters. Serving them unmerged lets requests for different adapters share a batch: the base matmul runs once for the whole batch, and each row adds its own (x·A_i)·B_i. S-LoRA introduced kernels that batch these heterogeneous low-rank products efficiently and page adapters between host and GPU memory. The trade-off against merging is a small per-token cost for great flexibility, which is why per-customer fine-tunes are economically viable.

What LoRA learns, and forgets

Biderman and colleagues compared LoRA with full fine-tuning on code and maths and found LoRA typically learns less of the new domain but forgets less of the base model's other abilities, behaving as a regulariser. In practice that makes LoRA excellent for style, format, tone and task specialisation, and weaker for large-scale knowledge injection or continued pre-training on a new language, where full fine-tuning (or much higher ranks on all layers) does better. Knowing this distinction is a common senior follow-up.

Where LoRA runs

Fine-tuning open models for customer support, extraction formats, coding conventions and brand voice; per-tenant customisation in SaaS products; instruction-tuning experiments where many variants are compared; image-generation styles (LoRA is widely used with diffusion models too); and serving stacks such as vLLM that load adapters per request. The Hugging Face PEFT library is the common implementation, with QLoRA via bitsandbytes for 4-bit bases.

Failure modes and symptoms

The model does not change. Loss barely moves. Causes: learning rate copied from full fine-tuning (too small), adapters attached to the wrong modules (names differ between architectures), adapters accidentally frozen, or both factors initialised to zero. Check the trainable-parameter count and that adapter weights change after one step.

Merged model worse than unmerged. Precision loss in the merge (Trap 3) or a wrong scale. Merge in float32, compare merged and unmerged outputs on a fixed batch, and only ship if they agree within tolerance.

Catastrophic forgetting despite LoRA. High rank on all layers with a large learning rate and many epochs can still erode general abilities. Evaluate on general benchmarks as well as the target task; lower the rank or learning rate; mix in general data.

Training instability at high learning rates. Because the update is a product of two learned matrices, its size grows roughly with the product of their norms, and a large learning rate can make it grow quickly and diverge. Gradient clipping, warm-up and a modest α keep it stable; monitoring the norm of (α/r)·A·B per layer shows which layers run away first.

Adapter and base mismatch. An adapter trained on one base checkpoint is loaded onto a slightly different one (a different revision or quantisation). The shapes match and outputs degrade. Record the base model's exact identifier and hash with every adapter.

Serving latency spikes with many adapters. Adapters swapped in from host memory on demand, or unbatched per-adapter computation. Use batched multi-adapter kernels and keep hot adapters resident.

A debugging checklist

When a LoRA run misbehaves, check in this order. Print the number of trainable parameters and confirm it is the expected fraction (a value of zero means no module matched; a value near 100% means the base was not frozen). Run one step and confirm that B changed and W did not. Compare the adapted model's output with the base model's output before training: they must be identical, or the initialisation or scale is wrong. Overfit a handful of examples to near-zero loss; if that fails, the learning rate is too small or the gradient is not reaching the adapters. Only then tune rank, α and data.

What to monitor and tune

Rank, α, target modules, learning rate and number of epochs are the main knobs; monitor training and validation loss, task metrics, a general-ability benchmark for forgetting, adapter weight norms, and for serving the per-adapter request mix and adapter cache hit rate.

A cost estimate

For a 7-billion-parameter model with 32 layers, applying rank-16 LoRA to all seven linear projections per layer (four attention, three MLP, with hidden size 4,096 and MLP size 11,008) adds about 16 × [4 × (4,096 + 4,096) + 3 × (4,096 + 11,008)] ≈ 1.25 million parameters per layer, 40 million in total, 0.57% of the model. In fp32 with Adam that is about 640 MB of trainable state, against roughly 112 GB for full fine-tuning at 16 bytes per parameter. The adapter file in bf16 is about 80 MB.

The reference for this problem

Hu and colleagues' "LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models" (arXiv 2106.09685) defines the method implemented here: freeze the pretrained weights and inject "trainable rank decomposition matrices into each layer", with a random Gaussian initialisation for A and zero for B so that the update is zero at the start, and the update scaled by α/r. Its abstract reports that, compared with GPT-3 175B fine-tuned with Adam, LoRA "can reduce the number of trainable parameters by 10,000 times and the GPU memory requirement by 3 times", with "no additional inference latency" because "we can explicitly compute and store W = W₀ + BA and perform inference as usual". It gives the concrete figures used in this lesson: training memory on GPT-3 175B reduced "from 1.2TB to 350GB", and with r = 4 on the query and value projections, checkpoints reduced "from 350GB to 35MB", so that storing 100 adapted models needs about 354 GB instead of 35 TB, plus a 25% training speed-up. (The paper writes the update as B·A with W₀ of shape d × k, the transpose of this lesson's NumPy convention.)

Goal: run the solution, then vary it, break it and time it. Python 3.12 with NumPy; PyTorch only for the optional cross-check.

  1. Run the four blocks as one script. All assertions pass and it prints the rank-4 and rank-2 fits and the adapter-path timing.
  2. Vary the inputs. Redo the hand example with target [2, 3] and learning rate 0.5 for two steps, by hand, including A's gradient on step two. Check with LoRALinear.
  3. Break the initialisation. Initialise B randomly and check the no-op assertion; then set A to zero as well and train (the drill's dead adapter).
  4. Explore rank. Fit the rank-4 target with r = 1, 2, 3, 4 and 8, recording the final loss; then make the target full-rank and repeat.
  5. Explore scale. Fix α = 8 and vary r from 2 to 32 with one learning rate; then fix α/r. Which converges more consistently across ranks?
  6. Measure precision. Merge trained adapters into float16 and bfloat16-like (round to 8 significant bits) copies of W and measure the output error against the unmerged float64 layer.

Finally, time naive versus factored at d = 256, 1,024 and 4,096 with r = 8, and plot the ratio against d.

What to look for: random B breaks the no-op guarantee; the dead adapter never moves; the loss stops falling once r reaches the target's rank; fixing α/r makes one learning rate work across ranks; and low-precision merges introduce errors of the size of the update itself.

Work it out

(a) Show in code what happens if both A and B are initialised to zero. (b) Estimate the GPU memory to QLoRA-fine-tune a 65B model: 4-bit base weights, rank-16 adapters on all linear layers (assume adapters total 0.4% of parameters), trained with Adam in fp32.

Answer 
dead = LoRALinear(W, r=4)
dead.A[:] = 0.0                                              # both factors zero
dA, dB = dead.grads(x, rng.normal(size=(N, d_out)))
assert np.all(dA == 0) and np.all(dB == 0)                   # stuck: no gradient can ever flow

params = 65e9
base_gb = params * 0.5 / 1e9                                 # 4-bit weights: 32.5 GB
adapter = 0.004 * params                                     # 260M trainable parameters
adapter_gb = adapter * (4 + 4 + 4 + 4) / 1e9                 # fp32 weights, grads, Adam m and v: ~4.2 GB
assert round(base_gb, 1) == 32.5 and round(adapter_gb, 1) == 4.2

(a) dB is proportional to x·A and dA to B; with both zero, both gradients are zero and nothing ever moves. One factor must be non-zero; making it A (with B = 0) keeps the initial function unchanged. (b) About 32.5 GB for the frozen base plus about 4 GB for the adapters and their optimiser state, roughly 37 GB before activations, which is why QLoRA fits a 65B fine-tune on a 48 GB GPU with gradient checkpointing, whereas full fine-tuning at 16 bytes per parameter would need over a terabyte.
