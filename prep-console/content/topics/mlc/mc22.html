A support engineer types "E1234 quota" into the company's internal assistant. The assistant uses retrieval-augmented generation: it searches thousands of help articles, pastes the best few into the prompt and asks a language model to answer. If the search returns articles about disks in general but not the one that mentions E1234, the model will answer confidently and wrongly. Another engineer asks "how do I get back into my account?" and the right article is titled "Password reset links"; it shares no words with the question. A good retrieval system has to handle both: exact rare strings and paraphrases.

Two families of methods exist for exactly these two cases. Lexical retrieval, with BM25 as its standard, scores documents by the words they share with the query, weighting rare words heavily; it finds E1234 instantly and needs no training. Dense retrieval embeds the query and the documents as vectors with a neural model and ranks by similarity; it understands that "get back into my account" means "password reset". Each fails where the other succeeds, so production systems run both and merge the rankings, most often with reciprocal rank fusion, a remarkably simple method that uses only the positions of documents in each list.

The stakes are higher in RAG than in classic search. A human scanning ten blue links can skip a bad result; a language model given a bad passage will often use it. Retrieval quality therefore sets an upper limit on answer quality, and most of the improvements teams make to RAG systems in practice are retrieval improvements rather than prompt changes.

Interviewers ask candidates to build this from scratch because it shows whether they understand search beyond calling a vector database. Can you write the BM25 formula and explain every part? Why does an inverted index make search fast? How do you select the top k of a million scores without sorting them all? Why must you not just add BM25 scores to cosine similarities? What breaks numerically? And how do you know whether the system is any good?

This lesson computes BM25 by hand on a three-document corpus, works a rank fusion to five decimal places, then builds a naive scanning scorer, an inverted index with precomputed weights (2,662 times faster on a 2,000-document corpus in the test), exact dense top-k with a selection algorithm, and reciprocal rank fusion. It tests them against each other and against the properties stated in the BM25 authors' own review, shows why score addition fails, demonstrates two numerical traps, and finishes with recall@k and mean reciprocal rank.

Imagine a library with no catalogue. To find books about quotas you would have to open every book. A library catalogue reverses the relationship: for every subject word, it lists the books that mention it. Searching then means looking up a few words and reading their lists. Search engines are built on exactly this structure, called an inverted index: a map from each word to the documents that contain it (its postings list).

The catalogue tells you which documents contain the query words; you still need to rank them. Three intuitions lead to BM25:

  • Rare words matter more. Every help article contains "the"; only one contains "E1234". A match on a rare word is strong evidence. This is measured by the inverse document frequency (idf): the fewer documents a word appears in, the larger its weight.
  • More mentions help, but with diminishing returns. An article that mentions "quota" three times is probably more about quotas than one that mentions it once, but the tenth mention adds almost nothing. BM25's term-frequency part saturates: it rises quickly and levels off at a ceiling, with a parameter k1 setting how fast.
  • Long documents match more by chance. A 5,000-word manual mentions many words in passing. BM25 compares each document's length to the average and discounts long ones, with a parameter b setting how strongly.

Multiply the idf by the saturated, length-adjusted term frequency for each query word the document contains, add those up, and you have the BM25 score. It is decades old and still the baseline every new method is compared against.

Dense retrieval takes a different route. A neural embedding model (lm10) turns each document and each query into a vector of a few hundred numbers, trained so that texts with similar meaning get vectors pointing in similar directions. Ranking means computing the cosine similarity between the query vector and every document vector and returning the highest. "Get back into my account" and "password reset" land close together even with no words in common.

The two approaches also differ in cost and maintenance. BM25 needs no model, no GPU and no training data, updates instantly when a document is added, and its results are easy to explain: you can point at the matching words. Dense retrieval needs an embedding model, a pass over every document to embed it, re-embedding whenever the model changes, and its matches are harder to explain. That asymmetry is another reason teams start with BM25 and add dense retrieval where it measurably helps.

When you have two ranked lists, one from each method, you need to merge them. The scores cannot simply be added: BM25 scores can be 3 or 30 depending on the query, while cosine similarities sit between about 0.2 and 0.9. Reciprocal rank fusion ignores the scores and uses positions: a document at rank r in a list earns 1/(60 + r), and its fused score is the sum over lists. A document that is near the top of both lists beats one that is first in one list and absent from the other. Where the library analogy breaks: a catalogue lists subjects chosen by a librarian, while an inverted index lists every word, and the ranking, not just the lookup, is where most of the quality comes from.

1. The problem as it is usually asked

"Implement BM25 search over a small corpus, a dense top-k retriever over given embeddings, and a way to combine them." Clarify: the tokenisation (here: lower-case, split into runs of letters and digits; real systems add stemming, stop-word handling and language-specific rules); the BM25 variant and parameters (k1 = 1.5, b = 0.75, and Lucene's always-positive idf); whether the dense side needs an approximate index (not at this size: exact search over a few thousand vectors is fast); and how many results to return. Outline: tokenise, build postings with precomputed per-posting weights, score queries by adding those weights, select the top k, do the same for dense similarities, fuse with RRF, and evaluate with recall@k.

2. The maths in plain words

idf. Count n, the number of documents containing the term, out of N. A term in almost every document should weigh almost nothing; a term in one document should weigh a lot. BM25's idf is the logarithm of roughly "documents without the term divided by documents with it", with 0.5 added to both counts to avoid dividing by zero; Lucene and most modern engines add 1 inside the logarithm so that the weight can never be negative.

Saturating term frequency. For a document of average length, the term-frequency part is tf·(k1 + 1)/(tf + k1). With k1 = 1.5 that is 1 for one occurrence, 1.43 for two, 1.67 for three and approaches 2.5 for very many. So the first occurrence carries most of the evidence, and a document cannot win simply by repeating a word.

Length normalisation. Replace k1 in the denominator by k1 × (1 − b + b × length/average length). A document twice the average length gets a larger denominator, so the same term frequency counts for less; one half the average gets a smaller denominator and counts for more. With b = 0 the length is ignored; with b = 1 it is fully applied.

Dense similarity. Divide each vector by its length (normalise it), then the dot product of two normalised vectors is their cosine similarity. Normalising the document vectors once at index time means a query is a single matrix-vector product.

Rank fusion. For each list, a document at rank r (counting from 1) earns 1/(k + r) with k = 60. Sum over lists; documents missing from a list earn nothing from it. Sort by the sum.

3. The equations

idf(t)        = ln( 1 + (N − n_t + 0.5) / (n_t + 0.5) )            (Lucene form, always ≥ 0)
classic idf   = ln( (N − n_t + 0.5) / (n_t + 0.5) )                 (RSJ with no relevance data; < 0 if n_t > N/2)

BM25(q, d)    = Σ_{t ∈ q ∩ d}  idf(t) · tf(t,d)·(k1 + 1) / ( tf(t,d) + k1·(1 − b + b·|d| / avg_len) )

impact(t, d)  = the summand above, precomputed at index time for every posting
query cost    = Σ_{t ∈ q} |postings(t)|        (not N)

dense:        s_d = (D_d / ‖D_d‖) · (q / ‖q‖)          top-k by argpartition: O(N) + O(k log k)

RRF(d)        = Σ_{lists L containing d}  1 / (k + rank_L(d)),   k = 60, ranks from 1

The (k1 + 1) factor in the numerator is the same for every term, so it scales all scores equally and never changes a ranking; Robertson and Zaragoza note it was included so that a single occurrence gets the same weight as the plain idf. The test checks this: dividing every impact by 2.5 leaves the ranking identical.

4. A worked example by hand

Three documents, k1 = 1.5, b = 0.75:

  • d0 "the cat sat down", 4 tokens; d1 "dog barked", 2 tokens; d2 "cat chased cat up a tree", 6 tokens. Average length (4 + 2 + 6)/3 = 4.
  • idf. "cat" is in n = 2 of N = 3 documents: ln(1 + (3 − 2 + 0.5)/(2 + 0.5)) = ln(1 + 0.6) = ln 1.6 = 0.4700. "dog" is in 1: ln(1 + 2.5/1.5) = ln 2.667 = 0.9808. The rarer word weighs twice as much.
  • d0 for "cat". tf = 1, length 4 = average, so the length factor is 1.5 × (0.25 + 0.75 × 1) = 1.5. The fraction is 1 × 2.5/(1 + 1.5) = 1 exactly. Score 0.4700.
  • d2 for "cat". tf = 2, length 6, factor 1.5 × (0.25 + 0.75 × 1.5) = 1.5 × 1.375 = 2.0625. Fraction 2 × 2.5/(2 + 2.0625) = 5/4.0625 = 1.2308. Score 0.4700 × 1.2308 = 0.5785. Twice the mentions, but only 23% more score, because of saturation and the longer length.
  • d1 for "dog". tf = 1, length 2, factor 1.5 × (0.25 + 0.75 × 0.5) = 0.9375. Fraction 2.5/1.9375 = 1.2903. Score 0.9808 × 1.2903 = 1.2656.
  • Query "cat dog". Each document contains only one of the terms, so the scores are those above: d1 1.2656, d2 0.5785, d0 0.4700. BM25 ranking: [d1, d2, d0].
  • Fusion. Suppose a dense retriever ranks [d2, d0, d1]. With k = 60: d0 = 1/63 + 1/62 = 0.015873 + 0.016129 = 0.03200; d1 = 1/61 + 1/63 = 0.016393 + 0.015873 = 0.03227; d2 = 1/62 + 1/61 = 0.016129 + 0.016393 = 0.03252. Fused ranking [d2, d1, d0]: d2, second and first, beats d1, first and last.

Notice how flat the RRF scores are: they differ in the fourth decimal place. With k = 60 the gap between rank 1 and rank 2 in one list (1/61 − 1/62 ≈ 0.00026) is tiny, so agreement across lists matters more than a single first place. The first solution block asserts every number above.

import math
import re
import time
from collections import Counter
import numpy as np

# The hand-worked example: three documents, lengths 4, 2 and 6, so the average length is 4.
hand_docs = ["the cat sat down", "dog barked", "cat chased cat up a tree"]
k1, b, N = 1.5, 0.75, 3
idf_cat = math.log(1 + (N - 2 + 0.5) / (2 + 0.5))          # "cat" is in 2 of 3 documents: ln 1.6
idf_dog = math.log(1 + (N - 1 + 0.5) / (1 + 0.5))          # "dog" is in 1 of 3: ln 2.667
assert round(idf_cat, 4) == 0.4700 and round(idf_dog, 4) == 0.9808
def term_score(idf, tf, length, avg=4.0):
    return idf * tf * (k1 + 1) / (tf + k1 * (1 - b + b * length / avg))
assert round(term_score(idf_cat, 1, 4), 4) == 0.4700       # average length: the tf part is exactly 1
assert round(term_score(idf_cat, 2, 6), 4) == 0.5785       # two mentions, but a longer document
assert round(term_score(idf_dog, 1, 2), 4) == 1.2656       # rare term in a short document
# Query "cat dog": d1 1.2656 > d2 0.5785 > d0 0.4700.  A second ranking (dense) says d2, d0, d1.
rrf_hand = {d: 1 / (60 + r1) + 1 / (60 + r2) for d, r1, r2 in [(0, 3, 2), (1, 1, 3), (2, 2, 1)]}
assert [round(rrf_hand[d], 5) for d in (0, 1, 2)] == [0.03200, 0.03227, 0.03252]
assert max(rrf_hand, key=rrf_hand.get) == 2                # first or second in both lists beats first-and-last

5. The naive implementation: scan everything

The most direct scorer tokenises every document, and for each query term counts how many documents contain it, computes the idf, then walks every document again to add its contribution:

def tokenize(s):
    return re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", s.lower())

def bm25_naive(query, docs, k1=1.5, b=0.75):
    """Score every document by scanning its tokens: no index, O(total corpus length) per query."""
    toks = [tokenize(d) for d in docs]
    N, avg = len(docs), sum(map(len, toks)) / len(docs)
    scores = [0.0] * N
    for term in set(tokenize(query)):
        n = sum(term in t for t in toks)                        # document frequency
        if n == 0:
            continue
        idf = math.log(1 + (N - n + 0.5) / (n + 0.5))
        for d, t in enumerate(toks):
            tf = t.count(term)
            if tf:
                scores[d] += idf * tf * (k1 + 1) / (tf + k1 * (1 - b + b * len(t) / avg))
    return scores

It is correct and a useful reference, but each query costs time proportional to the whole corpus: every token of every document is examined for every term. On 2,000 short synthetic documents it took 27 ms per query in the test; at a million documents a single query would take seconds, and web-scale collections are a thousand times larger again.

6. The indexed implementation

class BM25:
    """Inverted index with per-posting weights precomputed at index time; a query sums a few arrays."""

    def __init__(self, docs, k1=1.5, b=0.75):
        toks = [tokenize(d) for d in docs]
        self.N = len(docs)
        lens = np.array([len(t) for t in toks], float)
        norm = k1 * (1 - b + b * lens / lens.mean())            # per-document length factor
        post = {}
        for d, t in enumerate(toks):
            for term, tf in Counter(t).items():
                post.setdefault(term, ([], []))
                post[term][0].append(d); post[term][1].append(tf)
        self.index = {}
        for term, (ds, tfs) in post.items():
            ds, tfs = np.array(ds), np.array(tfs, float)
            idf = math.log(1 + (self.N - len(ds) + 0.5) / (len(ds) + 0.5))
            self.index[term] = (ds, idf * tfs * (k1 + 1) / (tfs + norm[ds]))   # "impact" per posting

    def scores(self, query):
        s = np.zeros(self.N)
        for term in set(tokenize(query)):
            if term in self.index:
                ds, w = self.index[term]
                s[ds] += w                                       # touch only documents containing the term
        return s

    def search(self, query, k=5):
        s = self.scores(query)
        return top_k(s, k, keep_zero=False)

def top_k(scores, k, keep_zero=True):
    k = min(k, len(scores))
    idx = np.argpartition(-scores, k - 1)[:k]                   # O(n) selection of the k best
    idx = idx[np.lexsort((idx, -scores[idx]))]                  # sort only those k; ties by doc id
    return [(int(i), float(scores[i])) for i in idx if keep_zero or scores[i] > 0]

def dense_top_k(q, D, k=5, eps=1e-12):
    qn = q / max(np.linalg.norm(q), eps)
    Dn = D / np.maximum(np.linalg.norm(D, axis=1, keepdims=True), eps)   # eps: a zero vector stays zero
    return top_k(Dn @ qn, k)

def rrf(rankings, k=60):
    """Reciprocal rank fusion: score(d) = sum over lists of 1 / (k + rank), ranks starting at 1."""
    score = Counter()
    for ranking in rankings:
        for rank, doc in enumerate(ranking, start=1):
            score[doc] += 1.0 / (k + rank)
    return sorted(score, key=lambda d: (-score[d], str(d)))

Indexing. Tokenise each document once, count term frequencies, and build for each term two arrays: the ids of the documents containing it and the term frequencies. Then, because everything in the BM25 summand except the query is known at index time (idf, tf, the document's length factor), compute each posting's full contribution, its impact, and store that instead of the raw frequency. Real engines do something similar (impact-ordered or quantised-score indexes) when the scoring function is fixed.

Querying. For each distinct query term, look up its postings and add the impacts into a score array with one vectorised fancy-indexing addition: s[ds] += w (safe because a term's postings contain each document at most once). The cost is the total length of the query terms' postings, independent of the corpus size for rare terms. The test measured 0.010 ms against 27 ms for the scan, a factor of 2,662.

Top-k. np.argpartition moves the k largest scores to the front in linear time without ordering them; sorting just those k gives the ranked result. Ties are broken by document id so results are deterministic, which matters for caching, testing and debugging. On a million scores the selection took 12.5 ms against 105 ms for a full sort.

Dense top-k normalises with a small epsilon guard (so an all-zero vector stays zero instead of becoming NaN) and reuses the same selection. RRF is a dictionary accumulation over the lists, with ranks counted from 1 as in the paper.

7. Testing it

The tests check: the indexed class reproduces the hand-worked scores, ranking and fused order; the naive scan and the index give identical scores on a 2,000-document corpus with a Zipf-like word distribution (a few very common words and a long tail of rare ones, as in real text), including queries with repeated and unknown terms; removing the (k1 + 1) factor leaves rankings unchanged and term frequency saturates below 2.5, as the BM25 review states; b = 0 makes a short and a long document score equally while b = 1 favours the short one; dense top-k equals a full sort and matches torch.topk; a constructed case shows score addition flipping when one BM25 score moves while RRF stays put; the two numerical traps; and the timings.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 1. The class reproduces the hand-worked scores and the fused order.
hb = BM25(hand_docs)
assert np.allclose(hb.scores("cat dog"), [0.4700, 1.2656, 0.5785], atol=1e-4)
assert [d for d, _ in hb.search("cat dog")] == [1, 2, 0]
assert rrf([[1, 2, 0], [2, 0, 1]]) == [2, 1, 0]

# 2. Naive scan and inverted index agree on a synthetic Zipf-like corpus.
vocab = [f"w{i}" for i in range(3000)]
pz = 1 / np.arange(1, 3001); pz /= pz.sum()
corpus = [" ".join(rng.choice(vocab, size=rng.integers(5, 60), p=pz)) for _ in range(2000)]
bm = BM25(corpus)
for q in ["w1 w50 w700", "w2999", "w3 w3 w10", "nothing here"]:
    assert np.allclose(bm.scores(q), bm25_naive(q, corpus))

# 3. Reference (Robertson & Zaragoza): the (k1 + 1) factor is the same for every term, so it scales
#    scores but never changes the ranking; tf saturates at idf * (k1 + 1).
plain = BM25(corpus); q = "w5 w80 w600"
s1 = plain.scores(q)
for term in plain.index:
    ds, w = plain.index[term]; plain.index[term] = (ds, w / 2.5)
assert np.array_equal(np.argsort(-s1, kind="stable"), np.argsort(-plain.scores(q), kind="stable"))
sat = [term_score(1.0, tf, 4) for tf in (1, 2, 10, 1000)]
assert sat[0] == 1.0 and sat[1] < 2 * sat[0] and sat[3] < 2.5 and sat[3] > 2.49

# 4. Length normalisation: b = 0 ignores length, b = 1 normalises fully.
short_long = ["apple pie", "apple " + "filler " * 20]
assert np.isclose(*BM25(short_long, b=0.0).scores("apple"))
s = BM25(short_long, b=1.0).scores("apple"); assert s[0] > 2 * s[1]

# 5. Dense top-k equals a full sort; PyTorch topk agrees (optional cross-check).
E, qv = rng.normal(size=(5000, 64)), rng.normal(size=64)
sims = (E / np.linalg.norm(E, axis=1, keepdims=True)) @ (qv / np.linalg.norm(qv))
assert [i for i, _ in dense_top_k(qv, E, 10)] == list(np.argsort(-sims)[:10])
try:

# 6. Why fuse ranks, not scores: BM25 scores are unbounded, cosines live in [-1, 1].
bm_scores = {"A": 14.2, "B": 13.9, "C": 2.1}                 # A barely beats B lexically
cos_scores = {"A": 0.20, "B": 0.85, "C": 0.84}               # B is clearly best by meaning
summed = max(bm_scores, key=lambda d: bm_scores[d] + cos_scores[d])

assert rrf([["A", "B", "C"], ["B", "C", "A"]])[0] == "B"     # ... but not the rank fusion

# 7. Numerical traps. (a) The classic RSJ idf goes negative for terms in more than half the documents.

assert idf_cat > 0                                           # the 1 + ... form never does
# (b) A zero embedding: without eps, normalising gives NaN and poisons the ranking.

The score-addition test deserves a closer look. Documents A and B have BM25 scores 14.2 and 13.9, essentially tied lexically, and cosine similarities 0.20 and 0.85, so B is clearly better by meaning. Adding gives B 14.75 against A 14.40: B wins, but only because the BM25 gap happened to be small. Raise A's BM25 score to 15.0, a change that could come from one extra occurrence of a query word, and the sum flips to A, ignoring the dense retriever's strong preference. RRF sees only that B is second and first while A is first and third, and picks B in both cases.

A note on what "reference" means for this problem. There is no single library function to compare BM25 against that is installed here (and libraries disagree in small ways: some use the classic idf, some the Lucene form, some drop the (k1 + 1) factor, some count repeated query terms). So the solution is checked three ways instead: against an independent, deliberately naive implementation of the same formula; against hand-computed numbers; and against the properties the BM25 authors state (ranking invariance to the (k1 + 1) factor, saturation below k1 + 1, the effect of b). Dense top-k is checked against a full sort and against PyTorch, and RRF against hand-computed sums. When you compare your BM25 with Elasticsearch or another engine, expect identical rankings only if the idf form, tokenisation and parameters match exactly.

8. Numerical and practical traps

Trap 1: negative idf. The classic form ln((N − n + 0.5)/(n + 0.5)) is negative when a term appears in more than half the documents. In the hand example, "cat" in 2 of 3 documents would get ln(1.5/2.5) = −0.51, so a document matching "cat" would score lower than one that does not. Some implementations clamp at zero or a small floor; Lucene's 1 + inside the log avoids it entirely.

Trap 2: zero vectors. An empty document or a failed embedding call can produce an all-zero vector. Dividing by its norm gives NaN, and a NaN in the score array makes sorting and selection unreliable. Normalise with max(norm, eps), and better still, catch empty embeddings upstream.

Trap 3: ties. Many documents can share a score, especially in BM25 for short queries or with low-precision embeddings. Without a tie-breaker, results can change between runs or machines. Break ties by document id.

Trap 4: tokenisation mismatch. Queries and documents must be tokenised identically. In the drill, "upload quota" fails to match "uploading" because there is no stemming; a tokeniser that splits "E1234" into "e" and "1234" makes the error code nearly useless. Most retrieval bugs are tokenisation bugs.

Trap 5: stale statistics. idf and the average length depend on the whole collection. If documents are added without updating them, scores drift. Engines recompute them per segment or periodically; a small index rebuilt from scratch avoids the problem. Tests should therefore build the index from the same snapshot of documents they query.

9. Edge cases worth naming

A query with no known terms returns nothing from BM25 (the search method filters zero scores), while dense retrieval always returns k results, however poor; a fused list then comes entirely from the dense side, which may or may not be what you want. Repeated query terms ("w3 w3 w10") are counted once here; some variants weight repeated query terms more. Very short documents (titles) get large length boosts; b may need lowering for collections with very variable lengths, or fields such as title and body may need separate parameters (BM25F). k larger than the number of documents must be clamped. And RRF needs a decision about documents present in only one list: they simply earn from that list alone.

Chunking: what a "document" is

In a RAG system the units being retrieved are rarely whole documents; they are chunks of a few hundred tokens cut from longer documents, often with some overlap. Chunk size interacts with everything in this lesson. BM25's length normalisation assumes lengths vary for reasons that matter; with fixed-size chunks, lengths are nearly uniform and b has little effect. Small chunks give precise matches but may separate a question's answer from the context that explains it; large chunks carry more context but dilute both BM25 term frequencies and embedding similarity, because one vector must summarise more topics. Common practice is 200 to 500 tokens with 10 to 20% overlap, splitting on headings and paragraphs rather than at fixed character counts, and storing the parent document id so that neighbouring chunks can be fetched after retrieval. A retrieval evaluation should be rerun whenever chunking changes, because it often moves recall more than switching embedding models.

Query-side choices

The query is text too, and how it is processed matters. A conversational assistant's latest message ("and what about the second one?") is often useless as a search query on its own; rewriting it with the help of the conversation history into a standalone question before retrieval is a standard step. Long natural-language questions contain many common words that BM25 largely ignores and a few rare ones that dominate, which is usually what you want. Dense retrieval benefits from asymmetric models that embed queries and passages differently, or from instructions prepended to the query ("Represent this question for retrieving supporting passages"), depending on how the embedding model was trained. And some queries should not go to retrieval at all; routing greetings or arithmetic elsewhere saves cost and avoids pasting irrelevant passages into the prompt.

10. Complexity

Indexing is linear in the total number of tokens. A BM25 query costs the sum of its terms' postings lengths: tiny for rare terms, close to N for stop words, which is why engines skip or down-weight them and why dynamic pruning (in the deep section) matters. Memory is proportional to the number of postings. Exact dense search is O(N·d) per query (a matrix-vector product) plus O(N) for selection; for millions of documents an approximate nearest-neighbour index (lm10) makes it sub-linear. RRF is linear in the total length of the lists, usually a few hundred entries.

11. Talking through the design

In the interview: write the BM25 formula and explain idf, saturation and length normalisation; build the inverted index and say why; precompute impacts; select top-k with argpartition; normalise embeddings once; fuse with RRF and explain why not to add scores; test against a direct scan and against known properties; then discuss evaluation (recall@k first), tokenisation, the numerical traps, and what changes at scale: compression, pruning, sharding, approximate vector search and a re-ranker.

Where BM25 comes from

BM25 is not an ad-hoc formula. Robertson and Zaragoza derive it from the probabilistic relevance framework: rank documents by the probability that they are relevant to the query, assume terms contribute independently, and model term frequencies with a mixture ("eliteness": a document is either about the term or mentions it incidentally). The exact model has too many unknowns, so BM25 approximates its shape: a weight that grows with term frequency but saturates, combined with the Robertson/Spärck Jones term weight. When no relevance judgements are available, that weight reduces to the familiar idf form ln((N − n + 0.5)/(n + 0.5)). Length normalisation comes from the observation that documents can be long either because they are verbose (the same content with more words, which should be normalised) or because they cover more topics (which should not), and b interpolates between those views. The review notes the model gives no guidance on k1 and b, which must be tuned on judged queries, and that 1.2 < k1 < 2 and 0.5 < b < 0.8 work well in many collections.

BM25 versus plain TF-IDF, in numbers

A useful way to see what saturation and length normalisation buy is to compare with the simplest TF-IDF score, tf times idf. Take two documents of average length, one mentioning "quota" once and one mentioning it ten times. TF-IDF scores the second ten times higher; BM25 with k1 = 1.5 scores it 2.17 times higher. Now take a document twice the average length that mentions the term twice: TF-IDF gives it double the one-mention score, while BM25 with b = 0.75 gives it a length factor of 1.5 × (0.25 + 1.5) = 2.625 and a fraction of 5/4.625 = 1.08, barely more than a single mention in an average document. In practice this stops keyword-stuffed or very long pages from dominating, which is exactly the behaviour search users expect.

Why RRF works so well

Cormack and colleagues introduced RRF as a simple baseline for experiments on learned fusion and found it was hard to beat. Their formula, Σ 1/(k + r(d)), has two properties. The reciprocal decays slowly, so lower-ranked documents still contribute, unlike an exponential discount that effectively only counts the top. The constant k limits the influence of any single list's top ranks, so one outlier system cannot dominate. In their pilot over 30 systems on TREC topics, mean average precision was 0.2072 at k = 0, rising to 0.2145 at k = 60 and falling only to 0.2098 at k = 500; the best individual system scored 0.2016, Condorcet fusion 0.2074 and CombMNZ 0.2039. Across their experiments RRF outperformed Condorcet, CombMNZ and the best system by 4 to 5% on average. Modern search engines (Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Vespa, many vector databases) offer it as the default hybrid method.

Making BM25 fast at a billion documents

Postings are sorted by document id and compressed: store the gaps between successive ids rather than the ids themselves (small numbers), then encode those with variable-byte or bit-packed SIMD codecs, typically a few bits per posting. Query evaluation merges postings lists in document order. Dynamic pruning exploits top-k: keep the current k-th best score as a threshold; for each term, precompute the maximum impact any posting can contribute; a document whose sum of term upper bounds cannot beat the threshold is skipped without being scored. WAND applies this per term; Block-Max WAND stores the maximum per block of postings, allowing much more skipping. Top-10 queries often touch a small fraction of the postings. Indexes are sharded by document; each shard returns its top k, and a coordinator merges them, which is exact because BM25 scores are per-document (using global statistics shared across shards).

Exact versus approximate dense search

Exact search is a matrix-vector product and a selection: perfectly accurate and simple, and on a GPU fast enough for a few million vectors. Beyond that, approximate nearest-neighbour indexes (HNSW graphs, IVF with product quantisation) trade a small loss of recall for sub-linear cost. The recall of the ANN index then becomes another term in the system's recall budget, and should be measured against exact search on a sample of queries.

Beyond the basic hybrid

Learned sparse retrieval (SPLADE) uses a transformer to assign weights to vocabulary terms, including expansion terms not in the text, served from an ordinary inverted index: lexical precision with some semantic recall. Late interaction (ColBERT) keeps one vector per token and scores with the sum over query tokens of the maximum similarity to any document token, more accurate than one vector per document at greater storage cost. Cross-encoder re-ranking reads query and document together and scores the top 50 to 200 candidates precisely. Weighted fusion: with labelled queries, a learned convex combination of normalised scores can beat RRF; RRF is the strong default when you have no labels.

Evaluating retrieval

Recall@k measures whether the relevant passages are in the top k at all; for RAG it is the ceiling on answer quality, so it comes first. Mean reciprocal rank rewards placing the first relevant passage high; nDCG handles graded relevance and multiple relevant passages. The BEIR benchmark evaluated many retrievers zero-shot across 18 datasets and found BM25 a robust baseline that many dense models trained on one domain failed to beat elsewhere, the empirical case for hybrid search.

Where this runs

Every RAG system, enterprise search box, e-commerce search, code search and documentation assistant uses some combination of these pieces. Elasticsearch and OpenSearch implement BM25 (Lucene's variant with the always-positive idf) and offer vector search and RRF; vector databases add BM25 or learned sparse vectors for hybrid search. Even when you use a managed service, the concepts here are what you tune and debug.

Failure modes and symptoms

Exact identifiers not found. Users search for an order number or error code and get topically related but wrong results. Cause: dense-only retrieval or a tokeniser that splits identifiers. Fix: hybrid with BM25, identifier-preserving tokenisation, and evaluation queries of this kind.

Paraphrases not found. The opposite: lexical-only search misses documents that use different words. Fix: add dense retrieval or query expansion.

One retriever dominates the fusion. Someone replaced RRF with a score sum, or one list returns many more candidates than the other. Check the fusion method and list depths (fusing the top 50 to 100 of each is typical).

Quality drops after an index update. Chunking changed, the embedding model was upgraded without re-embedding old documents (mixed embedding spaces), or collection statistics are stale. Version embeddings with the model that produced them; re-embed everything on a model change.

Latency spikes on certain queries. Queries with very common terms touch huge postings lists. Use stop-word handling, dynamic pruning and query timeouts; monitor slow queries.

Good retrieval, bad answers. Recall@k is high but the generator still fails: too many irrelevant passages around the right one, or the right passage cut off by chunking. Add a re-ranker, reduce k, and inspect chunks.

Duplicate passages crowd out diversity. The same paragraph appears in many documents (boilerplate, copied sections), and the top k is filled with near-identical chunks, so the model sees one piece of evidence k times. Deduplicate at indexing time by hashing normalised text, and consider diversity-aware selection (for example maximal marginal relevance) at query time.

Access control leaks. A retrieval index built over all company documents returns passages the user is not allowed to see, which the model then summarises. Filter by the user's permissions inside the retrieval query, not after generation.

What to monitor

Recall@k and MRR on a fixed labelled set, run on every index or model change; the share of queries with zero lexical matches; latency percentiles per retriever and for fusion; index size and freshness; the fraction of final results contributed by each retriever; and user signals such as clicks and reformulated queries.

A cost estimate

For 10 million passages of about 200 tokens: a BM25 index with compressed postings is a few gigabytes; 768-dimensional float32 embeddings take 10⁷ × 768 × 4 bytes ≈ 31 GB, or about 8 GB in int8, before ANN overhead. Exact dense search would be 7.7 billion multiply-adds per query, a few milliseconds on a GPU, and an ANN index brings CPU search down to a few milliseconds. Fusion and a cross-encoder over 100 candidates typically dominate latency, at tens of milliseconds.

The references for this problem

Robertson and Zaragoza's review "The Probabilistic Relevance Framework: BM25 and Beyond" (2009), by BM25's own authors, derives the formula used here: the saturating term-frequency function with k1, the length normalisation with b, and the Robertson/Spärck Jones weight that becomes the idf when no relevance information is available. It states that the (k1 + 1) factor "does not affect the ranking produced", which the tests confirm, and that experiments suggest "values such as 0.5 < b < 0.8 and 1.2 < k1 < 2 are reasonably good in many circumstances". Cormack, Clarke and Büttcher's SIGIR 2009 paper defines RRFscore(d) = Σ 1/(k + r(d)) with "k = 60 … fixed during a pilot investigation and not altered during subsequent validation", reports the pilot's flat MAP curve from k = 0 to k = 500, and finds RRF outperforms Condorcet, CombMNZ and the best individual system by 4 to 5% on average. The implementation in this lesson follows both definitions exactly, with Lucene's always-positive idf.

Work it out

Write recall_at_k and mrr over a small labelled query set and compare BM25 with fusion of BM25 and a second ranking. Why is recall@k the first metric to look at for a RAG system?

Answer 
def recall_at_k(ranked, relevant, k):
    return len(set(ranked[:k]) & relevant) / len(relevant)

def mean_reciprocal_rank(runs):
    return float(np.mean([next((1 / (i + 1) for i, d in enumerate(r) if d in rel), 0.0) for r, rel in runs]))

docs = ["error E1234 occurs when the disk quota is exceeded",
        "how to reset your password and recover the account",
        "the disk is full: free space by deleting old logs",
        "password reset links expire after one hour",
        "quota exceeded error when uploading large files to storage"]
small = BM25(docs)
assert small.search("E1234", 1)[0][0] == 0                   # a rare exact token wins outright
queries = {"disk full": {2, 0}, "reset password": {1, 3}, "uploading files over quota": {4}}
bm_runs = [([d for d, _ in small.search(q, 5)], rel) for q, rel in queries.items()]
assert all(recall_at_k(r, rel, 5) == 1.0 for r, rel in bm_runs)
assert mean_reciprocal_rank(bm_runs) == 1.0
fused_runs = [(rrf([r, list(reversed(r))]), rel) for r, rel in bm_runs]   # fuse with a poor second list
assert all(recall_at_k(r, rel, 5) == 1.0 for r, rel in fused_runs)

Try the last query as "upload quota": document 4 then ties with document 0, because without stemming "upload" does not match "uploading"; tokenisation and normalisation are part of retrieval quality, and a labelled query set is how you notice.

A generator can only use what retrieval puts in its context, so recall@k (with k equal to the number of passages you actually send) is an upper bound on the whole system's answer quality. If recall is low, no prompt or model change helps: fix chunking, the embedding model, hybrid search or query rewriting first. Once recall is high, precision metrics (MRR, nDCG) and the re-ranker decide how much noise surrounds the right passage.
