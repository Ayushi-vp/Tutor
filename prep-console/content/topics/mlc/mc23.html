Point a detector at a photo of a street and, before any post-processing, it does not say "three cars and a cyclist". It says something like: a car at this box with confidence 0.94, a car at a box shifted two pixels to the right with confidence 0.91, another slightly larger with 0.88, and so on, thousands of candidate boxes, many of them describing the same object. That is a consequence of how detectors work: they make a prediction at every position and scale of a feature map, and the positions near an object all fire. Shown directly to a user, or passed to a tracker or a robot's planner, the result is useless: one car appears as twenty.

Non-maximum suppression (NMS) is the step that turns that cloud into one box per object. It needs one measure, intersection over union (IoU), which says how much two boxes overlap, and one simple greedy rule: keep the most confident box, delete the boxes that overlap it heavily, and repeat. The same IoU also decides whether a detection counts as correct in evaluation, and variants of it are used as training losses, so it is one of the most frequently written functions in computer vision.

The cost of getting it wrong is concrete. In a self-checkout camera, a duplicate box can charge a customer twice; in a pedestrian detector, a deleted neighbour is a person the vehicle does not see; in a document parser, two overlapping text boxes produce repeated words. The algorithm is small, but it sits at the boundary between a model's raw output and every decision made from it.

It is also a classic interview question because it is short enough to write in twenty minutes and full of details that reveal experience. Do you clip the intersection at zero? Do you vectorise so that the loop runs once per kept box rather than once per pair? How do you stop a person from suppressing the bicycle they are riding? What happens in a crowd, where two real people overlap more than the threshold? Why does NMS resist parallelisation, and how do GPU implementations cope? And what do modern detectors do to avoid NMS entirely?

This lesson works IoU, NMS and soft-NMS by hand on three boxes, writes a looped implementation, a vectorised one and a GPU-style matrix version, checks that all three agree on 1,000 random boxes and that the result satisfies the contract in torchvision's documentation, adds per-class NMS and soft-NMS, shows three numerical traps (zero-area boxes, float16 coordinates and the legacy +1 pixel convention), times the versions, and implements GIoU in the drill.

Imagine several witnesses each pointing at where they saw a suspect in a crowd photo, each with a confidence. Many point at the same person, slightly differently. To produce a list of distinct suspects you would take the most confident witness's answer, discard the other answers that point at essentially the same spot, then take the most confident remaining answer, and so on. That is non-maximum suppression: keep the local maximum of confidence, suppress the rest.

"Essentially the same spot" needs a number. For two rectangles, the natural one is intersection over union (IoU): the area they share, divided by the total area they cover together. Identical boxes have IoU 1, boxes that do not touch have IoU 0, and a box shifted by half its width against an identical one has IoU 1/3 (it shares half its area, and together they cover one and a half boxes). IoU is scale-invariant: two 10-pixel boxes offset by 1 pixel overlap as much, relatively, as two 100-pixel boxes offset by 10.

A few reference values build intuition. Two equal boxes shifted by a tenth of their width have IoU 0.82; by a quarter, 0.6; by a third, 0.5; by half, 0.33. So the common threshold of 0.5 means, roughly, "these boxes are offset by less than a third of their size". A small box entirely inside a box four times its area has IoU 0.25, even though one contains the other, which is worth remembering when duplicates come in different sizes.

With IoU in hand, greedy NMS is three lines of description: sort the boxes by confidence; take the best and add it to the output; delete every remaining box whose IoU with it exceeds a threshold, typically 0.5; repeat until no boxes remain. Each object ends up represented by its most confident box, as long as the duplicates overlap it more than the threshold and different objects overlap each other less.

Where does NMS sit in the bigger picture? A detector is a pipeline: an image goes through a convolutional or transformer backbone that produces feature maps; a detection head predicts, at every location of those maps, box coordinates and class scores; those raw predictions are decoded into boxes in image pixels; low-confidence boxes are discarded; and NMS removes the duplicates among the rest. Only then do the boxes reach the user, a tracker or an evaluation script.

Two refinements matter in practice. First, boxes of different classes should not suppress each other: a person and the bicycle they ride overlap heavily but are both real. Per-class NMS runs the procedure separately for each class. Second, in crowds two real objects can overlap more than the threshold, and plain NMS deletes one of them. Soft-NMS lowers the scores of overlapping boxes in proportion to how much they overlap instead of deleting them, so a genuine neighbour survives with a reduced but still useful score. Where the witness analogy breaks: witnesses are independent, while a detector's duplicate boxes are highly correlated outputs of one model, which is why later research tried to have the model itself avoid producing duplicates.

1. The problem as it is usually asked

"Implement IoU between boxes and greedy NMS. Then make it class-aware." Clarify: the box format (corners [x1, y1, x2, y2] as here and in torchvision, versus centre, width and height as many detectors predict); whether coordinates are continuous or inclusive pixel indices (some old code adds 1 to widths); whether suppression is "greater than" or "greater than or equal to" the threshold (torchvision removes boxes with IoU strictly greater); how ties in score are broken; and the expected scale (tens of boxes in an interview, tens of thousands in a real detector). Outline: a broadcasting IoU, greedy NMS with one vectorised IoU per kept box, a matrix version, per-class by offsets, soft-NMS, and tests against invariants.

2. The maths in plain words

Intersection. Two axis-aligned rectangles overlap in a rectangle (possibly empty). Its left edge is the further-right of the two left edges, its right edge is the further-left of the two right edges, and similarly for top and bottom. If the left edge ends up to the right of the right edge, the boxes do not overlap in that direction, and the width is zero, not negative: that clipping is the single most common bug in IoU code, because two negative lengths multiply to a positive "area".

Union. Adding the two areas counts the overlap twice, so subtract it once. IoU is the ratio of the two.

Greedy NMS. Process boxes in decreasing score. A box is kept if no already-kept box overlaps it above the threshold; otherwise it is suppressed. Equivalently, each kept box removes its heavy overlaps from the remaining pool. Because a suppressed box never suppresses anything, the order of processing matters, and the result is defined by that order.

Soft-NMS. Instead of removing an overlapping box, multiply its score by a factor that shrinks with the overlap: 1 − IoU when IoU is above a threshold (linear), or exp(−IoU²/σ) (Gaussian, applied to every remaining box). After each selection, the next box chosen is the one with the highest current score, so rescored boxes can still be selected later.

3. The equations

box b = [x1, y1, x2, y2],  x2 > x1, y2 > y1         area(b) = (x2 − x1)·(y2 − y1)

intersection:  w = max(0, min(a.x2, b.x2) − max(a.x1, b.x1))
               h = max(0, min(a.y2, b.y2) − max(a.y1, b.y1))
IoU(a, b)    = w·h / (area(a) + area(b) − w·h + ε)

greedy NMS:    order = argsort(−scores)
               keep box i  ⇔  IoU(i, k) ≤ t for every kept k processed before i

soft-NMS (after selecting M, for every remaining box i):
   linear:     s_i ← s_i · (1 − IoU(M, i))  if IoU(M, i) ≥ N_t, else unchanged
   Gaussian:   s_i ← s_i · exp(−IoU(M, i)² / σ)

per-class:     b ← b + class · (max coordinate + 1)     (cross-class IoU becomes exactly 0)

GIoU(a, b)   = IoU − (area(C) − area(a ∪ b)) / area(C),  C the smallest box enclosing both

4. A worked example by hand: NMS on three boxes

Boxes A = [0, 0, 10, 10] with score 0.9, B = [1, 1, 11, 11] with 0.8 and C = [6, 0, 16, 10] with 0.7. Every box has area 100. Threshold 0.5.

  • IoU(A, B). Intersection x from max(0, 1) = 1 to min(10, 11) = 10, width 9; y likewise, height 9; area 81. Union 100 + 100 − 81 = 119. IoU = 81/119 = 0.6807.
  • IoU(A, C). x from 6 to 10, width 4; y from 0 to 10, height 10; area 40. Union 160. IoU = 0.25.
  • IoU(B, C). x from 6 to 11, width 5; y from 1 to 10, height 9; area 45. Union 155. IoU = 0.2903.
  • Greedy NMS. Sort: A (0.9), B (0.8), C (0.7). Keep A. B overlaps A by 0.68 > 0.5: suppressed. C overlaps A by 0.25 ≤ 0.5: survives. Next highest remaining is C; keep it; nothing remains. Result [A, C]. (B versus C never needed computing.)
  • Gaussian soft-NMS, σ = 0.5. Keep A. B's score becomes 0.8 × exp(−0.6807²/0.5) = 0.8 × exp(−0.927) = 0.8 × 0.396 = 0.3167. C's becomes 0.7 × exp(−0.0625/0.5) = 0.7 × exp(−0.125) = 0.7 × 0.8825 = 0.6177. Highest remaining is C: keep it. B decays again by exp(−0.2903²/0.5) = exp(−0.1686) = 0.845, to 0.2676. Keep B. Order [A, C, B]; nothing was deleted, but B has dropped to a low score.

Whether B is a duplicate of A or a second object half-hidden behind it cannot be known from the boxes alone. Hard NMS bets it is a duplicate and deletes it; soft-NMS hedges by ranking it last with a score of 0.27, which a downstream score threshold can then decide on. The first solution block asserts all of these numbers.

import math
import time
import numpy as np

# The hand-worked example: three boxes [x1, y1, x2, y2] with scores, IoU threshold 0.5.
A_, B_, C_ = [0, 0, 10, 10], [1, 1, 11, 11], [6, 0, 16, 10]
# A and B: overlap 9 × 9 = 81, union 100 + 100 − 81 = 119.
assert math.isclose(81 / 119, 0.680672, rel_tol=1e-5)
# A and C: overlap 4 × 10 = 40, union 160 -> 0.25.   B and C: overlap 5 × 9 = 45, union 155 -> 0.2903.
assert 40 / 160 == 0.25 and round(45 / 155, 4) == 0.2903
# Greedy NMS: keep A (0.9); B overlaps A by 0.68 > 0.5 -> removed; C overlaps A by 0.25 -> kept.  Result [A, C].
# Gaussian soft-NMS (sigma = 0.5): after A, B -> 0.8·exp(−0.6807²/0.5), C -> 0.7·exp(−0.25²/0.5).
sB = 0.8 * math.exp(-(81 / 119) ** 2 / 0.5); sC = 0.7 * math.exp(-0.25 ** 2 / 0.5)
assert round(sB, 4) == 0.3167 and round(sC, 4) == 0.6177
sB *= math.exp(-(45 / 155) ** 2 / 0.5)                     # then C is picked and decays B again
assert round(sB, 4) == 0.2676                              # order A, C, B: nothing deleted, B just ranked last

5. The naive implementation: Python loops

def iou_pair_naive(a, b):
    """IoU of two boxes with plain Python arithmetic."""
    w = min(a[2], b[2]) - max(a[0], b[0])
    h = min(a[3], b[3]) - max(a[1], b[1])
    inter = max(w, 0) * max(h, 0)                          # clip: disjoint boxes have no overlap
    union = (a[2] - a[0]) * (a[3] - a[1]) + (b[2] - b[0]) * (b[3] - b[1]) - inter
    return inter / union if union > 0 else 0.0

def nms_naive(boxes, scores, thresh=0.5):
    """Greedy NMS with explicit loops: O(N²) Python-level IoU computations in the worst case."""
    order = sorted(range(len(scores)), key=lambda i: -scores[i])
    removed, keep = set(), []
    for pos, i in enumerate(order):
        if i in removed:
            continue
        keep.append(i)
        for j in order[pos + 1:]:
            if j not in removed and iou_pair_naive(boxes[i], boxes[j]) > thresh:
                removed.add(j)
    return keep

This is a direct transcription of the definition: a scalar IoU, and NMS that walks the sorted boxes, skipping removed ones, and compares each kept box with every later one. It is easy to verify by reading, handles the clipping and the zero-union case explicitly, and serves as the reference for the faster versions. Its cost is Python-level work for every pair the loop touches: up to N²/2 IoU computations of a few dozen interpreted operations each. In the test, 2,000 random boxes took 2.5 seconds.

6. The vectorised implementations

def area(b):
    return (b[..., 2] - b[..., 0]) * (b[..., 3] - b[..., 1])

def iou_matrix(a, b, eps=1e-12):
    """(N, 4) and (M, 4) -> (N, M) IoU by broadcasting; eps guards zero-area boxes."""
    lt = np.maximum(a[:, None, :2], b[None, :, :2])          # top-left of each intersection
    rb = np.minimum(a[:, None, 2:], b[None, :, 2:])          # bottom-right
    wh = np.clip(rb - lt, 0, None)                           # clip negative widths/heights to 0
    inter = wh[..., 0] * wh[..., 1]
    return inter / (area(a)[:, None] + area(b)[None, :] - inter + eps)

def nms(boxes, scores, thresh=0.5):
    """Greedy NMS: one vectorised IoU per *kept* box against everything still alive."""
    boxes, scores = np.asarray(boxes, float), np.asarray(scores, float)
    order = np.argsort(-scores, kind="stable")
    keep = []
    while order.size:
        i = order[0]
        keep.append(int(i))
        order = order[1:][iou_matrix(boxes[i:i + 1], boxes[order[1:]])[0] <= thresh]
    return keep

def nms_matrix(boxes, scores, thresh=0.5):
    """GPU-style: compute the full IoU matrix once, then a cheap sequential scan over boolean rows."""
    boxes = np.asarray(boxes, float)
    order = np.argsort(-np.asarray(scores), kind="stable")
    over = iou_matrix(boxes[order], boxes[order]) > thresh  # over[i, j]: i would suppress j
    alive = np.ones(len(order), bool)
    for i in range(len(order)):
        if alive[i]:
            alive[i + 1:] &= ~over[i, i + 1:]
    return [int(k) for k in order[alive]]

def batched_nms(boxes, scores, classes, thresh=0.5):
    """Per-class NMS in one call: shift each class's boxes far apart so cross-class IoU is 0."""
    boxes = np.asarray(boxes, float)
    offset = np.asarray(classes, float)[:, None] * (boxes.max() + 1)
    return nms(boxes + offset, scores, thresh)

def soft_nms(boxes, scores, sigma=0.5, method="gaussian", thresh=0.3, min_score=0.001):
    """Decay overlapping scores instead of deleting (Bodla et al. 2017). Returns (kept order, new scores)."""
    boxes, s = np.asarray(boxes, float), np.asarray(scores, float).copy()
    idx, keep = np.arange(len(s)), []
    while idx.size:
        m = idx[np.argmax(s[idx])]
        keep.append(int(m))
        idx = idx[idx != m]
        if idx.size:
            ov = iou_matrix(boxes[m:m + 1], boxes[idx])[0]
            if method == "gaussian":
                s[idx] *= np.exp(-ov ** 2 / sigma)
            else:                                            # linear: only above the threshold
                s[idx] *= np.where(ov >= thresh, 1 - ov, 1.0)
            idx = idx[s[idx] >= min_score]
    return keep, s

iou_matrix computes IoU between every box in one set and every box in another by broadcasting: inserting axes so that an (N, 1, 2) array of top-left corners meets a (1, M, 2) array gives an (N, M, 2) array of intersection corners in one NumPy call. Clipping with np.clip(…, 0, None) handles non-overlap, and the epsilon in the denominator keeps zero-area boxes from producing NaN.

nms keeps the greedy loop but vectorises its body: each iteration computes the IoU between the newly kept box and all boxes still alive in one call, and filters them with a boolean mask. The loop runs once per kept box rather than once per pair. The stable sort makes tie-breaking deterministic (the lower index wins), which matters because torchvision's documentation warns that ties may resolve differently on CPU and GPU.

nms_matrix is the structure GPU implementations use: compute the whole N × N "would suppress" matrix in parallel, then perform a sequential scan in which each surviving row clears the boxes it suppresses. The matrix costs O(N²) memory and work but is massively parallel; the scan is a cheap pass over booleans (on a GPU, packed into 64-bit masks). On a CPU it is not faster than the per-kept version, since most of the matrix is never needed; on a GPU it is.

batched_nms implements per-class NMS by shifting each class's boxes by class × (maximum coordinate + 1) in both x and y. Boxes of different classes then cannot intersect, so one NMS call handles every class at once. soft_nms repeatedly picks the highest current score, decays the others with the chosen rule, and drops boxes whose score falls below a small floor.

Before NMS: decoding and filtering

In a real detector, NMS is the last of several steps, and bugs often hide in the earlier ones. The network usually predicts box offsets relative to an anchor or grid cell, often as centre, width and height in normalised units; these must be decoded into corner coordinates in pixels before IoU makes sense. Class scores are converted to probabilities (a sigmoid per class in most modern detectors), and each candidate box is paired with its best class or, in multi-label set-ups, duplicated once per class above the threshold. Then a score threshold (for example 0.25 or 0.001 for evaluation) and a top-k (for example 1,000) cut the candidates before NMS, and a maximum number of detections (for example 100 or 300) caps the output after it. Each of these settings affects both accuracy and latency, and mismatches between the training framework and the deployed engine are a common source of "the exported model is worse".

After NMS: matching detections for evaluation

Evaluation uses IoU differently. Sort the image's detections of a class by score; walk them in order; each detection is matched to the unmatched ground-truth box of the same class with which it has the highest IoU, if that IoU is at least the evaluation threshold (0.5 for PASCAL VOC). A matched detection is a true positive; an unmatched one, including a second detection of an already matched object, is a false positive. Precision and recall are computed down the ranked list, giving a precision-recall curve whose area is the class's average precision (mc9 covers ranking metrics). This is why duplicates that survive NMS cost precision directly, and why soft-NMS, which keeps neighbours at low scores, rarely hurts: low-scoring false positives sit at the end of the ranked list where they affect AP least.

7. Testing it

There is no torchvision in this environment, so the reference is its documented contract, checked directly: kept indices come back in decreasing score order, no two kept boxes overlap above the threshold, and every removed box overlaps some higher-scoring kept box above the threshold. Those three properties define greedy NMS given distinct scores. The tests also check: the hand-worked numbers through the vectorised code; IoU edge cases (identical, half-shifted, disjoint, contained, touching edges) and symmetry; agreement of the looped, vectorised and matrix versions on 1,000 random boxes at thresholds 0.3, 0.5 and 0.7; per-class NMS by offsets matching a literal loop over classes; soft-NMS keeping a crowded neighbour that hard NMS deletes; the three numerical traps; agreement of the IoU matrix with a PyTorch computation; and the timings.

The invariant test is worth adopting as a habit. A single example passing can hide a bug that appears only when boxes chain (A suppresses B, and B would have suppressed C), but the invariants must hold for every input, so checking them on a thousand random boxes exercises many configurations at once. The random boxes here are fairly sparse, so most survive (1,707 of 2,000 at threshold 0.5); a real detector's candidates cluster heavily around objects, so far fewer survive and the per-kept loop runs fewer times.

Why the order matters: a chain of three

A second small example shows why NMS is inherently sequential. Take A = [0, 0, 10, 10] (0.9), B = [3, 0, 13, 10] (0.8) and C = [6, 0, 16, 10] (0.7), three boxes in a row, each shifted by 3. IoU(A, B) = IoU(B, C) = 70/130 = 0.54 and IoU(A, C) = 40/160 = 0.25. Greedy NMS keeps A, removes B (0.54 > 0.5), and then keeps C, because the only box that overlapped C heavily, B, is already gone: the result is [A, C]. A shortcut that suppresses every box overlapped above the threshold by any higher-scoring box, kept or not, removes C as well and returns only [A]. That shortcut is exactly what makes the computation a single parallel matrix operation, and it is what Fast NMS accepts in exchange for speed. Whether C was a third object or a duplicate is again unknowable from the boxes, but the two algorithms disagree, and a correct greedy implementation must return [A, C].

8. Numerical and practical traps

Trap 1: no clipping. Without clipping widths and heights at zero, two boxes that are apart in both directions produce two negative lengths whose product is a positive "intersection". The edge-case test catches this: disjoint boxes must have IoU exactly 0.

Trap 2: zero-area boxes. A degenerate box (x2 = x1) can come from a regression head or a clipping step. Two of them give 0/0 = NaN, and NaN comparisons are always false, so a NaN IoU never suppresses anything. The epsilon makes the IoU 0; better still, filter boxes with tiny area before NMS.

Trap 3: float16 coordinates. Above 2,048, float16 can only represent even numbers. In the test, boxes [3000, 0, 3003, 10] and [3001, 0, 3004, 10] (IoU exactly 0.5) both round to the same box in float16, giving IoU 1.0. High-resolution images and mixed-precision inference make this real: keep box arithmetic in float32.

Trap 4: the +1 convention. Older code (some early detection frameworks and the PASCAL VOC evaluation code) treated coordinates as inclusive pixel indices and computed width as x2 − x1 + 1. For boxes [0, 0, 2, 2] and [1, 1, 3, 3], continuous IoU is 1/7 = 0.14 and the +1 version 4/14 = 0.29. Mixing conventions between training, NMS and evaluation silently shifts results.

Trap 5: ties. Equal scores make the result depend on sort stability; torchvision notes CPU and GPU may differ. Use a stable sort or an explicit secondary key when determinism matters.

Trap 6: thresholds in the wrong place. "Greater than" versus "greater than or equal" changes results when IoU lands exactly on the threshold, which happens with integer coordinates. Match the framework you are replicating (torchvision: strictly greater suppresses).

9. Edge cases worth naming

No boxes: return an empty list. One box: return it. All boxes identical: one survives, the one with the highest score (or the lowest index on ties). A box entirely inside another: IoU is the smaller area divided by the larger, so a small box inside a big one may survive (IoU 0.25 in the edge-case test), which is sometimes wrong for duplicates of different sizes; some pipelines also suppress by "intersection over the smaller area". Boxes with negative coordinates (partly outside the image) are fine for IoU but should usually be clipped to the image first. And per-class offsets need the maximum coordinate across all boxes, so they must be computed after any coordinate transform.

10. Complexity

Sorting costs O(N log N). The suppression loop costs O(N) per kept box, O(N·K) in total for K kept boxes, O(N²) in the worst case when nothing is suppressed. The matrix version always costs O(N²) time and memory but parallelises fully apart from a scan over N rows. Soft-NMS is O(N²) because every selection rescans all remaining boxes, as Bodla and colleagues note. In practice N is bounded by keeping only the top few thousand candidates by score, and per-class NMS reduces N per class. Timings in the test for 2,000 boxes: loops 2.5 s, per-kept vectorised 0.19 s, full matrix 0.23 s on a CPU.

11. Talking through the design

In the interview: define the box format; write IoU with clipping and an epsilon; write greedy NMS with one vectorised IoU per kept box; state the complexity; add per-class NMS with the offset trick; offer soft-NMS for crowds; test with invariants on random boxes. Then the system view: pre-filtering by score and top-k, GPU kernels and their bitmask scan, threshold tuning, and NMS-free detectors.

Soft-NMS in the original paper

Bodla and colleagues observe that greedy NMS sets the score of a neighbouring detection to zero whenever its overlap with the selected box exceeds the threshold, so "if an object lies within the predefined overlap threshold, it leads to a miss". Their fix rescales the score with a function of the overlap. The linear rule multiplies by (1 − IoU) above the threshold Nt, but it is discontinuous at Nt, so they prefer a Gaussian penalty, exp(−IoU²/σ), applied to all remaining boxes at each step. They note that soft-NMS is still greedy, that each step is O(N) so the whole is O(N²) like NMS, and that traditional NMS is the special case with a binary weighting function. With σ = 0.5 (Gaussian) and Nt = 0.3 (linear), they report improvements without retraining of 1.7% mAP on PASCAL VOC 2007 for both R-FCN and Faster R-CNN, and 1.3% and 1.1% on MS-COCO, with larger gains at higher IoU thresholds.

NMS on the GPU

The sequential dependency in greedy NMS (whether box j is suppressed depends on whether the box overlapping it was itself kept) cannot be removed, but its cost can be moved. GPU kernels launch a thread block per tile of the IoU matrix, write one bit per (i, j) pair saying "i would suppress j", and pack 64 such bits into each integer. The final scan walks the sorted boxes once, OR-ing the masks of kept boxes into a running "removed" mask: N steps of a few integer operations each. torchvision, TensorRT and ONNX Runtime all provide such operators. Variants such as Fast NMS (used in YOLACT) accept a slightly different result, suppressing a box if any higher-scoring box overlaps it, kept or not, to make the whole operation a single matrix computation.

Why greedy is accepted

One could ask for the best subset of boxes under some global objective, for example maximising the total score minus a penalty for overlapping pairs. That is a combinatorial problem, hard to solve exactly and far too slow for real-time use, and the objective would itself be a heuristic. Greedy NMS is fast, predictable and good enough when the detector's scores are well calibrated, which is why it survived for more than a decade while research focused on making detectors produce fewer duplicates in the first place.

Beyond hard and soft

Weighted NMS (or box voting) replaces the kept box with a score-weighted average of the boxes it suppresses, improving localisation because duplicates contain information about the object's extent. DIoU-NMS uses DIoU instead of IoU, so that two boxes with the same overlap but distant centres (likely two objects) are less likely to suppress each other. Adaptive NMS predicts a density per object and raises the threshold in crowded regions. Matrix NMS (from SOLOv2) computes decayed scores for all boxes in parallel, trading greedy exactness for speed.

Masks, rotated boxes and 3D

The same algorithm works for any shape with an overlap measure. Instance-segmentation models suppress by mask IoU, computed as the number of pixels in both masks divided by the number in either, which is more accurate for thin or diagonal objects but costs a pass over pixels per pair. Aerial-image and text detectors predict rotated boxes, whose intersection is a convex polygon computed by clipping one rectangle against the other; the IoU code becomes geometry, but NMS is unchanged. Lidar detectors for autonomous driving use 3D boxes and often compute IoU in the bird's-eye-view plane. In each case the cost of one IoU grows, which makes pre-filtering and GPU kernels more important.

Getting rid of NMS

DETR treats detection as set prediction: a transformer decoder outputs a fixed number of box slots, and training uses Hungarian matching to assign each ground-truth object to exactly one slot, with the rest trained to predict "no object". Since predicting the same object twice is penalised during training, no NMS is needed at inference. YOLOv10 brings the idea to real-time detectors with "consistent dual assignments": a one-to-many head provides rich training signal, a one-to-one head is used at inference, and NMS is removed from the deployment pipeline, which simplifies export and stabilises latency. Latency becomes stable because the post-processing no longer depends on how many candidate boxes a frame produces. The cost is in training: matching-based losses tend to converge more slowly than the dense assignments used by NMS-based detectors, which is one reason both styles of detector remain in wide use today and why interviewers still expect a clean NMS implementation.

IoU as a loss and as a metric

Plain IoU has zero gradient for non-overlapping boxes. GIoU (Rezatofighi and colleagues) subtracts the fraction of the smallest enclosing box not covered by the union, giving a value in (−1, 1] that keeps improving as boxes move closer. DIoU adds a normalised centre-distance penalty and CIoU an aspect-ratio term. As a metric, IoU decides matches: PASCAL VOC uses 0.5, and COCO averages AP over thresholds 0.5, 0.55, …, 0.95, rewarding precise localisation, which is also where soft-NMS gains most.

Where this runs

Every anchor-based or grid-based detector (Faster R-CNN, the YOLO family before v10, RetinaNet, SSD) ends with NMS; so do many instance-segmentation models (with mask IoU), open-vocabulary detectors such as OWL-ViT and Grounding DINO, OCR text detectors and face detectors. Tracking-by-detection pipelines use IoU to associate detections across frames. Evaluation code for all of these uses IoU to match predictions to ground truth.

Failure modes and symptoms

Missing people in crowds. Recall drops in dense scenes (queues, stadiums, retail shelves). Cause: hard NMS deleting genuine neighbours. Fix: soft-NMS, a higher threshold, DIoU-NMS or an NMS-free model; evaluate on crowded subsets separately.

Duplicate boxes in output. Two boxes on one object. Causes: threshold too high, class-agnostic duplicates of different classes (a "car" and a "truck" box on one vehicle), or NMS run on boxes before decoding them into the right coordinate frame. Consider class-agnostic NMS as a second pass when classes are confusable.

Latency spikes on busy frames. NMS time grows with the number of candidates. Set a score threshold and a top-k before NMS, and a maximum number of detections after it; monitor candidate counts per frame.

Accuracy differs between training framework and deployed engine. Different NMS semantics (strict versus non-strict threshold, +1 convention, class-aware versus agnostic, top-k limits) or float16 coordinates in the exported model. Run the same images through both and compare boxes before and after NMS.

Evaluation numbers that do not match published ones. Differences in IoU convention, matching rules or the maximum detections per image. Use the official evaluation code for benchmarks.

Tracks that flicker. In video, a tracker associates each frame's detections with existing tracks by IoU. If NMS keeps a different one of two near-duplicate boxes from frame to frame, or deletes an occluded person intermittently, tracks break and identities switch. Smoothing scores over time, soft-NMS, and association that tolerates a missing frame reduce flicker.

Score calibration shifts after retraining. A new model version produces systematically lower or higher scores, so the same score threshold passes many more or fewer boxes into NMS, changing both latency and recall. Re-tune thresholds for every model version rather than carrying them over.

What to monitor

Number of candidates before NMS and detections after, per frame; NMS latency percentiles; precision and recall at the operating threshold, sliced by crowd density and object size; and drift in score distributions, which changes how many boxes pass the pre-filter.

Choosing settings for a new deployment

Start from the framework's defaults (an IoU threshold around 0.5 to 0.7, a score threshold around 0.25 for production and near zero for computing mAP, and a cap of 100 to 300 detections per image). Then, on a validation set drawn from the deployment's own images, sweep the IoU threshold and the score threshold together and pick the pair that meets the product's precision or recall target. Check crowded and sparse images separately, measure end-to-end latency on the target hardware with realistic candidate counts, and record the chosen settings next to the model version so they move together. Revisit them whenever the camera, the image resolution or the mix of scenes changes.

A cost estimate

A YOLO-style detector at 640 × 640 predicts at three scales, roughly 8,400 grid positions with 80 class scores each. With a score threshold of 0.25 most frames keep a few hundred candidates, and NMS on a GPU takes well under a millisecond. Without a pre-filter, all 8,400 boxes enter NMS: the IoU matrix alone has 70 million entries, which is why every deployment sets the score threshold and a top-k.

The reference for this problem

The torchvision documentation for torchvision.ops.nms defines the behaviour implemented and tested here. It "performs non-maximum suppression (NMS) on the boxes according to their intersection-over-union (IoU)" and "iteratively removes lower scoring boxes which have an IoU greater than iou_threshold with another (higher scoring) box". Boxes are "expected to be in (x1, y1, x2, y2) format with 0 <= x1 < x2 and 0 <= y1 < y2", and the return value is the indices "that have been kept by NMS, sorted in decreasing order of scores". It also warns that if several boxes have exactly the same score, "the selected box is not guaranteed to be the same between CPU and GPU". The tests check each of these properties. Soft-NMS follows Bodla and colleagues (arXiv 1704.04503), with their Gaussian σ = 0.5 and linear Nt = 0.3 as defaults.

Work it out

Implement GIoU for two boxes. For a reference box [0, 0, 2, 2], compute IoU and GIoU against [3, 0, 5, 2] and [6, 0, 8, 2]. Why does this matter when IoU is used as a training loss?

Answer 
def giou(a, b):
    """Generalised IoU: IoU minus the fraction of the enclosing box not covered by the union."""
    inter = max(min(a[2], b[2]) - max(a[0], b[0]), 0) * max(min(a[3], b[3]) - max(a[1], b[1]), 0)
    union = (a[2] - a[0]) * (a[3] - a[1]) + (b[2] - b[0]) * (b[3] - b[1]) - inter
    hull = (max(a[2], b[2]) - min(a[0], b[0])) * (max(a[3], b[3]) - min(a[1], b[1]))
    return inter / union - (hull - union) / hull

near, far = [3, 0, 5, 2], [6, 0, 8, 2]
base = [0, 0, 2, 2]
assert iou_pair_naive(base, near) == iou_pair_naive(base, far) == 0.0      # IoU cannot tell them apart
assert math.isclose(giou(base, near), -0.2) and math.isclose(giou(base, far), -0.5)
assert math.isclose(giou(base, base), 1.0)

Both boxes miss the reference, so IoU is 0 for each and its gradient is zero: a model trained with an IoU loss gets no signal to move either prediction. The enclosing box for the nearer one is [0, 0, 5, 2], area 10, of which the union covers 8, so GIoU = 0 − 2/10 = −0.2. For the farther one the enclosing box has area 16, union 8, GIoU = −0.5. GIoU keeps improving as the prediction moves towards the target, so it provides a useful gradient before any overlap exists, and it equals IoU once the boxes overlap tightly.
