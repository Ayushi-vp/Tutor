A large language model generates text one token at a time: each new token needs a full forward pass through the network, and each pass must wait for the previous token. On a GPU, that pass is dominated not by arithmetic but by reading every weight from memory, so a 70-billion-parameter model spends most of each step moving 140 GB of 16-bit weights to produce one token — and could have processed several tokens in that same pass almost for free, if only it knew what they were. A small model, a tenth or a hundredth the size, can guess the next few tokens quickly and is often right on the easy ones ("the", a closing bracket, the rest of a common word).

Speculative decoding turns that observation into an algorithm. The small draft model proposes γ tokens. The large target model scores all γ positions in one forward pass. A careful acceptance rule keeps drafted tokens with probability min(1, p/q), and on the first rejection samples a replacement from a specific residual distribution. The remarkable property, proved by Leviathan, Kalman and Matias and independently by Chen and colleagues at DeepMind in 2023, is that the result is distributed exactly as if the target had generated every token itself. The draft changes only the speed. Chen et al. reported 2-2.5× faster decoding on the 70-billion-parameter Chinchilla.

The interview problem asks you to implement the rule and prove it, which tests probability reasoning as much as NumPy. This lesson does both: the rule in plain words and equations; a three-token worked example where the output distribution can be added up by hand (and the most common bug gives the wrong answer by 0.1); the expected number of tokens per target call, (1 − α^(γ+1))/(1 − α), worked for α = 0.8; a loop implementation and a batched one on tiny random-weight language models; and tests that check the rule analytically on 1,000 random distributions, that the two implementations agree draw for draw, that 20,000 generated sequences match the target's exact distribution as closely as plain sampling does, that the buggy variant is detectably biased, and that tokens per call match the formula.

The analogy: a junior drafting, a senior signing off

A senior lawyer's time is expensive, and writing a contract clause by clause, each one waiting for the last, wastes it. So a junior drafts the next five clauses quickly. The senior reads all five at once — reading is fast compared with writing — and goes through them in order. Each clause the senior would have been at least as likely to write is kept. A clause the junior was over-eager about is kept only some of the time, in proportion to how much the senior agrees. At the first clause the senior rejects, the senior writes a replacement, choosing among wordings the junior under-used, and the draft from there on is thrown away, since it was built on the rejected clause. If all five pass, the senior adds a sixth of their own. The final contract reads exactly as if the senior had written it alone — but the senior "wrote" several clauses per sitting instead of one.

The junior is the draft model q; the senior is the target model p; reading five clauses at once is the batched verification pass; keeping a clause with probability min(1, p/q) is the acceptance rule; writing a replacement from the under-used wordings is sampling the residual norm(max(0, p − q)); the sixth clause is the bonus token.

The analogy breaks in a useful place: a human senior who disagrees writes what they prefer. The algorithm's senior must sample the replacement from a precise distribution — the part of p that q left out — or the contract would no longer read like the senior's own. That one detail is what makes the method exact.

What the interviewer is checking

Three things, in order of how often candidates miss them. First, the probability argument: can you show, not just assert, that the output follows the target? The two-term sum — accepted mass min(p, q) plus residual mass p − min(p, q) — is the whole proof, and saying it cleanly is the strongest signal in this round. Second, the residual: many candidates know "accept with p/q" but resample from p on rejection, which is wrong in a way that is easy to demonstrate with three tokens. Third, the systems reasoning: why one target pass over several positions costs about the same as over one, why drafting cannot be parallelised, and when the trick stops paying. The implementation itself is short; the explanation around it is what is being graded.

1. The problem as it is usually asked

"Implement speculative sampling: given a draft model and a target model that both return next-token distributions, generate tokens using γ draft proposals per round, so that the output is distributed exactly as sampling from the target. Prove it, and say how much faster it is." Clarify: both models share a vocabulary; the target can evaluate many positions in one call; sampling is plain (temperature 1) unless stated; we need a proof, a working implementation, and a test that the output distribution is right. Outline: the rule and the proof by hand; the expected-speed formula; a loop implementation; a batched one; tests.

2. The maths in plain words

At one position the draft says "token x with probability q(x)" and the target says "x with probability p(x)". Draw x from the draft. If the target likes x at least as much as the draft did (p(x) ≥ q(x)), keep it. If the target likes it less, keep it only with probability p(x)/q(x) — the fraction of the draft's enthusiasm the target shares. This alone gives every token probability min(p(x), q(x)) of coming out, which is too little for tokens the draft under-proposed. The missing amount for each token is p(x) − min(p, q) = max(0, p(x) − q(x)). Rejections happen with total probability 1 − Σ min(p, q), and that is exactly the total missing amount. So on rejection, draw from the missing amounts, normalised — the residual — and every token ends up with exactly p(x).

The speed comes from doing this for several positions per target pass. The draft proposes x1…xγ one after another (each conditioned on the previous ones). The target computes p at all γ positions, plus the one after, in one batched pass. Then the checks run left to right; the first rejection ends the round with a residual sample, and if none occurs, the target's distribution at position γ + 1 provides a free extra token.

3. The equations

draft:        x_i ~ q_i = q(· | prefix, x_1..x_{i-1})            i = 1..γ      (γ sequential draft calls)
verify:       p_i = p(· | prefix, x_1..x_{i-1})                 i = 1..γ+1    (ONE batched target call)
accept x_i:   u_i ~ U(0,1);   accept  ⟺  u_i · q_i(x_i) < p_i(x_i)   (i.e. u < p/q; always if p ≥ q)
n = number accepted before the first rejection
if n < γ:     y ~ norm(max(0, p_{n+1} − q_{n+1}))                (residual)
else:         y ~ p_{γ+1}                                        (bonus token)
emit:         x_1 .. x_n, y                                       (n + 1 tokens per round)

exactness:    P(x) = q(x)·min(1, p(x)/q(x)) + (1 − β)·max(0, p(x) − q(x))/(1 − β)
                   = min(p, q)(x) + p(x) − min(p, q)(x) = p(x),      β = Σ_x min(p(x), q(x))
acceptance:   β = 1 − TV(p, q),   α = E[β]
speed:        E[tokens per target call] = (1 − α^{γ+1}) / (1 − α)
              walltime gain = (1 − α^{γ+1}) / ((1 − α)(γc + 1)),   c = draft cost / target cost

4. A worked example by hand

Three tokens. Target p = [0.5, 0.3, 0.2]; draft q = [0.3, 0.5, 0.2]. The draft under-proposes token 0, over-proposes token 1, and matches token 2.

  • Acceptance probability. β = min(0.5, 0.3) + min(0.3, 0.5) + min(0.2, 0.2) = 0.3 + 0.3 + 0.2 = 0.8. Equivalently, the total variation distance is ½(0.2 + 0.2 + 0) = 0.2.
  • Residual. max(0, p − q) = [0.2, 0, 0]; normalised, [1, 0, 0]. After a rejection the replacement is always token 0 — the only one the draft under-served.
  • Token 0. Drafted with probability 0.3; since p ≥ q it is always accepted: 0.3. Rejections (probability 0.2) always produce token 0: +0.2. Total 0.5.
  • Token 1. Drafted with 0.5; accepted with 0.3/0.5 = 0.6: 0.5 × 0.6 = 0.3. Never produced by the residual.
  • Token 2. Drafted with 0.2, always accepted: 0.2.

Exactly p. Now the bug: resample from p instead of the residual on rejection. Token 0: 0.3 + 0.2 × 0.5 = 0.40; token 1: 0.3 + 0.2 × 0.3 = 0.36; token 2: 0.2 + 0.2 × 0.2 = 0.24. The output leans toward the draft's over-proposed token 1, by 0.06 — a bias that compounds over a long generation.

Speed by hand. With α = 0.8 and γ = 4, the expected tokens per target call are (1 − 0.85)/(1 − 0.8) = (1 − 0.32768)/0.2 = 3.36. If a draft pass costs c = 0.05 of a target pass, a round costs 4 × 0.05 + 1 = 1.2 target-pass units, so the walltime gain is 3.36/1.2 = 2.80×. The paper's Table 1 lists 3.69× for α = 0.8, γ = 5 with c = 0, which the formula reproduces. The first block asserts every number.

import numpy as np

# The hand-worked example: three tokens, target p and draft q.
p = np.array([0.5, 0.3, 0.2]); q = np.array([0.3, 0.5, 0.2])
beta = np.minimum(p, q).sum()                                  # acceptance probability = 1 - TV(p, q)
residual = np.maximum(p - q, 0) / np.maximum(p - q, 0).sum()
out = q * np.minimum(1, p / q) + (1 - beta) * residual         # P(emit x) after one speculative token
assert np.isclose(beta, 0.8) and residual.tolist() == [1.0, 0.0, 0.0]
assert np.allclose(out, p)                                     # 0.3 + 0.2, 0.3, 0.2: exactly the target
wrong = q * np.minimum(1, p / q) + (1 - beta) * p              # resample from p instead of the residual
assert np.allclose(wrong, [0.4, 0.36, 0.24])                   # biased toward the draft's mistakes

# Expected tokens per target call and walltime improvement (Leviathan et al., Eq. 1 and Thm 3.8).
def expected_tokens(alpha, gamma):
    return (1 - alpha ** (gamma + 1)) / (1 - alpha)
def walltime_gain(alpha, gamma, c):
    return expected_tokens(alpha, gamma) / (gamma * c + 1)
assert round(expected_tokens(0.8, 4), 4) == 3.3616
assert round(walltime_gain(0.8, 4, 0.05), 2) == 2.80
assert round(expected_tokens(0.8, 5), 2) == 3.69               # the paper's Table 1 (c = 0)

5. The naive implementation: loops on tiny models

To test the algorithm we need two language models that are cheap, deterministic and whose exact output distributions can be computed. TinyLM is an order-2 model: random logits indexed by the previous two tokens, a stand-in for a transformer's next-token head. The target has logits of scale 2; the draft adds noise of scale 0.7 to the same logits, so it is a decent but imperfect approximation (at the empty context, β ≈ 0.84). Both expose probs(contexts), which evaluates many contexts in one call — the equivalent of a transformer's single forward pass over several positions. sample draws by inverse CDF from a supplied uniform number, so a test can feed two implementations the same random stream.

The naive round follows the algorithm literally: draft γ tokens in a loop, call the target once per position (deliberately unbatched), draw γ uniforms, check left to right with u · q(x) < p(x) — multiplying instead of dividing avoids a division by q, which matters when q is tiny — and on the first failure sample the residual (the sample function normalises by the cumulative total, so the unnormalised residual can be passed directly).

V, SEED = 4, 0                                                 # vocabulary size; tokens 0..3, 0 also pads

def softmax(z, axis=-1):
    z = z - z.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True)                     # stable: largest logit becomes 0
    e = np.exp(z)
    return e / e.sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True)

class TinyLM:
    """A random-weight order-2 language model: next-token logits depend on the last two tokens.
    Stands in for a transformer: probs(contexts) evaluates many contexts in ONE call."""
    def __init__(self, logits):
        self.logits = logits                                     # (V, V, V): [a, b] -> logits of next token

    def probs(self, ctx):                                        # ctx: (..., 2) integer array
        ctx = np.asarray(ctx)
        return softmax(self.logits[ctx[..., 0], ctx[..., 1]])

rng = np.random.default_rng(SEED)
target = TinyLM(rng.normal(scale=2.0, size=(V, V, V)))
draft = TinyLM(target.logits + rng.normal(scale=0.7, size=(V, V, V)))   # a cheaper, noisier guess of it

def sample(probs, u):
    """Inverse-CDF sampling: index of the first cumulative probability above u * total."""
    c = np.cumsum(probs, axis=-1)
    return int(np.searchsorted(c, u * c[-1], side="right").clip(max=len(probs) - 1))

def spec_step_naive(seq, gamma, rng):
    """One round of speculative sampling, written as plain loops."""
    ctx, qs, xs = list(seq), [], []
    for _ in range(gamma):                                       # 1. draft proposes gamma tokens
        q_i = draft.probs(ctx[-2:]); x = sample(q_i, rng.random())
        qs.append(q_i); xs.append(x); ctx.append(x)
    ps = [target.probs((list(seq) + xs[:i])[-2:]) for i in range(gamma + 1)]   # 2. target scores all
    us, out = [rng.random() for _ in range(gamma)], []
    for i, x in enumerate(xs):                                   # 3. accept or reject, left to right
        if us[i] * qs[i][x] < ps[i][x]:                          # u < p/q, written without dividing
            out.append(x)
        else:
            r = np.maximum(ps[i] - qs[i], 0.0)
            out.append(sample(r, rng.random()))                  # residual: norm(max(0, p - q))
            return out
    out.append(sample(ps[gamma], rng.random()))                  # all accepted: one bonus token from p
    return out

6. The batched implementation

Drafting stays a loop: each draft token conditions on the previous one, and that dependency is the whole reason the target is slow too. Everything else becomes array operations. The target's γ + 1 contexts are stacked into one array and scored with one probs call — the single verification pass. Acceptance for all γ positions is one comparison of a vector of uniforms against p/q, written as u * q[i, x_i] < p[i, x_i] with fancy indexing. The number accepted is γ if everything passed, otherwise the index of the first False, found with argmin on the boolean array. The last token comes from the residual at that index or from the bonus distribution. generate repeats rounds until enough tokens exist and counts target calls and accepted drafts.

def spec_step(seq, gamma, rng):
    """Same algorithm, vectorised where it can be: one batched target call over gamma + 1 contexts,
    one comparison for all acceptances, the first rejection found with argmin."""
    ctx = list(seq)
    q = np.empty((gamma, V)); xs = np.empty(gamma, dtype=int)
    for i in range(gamma):                                       # drafting is inherently sequential
        q[i] = draft.probs(ctx[-2:]); xs[i] = sample(q[i], rng.random()); ctx.append(int(xs[i]))
    full = np.array(list(seq) + xs.tolist())
    contexts = np.stack([full[len(seq) - 2 + i: len(seq) + i] for i in range(gamma + 1)])
    p = target.probs(contexts)                                   # (gamma + 1, V): ONE target call
    u = rng.random(gamma)
    ok = u * q[np.arange(gamma), xs] < p[np.arange(gamma), xs]
    n = gamma if ok.all() else int(np.argmin(ok))                # tokens accepted before the first rejection
    if n < gamma:
        last = sample(np.maximum(p[n] - q[n], 0.0), rng.random())
    else:
        last = sample(p[gamma], rng.random())
    return xs[:n].tolist() + [last], n

def generate(n_tokens, gamma, rng, start=(0, 0)):
    seq, calls, accepted = list(start), 0, 0
    while len(seq) - 2 < n_tokens:
        new, n = spec_step(seq, gamma, rng)
        seq += new; calls += 1; accepted += n
    return seq[2:2 + n_tokens], calls, accepted

7. Testing it

Six checks. (1) The exactness identity on 1,000 random pairs of 7-token distributions drawn from a Dirichlet with concentration 0.5 (so many entries are near zero, the hard case): q·min(1, p/q) + (1 − β)·residual equals p every time. (2) The naive and batched rounds return identical tokens for 200 seeds — same algorithm, same random stream. (3) The empirical test that matters: generate 20,000 three-token sequences with speculative decoding (γ = 3) and 20,000 with plain target sampling, enumerate the target's exact probability for all 43 = 64 sequences, and compare total variation distances. Both are about 0.017 (0.0169 and 0.0162 in the reference run), and they differ by far less than the sampling noise; the assertion requires both under 0.03 and within 0.01 of each other. Plain sampling is the yardstick: with finite samples TV is never zero, so the question is whether speculative decoding is as close as the real thing. (4) The test can see a bug: the resample-from-p variant, on the first token alone, lands at a TV of about 0.11 — more than four times the correct method's. (5) Tokens per target call: with context-independent p = [0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1] and q uniform, β is a constant 0.8; 20,000 rounds with γ = 4 average 3.35 tokens per call against the formula's 3.36. (6) If PyTorch is installed, the batched softmax matches torch.softmax to 10−12.

import itertools

# 1. The acceptance rule reproduces p exactly, for any p and q (analytic check, 1,000 random pairs).
r = np.random.default_rng(1)
for _ in range(1000):
    pp = r.dirichlet(np.ones(7) * 0.5); qq = r.dirichlet(np.ones(7) * 0.5)
    b = np.minimum(pp, qq).sum()
    res = np.maximum(pp - qq, 0); res = res / res.sum() if res.sum() > 0 else pp
    assert np.allclose(qq * np.minimum(1, pp / qq) + (1 - b) * res, pp)

# 2. Naive and vectorised steps agree draw for draw when fed the same random numbers.
for s in range(200):
    a = spec_step_naive([0, 0, 3], 4, np.random.default_rng(s))
    b2, _ = spec_step([0, 0, 3], 4, np.random.default_rng(s))
    assert a == b2

# 3. Empirical check: whole 3-token sequences from speculative decoding match the target's exact
#    distribution over all 4^3 = 64 sequences, as well as plain sampling from the target does.
def exact_target_dist(n=3, start=(0, 0)):
    dist = {}
    for seq in itertools.product(range(V), repeat=n):
        full, pr = list(start) + list(seq), 1.0
        for i in range(n):
            pr *= target.probs(full[i:i + 2])[seq[i]]
        dist[seq] = pr
    return dist
exact = exact_target_dist()
assert abs(sum(exact.values()) - 1) < 1e-12
N = 20_000
g = np.random.default_rng(7)
spec_counts, plain_counts = {}, {}
calls_total = acc_total = 0
for _ in range(N):
    s, calls, acc = generate(3, gamma=3, rng=g)
    spec_counts[tuple(s)] = spec_counts.get(tuple(s), 0) + 1
    calls_total += calls; acc_total += acc
    ctx = [0, 0]
    for _ in range(3):
        ctx.append(sample(target.probs(ctx[-2:]), g.random()))
    plain_counts[tuple(ctx[2:])] = plain_counts.get(tuple(ctx[2:]), 0) + 1
tv = lambda counts: 0.5 * sum(abs(counts.get(k, 0) / N - v) for k, v in exact.items())
tv_spec, tv_plain = tv(spec_counts), tv(plain_counts)
assert tv_spec < 0.03 and tv_plain < 0.03 and abs(tv_spec - tv_plain) < 0.01

# 4. The common bug -- resampling from p on rejection -- is visibly biased at this sample size.
def biased_first_token(n, rng):
    q0, p0 = draft.probs([0, 0]), target.probs([0, 0])
    x = np.array([sample(q0, u) for u in rng.random(n)])
    keep = rng.random(n) * q0[x] < p0[x]
    redo = np.array([sample(p0, u) for u in rng.random(n)])
    return np.where(keep, x, redo), p0
xb, p0 = biased_first_token(N, np.random.default_rng(3))
tv_biased = 0.5 * np.abs(np.bincount(xb, minlength=V) / N - p0).sum()
assert tv_biased > 4 * tv_spec                                  # about 0.11 versus 0.017

# 5. Tokens per target call match (1 - a^(g+1)) / (1 - a) when the acceptance rate is constant.
pc = np.array([0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1]); qc = np.array([0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25])
alpha_c = np.minimum(pc, qc).sum()                               # 0.25 + 0.25 + 0.2 + 0.1 = 0.8
gamma, steps, h = 4, 20_000, np.random.default_rng(11)
x = np.array([[sample(qc, u) for u in h.random(gamma)] for _ in range(steps)])
ok = h.random((steps, gamma)) * qc[x] < pc[x]
n_acc = np.where(ok.all(axis=1), gamma, np.argmin(ok, axis=1))
tokens_per_call = (n_acc + 1).mean()                             # accepted drafts plus one target token
assert np.isclose(alpha_c, 0.8) and abs(tokens_per_call - expected_tokens(0.8, gamma)) < 0.05

try:

On the tiny models, 60,000 tokens took about 29,400 target calls — roughly 2 tokens per call — with 66% of drafted tokens accepted. The calls are capped by the short sequences (three tokens each), which limits how many drafts a round can use; with longer generations and a closer draft, real systems see the 2-3× the papers report.

8. Numerical and practical traps

Dividing by q. p/q overflows or divides by zero when q(x) is tiny; compare u · q(x) < p(x) instead. A token the draft gives zero probability is never drafted, so q(x) = 0 never reaches the check. Residual of zero mass. If p = q exactly, β = 1 and rejection is impossible, but floating-point noise can still make the residual sum zero after a "rejection" at u ≈ 1; guard by falling back to p when the residual sums to zero. Negative residual entries from rounding. Always take max(0, ·) before normalising. Mismatched sampling settings. Temperature, top-k and top-p must already be applied to both p and q; the rule then preserves the transformed target distribution. Reusing random numbers. The uniform for the acceptance test and the one for the residual draw must be independent; reusing u biases the residual draw toward low cumulative probability.

9. Edge cases worth naming

γ = 0 degenerates to ordinary sampling from the target (one bonus token per call). Identical models accept everything: γ + 1 tokens per call. Disjoint supports accept nothing: one token per call plus wasted drafting. End of sequence: if a drafted or replacement token is the end-of-sequence token, stop and discard the rest. Greedy decoding: p and q are one-hot, so accept exactly while the draft's token equals the target's argmax, and on rejection emit the target's argmax.

10. Complexity

Per round: γ sequential draft passes and one target pass over γ + 1 positions, plus O(γ·V) arithmetic for acceptance and the residual, where V is the vocabulary size. Expected target passes per generated token: (1 − α)/(1 − αγ+1). Memory: the target's pass needs activations for γ + 1 positions instead of one, and both models keep KV caches. The wall-clock benefit assumes the target pass over γ + 1 positions costs about the same as over one, which holds when decoding is memory-bandwidth bound (small batches) and fades at large batch sizes.

11. Talking through the design

In an interview, lead with the rule and the one-line proof (min(p, q) from acceptance plus p − min(p, q) from the residual), then the speed formula with numbers, then implementation details: sequential drafting, one batched verification, multiply instead of divide, residual on rejection, bonus token on full acceptance, KV-cache rollback. Close with when it helps (latency-sensitive, small batches, a well-matched draft) and when it does not (large batches, low α, an expensive draft).

Where the draft comes from

α is everything, and α is the overlap between the two models' next-token distributions. The original papers used smaller models from the same family (Leviathan et al. observed that draft models about two orders of magnitude smaller worked best); Chen et al. trained a 4-billion-parameter draft for Chinchilla 70B. Distilling the draft from the target raises α further. Other designs drop the separate model: Medusa adds extra prediction heads to the target to propose several future tokens at once; EAGLE drafts from the target's own internal features rather than from tokens; n-gram or "prompt lookup" drafts copy spans from the prompt, which is surprisingly effective for editing and summarising tasks where the output repeats the input. Leviathan et al. note that even a trivial bigram draft gave α ≈ 0.2 and a 1.25× gain in one translation setting.

Tree speculation

One drafted sequence is a single bet: a rejection at position 2 wastes positions 3 to γ. Tree-based methods draft several candidate continuations at each step (the top few draft tokens), arrange them in a tree, and verify the whole tree in one target pass using an attention mask that lets each node see only its ancestors. The longest accepted path is kept. More candidates raise the expected accepted length per pass, at the cost of a wider target pass; since that pass is memory-bound, the extra width is nearly free up to a point. Preserving the exact distribution with multiple candidates needs a generalised acceptance rule that tries candidates in turn against updated residuals.

Why verification is nearly free

During decoding with a batch of one, each forward pass reads every weight once and performs about two floating-point operations per weight per token. A GPU can do hundreds of operations in the time it takes to read one weight from high-bandwidth memory, so processing 5 tokens instead of 1 adds arithmetic the hardware was idling through anyway. This is the arithmetic-intensity argument from lm13. It also explains the limit: at large batch sizes the weights are already reused across many sequences, decoding becomes compute-bound, and the γ + 1 positions really cost γ + 1 times as much — which is why serving systems often disable speculation under heavy load.

The proof across positions

The single-position identity is the heart of it, but a round emits several tokens, so the full argument is an induction. Condition on the prefix. The first emitted token is distributed as p1 by the identity. Given that the first emitted token is x1 and it was the accepted draft, the second position's draft and target distributions are q2 and p2 conditioned on the prefix plus x1, so the identity applies again. If instead the first token came from the residual, the round ends, and the next round starts afresh from the new prefix — again with the correct conditional. Either way each emitted token has the target's conditional distribution given everything before it, so the whole sequence has the target's joint distribution. The empirical test on full three-token sequences checks exactly this joint claim.

Speculation inside a batched server

Real servers decode many requests at once, and each request's acceptance in a round differs: one accepts 5 tokens, another 0. A batched implementation must handle ragged results — different numbers of new tokens per sequence — by tracking per-sequence lengths, and the target's verification pass covers γ + 1 positions for every sequence in the batch, even those that will reject early. As the batch grows, that extra work is no longer free, which is why servers often tie γ to load: generous speculation when traffic is light and requests are latency-sensitive, little or none when the GPU is saturated and throughput matters more.

Lossless versus lossy variants

Some systems relax exactness for speed: accepting a draft token if it is in the target's top-k, or if p(x) exceeds a threshold, raises the acceptance rate but changes the output distribution. These "lossy" variants can be fine for some applications, but they are a different algorithm with different guarantees, and evaluations must then compare output quality, not just speed. The appeal of the rule in this lesson is that it needs no quality evaluation at all: correctness is a theorem.

Where speculative decoding runs

Most production inference servers support some form of speculative decoding — separate draft models, extra decoding heads, or n-gram drafts — because single-request latency is what users feel in chat and coding assistants. It is typically turned on for interactive, low-batch traffic and adjusted or turned off as batch sizes grow and the hardware becomes compute-bound.

Failure modes and symptoms

No speedup. Symptom: tokens per second unchanged or worse. Causes: low α (the draft is poorly matched, or sampling settings differ between models), a draft that is not cheap enough, or a server already compute-bound at its batch size. Diagnose with acceptance rate and mean accepted length per round.

Subtle output drift. Symptom: outputs with speculation differ in style or quality from outputs without. Cause: an implementation bug — resampling from p on rejection, mismatched temperature or top-p between p and q, reused random numbers. Diagnose by comparing token-level statistics with and without speculation on the same prompts, or by running the exactness test on a small model.

Cache corruption. Symptom: garbage after the first rejection. Cause: rejected positions not rolled back in the KV cache of the target or the draft.

Latency spikes. Symptom: occasional slow tokens. Cause: long runs of rejections, each wasting γ draft passes. Adaptive γ — shrinking it when recent acceptance is low — smooths this.

What to monitor and tune

Acceptance rate per model pair and per traffic type (code, chat, structured output have very different α); mean accepted tokens per round; draft time as a fraction of round time (the measured c); tokens per second per request with and without speculation; and γ. Tune γ from measured α and c using the walltime formula, revisit when the target or draft changes, and consider disabling speculation above a batch-size threshold.

A cost estimate

A 70B target at 16 bits is about 140 GB of weights; on a GPU with 3 TB/s of memory bandwidth, one decoding pass takes at least about 47 ms (140 / 3,000 s) at batch size one, about 21 tokens per second. A 1B draft costs about 0.7 ms per pass (c ≈ 0.015). With α = 0.8 and γ = 5: expected tokens per round (1 − 0.86)/0.2 = 3.69; round cost 47 + 5 × 0.7 ≈ 50.5 ms; about 73 tokens per second — roughly 3.5× faster, before overheads.

The reference for this problem

Leviathan, Kalman and Matias, "Fast Inference from Transformers via Speculative Decoding" (ICML 2023), define the sampling rule — keep x if q(x) ≤ p(x), otherwise reject with probability 1 − p(x)/q(x) and resample from norm(max(0, p(x) − q(x))) — prove it preserves p, define β = Σ min(p, q) and α = E[β], derive the expected tokens per run (1 − αγ+1)/(1 − α) and the walltime improvement (1 − αγ+1)/((1 − α)(γc + 1)), and tabulate speeds (for example 3.69× at α = 0.8, γ = 5, c = 0). Chen et al., "Accelerating Large Language Model Decoding with Speculative Sampling" (DeepMind, 2023), arrived at the same rule independently and reported 2-2.5× decoding speedups on Chinchilla 70B in a distributed setup.

Work it out

(a) A team measures α = 0.7 and c = 0.1 for their draft. Find the best γ from 1 to 10 and the expected walltime gain. (b) They propose a cheaper draft with c = 0.02 but α = 0.55. Which pair is better?

Answer 
def best_gamma(alpha, c, max_gamma=10):
    gains = {g: walltime_gain(alpha, g, c) for g in range(1, max_gamma + 1)}
    g = max(gains, key=gains.get)
    return g, gains[g]
g_a, gain_a = best_gamma(0.7, 0.1)
g_b, gain_b = best_gamma(0.55, 0.02)
assert (g_a, round(gain_a, 2)) == (4, 1.98)
assert (g_b, round(gain_b, 2)) == (5, 1.96)

(a) The gain (1 − 0.7γ+1)/(0.3(0.1γ + 1)) rises to γ = 4 — (1 − 0.75)/0.3 = 2.77 tokens per call, divided by a round cost of 1.4 — giving 1.98×, then falls (1.96× at γ = 5). (b) With α = 0.55 and c = 0.02 the peak is at γ = 5: (1 − 0.556)/0.45 = 2.16 tokens per call, divided by 1.1 = 1.96×. The two drafts are nearly tied: the cheaper one makes more speculation affordable but wastes more of it. Exactly this kind of near-tie is why the decision should be made from measured α and c with the walltime formula rather than intuition — and the lower-α draft will also have more variable latency, since long runs of rejections are more likely.
