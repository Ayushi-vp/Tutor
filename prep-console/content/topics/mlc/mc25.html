The feed-forward blocks of a transformer hold most of its parameters, and every token passes through all of them. Doubling the model's capacity doubles the compute for every token generated. A mixture-of-experts layer breaks that link: replace the one feed-forward block with E of them — the experts — and add a small router that, for each token, picks k of the E. With 8 experts and k = 2, the layer holds four times the parameters of a top-2 dense equivalent while each token pays for only two experts. That is how Mixtral 8x7B has about 47 billion parameters but uses about 13 billion per token, and how Switch Transformers scaled to a trillion parameters.

The idea is simple; the engineering is not. Routing is data-dependent, so the number of tokens per expert varies from batch to batch, while accelerators want fixed-size work. The answer is expert capacity — a fixed number of slots per expert — which means some tokens get dropped when an expert is popular. Popular experts become more popular, because they receive more gradient, so routers collapse onto a few experts unless an auxiliary loss pushes the load back toward uniform. And a fast implementation must move tokens into per-expert buffers and back without a Python loop over tokens.

This lesson builds the layer from scratch in NumPy, in the problem's usual order: the maths in plain words; the equations; a worked example of 4 tokens and 3 experts with top-2 routing, capacity 3, one dropped assignment and the load-balancing loss computed by hand; a per-token loop that defines the correct behaviour, capacity rule included; a vectorised dispatch-and-combine version using a cumulative count, an (E, C, d) buffer and batched einsums; the Switch Transformer auxiliary loss with its hand-derived gradient; and tests showing the vectorised layer matches the loop exactly — including which assignments are dropped — matches a dense masked computation, and that gradient descent on the auxiliary loss rebalances a router biased towards one expert from 62% of tokens to an even 25% each.

The analogy: a hospital triage desk

A hospital has several specialist clinics instead of one general ward. At the entrance a triage nurse looks at each patient and decides which two clinics they should see, giving each a weight — "mostly cardiology, a bit of respiratory" — and the patient's final treatment plan blends the two clinics' advice in those proportions. Each clinic can see only so many patients per hour. When cardiology is full, a patient whose second choice was cardiology simply does not get that consultation today and leaves with only their first clinic's plan; patients' first choices are booked before anyone's second choices, so nobody loses their main consultation to someone else's backup. And the hospital board watches the numbers: if the triage nurse sends nearly everyone to cardiology, the board nudges the nurse to spread patients out, or the other clinics sit idle while cardiology turns people away.

Patients are tokens; clinics are experts; the triage nurse is the router; "which two, with what weights" is top-k routing with renormalised gates; clinic slots per hour are expert capacity; the missed consultation is a dropped token, and leaving with only the first plan is the residual connection carrying the rest; booking first choices first is the priority order; the board's nudge is the load-balancing loss.

The analogy breaks in one instructive way: real clinics specialise by medical field, chosen by people. MoE experts are blocks of parameters whose "specialities" emerge from training, and they rarely line up with human categories.

Sparse compute, dense memory

The headline trade is worth stating precisely. Per token, the layer does about k experts' worth of arithmetic, so a top-2 layer with 8 experts costs about the same per token as a dense layer twice the size of one expert. But all 8 experts' weights exist and must be stored, trained and, at inference, kept in memory. So MoE models are cheap in FLOPs per token and expensive in memory and communication. That asymmetry explains most design choices that follow: capacity limits (to keep work regular), load balancing (to keep every expert's parameters useful and every device equally busy), and the attention paid to how tokens move between devices.

What the interviewer is checking

Whether the router and gates are right (softmax, top-k, renormalisation, stable tie-breaking); whether capacity and dropping are handled with a defined priority rather than ignored; whether the implementation avoids a Python loop over tokens — the cumulative-count trick and the batched einsum are the usual signals; whether the combine step accumulates correctly when a token goes to several experts; and whether the candidate knows why the load-balancing loss exists and what its value means. Testing against a loop and a dense masked computation shows the candidate can verify an optimised implementation, which matters more here than in most problems because routing bugs produce plausible-looking numbers.

1. The problem as it is usually asked

"Implement a mixture-of-experts feed-forward layer in NumPy with a top-k router. Handle expert capacity, and implement the load-balancing loss. Verify the output." Clarify: what an expert is (here a two-layer ReLU network d → h → d); k (1 or 2); whether gates are renormalised over the top k (yes here, as in Mixtral's top-2; Switch's top-1 uses the raw probability); the capacity formula and drop policy (ceil(k·T/E × factor), first choices before second choices, drops add nothing); and which auxiliary loss (Switch's). Outline: hand example, a per-token loop as the reference, a vectorised dispatch/combine version, the loss and its gradient, tests.

2. The maths in plain words

For each token, the router multiplies the token's vector by a small matrix to get one score per expert, and softmax turns scores into probabilities. Keep the k highest; divide each by their sum, so the k weights add to one — these are the gates. The token's output is each chosen expert's output scaled by its gate, summed.

Capacity makes it practical. If every expert received exactly its share, each would get k·T/E assignments in a batch of T tokens. Allow a little more — multiply by the capacity factor — and round up: that is C slots per expert. Fill the slots in priority order: every token's first choice, then every token's second choice, each in token order. An assignment that finds its expert full is dropped and contributes nothing.

The load-balancing loss measures how unevenly tokens are spread. For each expert, multiply the fraction of tokens that picked it first (f) by its average router probability (P), add these products over experts, and scale by α·E. If the router is uniform, every f and P is 1/E, the sum is 1/E, and the loss is exactly α. If everything goes to one expert, f and P are 1 for it and 0 elsewhere, and the loss is α·E. Only P can carry gradient — f is a count — and that gradient lowers the probability of experts that are receiving too much.

3. The equations

router:      z = x·Wr            p = softmax(z)            (T, E)
top-k:       S_t = indices of the k largest p_t          gates g_te = p_te / Σ_{e∈S_t} p_te
capacity:    C = ceil(k·T/E × capacity_factor)
priority:    assignments ordered (rank 1 for all t), (rank 2 for all t), …
             pos(t, e) = number of earlier assignments to expert e;   kept ⟺ pos < C
expert e:    E_e(x) = relu(x·W1[e])·W2[e]               W1: (E, d, h)  W2: (E, h, d)
output:      y_t = Σ_{e∈S_t, kept} g_te · E_e(x_t)          (residual adds x_t outside the layer)

aux loss:    f_i = (1/T) Σ_t 1[argmax_e p_te = i]      P_i = (1/T) Σ_t p_ti
             L_aux = α · E · Σ_i f_i · P_i               (= α at uniform routing; = α·E if collapsed)
gradient:    ∂L_aux/∂z_tj = (α·E/T) · p_tj · (f_j − Σ_i f_i p_ti)        (f treated as constant)
compute:     per token ≈ k experts' FLOPs (+ router), parameters ≈ E experts

import math
import numpy as np

# The hand-worked example: 4 tokens, 3 experts, top-2 routing, capacity factor 1.0.
probs_h = np.array([[0.6, 0.3, 0.1],

                    [0.2, 0.7, 0.1],
                    [0.7, 0.2, 0.1]])
top2 = np.argsort(-probs_h, axis=1)[:, :2]                      # [[0,1],[0,2],[1,0],[0,1]]
g = np.take_along_axis(probs_h, top2, axis=1)
g = g / g.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)                            # renormalise over the chosen two
assert top2.tolist() == [[0, 1], [0, 2], [1, 0], [0, 1]]

y = [g[0, 0] * 1 + g[0, 1] * 10, g[1, 0] * 1 + g[1, 1] * 100, g[2, 0] * 10, g[3, 0] * 1 + g[3, 1] * 10]
assert np.allclose(y, [4.0, 45.0, 70 / 9, 3.0])                  # t2 loses its second expert's share
f = np.bincount(probs_h.argmax(axis=1), minlength=3) / 4         # fraction routed (top-1) to each expert

assert np.allclose(f, [0.75, 0.25, 0.0]) and np.allclose(P, [0.5, 0.325, 0.175])
assert np.isclose(aux, 0.0136875)                                # uniform routing would give exactly 0.01

5. The naive implementation: a loop per token

The reference implementation is deliberately plain. route computes softmax probabilities (stable: subtract the row maximum), takes the top k with a stable argsort so ties resolve deterministically, and renormalises. moe_loop applies capacity by walking ranks outer and tokens inner — all first choices, then all second choices — counting each expert's load and marking each assignment kept or dropped; then for each token and each kept assignment it runs that expert on that token alone and accumulates the gate-weighted output. It is slow (one tiny matrix product per token per expert), but every line maps to the definition, which is what a reference needs.

def softmax(z, axis=-1):
    z = z - z.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True)
    e = np.exp(z)
    return e / e.sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True)

def init_moe(d, h, E, seed=0):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    return {"Wr": r.normal(scale=0.5, size=(d, E)),                       # router
            "W1": r.normal(scale=d ** -0.5, size=(E, d, h)),              # expert e: d -> h -> d
            "W2": r.normal(scale=h ** -0.5, size=(E, h, d))}

def expert(params, e, x):                                                 # x: (n, d)
    return np.maximum(x @ params["W1"][e], 0.0) @ params["W2"][e]

def route(params, X, k):
    """Router: softmax over experts, top-k, gates renormalised over the chosen k."""
    probs = softmax(X @ params["Wr"])                                      # (T, E)
    idx = np.argsort(-probs, axis=1, kind="stable")[:, :k]                 # (T, k), best first
    gates = np.take_along_axis(probs, idx, axis=1)
    return probs, idx, gates / gates.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

def moe_loop(params, X, k, capacity=None):
    """Reference: per token, per chosen expert, with the same capacity rule (first choices first)."""
    probs, idx, gates = route(params, X, k)
    T, E = probs.shape
    load = np.zeros(E, dtype=int)
    keep = np.zeros((T, k), dtype=bool)
    for rank in range(k):                                                  # all first choices, then seconds
        for t in range(T):
            e = idx[t, rank]
            if capacity is None or load[e] < capacity:
                load[e] += 1; keep[t, rank] = True
    Y = np.zeros_like(X)
    for t in range(T):
        for rank in range(k):
            if keep[t, rank]:
                Y[t] += gates[t, rank] * expert(params, idx[t, rank], X[t:t + 1])[0]
    return Y, keep

6. The vectorised layer: dispatch, batched experts, combine

Flatten in priority order. The T·k assignments are laid out rank-major: tok = tile(arange(T), k), exp_ = idx.T.reshape(-1), gate = gates.T.reshape(-1), so all first choices come before all second choices, each in token order — exactly the loop's order.

Positions by cumulative count. A one-hot matrix of shape (k·T, E) marks each assignment's expert; its cumulative sum down the rows, minus one, read at each row's own expert, is how many earlier assignments chose the same expert — the assignment's slot. Slots ≥ C are dropped. This is the trick that replaces the loop's load counters with one cumsum.

Dispatch. A zero buffer of shape (E, C, d) receives each kept token's vector at (expert, slot) with one fancy-indexed assignment. Empty slots stay zero — the padding the capacity factor pays for.

All experts at once. With expert weights stacked as W1 (E, d, h) and W2 (E, h, d) — W1 and W2 being each expert's first and second weight matrices, stacked along a leading expert axis —, two einsums compute every expert on its own slots: einsum("ecd,edh->ech"), ReLU, einsum("ech,ehd->ecd"). On an accelerator this is a batched matrix multiply; across devices it is where expert parallelism splits the E dimension.

Combine. Each kept assignment reads its expert's output at its slot, multiplies by its gate, and adds into its token's row of Y. A token appears up to k times, so the addition must accumulate on repeated indices: np.add.at, which ordinary fancy-indexed += does not do. The function also returns the capacity, drop count, per-expert load, and the f and P vectors for the loss. aux_loss and its gradient implement section 3's formulas.

def moe_forward(params, X, k, capacity_factor=1.25):
    """Vectorised top-k MoE: assignments sorted by (choice rank, token), positions within each expert
    from a running count, capacity mask, dispatch into an (E, C, d) buffer, all experts in one batched
    einsum, then combine with gate weights by scatter-add."""
    probs, idx, gates = route(params, X, k)
    T, E = probs.shape
    C = math.ceil(k * T / E * capacity_factor)
    # Flatten assignments in priority order: every token's first choice before any second choice.
    tok = np.tile(np.arange(T), k)                                         # (k*T,)
    exp_ = idx.T.reshape(-1)                                               # rank-major order
    gate = gates.T.reshape(-1)
    one_hot = np.eye(E, dtype=int)[exp_]                                   # (k*T, E)
    pos = (np.cumsum(one_hot, axis=0) - 1)[np.arange(k * T), exp_]         # slot within the expert
    kept = pos < C
    buf = np.zeros((E, C, X.shape[1]))
    buf[exp_[kept], pos[kept]] = X[tok[kept]]                              # dispatch
    H = np.maximum(np.einsum("ecd,edh->ech", buf, params["W1"]), 0.0)      # every expert at once
    out = np.einsum("ech,ehd->ecd", H, params["W2"])                       # (E, C, d)
    Y = np.zeros_like(X)
    np.add.at(Y, tok[kept], gate[kept, None] * out[exp_[kept], pos[kept]])  # combine
    f = np.bincount(idx[:, 0], minlength=E) / T                            # top-1 dispatch fraction
    P = probs.mean(axis=0)
    stats = {"capacity": C, "dropped": int((~kept).sum()), "load": np.bincount(exp_[kept], minlength=E),
             "f": f, "P": P}
    return Y, kept.reshape(k, T).T, stats

def aux_loss(probs, top1, alpha=0.01):
    """Switch Transformer load-balancing loss: alpha * E * sum_i f_i * P_i (minimised at uniform, = alpha)."""
    T, E = probs.shape
    f = np.bincount(top1, minlength=E) / T
    return alpha * E * float(f @ probs.mean(axis=0))

def aux_loss_grad_logits(probs, top1, alpha=0.01):
    """d aux / d router logits. f is a count (no gradient); P_i = mean_t softmax_i, so
    d aux / d z_tj = alpha * E / T * p_tj * (f_j - sum_i f_i p_ti)."""
    T, E = probs.shape
    f = np.bincount(top1, minlength=E) / T
    return alpha * E / T * probs * (f[None, :] - (probs @ f)[:, None])

7. Testing it

Eight checks. (1) The hand example pushed through the same cumulative-count dispatch reproduces outputs [4, 45, 7.78, 3] with exactly one dropped assignment. (2) With capacity large enough that nothing drops, the vectorised layer equals the per-token loop for k = 1 and k = 2. (3) With tight capacity factors 0.5, 1.0 and 1.25 on 64 tokens and 4 experts (capacity 16, 32 and 40 slots), both implementations drop exactly the same assignments — the boolean "kept" matrices are identical — and produce the same outputs, and no expert's load exceeds its capacity; at factor 0.5, 64 of the 128 assignments are dropped, at 1.0 four are, at 1.25 none. (4) A dense computation — every expert on every token, a (T, E, d) array costing E times the work — masked to the chosen experts and gates equals the MoE output. (5) The auxiliary loss is exactly α for uniform routing and α·E when everything goes to one expert. (6) Its analytic gradient with respect to router logits matches central finite differences to 10−9, and the summed gradient for the most-loaded expert is positive, so gradient descent lowers its logits. (7) A router biased towards expert 0 (62.5% of tokens' first choice), trained for 200 steps on the auxiliary loss alone, ends at an exactly even 25% per expert. (8) If PyTorch is installed, torch.topk on torch.softmax gives the same indices and renormalised gates, and one expert's forward pass matches.

8. Numerical and practical traps

Ties in the router. Equal probabilities make top-k ambiguous; use a stable sort (or add a tiny index-based tie-break) so two implementations — and two devices — agree. Fancy-index += does not accumulate. Y[tok] += v with repeated tok keeps only one addition; use np.add.at (or sort and segment-sum). Renormalising after drops. Rescaling the surviving gates makes a token's output depend on which other tokens shared the batch; most designs do not. Low-precision routing. In bfloat16 the router's softmax can flip near-tied decisions between steps; Switch Transformers cast the router's input to float32 within the router. Large logits. Routers can drift to large logits that make softmax near one-hot and gradients vanish; a router z-loss (penalising the log-sum-exp of logits) counters it. Capacity rounding. Use ceil, not floor, or a capacity factor of 1.0 with uneven division gives fewer slots than assignments even under perfect balance.

9. Edge cases worth naming

k = E makes the layer a dense, gate-weighted ensemble. k = 1 (Switch routing): gates can be the raw top probability rather than renormalised (renormalising one value gives 1 and kills the router's gradient through the gate). Capacity factor ≥ E/k: nothing can ever be dropped. A token with all assignments dropped: output zero; the residual carries it unchanged. Inference with one token: capacity per expert is tiny or the rule is often disabled at inference (no drops), which is one reason training and inference outputs can differ slightly.

10. Complexity

Router: O(T·d·E). Dispatch and combine: O(T·k·d) data movement plus O(T·k·E) for the one-hot cumulative count (O(T·k) with a scatter-based counter). Experts: O(E·C·d·h) = O(capacity_factor · k · T · d · h), i.e. about k feed-forward blocks per token times the capacity factor, versus O(E · T · d · h) for the dense masked computation. Memory: expert weights O(E·d·h); the dispatch buffer O(E·C·d). Parameters scale with E, per-token compute with k.

11. Talking through the design

Lead with the purpose (parameters without per-token compute), the router and top-k gates, then capacity and drops with the first-choices-first rule, the vectorised dispatch via cumulative counts and batched einsums, and the load-balancing loss with its values at uniform and collapsed routing. Mention the training stabilisers (float32 router, z-loss), and close with systems: all-to-all communication, stragglers from imbalance, and the inference memory cost of holding every expert.

Noisy top-k gating (Shazeer et al., 2017)

The sparsely-gated MoE paper that revived the idea added tunable Gaussian noise to the router's logits before taking the top k — the noise scale itself learned through a softplus — and set non-top-k logits to minus infinity before the softmax. The noise helps exploration and load balance early in training, since near-ties between experts are broken randomly rather than always the same way. It used two balancing terms: an importance loss (the coefficient of variation of each expert's total gate weight) and a load loss (a smooth estimate of how many tokens each expert receives). Switch Transformers later folded these into the single f·P loss used here.

Why f·P and not just f

The quantity we want balanced is f, the fraction of tokens each expert actually receives, but f comes from an argmax and has no gradient. P, the mean router probability, is differentiable and correlated with f. Multiplying them weights each expert's probability-gradient by how overloaded it actually is: the gradient (α·E/T)·p_tj·(f_j − Σ_i f_i·p_ti) is positive for experts with above-average f, so descent reduces their logits, and negative for underused ones. It is a cheap, stable surrogate; its minimum at uniform routing is what makes it a balance loss rather than just a penalty.

Expert choice: letting experts pick tokens

Capacity overflow comes from tokens choosing experts. Expert-choice routing inverts it: each expert picks its top C tokens by router score, so every expert is exactly full and load is balanced by construction, with no auxiliary loss. A token may then be chosen by several experts or by none. It works well for training encoder-style models, but it is awkward for autoregressive decoding, where an expert's choice would depend on future tokens in the batch.

Experts in parallel: all-to-all

At scale, experts live on different devices. Each device routes its tokens, packs them into per-expert buffers of fixed capacity, and an all-to-all exchange sends each buffer to the device holding that expert; after the experts compute, a second all-to-all sends results back for combining. Fixed capacity is what makes those messages fixed-size, which collective-communication libraries and accelerators prefer. Communication can dominate step time, and imbalance turns into stragglers: the most loaded device sets the pace. That is why load balancing is not only about parameter utilisation but about wall-clock time.

The router z-loss

Load balancing controls where tokens go; it does not control how confident the router is. Routers can drift to very large logits, making the softmax nearly one-hot: gates become 0 or 1, gradients through them vanish, and in low precision the exponentials overflow or round badly. The router z-loss, introduced in later sparse-model work (ST-MoE), adds a small penalty on the squared log-sum-exp of each token's router logits, pulling logits towards modest magnitudes without changing which expert is preferred. It is cheap — one log-sum-exp per token, already computed for the softmax — and commonly used alongside the balancing loss.

Finer-grained and shared experts

Recent designs split experts more finely — many small experts with a larger k — so that combinations of experts can represent more specialised functions for the same compute, and add one or more always-on "shared" experts that every token passes through, so common knowledge does not have to be duplicated in every routed expert. Both keep the same mechanics as this lesson — router, top-k, capacity, dispatch, combine, balance — with different values of E and k and a dense branch added to the output.

MoE at inference

Compute per token is low, but every expert must be in memory: Mixtral's roughly 47B parameters all need to be loaded even though about 13B are active per token. At batch size one, decoding reads only the active experts' weights per token, so it behaves like a 13B model in bandwidth terms; at larger batches, different tokens activate different experts and most of the weights are read every step. Serving systems therefore shard experts across GPUs, offload rarely used experts, or quantise them (mc27), and capacity limits are usually relaxed at inference so no token is dropped.

Where MoE layers run

MoE feed-forward layers appear in many large open and closed language models; the published examples include Switch Transformers (top-1, up to a trillion parameters), GLaM, and Mixtral 8x7B (8 experts per layer, top-2, about 47B parameters with about 13B active per token). They typically replace the feed-forward block in every layer or every other layer, keeping attention dense.

Failure modes and symptoms

Routing collapse. Symptom: a few experts receive most tokens; per-expert load histograms are skewed; dropped-token rates climb. Cause: auxiliary loss too weak or absent. Fix: raise its weight, add noise, check the router's initialisation scale.

Training instability. Symptom: loss spikes or divergence, especially in bfloat16. Cause: router logits growing and decisions flipping. Fix: float32 router computation, router z-loss, smaller router initialisation.

High drop rates. Symptom: many tokens skip experts, quality lags a dense baseline. Cause: capacity factor too low for the achieved balance. Fix: raise the factor or improve balance.

Slow steps. Symptom: GPU utilisation low on some devices. Cause: imbalance creating stragglers, or all-to-all communication dominating. Fix: better balance, capacity tuning, overlapping communication with computation.

Train-inference mismatch. Symptom: evaluation differs between batched and single-sequence inference. Cause: capacity drops depend on batch composition. Fix: disable drops at inference or use a generous capacity.

Dead experts. Symptom: some experts receive almost no tokens for thousands of steps and their weights stop changing. Cause: early collapse that the balancing loss never fully reversed. Fix: re-initialise or merge them, or increase router noise for a period.

What to monitor and tune

Per-layer, per-expert token counts (f) and mean probabilities (P); the auxiliary loss value relative to α; dropped-assignment rate per layer; router logit magnitudes (for z-loss); and device step-time spread. Tune the capacity factor (1.0-1.25 is typical in the Switch results), the auxiliary loss weight (10⁻² in Switch), k, and the number of experts.

A cost estimate

A feed-forward block with d = 4,096 and h = 14,336 (Mixtral-like) has 2 × 4,096 × 14,336 ≈ 117M parameters (ignoring gating projections). Eight experts hold about 940M parameters per layer; top-2 routing computes about 235M parameter-multiplies per token, a quarter of the parameters, so the layer is 4× larger than its compute suggests. With capacity factor 1.25, buffers hold 25% more slots than the balanced share, so the expert compute actually performed is up to 1.25× the useful amount when many slots are padding.

The reference for this problem

Fedus, Zoph and Shazeer's Switch Transformers paper defines the pieces used here: expert capacity = (tokens per batch / number of experts) × capacity factor; tokens beyond an expert's capacity skip it and pass through the residual connection, with drop rates typically under 1% in their experiments; the auxiliary loss α·N·Σ f_i·P_i with f_i the fraction of tokens dispatched to expert i and P_i the fraction of router probability for expert i, minimised under uniform routing and used with α = 10⁻²; and selective casting of the router to float32 for stability under bfloat16. Shazeer et al. (2017) introduced the sparsely-gated layer with noisy top-k gating; the Mixtral paper documents a widely used top-2 configuration.

Work it out

(a) A layer has E = 64 experts, top-1 routing, and batches of 8,192 tokens. What is the capacity at factors 1.0 and 1.25, and how many slots are padding per batch at factor 1.25 if routing is perfectly balanced? (b) After training, f for one layer is 0.2 for two experts and spread evenly over the other 62. What is the auxiliary loss with α = 0.01, assuming P ≈ f?

Answer 
E_d, T_d = 64, 8192
cap_10 = math.ceil(1 * T_d / E_d * 1.0)
cap_125 = math.ceil(1 * T_d / E_d * 1.25)
padding = E_d * cap_125 - T_d
f_d = np.array([0.2, 0.2] + [0.6 / 62] * 62)
loss_d = 0.01 * E_d * float(f_d @ f_d)
assert (cap_10, cap_125, padding) == (128, 160, 2048)
assert round(loss_d, 4) == 0.0549

(a) Capacity is 8,192/64 = 128 slots per expert at factor 1.0 and 160 at 1.25. With perfect balance each expert gets 128 tokens, so 64 × 32 = 2,048 padding slots per batch — 25% extra expert compute that does nothing, the price of headroom. (b) Σ f·P ≈ 2 × 0.2² + 62 × (0.6/62)² = 0.08 + 0.0058 = 0.0858; × 0.01 × 64 = 0.0549, about 5.5 times the uniform value of 0.01. Two experts receiving 20% each — about 13 times their fair share of 1/64 — would also overflow badly: each needs 1,638 slots against 160, so most of their tokens are dropped. The loss is signalling a real imbalance.
