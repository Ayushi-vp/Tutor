A supervised fine-tuned model writes reasonable answers, but people prefer some answers over others: clearer, safer, more helpful. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) turns those preferences into training: fit a reward model to pairs of responses where a person picked one over the other, then optimise the language model to maximise that reward while staying close to the original model, using a reinforcement learning algorithm such as PPO. It works, and it is complicated: a second model to train, sampling from the policy during training, a value function, many hyperparameters, and instabilities.

Direct preference optimisation, published by Rafailov and colleagues in 2023, removes most of that machinery. It shows that the optimum of the KL-regularised RLHF objective defines a reward implicitly in terms of the policy itself — β times the log-ratio of the policy's and the reference model's probabilities — so the preference likelihood can be written and maximised directly. The result is a loss you could explain on one line: for each pair, compute how much the policy has raised the chosen response's log-probability relative to the reference, minus the same for the rejected response, scale by β, and take −log σ of that. Training looks like supervised learning on pairs.

The interview version asks you to implement that loss and its gradient from scratch and to show it behaves as claimed. That tests sequence log-probabilities, numerical stability (log σ of a large negative number overflows if written naively), deriving a gradient through a sigmoid and a softmax, and checking it. This lesson works the loss and a gradient step by hand on a three-token policy; implements a loop version and a vectorised version on a toy policy over 8 prompts and 5-token responses; checks them against each other, finite differences and PyTorch autograd; and trains for 300 steps to show the loss falling from log 2 to 0.15, the margin growing from 0 to 1.9, preference accuracy reaching 100%, and a larger β keeping the policy measurably closer to the reference.

The analogy: a tutor adjusting a student's habits relative to a baseline

A writing tutor has a student who already writes competently — the baseline. The tutor is shown pairs of the student's drafts for the same assignment and told which one readers preferred. The tutor does not grade drafts on an absolute scale. Instead, for each pair, the tutor asks: compared with the student's old habits, has the student become more inclined to write the preferred draft and less inclined to write the other? If yes, little to do. If the student has drifted the wrong way, a strong correction. And the tutor is cautious about changing the student too much at once — a student who abandons all their old habits to chase reader preferences tends to start writing oddly.

The student's current habits are the policy πθ; the old habits are the frozen reference πref; "more inclined than before" is the log-ratio log πθ(y) − log πref(y); comparing the preferred draft's change with the other's is the margin; "strong correction when wrong, little when right" is the gradient weight σ(−z); and the caution about changing too much is β.

The analogy breaks in one technical way: the student's "inclination" to write a draft is a product of word-by-word probabilities, so the log-ratio is a sum over every token of the response. That is why sequence log-probabilities, not single-token ones, sit at the centre of the implementation.

Where DPO sits in the training pipeline

A modern assistant model is typically built in stages: pre-training on large text corpora, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on demonstrations of good responses, and then preference tuning on pairs where one response was judged better. Preference tuning is where RLHF — reinforcement learning from human feedback, usually with the PPO (proximal policy optimisation) algorithm — or DPO comes in. The SFT model plays two roles in DPO: it is the starting point of the policy and, frozen, the reference. Everything DPO learns is expressed relative to it, which is why the reference is not an implementation detail but part of the objective.

From the loss to the gradient, in one picture

Everything in DPO flows from one scalar per pair, the margin z. The loss is a fixed function of z; the gradient with respect to z is σ(z) − 1; and z depends on the policy only through the two sequence log-probabilities. So the implementation has three layers — sequence log-probabilities, the margin, the sigmoid — and the gradient runs back through them in reverse. Outline: hand example, stable log-sigmoid, loop implementation, vectorised implementation, tests and a short training run.

2. The maths in plain words

For each response, ask how much more (or less) likely the policy makes it than the reference does, in log terms: the log-ratio. Multiply by β to get the response's implicit reward. The margin is the chosen response's implicit reward minus the rejected response's. Pass the margin through a sigmoid to get the probability that the chosen response is preferred under the Bradley-Terry model, and take the negative log: that is the loss. It is zero only in the limit of an infinitely large margin and equals log 2 when the margin is zero.

The gradient falls out of the chain rule. The loss's slope with respect to the margin is σ(z) − 1: close to −1 when the margin is very negative (the pair is badly misordered), close to 0 when it is large and positive (already right). The margin rises one-for-one (times β) with the chosen response's log-probability and falls with the rejected's. And a response's log-probability, for a softmax model, has the familiar gradient at each position: one-hot of the actual token minus the probabilities.

3. The equations

sequence log-prob:  log π(y | x) = Σ_t log softmax(logits_t)[y_t]           (sum over response tokens)
log-ratios:         ρ_w = log π_θ(y_w|x) − log π_ref(y_w|x),   ρ_l = log π_θ(y_l|x) − log π_ref(y_l|x)
implicit rewards:   r̂_w = β·ρ_w,   r̂_l = β·ρ_l
margin:             z = r̂_w − r̂_l = β·(ρ_w − ρ_l)
loss:               L = −log σ(z) = softplus(−z)        (mean over pairs)
stable:             log σ(z) = min(z, 0) − log1p(exp(−|z|))

gradient:           dL/dz = σ(z) − 1 = −σ(−z)
                    ∇_θ L = −β·σ(−z)·(∇_θ log π_θ(y_w|x) − ∇_θ log π_θ(y_l|x))
softmax policy:     ∂ log π(y|x) / ∂ logits_t = onehot(y_t) − softmax(logits_t)
toy policy here:    logits_t = θ[x, t]   ⇒   ∂z/∂θ[x, t] = β·(onehot(y_w,t) − onehot(y_l,t))   (softmax terms cancel)

4. A worked example by hand

The loss. β = 0.1. A pair whose chosen response has log-probability −5.0 and rejected −4.0 under the reference (the rejected one was more likely to begin with). At the start the policy equals the reference: both log-ratios are 0, z = 0, σ(0) = 0.5, and L = −log 0.5 = 0.6931 = log 2. After some training, suppose the policy gives the chosen response −4.5 (up 0.5 nats) and the rejected one −4.5 (down 0.5 nats): ρw = +0.5, ρl = −0.5, implicit rewards +0.05 and −0.05, z = 0.1 × (0.5 − (−0.5)) = 0.1, and L = log(1 + e−0.1) = 0.6444. Notice that the rejected response is still exactly as likely as the chosen one in absolute terms; the loss rewards the change relative to the reference, not absolute probability.

A gradient step. A one-position policy over 3 tokens with logits θ = [0, 0, 0], so π = [1/3, 1/3, 1/3]; the reference is the same. Chosen token 0, rejected token 1. The gradient of log π(0) with respect to θ is e0 − π = [2/3, −1/3, −1/3]; of log π(1), [−1/3, 2/3, −1/3]. Their difference is [1, −1, 0] — the probabilities cancel. At z = 0, dL/dz = σ(0) − 1 = −0.5, so ∇L = −0.5 × 0.1 × [1, −1, 0] = [−0.05, 0.05, 0]. A gradient-descent step with learning rate 1 gives θ = [0.05, −0.05, 0]. Now log π(0) − log π(1) = θ0 − θ1 = 0.1 (the normaliser cancels), and since the reference difference is 0, z = 0.1 × 0.1 = 0.01; the loss falls to log(1 + e−0.01) = 0.68816. Token 2, not involved in the pair, was untouched.

import math
import numpy as np

# The hand-worked example. Sequence log-probabilities under the policy and the frozen reference.
beta = 0.1
def dpo_from_logps(pw, pl, rw, rl, beta):
    z = beta * ((pw - rw) - (pl - rl))                      # margin of implicit rewards
    return z, math.log1p(math.exp(-z))                       # loss = -log sigmoid(z) = softplus(-z)
z0, L0 = dpo_from_logps(-5.0, -4.0, -5.0, -4.0, beta)        # policy == reference
assert z0 == 0.0 and abs(L0 - math.log(2)) < 1e-12           # 0.6931: the loss starts at log 2
z1, L1 = dpo_from_logps(-4.5, -4.5, -5.0, -4.0, beta)        # chosen up 0.5 nats, rejected down 0.5
assert abs(z1 - 0.1) < 1e-12 and round(L1, 4) == 0.6444
r_w, r_l = beta * (-4.5 + 5.0), beta * (-4.5 + 4.0)          # implicit rewards beta * log(pi / pi_ref)
assert np.allclose([r_w, r_l], [0.05, -0.05])

# Gradient by hand for a one-position policy over 3 tokens, logits theta = [0, 0, 0], y_w = 0, y_l = 1.
theta = np.zeros(3); pi = np.exp(theta) / np.exp(theta).sum()  # [1/3, 1/3, 1/3]
g_w = np.eye(3)[0] - pi                                       # d log pi(y_w) / d theta = onehot - softmax
g_l = np.eye(3)[1] - pi
sig = lambda t: 1 / (1 + math.exp(-t))
dL = (sig(0.0) - 1) * beta * (g_w - g_l)                      # dL/dz = sigma(z) - 1 = -0.5 at z = 0
assert np.allclose(g_w - g_l, [1, -1, 0]) and np.allclose(dL, [-0.05, 0.05, 0])
theta = theta - 1.0 * dL                                      # one step, learning rate 1
z2 = beta * (theta[0] - theta[1])                             # log pi(y_w) - log pi(y_l) = theta0 - theta1
assert np.isclose(z2, 0.01) and round(math.log1p(math.exp(-z2)), 5) == 0.68816

5. The naive implementation

The toy policy has a logit table θ of shape (prompts, positions, vocabulary): the probability of token v at position t of a response to prompt x is softmax(θ[x, t])[v], independent of earlier tokens. That keeps the gradient readable while preserving the two things that matter — sequence log-probabilities are sums over positions, and gradients are one-hot minus softmax. The reference is a frozen copy. The naive version computes each sequence log-probability with an explicit loop and a log-sum-exp written the risky way, then for each pair computes z, the loss with the stable log-sigmoid, and the gradient position by position.

def log_sigmoid(z):
    """Stable log(sigmoid(z)) = -softplus(-z): never exponentiates a large positive number."""
    z = np.asarray(z, dtype=float)
    return np.minimum(z, 0) - np.log1p(np.exp(-np.abs(z)))

def log_softmax(a, axis=-1):
    a = a - a.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True)
    return a - np.log(np.exp(a).sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True))

def seq_logp_naive(theta, x, y):
    """log pi(y | x) for a toy policy: independent softmax over V tokens at each of L positions,
    with logits theta[x, t]. Summed over positions, as for a real sequence."""
    total = 0.0
    for t, tok in enumerate(y):
        logits = theta[x, t]
        total += logits[tok] - math.log(sum(math.exp(v) for v in logits))   # unstable on purpose
    return total

def dpo_naive(theta, ref, batch, beta):
    """Mean DPO loss and its gradient, one pair at a time."""
    loss, grad = 0.0, np.zeros_like(theta)
    for x, yw, yl in batch:
        z = beta * ((seq_logp_naive(theta, x, yw) - seq_logp_naive(ref, x, yw))
                    - (seq_logp_naive(theta, x, yl) - seq_logp_naive(ref, x, yl)))
        loss += -float(log_sigmoid(z))
        coef = (1 / (1 + math.exp(-z)) - 1) * beta            # dL/dz * dz/d(logp_w - logp_l)
        for t in range(len(yw)):
            p = np.exp(log_softmax(theta[x, t]))
            grad[x, t] += coef * ((np.eye(len(p))[yw[t]] - p) - (np.eye(len(p))[yl[t]] - p))
    return loss / len(batch), grad / len(batch)

6. The vectorised implementation

seq_logp indexes the logit table by each pair's prompt to get a (B, L, V) block, applies a stable log-softmax along the vocabulary, gathers each actual token's log-probability with take_along_axis, and sums over positions: one array expression per batch. dpo_loss_and_grad computes the margins for all pairs, the mean loss with the stable log-sigmoid, and the per-pair coefficient β·(σ(z) − 1)/B — computing σ(z) as exp(log σ(z)) so it inherits the stable form. The per-position gradient is (one-hot(yw) − p) − (one-hot(yl) − p), which the code writes out to mirror the derivation even though the p terms cancel. Several pairs can share a prompt, so gradients are accumulated into θ with np.add.at. The function also returns the margins, preference accuracy and the chosen and rejected implicit rewards — the quantities worth logging. kl_to_ref measures the average KL divergence between policy and reference per position.

def seq_logp(theta, X, Y):
    """Vectorised: X (B,), Y (B, L) -> (B,) sequence log-probabilities."""
    lp = log_softmax(theta[X])                                 # (B, L, V)
    return np.take_along_axis(lp, Y[..., None], axis=-1)[..., 0].sum(axis=1)

def dpo_loss_and_grad(theta, ref, X, YW, YL, beta):
    """DPO loss  -mean log sigmoid(beta * [(lp_w - ref_w) - (lp_l - ref_l)])  and d loss / d theta."""
    B, L = YW.shape
    V = theta.shape[-1]
    z = beta * ((seq_logp(theta, X, YW) - seq_logp(ref, X, YW))
                - (seq_logp(theta, X, YL) - seq_logp(ref, X, YL)))
    loss = -log_sigmoid(z).mean()
    coef = beta * (np.exp(log_sigmoid(z)) - 1) / B             # (B,) = beta * (sigma(z) - 1) / B
    p = np.exp(log_softmax(theta[X]))                          # (B, L, V)
    oh_w, oh_l = np.eye(V)[YW], np.eye(V)[YL]                  # (B, L, V)
    per_pair = coef[:, None, None] * ((oh_w - p) - (oh_l - p))   # the softmax terms cancel: oh_w - oh_l
    grad = np.zeros_like(theta)
    np.add.at(grad, X, per_pair)                               # several pairs may share a prompt
    stats = {"z": z, "acc": float((z > 0).mean()),
             "chosen_reward": beta * (seq_logp(theta, X, YW) - seq_logp(ref, X, YW)),
             "rejected_reward": beta * (seq_logp(theta, X, YL) - seq_logp(ref, X, YL))}
    return float(loss), grad, stats

def kl_to_ref(theta, ref):
    """Mean over prompts and positions of KL(pi || pi_ref) for the per-position softmaxes."""
    lp, lr = log_softmax(theta), log_softmax(ref)
    return float((np.exp(lp) * (lp - lr)).sum(axis=-1).mean())

7. Testing it

Seven checks on a batch of 64 random pairs over 8 prompts, 5 positions and 6 tokens. (1) The stable log-sigmoid equals the naive formula from −30 to 1000, returns exactly −1000 at z = −1000 where the naive one returns −inf, and exactly 0 at z = 1000. (2) At θ = reference every margin is 0 and the loss is log 2, whatever the data. (3) Away from the reference, the vectorised and naive implementations agree on loss and gradient. (4) Central finite differences at three coordinates match the analytic gradient to 10−8. (5) Gradient descent for 300 steps at β = 0.1 lowers the loss from 0.693 to about 0.152 — monotonically in this run — raises the mean margin from 0 to about 1.9, and brings preference accuracy from 0 (no margin is strictly positive at the start) to 100%. (6) The same training at β = 0.5 ends with a mean per-position KL to the reference of about 0.80 against 1.25 for β = 0.1: β is the leash. (7) If PyTorch is installed, the same loss built with torch.log_softmax, gather and logsigmoid, differentiated by autograd, matches the loss and the gradient to 10−12.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
NP, L, V, B = 8, 5, 6, 64                                      # prompts, positions, vocabulary, pairs
ref = rng.normal(scale=1.0, size=(NP, L, V))                   # the frozen SFT reference
X = rng.integers(0, NP, size=B)
YW = rng.integers(0, V, size=(B, L)); YL = rng.integers(0, V, size=(B, L))
batch = list(zip(X.tolist(), YW.tolist(), YL.tolist()))

# 1. The stable log-sigmoid matches the naive formula where that works and survives where it fails.
zs = np.array([-1000.0, -30.0, -1.0, 0.0, 1.0, 30.0, 1000.0])
with np.errstate(over="ignore", divide="ignore"):
    naive = np.log(1 / (1 + np.exp(-zs)))                       # -inf at -1000 (exp overflows)
assert np.isneginf(naive[0]) and np.allclose(log_sigmoid(zs)[1:], naive[1:])
assert log_sigmoid(-1000.0) == -1000.0 and log_sigmoid(1000.0) == 0.0

# 2. At theta = ref every margin is 0 and the loss is log 2, whatever the data.
loss0, g0, st0 = dpo_loss_and_grad(ref.copy(), ref, X, YW, YL, beta=0.1)
assert np.isclose(loss0, math.log(2)) and np.all(st0["z"] == 0)

# 3. Vectorised and naive agree (loss and gradient) away from the reference.
theta = ref + rng.normal(scale=0.3, size=ref.shape)
lv, gv, _ = dpo_loss_and_grad(theta, ref, X, YW, YL, beta=0.1)
ln, gn = dpo_naive(theta, ref, batch, beta=0.1)
assert np.isclose(lv, ln) and np.allclose(gv, gn)

# 4. Finite-difference check of the analytic gradient.
for idx in [(0, 0, 0), (3, 2, 5), (7, 4, 1)]:
    tp, tm = theta.copy(), theta.copy(); tp[idx] += 1e-6; tm[idx] -= 1e-6
    num = (dpo_loss_and_grad(tp, ref, X, YW, YL, 0.1)[0] - dpo_loss_and_grad(tm, ref, X, YW, YL, 0.1)[0]) / 2e-6
    assert abs(num - gv[idx]) < 1e-8

# 5. A few gradient steps: loss falls, the reward margin grows, preference accuracy rises.
def train(beta, steps=300, lr=20.0):
    th, hist = ref.copy(), []
    for _ in range(steps):
        loss, g, st = dpo_loss_and_grad(th, ref, X, YW, YL, beta)
        hist.append((loss, float(st["z"].mean()), st["acc"]))
        th -= lr * g
    return th, hist
th01, h01 = train(0.1)
assert h01[-1][0] < h01[0][0] - 0.2 and h01[-1][1] > h01[0][1] + 0.5 and h01[-1][2] > 0.9
assert all(a >= b - 1e-12 for a, b in zip([h[0] for h in h01], [h[0] for h in h01[1:]]))  # monotone here

# 6. beta is the leash: for the same number of steps, a larger beta stays closer to the reference.
th05, h05 = train(0.5)
assert kl_to_ref(th05, ref) < kl_to_ref(th01, ref)

# 7. Cross-check loss and gradient with PyTorch autograd, if available.
try:
    import torch
    T = torch.tensor(theta, requires_grad=True); R = torch.tensor(ref)
    Xt, Wt, Lt = torch.tensor(X), torch.tensor(YW), torch.tensor(YL)
    def lp(P, Yt):
        return torch.log_softmax(P[Xt], -1).gather(-1, Yt[..., None])[..., 0].sum(1)
    zt = 0.1 * ((lp(T, Wt) - lp(R, Wt)) - (lp(T, Lt) - lp(R, Lt)))
    lt = -torch.nn.functional.logsigmoid(zt).mean()
    lt.backward()
    assert np.isclose(lt.item(), lv) and np.allclose(T.grad.numpy(), gv, atol=1e-12)
except ImportError:

8. Numerical and practical traps

Log-sigmoid overflow. Covered above; use the stable form, also for σ(z) itself in the gradient. Summing versus averaging token log-probabilities. DPO uses the sum; averaging per token changes the objective and biases it toward length. Either way, mask prompt and padding tokens so only response tokens count. Recomputing the reference. Computing reference log-probabilities in a different precision, with a different dropout state, or after accidentally updating the reference makes the step-zero loss differ from log 2 — the first sanity check. Gradient flowing into the reference. In autograd frameworks, wrap the reference forward in a no-gradient context; otherwise memory doubles and the "frozen" model may be updated. Large margins late in training. σ(−z) → 0 so learning stalls, and in low precision z itself can be large enough to overflow naive code.

9. Edge cases worth naming

Identical chosen and rejected responses: the margin is always 0 and the gradient is 0 — such pairs only add log 2 to the loss and should be filtered. Responses of very different lengths: summed log-probabilities scale with length, so length differences dominate margins; some variants normalise by length. Ties in human labels: drop them or use a variant that models ties. Label noise: a misordered pair keeps pushing with weight near 1; robust variants smooth the target (conservative DPO).

10. Complexity

Per pair, two policy forward passes (chosen and rejected) and two reference passes, which can be precomputed once. With a transformer, cost is dominated by the forward and backward passes: about four times the forward FLOPs of SFT on the same tokens for the policy (two responses, forward and backward), plus the reference forwards, and memory for two models unless the reference log-probabilities are cached. In the toy, the loss and gradient are O(B·L·V).

11. Talking through the design

Lead with the loss on one line, explain each term (log-ratios, β, Bradley-Terry), state the step-zero value of log 2, then derive the gradient through σ and the softmax and point out the σ(−z) weighting. Implement with a stable log-sigmoid and summed, masked sequence log-probabilities; verify against finite differences and autograd; show a few steps with the margin and accuracy improving; and name what to monitor beyond the loss.

Where the loss comes from

RLHF optimises maxπ Ex, y∼π[r(x, y)] − β·KL(π(·|x) || πref(·|x)). For a fixed reward, the maximiser has a closed form: π*(y|x) = πref(y|x)·exp(r(x, y)/β)/Z(x), a reweighting of the reference by exponentiated reward. Solve for the reward: r(x, y) = β·log(π*(y|x)/πref(y|x)) + β·log Z(x). Preference data are modelled with Bradley-Terry, P(yw ≻ yl | x) = σ(r(x, yw) − r(x, yl)). Substituting, the partition function Z(x) — impossible to compute, since it sums over every possible response — appears in both rewards and cancels in the difference. What remains is σ(β·log-ratiow − β·log-ratiol), a likelihood that depends only on the policy and the reference. Maximum likelihood on the preference data, with the policy as the parameterisation, is the DPO loss.

What the margin can hide

Because only the difference of log-ratios matters, the loss is indifferent to moving both log-probabilities down together. In practice, DPO-trained models often lower the absolute likelihood of chosen responses while lowering rejected ones faster, shifting probability mass to responses outside the dataset. Sometimes that is benign; sometimes it produces verbose or off-distribution outputs. Remedies studied in the literature include adding a supervised loss on chosen responses, constraining the chosen log-probability not to fall, or alternative objectives. The practical lesson for the implementer is to log chosen and rejected log-probabilities separately from day one.

The role of β, made concrete

In the RLHF objective β weights the KL penalty: large β means "stay close to the reference". DPO inherits it: margins are β times log-ratio differences, so with larger β a given margin — and therefore a given loss — is reached with smaller changes in log-probability, after which σ(−z) is small and the gradient fades. The toy run shows the effect directly: after the same 300 steps, β = 0.5 sits at a KL of about 0.80 from the reference against 1.25 for β = 0.1. Choosing β is a trade between fitting preferences and preserving the reference's general behaviour, and it interacts with the learning rate and the number of epochs.

Variants built on the same skeleton

Many later methods change one piece of the formula while keeping the implementation shape of this lesson. IPO replaces −log σ(z) with a squared loss on the margin around a target, avoiding unbounded margins. Conservative or label-smoothed DPO mixes in the loss for the flipped label to tolerate noisy preferences. Length-normalised variants average rather than sum log-probabilities. Reference-free variants such as SimPO drop πref and use average log-probability with a target margin. lm23 compares them; implementing any of them is a small edit to dpo_loss_and_grad.

Why the softmax terms cancel, and when they do not

For the toy policy, the gradient of log π(yw) − log π(yl) with respect to position t's logits is (onehot(yw,t) − pt) − (onehot(yl,t) − pt) = onehot(yw,t) − onehot(yl,t): the probabilities cancel because both responses are scored with the same logits at that position. In a real transformer the logits at position t depend on the tokens before t, so once the two responses diverge, their contexts differ and the cancellation no longer holds exactly. The shared prefix still gets zero net gradient — identical tokens in identical contexts — which is one reason pairs that differ only in a short span produce focused, efficient updates, and pairs that differ everywhere produce diffuse ones.

Offline data and distribution shift

DPO trains on a fixed set of pairs, usually sampled from the reference or another model. As the policy changes, its own outputs drift away from the responses in the data, and the loss says nothing about responses it never sees. Online and iterative variants periodically sample new responses from the current policy, label them (with people or a judge model) and continue training, which keeps the data on the policy's distribution — at the cost of reintroducing some of RLHF's sampling machinery.

Where DPO runs

DPO and its variants are widely used for preference tuning of open models because they need only a preference dataset, a reference model and a standard training loop; libraries for fine-tuning language models provide DPO trainers built on exactly the loss in this lesson, with the reference log-probabilities computed under no-gradient or precomputed. Typical runs start from an SFT checkpoint, use β around 0.1, and train for one to a few epochs over the pairs.

Failure modes and symptoms

Loss not starting at 0.693. Cause: the reference is not the policy's initial weights, or log-probabilities are computed differently (masking, dropout, precision). Fix before anything else.

Loss falls, quality falls. Cause: both chosen and rejected log-probabilities dropping, over-fitting a small dataset, or β too small. Diagnose with separate chosen/rejected curves and KL to the reference; remedy with a larger β, fewer epochs or an SFT term.

Verbosity creep. Cause: annotators preferring longer answers, and summed log-probabilities making length a strong signal. Remedies: length-controlled data, length-normalised variants, evaluation that controls for length.

NaN losses. Cause: naive log-sigmoid or σ overflow at large margins, often in low precision. Fix: stable formulations, compute the loss in float32.

Memory blow-up. Cause: two models in memory plus two responses per example. Fix: precompute reference log-probabilities, use parameter-efficient fine-tuning (mc21) with the base as the reference.

Silent data problems. Cause: chosen and rejected swapped for a slice of the data, or duplicated pairs with opposite labels. Symptom: preference accuracy plateaus well below 100% on training data even as other pairs saturate. Fix: inspect the pairs with the most negative margins late in training; they are often mislabelled.

What to monitor and tune

Loss, mean margin, preference accuracy on training and held-out pairs, chosen and rejected implicit rewards and log-probabilities, KL to the reference on held-out prompts, response length, and periodic samples rated by a judge or people. Tune β, learning rate, epochs and data mixture; consider an SFT term when chosen log-probabilities fall.

A cost estimate

For a 7B policy and 100,000 pairs with 300-token responses and 200-token prompts: each pair needs two policy forward-and-backward passes over 500 tokens (about 6 × 7×109 × 1,000 ≈ 4.2 × 1013 FLOPs) plus two reference forwards (about 2 × 7×109 × 1,000 ≈ 1.4 × 1013), roughly 5.6 × 1013 FLOPs per pair and 5.6 × 1018 for an epoch — under an hour on eight modern accelerators at realistic utilisation. Precomputing reference log-probabilities saves a quarter of that and half the model memory.

The reference for this problem

Rafailov, Sharma, Mitchell, Ermon, Manning and Finn, "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model" (NeurIPS 2023), derive the loss from the KL-regularised objective and the Bradley-Terry model, define the implicit reward r̂θ(x, y) = β·log(πθ(y|x)/πref(y|x)), and write the gradient as −β·E[σ(r̂θ(x, yl) − r̂θ(x, yw))·(∇log π(yw|x) − ∇log π(yl|x))], noting that examples are weighted by how incorrectly the implicit reward orders them and that a naive version without this weighting can make the model degenerate. In this lesson's notation σ(r̂l − r̂w) = σ(−z).

Goal: run the solution, then vary it, break it and measure it. Python 3.12 with NumPy (the gpt_env venv); PyTorch only for the optional cross-check.

  1. Run the four blocks as one script. All assertions pass and it prints the loss, margin and accuracy before and after training, and the KL at two values of β.
  2. Vary by hand. Redo section 4's step with chosen token 2 and rejected token 0 and β = 0.5: compute the gradient and the new margin, then check with three lines of code.
  3. Break the stability. Replace log_sigmoid with the naive formula and train with β = 5 and a large learning rate until margins exceed 700. When does the loss become NaN or infinite?
  4. Remove the weighting. Replace σ(−z) in the gradient with 1 (always push chosen up, rejected down equally). Train and plot the KL to the reference: what happens?
  5. Watch the hidden quantity. Log the mean chosen and rejected log-probabilities over training. Does the chosen log-probability ever fall while the loss falls?
  6. Try IPO. Replace the loss with (z/β − 1/(2β))² (IPO's form with τ = β) and its gradient; compare margins after 300 steps.

Each run takes a second or two, so it is practical to sweep β and the learning rate and plot loss, margin and KL together. What to look for: stability matters once margins are large; without the σ(−z) weighting the policy drifts much further from the reference; and the chosen log-probability is a separate signal the loss does not protect.

Work it out

A pair has reference log-probabilities −20 (chosen) and −18 (rejected). After training, the policy gives −21 and −24. With β = 0.1: (a) compute the implicit rewards, margin, loss and dL/dz; (b) the chosen response became less likely — is this a problem for the loss, and what would you check?

Answer 
rw_d, rl_d = 0.1 * (-21 - (-20)), 0.1 * (-24 - (-18))
z_d = rw_d - rl_d
loss_d = -float(log_sigmoid(z_d))
dz_d = 1 / (1 + math.exp(-z_d)) - 1
assert np.isclose(rw_d, -0.1) and np.isclose(rl_d, -0.6) and np.isclose(z_d, 0.5)
assert round(loss_d, 4) == 0.4741 and round(dz_d, 4) == -0.3775

(a) Implicit rewards: chosen 0.1 × (−21 + 20) = −0.1, rejected 0.1 × (−24 + 18) = −0.6. Margin z = −0.1 − (−0.6) = 0.5. Loss = log(1 + e−0.5) = 0.4741, below log 2. dL/dz = σ(0.5) − 1 = −0.3775, so this pair still pushes, but with about three-quarters of the weight of a pair at z = 0. (b) The loss is satisfied — the chosen response gained relative to the rejected one — even though its own probability fell by a factor of e. That is the behaviour the margin can hide. Check the chosen log-probability trend across the dataset, the KL to the reference, and samples: if probability mass is leaving both responses for something else, add an SFT term on chosen responses, raise β, or stop earlier.
