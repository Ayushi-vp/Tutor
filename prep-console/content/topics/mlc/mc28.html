Labelled datasets are expensive; paired data is everywhere. The internet is full of images with captions, questions with answers, code with its documentation, audio with transcripts. Contrastive learning turns pairs into a training signal without any labels: train two encoders so that the embeddings of things that belong together are close and those of things that do not are far apart. CLIP did this with 400 million image-text pairs and produced image and text encoders whose shared embedding space supports zero-shot classification — classify an image by finding which caption ("a photo of a dog", "a photo of a cat") it is closest to — and became a backbone of multimodal models and retrieval systems.

The loss behind it is InfoNCE, introduced by Oord, Li and Vinyals in 2018. It is cross-entropy in disguise: within a batch of N pairs, each image is a classification problem with N candidate captions, exactly one correct. The other N − 1 captions are free negatives. Compute all N × N similarities in one matrix product, divide by a temperature, apply a softmax along each row and each column, and penalise the probability on the diagonal. Everything that matters in practice — why batch size changes the loss, what temperature does to the gradient, why embeddings are normalised, why CLIP learns its temperature and clips it — follows from looking at that formula carefully.

The interview asks you to implement it from scratch. This lesson works a two-pair example by hand at two temperatures; derives the gradient through the similarity matrix, the unit-length normalisation and the temperature; implements a loop version and a vectorised one; checks them against each other, finite differences and PyTorch autograd; shows that random embeddings give log N, that a larger batch raises the loss at fixed quality, and that lower temperature focuses the gradient on the hardest negative; and trains two linear encoders on 200 synthetic pairs from a loss of 12.5 to 0.1 and 98% top-1 retrieval.

The analogy: matching name badges at a conference

A conference organiser has N people and N name badges in a pile, photographed separately. To check a badge-reading system, they show it each person's photo and ask: which of the N badges belongs to this person? Then the reverse: for each badge, which of the N people? The system gets credit for putting high confidence on the right answer, and since all the other badges are on the table, every wrong badge is a free test of whether the system can tell people apart. With 8 people this is easy; with 512 it is much harder, because there are many more look-alikes to rule out. And the organiser can make the system answer boldly (low temperature: "it's definitely this one") or cautiously (high temperature: "could be any of these").

The photos are image embeddings, the badges text embeddings, "which badge?" is a row of the similarity matrix with a softmax over it, the reverse question is the column direction, the other badges are in-batch negatives, the boldness is the temperature.

The analogy breaks usefully: the organiser's question has one right answer, but in real data two different captions can both describe an image well (two photos of the same landmark, two similar captions). InfoNCE still treats them as negatives — false negatives — which is one reason the loss never reaches zero on real data and why deduplication matters.

Where the loss sits in a model

A CLIP-style model has two encoders — a vision network for images and a transformer for text — each followed by a linear projection into a shared embedding space of a few hundred dimensions. The contrastive loss sits on top of those projections and is the only training signal; there is no classification head and no decoder. After training, the encoders are used separately: index images by their embeddings, embed a text query, and retrieve by cosine similarity, or classify zero-shot by embedding one caption per class. In this lesson the encoders are replaced by random embeddings and, in the training test, by two linear maps, which isolates the loss itself. CLIP (contrastive language–image pre-training) is the best-known example, but nothing in the loss is specific to images.

What the interviewer is checking

That you see the batch as an N-way classification with the diagonal as labels and implement it with one matrix product rather than loops; that you include both directions; that you normalise and divide by τ; that the softmax is stable; and that you can say what the loss value means — log N at random, rising with N at fixed quality, strongly dependent on τ. Deriving the gradient, especially through the normalisation and the temperature, is the depth signal; knowing why CLIP learns and clips τ is the practical one.

1. The problem as it is usually asked

"Given a batch of paired image and text embeddings, implement the CLIP (symmetric InfoNCE) loss with a temperature, numerically stably, and its gradients. Show what happens as batch size and temperature change." Clarify: embeddings are normalised inside the loss (yes, as in CLIP); the temperature is a parameter to differentiate (yes, learned in log space); labels are the diagonal (pair i matches pair i); loss averaged over the batch and over both directions. Outline: hand example, loop version, vectorised version with gradients, tests including a batch and temperature sweep and a small training run.

2. The maths in plain words

Normalise every embedding to length one, so the dot product of an image and a text is their cosine similarity. Stack the images as rows of one matrix and the texts as rows of another; multiplying the first by the transpose of the second gives every image-text similarity at once. Divide by the temperature. Row i is a list of scores for "which text goes with image i?"; turn it into probabilities with a softmax and take minus the log of the probability on the correct text, i. Do the same down each column for "which image goes with text j?". Average the two directions and over the batch. That is the loss.

The gradient is the familiar softmax-cross-entropy rule applied twice: for each row, the probabilities minus the one-hot of the right answer; the same for each column; averaged. That gradient on the similarity matrix flows back to each embedding as a weighted sum of the other side's embeddings — pull towards the partner, push away from negatives in proportion to how much probability each currently takes — and through the normalisation, which keeps only the part that changes direction.

3. The equations

normalise:    Î = I / ‖I‖ (rows),  T̂ = T / ‖T‖           (N, d)
similarities: S = Î · T̂ᵀ / τ                              (N, N)
loss:         L_i2t = −(1/N) Σ_i log softmax_row(S)[i, i]
              L_t2i = −(1/N) Σ_j log softmax_col(S)[j, j]
              L = ½ (L_i2t + L_t2i)
gradients:    dS = ½ [ (softmax_row(S) − I_N) + (softmax_col(S) − I_N) ] / N
              dÎ = dS · T̂ / τ,      dT̂ = dSᵀ · Î / τ
              dI = (dÎ − Î ⊙ rowsum(Î ⊙ dÎ)) / ‖I‖        (same for T)
              dL/dτ = −Σ (dS ⊙ S) / τ
fixed gap:    if every positive beats every negative by g in cosine,
              L = log(1 + (N − 1)·e^(−g/τ))
InfoNCE bound: I(X; Y) ≥ log N − L

4. A worked example by hand

Two pairs. Images I1 = [1, 0], I2 = [0, 1]; texts T1 = [0.8, 0.6], T2 = [0.6, 0.8] — all already unit length. Similarities: I1·T1 = 0.8, I1·T2 = 0.6, I2·T1 = 0.6, I2·T2 = 0.8. Each positive beats its negative by a gap of 0.2.

  • τ = 1. Row 1 logits [0.8, 0.6]: −log(e0.8/(e0.8 + e0.6)) = log(1 + e−0.2) = 0.5981. Row 2 is the same by symmetry, and so are both columns, so L = 0.5981.
  • τ = 0.1. Logits [8, 6]: log(1 + e−2) = 0.1269. The embeddings have not changed; dividing by a smaller temperature made the same gap look ten times bigger.
  • More negatives. If a batch had N pairs and every positive beat every negative by the same 0.2, the loss would be log(1 + (N − 1)e−0.2/τ). At N = 1,024: 4.938 at τ = 0.1 and 4.090 at τ = 0.07. The same quality of embeddings gives a very different loss depending on N and τ.
  • Information bound. InfoNCE implies I(X; Y) ≥ log N − L: with N = 1,024 and τ = 0.07, log 1,024 − 4.090 = 2.841 nats. The bound can never exceed log N — about 6.93 nats here — however good the embeddings.
import math
import numpy as np

# The hand-worked example: two image and two text embeddings (already unit length).
I_h = np.array([[1.0, 0.0], [0.0, 1.0]])
T_h = np.array([[0.8, 0.6], [0.6, 0.8]])
S_h = I_h @ T_h.T                                              # cosine similarities
assert np.allclose(S_h, [[0.8, 0.6], [0.6, 0.8]])
def row_ce_diag(logits):                                       # cross-entropy with the matching item as label
    return np.mean([math.log(sum(math.exp(v) for v in row)) - row[i] for i, row in enumerate(logits)])
for tau, expected in [(1.0, 0.5981), (0.1, 0.1269)]:
    L_i2t = row_ce_diag(S_h / tau); L_t2i = row_ce_diag((S_h / tau).T)
    assert round((L_i2t + L_t2i) / 2, 4) == expected            # log(1 + e^(-0.2/tau))
# With the same similarity gap, more negatives make the task harder: log(1 + (N-1) e^(-gap/tau)).
gap_loss = lambda N, tau, gap=0.2: math.log(1 + (N - 1) * math.exp(-gap / tau))
assert round(gap_loss(2, 0.1), 4) == 0.1269 and round(gap_loss(1024, 0.1), 3) == 4.938
assert round(gap_loss(1024, 0.07), 3) == 4.090
# The InfoNCE bound: mutual information >= log N - loss (in nats).
assert round(math.log(1024) - gap_loss(1024, 0.07), 3) == 2.841

5. The naive implementation

The loop version normalises, builds the similarity matrix as a list of lists, and for each i computes the row's log-sum-exp (subtracting the row maximum first) minus the diagonal entry, and the same for column i. It is slow and obviously right, and serves as the reference for the vectorised loss.

def l2_normalise(x, eps=1e-12):
    n = np.sqrt((x * x).sum(axis=1, keepdims=True))
    return x / np.maximum(n, eps), n

def clip_loss_naive(img, txt, tau):
    """Symmetric InfoNCE with explicit loops: image i should pick text i, and text i image i."""
    N = len(img)
    I, _ = l2_normalise(img); T, _ = l2_normalise(txt)
    S = [[float(I[i] @ T[j]) / tau for j in range(N)] for i in range(N)]
    loss = 0.0
    for i in range(N):
        row = S[i]; col = [S[j][i] for j in range(N)]
        m_r, m_c = max(row), max(col)                           # subtract the max before exp
        loss += (m_r + math.log(sum(math.exp(v - m_r) for v in row))) - row[i]
        loss += (m_c + math.log(sum(math.exp(v - m_c) for v in col))) - col[i]
    return loss / (2 * N)

6. The vectorised implementation with gradients

One matrix product builds S; log_softmax along axis 1 gives the row distributions and along axis 0 the column distributions, both stable because each subtracts its maximum. The loss is minus half the sum of the two diagonals, divided by N. The gradient on S follows the softmax-minus-one-hot rule: exp(lp_r) − eye for rows and exp(lp_c) − eye for columns, averaged and divided by N. Through S = ÎT̂ᵀ/τ, the normalised embeddings receive dS·T̂/τ and dSᵀ·Î/τ, and the temperature −Σ(dS ⊙ S)/τ. Finally the normalisation: for x̂ = x/‖x‖, the Jacobian is (I − x̂x̂ᵀ)/‖x‖, so each row's gradient has its component along x̂ removed and is divided by the original norm. The function also returns top-1 retrieval accuracy averaged over both directions.

def log_softmax(z, axis=-1):
    z = z - z.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True)
    return z - np.log(np.exp(z).sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True))

def clip_loss_and_grads(img, txt, tau):
    """Vectorised symmetric InfoNCE (CLIP loss) and gradients w.r.t. the raw embeddings and tau.
    L = 0.5 * (CE(S, diag) + CE(S^T, diag)),  S = (I_hat T_hat^T) / tau."""
    N = len(img)
    I, ni = l2_normalise(img); T, nt = l2_normalise(txt)
    S = (I @ T.T) / tau
    lp_r, lp_c = log_softmax(S, axis=1), log_softmax(S, axis=0)
    loss = -0.5 * (np.trace(lp_r) + np.trace(lp_c)) / N
    # dL/dS: rows give softmax - onehot, columns likewise; each averaged over N and halved.
    dS = 0.5 * ((np.exp(lp_r) - np.eye(N)) + (np.exp(lp_c) - np.eye(N))) / N
    dI_hat = dS @ T / tau
    dT_hat = dS.T @ I / tau
    dtau = -float((dS * S).sum()) / tau                         # S depends on tau as (I T^T) / tau
    # Back through x_hat = x / ||x||:  dx = (dx_hat - x_hat * (x_hat . dx_hat)) / ||x||
    d_img = (dI_hat - I * (I * dI_hat).sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)) / ni
    d_txt = (dT_hat - T * (T * dT_hat).sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)) / nt
    acc = float(((S.argmax(axis=1) == np.arange(N)).mean() + (S.argmax(axis=0) == np.arange(N)).mean()) / 2)
    return float(loss), d_img, d_txt, dtau, acc

7. Testing it

Seven checks. (1) Loop and vectorised losses agree at τ = 1, 0.1 and 0.03, and the hand example gives 0.1269. (2) 512 random, unrelated pairs give a loss of 6.258 against log 512 = 6.238; identical pairs at τ = 0.01 give almost zero. (3) Central finite differences at three coordinates of each embedding matrix match the analytic gradients to 10−7, and the temperature gradient matches too. (4) The share of row 0's negative gradient on its single hardest negative rises as τ goes from 3.0 to 0.3 to 0.03. (5) With the same noise level between pairs, the loss at τ = 0.1 is 0.006 for N = 8, 0.059 for N = 64 and 0.415 for N = 512 — more negatives, harder task. (6) Two linear encoders (20 → 8 for "images", 12 → 8 for "texts") trained with plain gradient descent on batches of 64 from 200 pairs that share a 6-dimensional latent: the batch loss falls from 12.5 to about 0.1 and top-1 retrieval among all 200 pairs reaches 98%; the learned temperature runs to its 0.01 floor, as CLIP's would without clipping. (7) PyTorch with F.normalize and F.cross_entropy in both directions gives the same loss and the same gradients for both embeddings and the temperature.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N, d = 16, 8
img = rng.normal(size=(N, d)); txt = img + 0.8 * rng.normal(size=(N, d))   # matching pairs are correlated

# 1. Loop and vectorised losses agree; the hand example reproduces.
for tau in (1.0, 0.1, 0.03):
    assert np.isclose(clip_loss_naive(img, txt, tau), clip_loss_and_grads(img, txt, tau)[0])
assert round(clip_loss_and_grads(I_h, T_h, 0.1)[0], 4) == 0.1269

# 2. Random, unrelated embeddings give a loss near log N; a perfect match at low tau goes to 0.
far = rng.normal(size=(512, 32))
L_rand = clip_loss_and_grads(far, rng.normal(size=(512, 32)), 1.0)[0]
assert abs(L_rand - math.log(512)) < 0.05
assert clip_loss_and_grads(far, far, 0.01)[0] < 1e-3

# 3. Finite-difference checks of the gradients w.r.t. image, text and tau.
L0, gI, gT, gtau, _ = clip_loss_and_grads(img, txt, 0.1)
for arr, g in ((img, gI), (txt, gT)):
    for idx in [(0, 0), (5, 3), (15, 7)]:
        old = arr[idx]
        arr[idx] = old + 1e-6; lp = clip_loss_and_grads(img, txt, 0.1)[0]
        arr[idx] = old - 1e-6; lm = clip_loss_and_grads(img, txt, 0.1)[0]
        arr[idx] = old
        assert abs((lp - lm) / 2e-6 - g[idx]) < 1e-7
num_tau = (clip_loss_and_grads(img, txt, 0.1 + 1e-7)[0] - clip_loss_and_grads(img, txt, 0.1 - 1e-7)[0]) / 2e-7
assert abs(num_tau - gtau) < 1e-5

# 4. Lower temperature concentrates the gradient on the hardest negative.
def weight_on_hardest(tau):
    I, _ = l2_normalise(img); T, _ = l2_normalise(txt)
    p = np.exp(log_softmax((I @ T.T)[0] / tau)); p[0] = 0      # negatives of row 0 only
    return p.max() / p.sum()
assert weight_on_hardest(0.03) > weight_on_hardest(0.3) > weight_on_hardest(3.0)

# 5. With fixed embedding quality, bigger batches mean a bigger loss (more negatives to beat).
losses = [clip_loss_and_grads(*(lambda a: (a, a + 0.8 * rng.normal(size=a.shape)))(rng.normal(size=(n, 64))), 0.1)[0]
          for n in (8, 64, 512)]
assert losses[0] < losses[1] < losses[2]

# 6. Training two linear encoders on paired data: loss falls, retrieval accuracy rises.
P = 200
z = rng.normal(size=(P, 6))                                   # shared latent content
Ximg = z @ rng.normal(size=(6, 20)) + 0.3 * rng.normal(size=(P, 20))
Xtxt = z @ rng.normal(size=(6, 12)) + 0.3 * rng.normal(size=(P, 12))
Wi, Wt = 0.1 * rng.normal(size=(20, 8)), 0.1 * rng.normal(size=(12, 8))
log_tau = math.log(0.07)                                      # learn tau in log space, as CLIP does
hist = []
for step in range(300):
    b = rng.choice(P, 64, replace=False)
    L, gI_, gT_, gtau_, acc = clip_loss_and_grads(Ximg[b] @ Wi, Xtxt[b] @ Wt, math.exp(log_tau))
    Wi -= 0.5 * Ximg[b].T @ gI_; Wt -= 0.5 * Xtxt[b].T @ gT_
    log_tau = min(max(log_tau - 0.05 * gtau_ * math.exp(log_tau), math.log(0.01)), math.log(1.0))
    hist.append((L, acc))
L_full, *_, acc_full = clip_loss_and_grads(Ximg @ Wi, Xtxt @ Wt, math.exp(log_tau))
assert hist[-1][0] < 0.5 * hist[0][0] and acc_full > 0.5        # top-1 retrieval among all 200 pairs

# 7. Cross-check the loss and gradients with PyTorch autograd, if available.
try:
    import torch
    ti = torch.tensor(img, requires_grad=True); tt = torch.tensor(txt, requires_grad=True)
    tau_t = torch.tensor(0.1, dtype=torch.float64, requires_grad=True)
    Sn = torch.nn.functional.normalize(ti, dim=1) @ torch.nn.functional.normalize(tt, dim=1).T / tau_t
    lab = torch.arange(N)
    Lt = 0.5 * (torch.nn.functional.cross_entropy(Sn, lab) + torch.nn.functional.cross_entropy(Sn.T, lab))
    Lt.backward()
    assert np.isclose(Lt.item(), L0) and np.allclose(ti.grad.numpy(), gI) and np.allclose(tt.grad.numpy(), gT)
    assert np.isclose(tau_t.grad.item(), gtau)
except ImportError:
    pass
if __name__ == "__main__":
    print(f"random loss {L_rand:.3f} vs log 512 = {math.log(512):.3f}; batch losses {np.round(losses, 3)}")
    print(f"training: loss {hist[0][0]:.3f} -> {hist[-1][0]:.3f}, full-set top-1 {acc_full:.2f}, tau {math.exp(log_tau):.3f}")

8. Numerical and practical traps

Small τ overflow. At τ = 0.01 logits reach 100 and e100 overflows float16; always subtract the maximum (log-softmax) and compute the loss in float32. Zero-norm embeddings. Guard the normalisation with a small epsilon. One direction only. Easy to forget the column term; the gradient then pushes only images. Temperature collapse. Without a floor, τ heads to zero; clip the logit scale (CLIP: at most 100). False negatives. Duplicates in a batch are treated as negatives; deduplicate or mask them. Comparing losses across batch sizes. Not meaningful; report retrieval accuracy.

9. Edge cases worth naming

N = 1: there are no negatives and the loss is always zero — contrastive learning needs at least two pairs. Unequal numbers of images and texts (several captions per image): use a rectangular matrix and multi-positive targets. Identical items in a batch: both diagonal and off-diagonal entries equal, so the best possible row probability is ½, and the loss cannot fall below log 2 for that row. Very large N: the N × N matrix dominates memory (32,768² entries is about 4 GB in float32), so it is computed in blocks across devices.

10. Complexity

The similarity matrix costs O(N²·d) multiply-adds and O(N²) memory; the softmaxes and gradients are O(N²); back-propagation to embeddings is another O(N²·d). Normalisation is O(N·d). Encoder forward and backward passes dominate for moderate N; the N² terms dominate for very large batches, which is why large-batch contrastive training is sharded.

11. Talking through the design

Lead with the matrix picture (diagonal positives, in-batch negatives), write the loss in both directions with τ, mention stability, derive dS and its path back through normalisation, then discuss the behaviour: log N at random, dependence on N and τ, hard-negative focus at low τ, CLIP's learned and clipped temperature, and what you would monitor (retrieval accuracy both ways).

Why InfoNCE bounds mutual information

Oord et al. frame each row as identifying the true partner among N candidates, N − 1 of them drawn independently. The optimal scoring function for that task is proportional to the density ratio p(y|x)/p(y), and plugging it in shows that the expected loss is at least log N − I(X; Y). Rearranged, I(X; Y) ≥ log N − L. Two consequences matter in practice: the bound saturates at log N, so estimating or learning large amounts of shared information needs many negatives; and lowering the loss at fixed N tightens the bound, which is the formal sense in which contrastive training extracts information shared between the two views.

Temperature as a hardness dial

The gradient on a negative j in row i is proportional to softmax(Si)j, which for small τ is dominated by the largest similarities. So τ decides whether learning focuses on separating the anchor from the few negatives that look most like it (low τ) or from all negatives roughly equally (high τ). Very low τ makes training sensitive to false negatives — a near-duplicate caption takes most of the gradient — and to noise; high τ wastes effort on easy negatives. Learning τ lets the model pick a hardness level as training proceeds, which in practice drifts towards low values as embeddings improve.

Uniformity and alignment

Another way to read the contrastive loss: the positive term pulls matched pairs together (alignment), and the log-sum-exp over negatives pushes all embeddings apart on the unit sphere (uniformity). A model that maps everything to one point is perfectly aligned but has a loss of log N; one that spreads points uniformly but ignores pairing also gets log N. Good representations do both, which is why normalisation to the sphere is part of the method, not a detail.

SigLIP: dropping the softmax

The softmax couples every item in a row: each probability depends on all N similarities, so the loss needs the full matrix and its normalisation. SigLIP replaces it with an independent binary classification per cell — sigmoid of (similarity × scale + bias), target 1 on the diagonal and 0 elsewhere — so cells can be computed in blocks without a global normaliser, which simplifies very large batches and distributed training. It shows the core idea of this lesson — paired data with in-batch negatives — is separable from the particular softmax form.

Getting many negatives without a huge batch

Memory limits the batch, and the batch limits the negatives. Three techniques decouple them. A memory bank or queue (as in MoCo) keeps embeddings from recent batches and uses them as extra negatives, often produced by a slowly updated momentum encoder so they stay consistent. Gradient caching computes all embeddings of a large batch without storing activations, computes the contrastive loss and the gradient with respect to each embedding, then re-runs each sub-batch's encoder with those embedding gradients — exact large-batch training in small-batch memory. Distributed all-gather pools embeddings across devices so the global batch provides the negatives. All three exist because the loss in this lesson gets better with more negatives.

More than one positive

Real datasets often have several valid partners per item — five captions per image, several relevant passages per query. The cross-entropy target then becomes a distribution spread over all positives in the row, or each positive is treated in turn with the others masked out of the negatives. Treating extra positives as negatives (what the plain loss does) pushes apart things that should be close and caps achievable accuracy.

Beyond images and text

The same loss trains text embedding models for retrieval (query and relevant passage as the pair, other passages in the batch as negatives), code search, audio-text models and recommendation two-tower models (user and item embeddings). The practical differences are in how pairs and hard negatives are mined, how false negatives are filtered, and whether the two encoders share weights. The implementation in this lesson carries over unchanged.

Why the loss transfers zero-shot

A classifier trained with cross-entropy learns a fixed set of class vectors; a contrastive model learns an encoder for the class descriptions themselves. At test time any set of labels can be written as captions, embedded, and used as the rows of the classifier — which is why a CLIP model can classify into categories it never saw as labels. The same view explains its weaknesses: the zero-shot classifier is only as good as the caption wording (prompt templates such as "a photo of a {label}" measurably help), and fine-grained or abstract categories that rarely appear in captions are hard.

Where this loss runs

Contrastive losses train CLIP-style vision-language encoders, sentence and passage embedding models used for semantic search and retrieval-augmented generation, and two-tower retrieval models in recommendation. In all of them the batch is the main source of negatives, often augmented with mined hard negatives, and training is distributed so that the global batch — and therefore the number of negatives — is large.

Failure modes and symptoms

Loss stuck near log N. Encoders are not learning: check normalisation, the learning rate, and that pairs are actually aligned (shuffled captions produce exactly this).

Temperature collapse and instability. Logit scale climbs until training diverges; clip it.

False negatives. Duplicate or near-duplicate pairs in a batch are pushed apart, hurting retrieval for popular items; deduplicate data and mask known duplicates.

Batch-size mismatch. A model fine-tuned with a much smaller batch than it was pre-trained with sees easier tasks and can lose quality; use gradient caching or memory banks to keep negatives numerous.

Asymmetric quality. Text-to-image retrieval much worse than image-to-text: often a missing or down-weighted direction in the loss.

Shortcut learning. Symptom: excellent training retrieval, poor transfer. Cause: pairs can be matched by a trivial cue — a watermark, a timestamp in the caption, image size, or a source-specific template — so the encoders learn the cue instead of the content. Fix: audit what distinguishes pairs, strip boilerplate, and evaluate on data from a different source.

Distributed gather bugs. Symptom: multi-device training is worse than single-device at the same global batch. Cause: each device computes the loss only against its local negatives, or the gathered embeddings do not carry gradient back. Fix: gather embeddings from all devices for the similarity matrix and make sure the local slice still gets gradient.

Embedding drift between index and query. Symptom: retrieval quality drops after a model update. Cause: stored item embeddings came from the old encoder while queries use the new one; contrastive spaces are not compatible across training runs. Fix: re-embed the whole index when either encoder changes, or train with a compatibility constraint.

What to monitor and tune

Top-1 and top-5 retrieval in both directions on held-out pairs at a fixed batch size; the learned temperature; the loss relative to log N; gradient norms; and for production retrieval, recall@k on real queries. Tune batch size (as large as memory allows), the temperature initialisation and floor, the learning rate, and the mix of hard negatives.

A cost estimate

At CLIP's batch of 32,768 with 512-dimensional embeddings, the similarity matrix needs 32,768² × 512 ≈ 5.5 × 1011 multiply-adds and holds about 1.07 billion entries (4.3 GB in float32) if materialised whole — small next to the encoders' compute but large enough in memory that it is computed in per-device blocks.

The reference for this problem

Radford et al.'s CLIP (Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training) paper gives the loss as numpy-like pseudocode — normalise both embeddings, compute logits = I_e · T_eᵀ · exp(t), take cross-entropy along both axes with labels arange(n), and average — and reports that the temperature τ "was initialized to the equivalent of 0.07 … and clipped to prevent scaling the logits by more than 100", that it is "directly optimized during training as a log-parameterized multiplicative scalar", and that training used "a very large minibatch size of 32,768". Oord, Li and Vinyals introduced InfoNCE and the mutual-information bound.

Goal: run the solution, then vary it, break it and measure it. Python 3.12 with NumPy (the gpt_env venv); PyTorch only for the optional cross-check.

  1. Run the four blocks as one script. All assertions pass and it prints the random-embedding loss, the batch sweep and the training summary.
  2. Vary by hand. Redo section 4 with T1 = [0.6, 0.8] and T2 = [0.8, 0.6] (the captions swapped). What is the loss at τ = 0.1, and what does the model "believe"?
  3. Break it. Drop the column term from the loss and gradient. Train again and measure text-to-image retrieval separately from image-to-text.
  4. Break the normalisation. Remove l2_normalise from training and watch the embedding norms and the loss.
  5. Sweep temperature. Train with τ fixed at 1.0, 0.1 and 0.01 (no learning) and compare final retrieval accuracy.
  6. Add false negatives. Duplicate 20 of the 200 pairs so duplicates often share a batch, and measure the effect on retrieval for those items.

Each run takes a second or two, so record a small table — setting, loss, retrieval in each direction, final τ — for every experiment; the comparisons are the point. What to look for: swapped captions give a loss above log 2 — the model is confidently wrong; one direction only hurts the other direction's retrieval; unnormalised embeddings grow without bound; τ that is too high learns slowly, too low is unstable; duplicates in a batch cap how well those items can be retrieved. Write down your expected result before each run; surprises are where you learn the most.

Work it out

(a) Your embeddings separate every positive from every negative by a cosine gap of 0.3. What is the loss at N = 256 for τ = 0.05 and τ = 0.2? (b) A team reports that their new model's contrastive loss dropped from 2.1 to 1.4 after doubling the batch from 4,096 to 8,192. Is that evidence the embeddings improved?

Answer 
L_low = gap_loss(256, 0.05, gap=0.3)
L_high = gap_loss(256, 0.2, gap=0.3)
assert round(L_low, 3) == 0.490 and round(L_high, 3) == 4.059

(a) log(1 + 255·e−0.3/0.05) = log(1 + 255·e−6) = 0.490; at τ = 0.2, log(1 + 255·e−1.5) = 4.059. Identical embeddings, losses more than eight times apart: temperature dominates the number. (b) It is strong evidence, but for a reason the team may not have intended: doubling the number of negatives should raise the loss at fixed quality (adding about log 2 ≈ 0.69 when many negatives are close), so a 0.7 drop alongside it means the embeddings improved substantially — unless the temperature also changed, which can move the loss by more than that. Check the learned τ at both points and compare retrieval accuracy at a fixed evaluation batch size before concluding.
