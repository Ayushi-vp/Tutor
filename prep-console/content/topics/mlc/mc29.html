Before 2013, most language models represented words as indices into a vocabulary — one-hot vectors with no notion that "king" and "queen" are related. word2vec changed that with an almost embarrassingly simple idea: train a vector for every word so that it can predict the words around it. Words that appear in similar contexts end up with similar vectors, and the resulting embeddings captured relations well enough that "king − man + woman" landed near "queen". The method trained on billions of words on a single machine, and dense word vectors became the default input to NLP systems for years, until contextual models like BERT and GPT replaced them. The ideas — a context window as supervision, learning by telling real pairs from noise, and embedding tables updated sparsely — run through modern recommendation, retrieval and contrastive learning.

The obstacle Mikolov and colleagues had to remove was the softmax. Predicting a context word means a probability distribution over the whole vocabulary — a million words — for every training pair. Their fix, negative sampling, turns each pair into a small binary classification: is this word a real context of the centre word, or a random word drawn from a noise distribution? Each update then touches only k + 1 output vectors. Two further details made it work well: draw the noise words from the word frequencies raised to the 3/4 power, and randomly drop occurrences of very frequent words.

Implementing skip-gram with negative sampling (SGNS) from scratch is a standard ML coding question because it tests pair generation, a sampled loss, deriving gradients through a sigmoid for two embedding tables, and the sparse-update trap that catches most first implementations. This lesson computes the noise distribution and one full update by hand; writes a per-pair SGD loop and a vectorised mini-batch version that accumulates repeated words with np.add.at; checks the gradients with finite differences and PyTorch autograd; and trains 16-dimensional vectors on a synthetic corpus of 3,000 sentences, after which each royal's, animal's, food's and place's nearest neighbours are the other members of its group. It also records the failure on the way: with a learning rate of 0.5 per pair, the frequent word "the" received dozens of summed updates per batch and the whole model diverged to infinity.

The analogy: guessing who sits near whom at a wedding

At a large wedding, you never see the guest list, but you see who sits near whom at every table. After enough tables, you can tell that the bride's college friends cluster together and so do the groom's colleagues, and that the two cousins who always sit by the same aunt are similar in some sense — not because you know anything about them, but because their neighbours overlap. If someone asks "is Sam likely to sit next to Priya?", you can answer by comparing what you have learned about each. And to learn faster, you do not consider every possible guest each time: for each real neighbour pair you saw, you also pick a few random guests and remind yourself they were not sitting there.

The guests are words, the tables are context windows, "who sits near whom" is the set of (centre, context) pairs, what you learn about each guest is its vector, comparing two guests is a dot product, and the random guests you remind yourself about are negative samples. Picking random guests mostly from the frequently seen ones — but not exclusively — is the 3/4-power noise distribution.

The analogy breaks in one technical way: each word has two descriptions, one for when it is the guest in focus (the input vector) and one for when it is a neighbour (the output vector). That separation is what lets "dog" be a likely neighbour of "cat" without asking any word to be its own neighbour.

Why "context predicts meaning" works at all

The idea behind word2vec is older than the method: linguists observed that you can learn a lot about a word from the company it keeps. If "sceptre" appears near "king", "queen", "throne" and "crown", and "wand" appears near "wizard", "spell" and "magic", a learner that only sees which words appear together can still place "sceptre" near "crown" and far from "spell". word2vec makes the learner a pair of vector tables and the lesson a prediction task, and lets gradient descent find vectors in which these co-occurrence patterns become geometry: words with overlapping neighbourhoods point in similar directions. Nothing in the training says what any word means; the structure comes entirely from the statistics of the corpus — which is both why it works with no labels and why it reproduces the corpus's quirks.

What the interviewer is checking

That you can generate pairs correctly (window bounds, no self-pairs, sentence boundaries); that you know why negative sampling exists and write its loss with σ and the minus sign on negatives; that you derive the three gradients; that your vectorised version accumulates repeated indices; that you sample negatives from the right distribution; and that you know the practical details — learning rate per pair, subsampling, two embedding tables — and what the result does and does not capture.

2. The maths in plain words

Give every word two vectors: one for when it is the centre (v) and one for when it appears as a context (u). For a real pair, the dot product ucontext·vcentre should be large, so that σ of it — the predicted probability the pair is real — is near 1. For each of k noise words drawn at random, the dot product should be small, so σ is near 0. The loss is the sum of the log-losses of these k + 1 tiny logistic regressions.

The gradients are the logistic-regression rule (prediction minus label, times the input) applied to each dot product. For the real pair, the label is 1, so the factor is σ(s) − 1, negative: the centre vector moves towards the context vector and the context vector towards the centre. For a noise word, the label is 0, so the factor is σ(sk), positive: they move apart. The centre vector collects all k + 1 contributions.

3. The equations

pairs:       (c, o) for each centre c and each o within ±w positions, same sentence
noise:       P_n(w) = count(w)^(3/4) / Σ_w' count(w')^(3/4)
loss:        ℓ(c, o) = −log σ(u_o·v_c) − Σ_{i=1..k} log σ(−u_{n_i}·v_c),     n_i ~ P_n
gradients:   g = σ(u_o·v_c) − 1,     g_i = σ(u_{n_i}·v_c)
             ∂ℓ/∂v_c = g·u_o + Σ_i g_i·u_{n_i}
             ∂ℓ/∂u_o = g·v_c,        ∂ℓ/∂u_{n_i} = g_i·v_c
mini-batch:  average ℓ over B pairs; scatter-add each row's gradient into V and U (np.add.at)
subsampling: discard an occurrence of w with probability 1 − √(t / f(w))
stability:   log σ(z) = min(z, 0) − log1p(exp(−|z|))

4. A worked example by hand

Noise distribution. Three words with counts 100, 10 and 1 ("the", "king", "sceptre"). Unigram shares: 100/111 = 0.901, 0.090, 0.009. Raise counts to 3/4: 1000.75 = 31.62, 100.75 = 5.62, 10.75 = 1; total 38.25. Noise shares: 0.827, 0.147, 0.026. "sceptre" is now drawn almost three times as often as its frequency alone would suggest, "the" a little less.

One update. Centre vc = [0.5, 0], true context uo = [0.4, 0.2], one negative uk = [0.2, −0.4].

  • Scores: s = uo·vc = 0.2; sk = uk·vc = 0.1.
  • Loss: −log σ(0.2) − log σ(−0.1) = log(1 + e−0.2) + log(1 + e0.1) = 0.5981 + 0.7444 = 1.3425.
  • Factors: g = σ(0.2) − 1 = 0.5498 − 1 = −0.4502; gk = σ(0.1) = 0.5250.
  • Gradients: ∂ℓ/∂vc = −0.4502·[0.4, 0.2] + 0.5250·[0.2, −0.4] = [−0.1801 + 0.1050, −0.0900 − 0.2100] = [−0.0751, −0.3000]; ∂ℓ/∂uo = −0.4502·[0.5, 0] = [−0.2251, 0]; ∂ℓ/∂uk = 0.5250·[0.5, 0] = [0.2625, 0].
  • Step with learning rate 0.5: vc = [0.5375, 0.1500], uo = [0.5125, 0.2], uk = [0.0688, −0.4]. New scores 0.306 and −0.023; loss 1.2337.

The centre moved towards the real context (its second coordinate grew towards uo's positive 0.2) and away from the negative (whose second coordinate is negative). The block asserts all of it.

import math
import numpy as np

# The hand-worked example. Noise distribution: unigram counts raised to 3/4, renormalised.
counts = np.array([100.0, 10.0, 1.0])                          # "the", "king", "sceptre"
unigram = counts / counts.sum()
noise = counts ** 0.75 / (counts ** 0.75).sum()
assert np.allclose(unigram, [0.9009, 0.0901, 0.0090], atol=1e-4)
assert np.allclose(noise, [0.8268, 0.1470, 0.0261], atol=1e-4)   # rare words sampled ~3x more often

# One SGNS update by hand: centre v_c, true context u_o, one negative u_k (2-d embeddings).
sig = lambda t: 1 / (1 + math.exp(-t))
v_c, u_o, u_k = np.array([0.5, 0.0]), np.array([0.4, 0.2]), np.array([0.2, -0.4])
s_pos, s_neg = u_o @ v_c, u_k @ v_c                            # 0.2 and 0.1
loss = -math.log(sig(s_pos)) - math.log(sig(-s_neg))
assert round(loss, 4) == 1.3425                                # 0.5981 + 0.7444
g_pos, g_neg = sig(s_pos) - 1, sig(s_neg)                      # -0.4502, 0.5250
d_vc = g_pos * u_o + g_neg * u_k
d_uo, d_uk = g_pos * v_c, g_neg * v_c
assert np.allclose(d_vc, [-0.0751, -0.3000], atol=1e-4)
assert np.allclose(d_uo, [-0.2251, 0.0], atol=1e-4) and np.allclose(d_uk, [0.2625, 0.0], atol=1e-4)
lr = 0.5
v2, uo2, uk2 = v_c - lr * d_vc, u_o - lr * d_uo, u_k - lr * d_uk
loss2 = -math.log(sig(uo2 @ v2)) - math.log(sig(-(uk2 @ v2)))
assert loss2 < loss and round(loss2, 4) == 1.2337

5. The naive implementation

build_corpus generates 3,000 sentences from six templates — royals rule or live in places, animals chase animals or run, people eat or like foods — so the "right answer" for nearest neighbours is known: words that fill the same slot. skipgram_pairs slides a ±2 window inside each sentence. sgns_step_naive is the textbook per-pair stochastic gradient descent: for each pair, compute the positive score and factor, update the context's output vector, loop over its k negatives updating each output vector and accumulating the centre's gradient, then update the centre's input vector — reading the centre vector before any update so all gradients are computed at the same point.

def build_corpus(seed=0, n_sentences=3000):
    """A tiny synthetic corpus with structure: royals rule places, animals chase animals, people eat food."""
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    royals, places = ["king", "queen", "prince", "princess"], ["kingdom", "castle", "realm"]
    animals, foods = ["dog", "cat", "fox", "wolf"], ["bread", "cheese", "apple", "soup"]
    people = ["man", "woman", "child"]
    templates = [
        lambda: ["the", r.choice(royals), "rules", "the", r.choice(places)],
        lambda: ["the", r.choice(royals), "lives", "in", "the", r.choice(places)],
        lambda: ["a", r.choice(animals), "chases", "a", r.choice(animals)],
        lambda: ["the", r.choice(animals), "runs", "fast"],
        lambda: ["the", r.choice(people), "eats", r.choice(foods)],
        lambda: ["a", r.choice(people), "likes", r.choice(foods)],
    ]
    sents = [templates[r.integers(len(templates))]() for _ in range(n_sentences)]
    vocab = sorted({w for s in sents for w in s})
    idx = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}
    return [[idx[w] for w in s] for s in sents], vocab

def skipgram_pairs(sents, window=2):
    """(centre, context) for every word and every neighbour within the window, same sentence."""
    pairs = []
    for s in sents:
        for i, c in enumerate(s):
            for j in range(max(0, i - window), min(len(s), i + window + 1)):
                if j != i:
                    pairs.append((c, s[j]))
    return np.array(pairs)

def sgns_step_naive(V, U, pairs, negs, lr):
    """Plain SGD, one (centre, context) pair at a time, K negatives each. V: centre vectors, U: context vectors."""
    total = 0.0
    for (c, o), ks in zip(pairs, negs):
        v = V[c].copy()
        s = U[o] @ v
        g = 1 / (1 + math.exp(-s)) - 1                           # d loss / d s_pos
        dv = g * U[o]
        total += -math.log(1 / (1 + math.exp(-s)))
        U[o] -= lr * g * v
        for k in ks:
            sk = U[k] @ v
            gk = 1 / (1 + math.exp(-sk))                         # d loss / d s_neg
            dv += gk * U[k]
            total += -math.log(1 / (1 + math.exp(sk)))
            U[k] -= lr * gk * v
        V[c] -= lr * dv
    return total / len(pairs)

6. The vectorised implementation

sgns_loss_grads processes a batch of B pairs with k negatives each. It gathers the centre vectors (B, d), computes positive scores with a row-wise dot product and negative scores with one einsum against the gathered negatives (B, k, d), and computes the mean loss with the stable log-sigmoid. The factors σ(s) − 1 and σ(sk) come from exp(log σ) so they inherit stability. The centre gradients are one expression, and then the essential step: np.add.at scatters each row's gradient into the full V and U matrices, accumulating when the same word appears several times in the batch as a centre, context or negative. Plain fancy-indexed assignment would keep only one of those contributions. noise_distribution computes unigram0.75 from the corpus, and nearest ranks words by cosine similarity of their input vectors.

def log_sigmoid(z):
    return np.minimum(z, 0) - np.log1p(np.exp(-np.abs(z)))

def sgns_loss_grads(V, U, C, O, K):
    """Mini-batch SGNS. C, O: (B,) centre and context ids; K: (B, k) negative ids.
    loss = mean_b [ -log s(u_o.v_c) - sum_k log s(-u_k.v_c) ]."""
    v = V[C]                                                     # (B, d)
    s_pos = (U[O] * v).sum(axis=1)                               # (B,)
    s_neg = np.einsum("bkd,bd->bk", U[K], v)                     # (B, k)
    loss = -(log_sigmoid(s_pos).sum() + log_sigmoid(-s_neg).sum()) / len(C)
    g_pos = (np.exp(log_sigmoid(s_pos)) - 1) / len(C)            # sigma(s) - 1
    g_neg = np.exp(log_sigmoid(s_neg)) / len(C)                  # sigma(s_k)
    dv = g_pos[:, None] * U[O] + np.einsum("bk,bkd->bd", g_neg, U[K])
    dV, dU = np.zeros_like(V), np.zeros_like(U)
    np.add.at(dV, C, dv)                                         # words repeat in a batch: accumulate
    np.add.at(dU, O, g_pos[:, None] * v)
    np.add.at(dU, K, g_neg[:, :, None] * v[:, None, :])
    return float(loss), dV, dU

def noise_distribution(ids, n_vocab, power=0.75):
    c = np.bincount(ids, minlength=n_vocab).astype(float)
    p = c ** power
    return p / p.sum()

def nearest(V, vocab, word, k=3):
    E = V / np.linalg.norm(V, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    sims = E @ E[vocab.index(word)]
    order = [i for i in np.argsort(-sims) if vocab[i] != word]
    return [str(vocab[i]) for i in order[:k]]

7. Testing it

Six checks. (1) Pair construction: a five-word sentence with window 2 gives 14 pairs, and a three-word sentence with window 1 gives exactly the four adjacent pairs. (2) On the corpus, the 3/4 power lowers "the"'s share and raises the rarest word's. (3) Central finite differences at three entries of each matrix — chosen to be a centre, a context and a negative in the batch — match the analytic gradients to 10−8. (4) Twenty passes of the naive per-pair SGD over a fixed batch lower its loss. (5) Six epochs of mini-batch SGD over 37,940 pairs (batches of 128, learning rate 0.025 per pair, input vectors initialised small and output vectors at zero as in word2vec) lower the loss from about 3.47 to 1.77; and every group's nearest neighbours are exactly its other members: king → prince, queen, princess; dog → wolf, fox, cat; bread → soup, apple, cheese; castle → realm, kingdom. (6) PyTorch autograd on the same loss built from logsigmoid gives identical loss and gradients for both matrices.

sents, vocab = build_corpus()
pairs = skipgram_pairs(sents)
n_vocab, dim, k = len(vocab), 16, 5
flat = np.array([w for s in sents for w in s])
P_noise = noise_distribution(flat, n_vocab)
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)

# 1. Pair construction: a 5-word sentence with window 2 gives 2+3+4+3+2 = 14 pairs.
assert len(skipgram_pairs([[0, 1, 2, 3, 4]])) == 14
assert set(map(tuple, skipgram_pairs([[7, 8, 9]], window=1))) == {(7, 8), (8, 7), (8, 9), (9, 8)}

# 2. The 3/4 power flattens the distribution: frequent words lose share, rare ones gain.
uni = np.bincount(flat, minlength=n_vocab) / len(flat)
the, rare = vocab.index("the"), int(np.argmin(uni))
assert P_noise[the] < uni[the] and P_noise[rare] > uni[rare]

# 3. Gradient check of the mini-batch loss.
V = rng.normal(scale=0.3, size=(n_vocab, dim)); U = rng.normal(scale=0.3, size=(n_vocab, dim))
b = rng.choice(len(pairs), 32, replace=False)
C, O = pairs[b, 0], pairs[b, 1]
K = rng.choice(n_vocab, size=(32, k), p=P_noise)
L0, dV, dU = sgns_loss_grads(V, U, C, O, K)
for M, G in ((V, dV), (U, dU)):
    for i in [(int(C[0]), 0), (int(O[3]), 5), (int(K[2, 1]), 15)]:
        old = M[i]; M[i] = old + 1e-6; lp = sgns_loss_grads(V, U, C, O, K)[0]
        M[i] = old - 1e-6; lm = sgns_loss_grads(V, U, C, O, K)[0]; M[i] = old
        assert abs((lp - lm) / 2e-6 - G[i]) < 1e-8

# 4. The naive per-pair SGD and the batched version both reduce the loss on the same data.
Vn, Un = V.copy(), U.copy()
before = sgns_loss_grads(Vn, Un, C, O, K)[0]
for _ in range(20):
    sgns_step_naive(Vn, Un, list(zip(C.tolist(), O.tolist())), K.tolist(), lr=0.05)
assert sgns_loss_grads(Vn, Un, C, O, K)[0] < before

# 5. Train with mini-batch SGD; words used in the same contexts end up as nearest neighbours.
V = rng.normal(scale=0.1, size=(n_vocab, dim)); U = np.zeros((n_vocab, dim))   # word2vec-style init
hist = []
for epoch in range(6):
    order = rng.permutation(len(pairs))
    for start in range(0, len(order), 128):
        bb = order[start:start + 128]
        Kb = rng.choice(n_vocab, size=(len(bb), k), p=P_noise)
        L, dV, dU = sgns_loss_grads(V, U, pairs[bb, 0], pairs[bb, 1], Kb)
        V -= 0.025 * len(bb) * dV; U -= 0.025 * len(bb) * dU                # lr 0.025 per pair, word2vec's default
        hist.append(L)
assert np.mean(hist[-20:]) < 0.6 * np.mean(hist[:20])
groups = {"king": {"queen", "prince", "princess"}, "dog": {"cat", "fox", "wolf"},
          "bread": {"cheese", "apple", "soup"}, "castle": {"kingdom", "realm"}}
for w, same in groups.items():
    assert set(nearest(V, vocab, w, k=len(same))) == same, (w, nearest(V, vocab, w))

# 6. Cross-check loss and gradients with PyTorch autograd, if available.
try:
    import torch
    Vt = torch.tensor(V, requires_grad=True); Ut = torch.tensor(U, requires_grad=True)
    Ct, Ot, Kt = torch.tensor(C), torch.tensor(O), torch.tensor(K)
    vv = Vt[Ct]
    Lt = -(torch.nn.functional.logsigmoid((Ut[Ot] * vv).sum(1)).sum()
           + torch.nn.functional.logsigmoid(-(Ut[Kt] @ vv[:, :, None])[..., 0]).sum()) / len(C)
    Lt.backward()
    Ln, dVn, dUn = sgns_loss_grads(V, U, C, O, K)
    assert np.isclose(Lt.item(), Ln) and np.allclose(Vt.grad.numpy(), dVn) and np.allclose(Ut.grad.numpy(), dUn)
except ImportError:
    pass
if __name__ == "__main__":
    print(f"{len(sents)} sentences, {len(pairs)} pairs, vocab {n_vocab}; loss {np.mean(hist[:20]):.3f} -> {np.mean(hist[-20:]):.3f}")
    for w in groups:
        print(w, "->", nearest(V, vocab, w))

The first version of test 5 used a learning rate of 0.5 per pair with batches of 256 and diverged within an epoch: losses overflowed to 10290. The cause was not a bug in the gradient — the gradient check passed — but the interaction of a correct scatter-add with frequent words: "the" appears in a large share of pairs, so its summed gradient in one batch was dozens of single-pair updates applied at once. Dropping to word2vec's default 0.025 per pair fixed it.

8. Numerical and practical traps

Accumulation. Use np.add.at (or a scatter-add); fancy-index assignment silently drops repeats. Learning rate with repeats. Set the rate per pair and keep it small; frequent words multiply it. Log-sigmoid stability. Scores grow as training proceeds; use the stable form. Initialisation. Output vectors at zero and small random input vectors (word2vec's choice) start every score at zero; initialising both large makes early gradients noisy. Negatives that equal the context. Occasionally the sampled negative is the true context; word2vec does not exclude it, and the effect averages out. Sentence boundaries. Windows should not cross sentences or documents.

9. Edge cases worth naming

Words seen once get few updates and poor vectors; word2vec drops words below a minimum count (5 by default in the original tool). Out-of-vocabulary words have no vector at all; fastText fixes this with character n-gram vectors. Very short sentences produce few pairs. Polysemy: "bank" gets one vector mixing its senses. A vocabulary of millions makes the noise sampling itself a cost; word2vec precomputes a large table of word ids in proportion to Pn and samples indices from it.

10. Complexity

Per pair: O((k + 1)·d) for scores and gradients, independent of vocabulary size — the point of negative sampling — versus O(V·d) for a full softmax. An epoch over a corpus of T tokens with window w costs O(T·2w·(k + 1)·d). Memory: two V × d matrices. In the toy: 37,940 pairs × 6 negatives × 16 dimensions per epoch, a fraction of a second in NumPy.

11. Talking through the design

Start with the softmax objective and why it is too expensive, introduce negative sampling as k + 1 logistic regressions, give the noise distribution and the reason for 3/4, derive the gradients, then implement with attention to accumulation and learning rate. Finish with subsampling, what the vectors capture (context similarity, including bias), and the link to PMI matrix factorisation.

SGNS as matrix factorisation

Levy and Goldberg showed that if the embedding dimension is large enough, the SGNS objective is optimised when uo·vc = PMI(c, o) − log k, where PMI is the pointwise mutual information log(P(c, o)/(P(c)·P(o))). So word2vec implicitly factorises a shifted PMI matrix of word-context co-occurrences into V·Uᵀ. That explains several empirical facts: why two vectors per word are needed (the matrix is not symmetric in general), why k acts as a shift that discourages low-PMI pairs, and why count-based methods — build the PMI matrix, take a truncated SVD — perform comparably when their hyperparameters are tuned the same way. GloVe takes the explicit route, fitting log co-occurrence counts with a weighted least-squares loss.

CBOW, the sibling model

Continuous bag-of-words reverses skip-gram: average the context words' input vectors and predict the centre. It is faster (one prediction per position rather than 2w) and smooths over contexts, which tends to help frequent words; skip-gram treats each context pair separately and tends to give better vectors for rare words. Both can use negative sampling or hierarchical softmax, and the loss and gradient structure is the same as in this lesson with the averaged context in place of the centre vector.

Hierarchical softmax

The alternative to negative sampling in the paper replaces the flat softmax with a binary tree over the vocabulary — a Huffman tree, so frequent words have short paths — and models p(w | c) as the product of left/right sigmoid decisions along w's path, about log2(V) of them. It computes a proper normalised probability at O(log V) cost, whereas negative sampling computes none at O(k). For learning vectors, negative sampling usually works better and is simpler, which is why it became the default.

From word2vec to modern embeddings

Static word vectors give each word one vector regardless of context, so "bank" in "river bank" and "bank account" are the same. Contextual models compute a vector per occurrence from the whole sentence and have replaced static vectors for most tasks. But the training ideas persist: predicting neighbours as self-supervision (masked language modelling generalises it), contrasting real pairs with noise (mc28's InfoNCE), and sparse embedding tables trained with negative sampling, which remain central to large-scale recommendation, where item-to-item "word2vec" over user sessions is still a strong baseline.

Why the learning rate is per pair

The original word2vec tool processes one pair at a time with a learning rate that starts at 0.025 and decays linearly to near zero over training, with several threads updating the shared matrices without locks. Each update is small and immediately visible to the next pair. A mini-batch implementation that averages gradients over B pairs and steps with rate η is equivalent to applying B single-pair updates simultaneously, each at rate η/B — except that the gradients are all computed at the same old point. For rare words that difference is negligible; for a word that appears in a quarter of the batch, its single summed update equals dozens of sequential ones computed at a stale position, which overshoots. Scaling the step as rate × B × mean gradient (as the lesson does) keeps the per-pair rate fixed, so 0.025 per pair matches the original tool's behaviour and stays stable.

Bias in, bias out

Because vectors encode co-occurrence statistics, they encode whatever associations the corpus contains, including social stereotypes; well-known analyses found occupation words aligned with gender directions in news-trained embeddings. Debiasing methods project out identified directions, but the safer engineering stance is to measure associations relevant to the application and to remember that nearest neighbours reflect the training text, not ground truth.

Where this still runs

Pre-trained word2vec, GloVe and fastText vectors remain common in lightweight classifiers, feature pipelines and resource-constrained settings. The SGNS training recipe itself is widely used beyond words: "item2vec"-style embeddings trained on sequences of products viewed or bought, graph embeddings trained on random walks (DeepWalk, node2vec), and candidate-generation embeddings in recommender systems, all with negative sampling and sparse updates.

Failure modes and symptoms

Divergence or NaNs. Frequent ids receiving summed updates with too large a learning rate; fix with a smaller per-pair rate, subsampling, or adaptive optimisers.

Rare items with junk vectors. Too few updates; raise the minimum count, use character or attribute features (fastText), or share parameters.

Popularity dominating neighbours. Everything is near the most frequent items; negative sampling distribution too flat, or no subsampling. Tune the noise exponent and subsampling threshold.

Stale vocabulary. New words or items have no vectors; plan for periodic retraining or a fallback.

Leaking sentence boundaries. Symptom: unrelated words become neighbours because they often end and start consecutive documents. Cause: windows crossing document or sentence boundaries. Fix: build pairs within sentences or documents only.

What to monitor and tune

Training loss per epoch; nearest-neighbour spot checks on known groups; downstream task metrics; vector norms (exploding norms precede divergence). Tune dimension (100-300 for words), window (larger for topical similarity, smaller for syntactic), negatives k, the noise exponent (0.75 default), subsampling threshold t, minimum count and the learning rate schedule (word2vec decays linearly from 0.025).

A cost estimate

A corpus of 1 billion tokens, window 5, k = 5, d = 300: about 10 pairs per token and 6 dot products of 300 dimensions per pair, plus the same again for gradients — roughly 109 × 10 × 6 × 300 × 2 ≈ 3.6 × 1013 multiply-adds per epoch, minutes to hours on a multi-core CPU, which is why word2vec could be trained without accelerators. Subsampling frequent words typically removes a large share of tokens and speeds this further.

The reference for this problem

Mikolov, Sutskever, Chen, Corrado and Dean (2013) define the negative-sampling objective log σ(v′wOᵀvwI) + Σi=1..k Ewi∼Pn(w)[log σ(−v′wiᵀvwI)], report that k in the range 5-20 is useful for small training datasets and 2-5 for large ones, find that the unigram distribution raised to the 3/4 power outperformed the unigram and uniform distributions, and introduce subsampling of frequent words with discard probability 1 − √(t/f(w)). They note explicitly that, unlike NCE, negative sampling does not approximately maximise the softmax log-probability, which does not matter for their purpose.

Goal: run the solution, then vary it, break it and measure it. Python 3.12 with NumPy (the gpt_env venv); PyTorch only for the optional cross-check.

  1. Run the four blocks as one script. All assertions pass and it prints the loss before and after training and each group's nearest neighbours.
  2. Vary by hand. Redo the update in section 4 with two negatives, adding uk2 = [−0.3, 0.1], and check the new centre gradient with the batched function.
  3. Break the accumulation. Replace np.add.at(dV, C, dv) with dV[C] = dv. Does the gradient check catch it? Does training still find the groups?
  4. Reproduce the divergence. Set the learning rate back to 0.5 per pair with batches of 256 and watch the vector norm of "the" each epoch.
  5. Change the noise. Train with exponents 0 (uniform), 0.75 and 1.0 (unigram) and compare the nearest neighbours and final loss.
  6. Add subsampling. Drop each occurrence of a word with probability max(0, 1 − √(t/f)) for t = 0.01 before building pairs; how many pairs remain, and how do the neighbours change?

Each run takes a few seconds; keep the seed fixed when comparing settings so differences come from the change, not from sampling. What to look for: the overwrite bug passes the single-coordinate gradient check only when the checked index is not repeated; divergence starts with the most frequent word; uniform noise makes negatives too easy; subsampling removes most "the" pairs without hurting the groups.

Work it out

(a) A corpus has 1,000,000 tokens; "the" occurs 60,000 times and "sceptre" 20. With subsampling threshold t = 10−4, what fraction of each word's occurrences are discarded? (b) After subsampling, what are their shares in a unigram0.75 noise distribution relative to each other?

Answer 
f_the, f_sc = 60_000 / 1e6, 20 / 1e6
drop = lambda f, t=1e-4: max(0.0, 1 - math.sqrt(t / f))
assert round(drop(f_the), 3) == 0.959 and drop(f_sc) == 0.0
kept_the = 60_000 * (1 - drop(f_the))
ratio = (kept_the / 20) ** 0.75
assert round(kept_the) == 2449 and round(ratio, 1) == 36.8

(a) "the" has frequency 0.06, so it is discarded with probability 1 − √(10−4/0.06) = 1 − 0.0408 = 95.9%; "sceptre" has frequency 2 × 10−5, below t, so √(t/f) > 1 and nothing is discarded. (b) About 2,449 occurrences of "the" remain against 20 of "sceptre", a ratio of 122; raised to 3/4 it becomes 36.8: "the" is still drawn as a negative about 37 times as often as "sceptre", against 3,000 times in the raw unigram distribution. Subsampling and the 3/4 power together move a lot of training effort from frequent to rare words.
