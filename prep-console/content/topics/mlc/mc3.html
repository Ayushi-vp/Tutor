A music app wants a "listeners like you also played" feature on launch day, before anyone has trained a recommendation model. It has a table of users, each described by a few dozen numbers: hours per genre, tempo preference, time of day they listen. The simplest thing that works is to find the ten users most similar to you and recommend what they played. A clinic does the same with patients ("which past cases look most like this one?"), a fraud team with transactions, and every retrieval-augmented chatbot with text embeddings ("which ten passages are closest to this question?"). The idea is the same each time: to predict something about a new point, look at the points nearest to it.

That is k-nearest neighbours (k-NN), and as an algorithm it is almost too simple to need explaining: compute distances, take the k smallest, vote. Which is exactly why it is an interview favourite for ML coding. With no maths to hide behind, the round is all about the code: can you compute a million distances without a Python loop, find the smallest k without sorting everything, count votes without a NumPy indexing bug that silently undercounts, and answer the question "what if there are 100,000 queries and a million training points?" without allocating 800 GB?

Each of those has a cost in real systems. The two-loop distance function in this lesson took 1.3 seconds where one matrix product took 3 ms on the same data, a factor of almost 400. The fancy-indexing vote bug produces a classifier that is plausibly accurate but wrong, which is the worst kind of bug. And the textbook vectorised formula, used far from the origin in float32, returned distances off by 39 when the true values ranged from 0 to 63, quietly reordering neighbours.

This lesson starts from the idea of distance and builds to an implementation an interviewer would be happy with. It works one query through by hand with five points and three values of k, writes the naive two-loop version, derives the vectorised distance expansion and shows why it needs a clamp and centring, replaces sorting with partitioning, counts votes correctly, bounds memory with batching, and tests everything against the hand example, the naive version and PyTorch. It closes with how to choose k cheaply, the 1967 Cover and Hart bound on what nearest-neighbour classification can achieve, why high dimensions hurt, and how the same idea scales to vector databases.

Picture a map with coloured pins: red pins are customers who cancelled, blue pins customers who stayed, placed by two features such as "months as a customer" and "support tickets last quarter". A new customer arrives and you drop a white pin at their position. To guess their colour, draw a small circle around the white pin, grow it until it contains k coloured pins, and go with the majority colour inside. That is the whole algorithm. With k = 1 you copy the single nearest pin; with k = 15 you take a vote over a wider neighbourhood.

Three ideas make it work and each becomes a piece of code.

Distance. "Nearest" needs a ruler. The usual one is Euclidean distance, the straight-line distance: subtract the two points coordinate by coordinate, square the differences, add them up, take the square root. Because the square root never changes which of two distances is smaller, implementations work with the squared distance and skip the root entirely. The ruler only means something if every feature is measured on a comparable scale: if one axis is salary in pounds and the other age in years, a £1,000 difference swamps a 40-year difference, so features are usually standardised first (mean 0, standard deviation 1).

Selection. Among all the distances from the query, we need the k smallest. Sorting all n of them and taking the first k works but does more than needed. Selecting the k smallest without ordering the rest is cheaper, and NumPy has a function for exactly that.

Voting. Each of the k neighbours contributes its label; the most common label wins. Variants weight closer neighbours more heavily, or, for predicting a number rather than a class, average the neighbours' values.

The same three steps predict numbers as well as classes. To estimate a house's price, find the k most similar houses already sold and average their prices; weighting closer houses more heavily is what estate agents do informally when they say "the one next door sold for more than the one across town". In code, k-NN regression differs from k-NN classification only in the final line, an average instead of a vote, and the lesson's hand example does both.

k-NN is called a lazy learner: "training" just stores the data, and all the work happens at prediction time. That makes it the opposite of the models in mc1 and mc2, which work hard once and then predict with a cheap dot product. The cost of a lazy learner is paid on every query: compare against every stored point. Making that comparison fast, in time and memory, is what this round is really about.

Where the map picture breaks: maps have two dimensions, and real data often has hundreds. In high dimensions the circle that contains your k nearest pins is almost as large as the whole map, because random points all end up roughly the same distance apart. The lesson comes back to this, because it is the reason k-NN works brilliantly on learned embeddings and poorly on raw high-dimensional features.

1. The problem as it is usually asked

"Implement a k-NN classifier. predict must be vectorised and must work for 100,000 queries without running out of memory." Clarify before writing: which distance (Euclidean unless told otherwise)? How should ties be broken? Can labels be strings? Can k exceed the number of training points? Are the features already scaled? And how big are n (training points) and d (dimensions)? The last question decides whether brute force is even the right design, and asking it signals that you know it might not be.

2. The maths in plain words

For a query point q and a training point x, both lists of d numbers, the squared Euclidean distance is the sum over coordinates of the squared difference. For many queries against many training points, we want a whole table: row i, column j holds the squared distance from query i to training point j. Then, row by row, find the positions of the k smallest entries, look up those training points' labels, and count.

The trick that makes the table fast comes from expanding the square. The squared length of a difference equals the squared length of the first vector, plus the squared length of the second, minus twice their dot product. The two squared lengths are cheap (one number per point), and the dot products between every query and every training point are exactly what a matrix multiplication computes. So the whole table is "one matrix product plus two broadcasts", and a matrix product is the single most optimised operation in numerical computing.

3. The equations

‖a − b‖² = Σₖ (aₖ − bₖ)²  =  ‖a‖²  −  2 a·b  +  ‖b‖²

For queries A (m, d) and training points B (n, d):
D = rowsum(A²)[:, None]  −  2 A Bᵀ  +  rowsum(B²)[None, :]       shape (m, n)
      (m, 1)                 (m, n)         (1, n)

The shapes are what make it work. A Bᵀ is (m, d) @ (d, n) = (m, n). The query norms form a column (m, 1) and the training norms a row (1, n); broadcasting, NumPy's rule for combining arrays by stretching dimensions of size 1, adds the column to every column of the matrix and the row to every row. The figure shows the three pieces.

For selection, the operation is top-k: the indices of the k smallest values in each row. np.argpartition(D, k − 1, axis=1) rearranges each row's indices so that position k − 1 holds the index of the k-th smallest value, everything before it is no larger, and everything after is no smaller. Taking the first k columns gives the k nearest, in no particular order, in linear time on average.

4. A worked example by hand

Five training points with two features and a label: A(1, 1) class 0, B(3, 3) class 1, C(4, 1) class 0, D(5, 4) class 1, E(0, 2) class 0. One query, q = (3, 2).

  • Squared distances, directly. A: (1−3)² + (1−2)² = 4 + 1 = 5. B: 0 + 1 = 1. C: 1 + 1 = 2. D: 4 + 4 = 8. E: 9 + 0 = 9.
  • The same via the expansion, for B: ‖q‖² = 9 + 4 = 13, ‖B‖² = 9 + 9 = 18, q·B = 9 + 6 = 15, so 13 − 30 + 18 = 1. It agrees, and it needed only the two norms (computed once each, whatever the number of pairs) and one dot product.
  • Order. B (1), C (2), A (5), D (8), E (9).
  • k = 1. B alone: class 1.
  • k = 3. B, C, A: labels 1, 0, 0, so class 0 wins two to one.
  • k = 5. Everyone: three class 0 (A, C, E) against two class 1 (B, D): class 0.
  • Distance-weighted, k = 3, weights 1/distance (the true distance, so square roots): B gets 1/1 = 1.0 for class 1; C gets 1/√2 = 0.707 and A 1/√5 = 0.447 for class 0, total 1.154. Class 0 still wins, but narrowly: B's closeness nearly outweighs two farther votes.

Regression on the same points. Give the five training points numeric targets instead: A 10, B 30, C 14, D 40, E 8. With k = 3 the neighbours are again B, C and A, so the plain prediction is the mean (30 + 14 + 10)/3 = 18.0. The distance-weighted prediction divides the weighted sum by the total weight: (1.0·30 + 0.707·14 + 0.447·10) / (1.0 + 0.707 + 0.447) = 44.37 / 2.154 = 20.6. Weighting pulls the estimate toward B's 30 because B is the closest point.

The flip between k = 1 and k = 3 is the bias-variance trade-off in miniature. B is a single class-1 point sitting in a class-0 region. If it is noise (a mislabelled example), k = 1 is fooled by it; if it marks a genuine small pocket of class 1, k = 3 smooths it away. Only validation data can say which. The first block asserts all of these numbers.

import time
import numpy as np

# The hand-worked example: five labelled points and one query at (3, 2).
X_hand = np.array([[1.0, 1.0], [3.0, 3.0], [4.0, 1.0], [5.0, 4.0], [0.0, 2.0]])   # A B C D E
y_hand = np.array([0, 1, 0, 1, 0])
q = np.array([3.0, 2.0])

sq = ((X_hand - q) ** 2).sum(1)
assert sq.tolist() == [5.0, 1.0, 2.0, 8.0, 9.0]             # A 5, B 1, C 2, D 8, E 9
order = np.argsort(sq)
assert order.tolist() == [1, 2, 0, 3, 4]                     # B, C, A, D, E

def vote(k):
    return np.bincount(y_hand[order[:k]], minlength=2)
assert vote(1).tolist() == [0, 1]                            # k = 1: B alone says class 1
assert vote(3).tolist() == [2, 1]                            # k = 3: C and A outvote B
assert vote(5).tolist() == [3, 2]

# The expansion ||q||² - 2 q·b + ||b||² gives the same numbers: for B, 13 - 2·15 + 18 = 1.
assert q @ q == 13 and q @ X_hand[1] == 15 and X_hand[1] @ X_hand[1] == 18

# Distance-weighted vote with k = 3 and weights 1/distance.
w = 1 / np.sqrt(sq[order[:3]])                               # 1, 1/√2, 1/√5
assert np.isclose(w[0], 1.0) and np.isclose(w[1:].sum(), 1 / np.sqrt(2) + 1 / np.sqrt(5))
assert w[1:].sum() > w[0]                                    # 1.154 vs 1.0: still class 0

# Regression: targets A 10, B 30, C 14, D 40, E 8. k = 3 mean and 1/distance-weighted mean.
t_hand = np.array([10.0, 30.0, 14.0, 40.0, 8.0])
nn3 = order[:3]
assert np.isclose(t_hand[nn3].mean(), 18.0)
assert np.isclose((w * t_hand[nn3]).sum() / w.sum(), 20.597, atol=1e-3)

5. The naive implementation

The literal version has two nested loops to fill the distance table, one Python iteration per (query, training point) pair, then sorts each row in full. It is the version CS231n's well-known assignment asks students to write first, precisely so that the vectorised one can be checked against it.

def sq_dists_two_loops(A, B):
    """The first draft: one Python iteration per (query, training point) pair."""
    D = np.empty((len(A), len(B)))
    for i in range(len(A)):
        for j in range(len(B)):
            diff = A[i] - B[j]
            D[i, j] = diff @ diff
    return D

def predict_naive(X, y, Xq, k):
    D = sq_dists_two_loops(Xq, X)
    out = []
    for row in D:
        nn = np.argsort(row)[:k]                             # full sort: O(n log n) per query
        out.append(np.bincount(y[nn]).argmax())
    return np.array(out)

It does m × n iterations of interpreted Python, each creating a temporary array. For 200 queries against 2,000 points in 16 dimensions that is 400,000 iterations: 1.27 seconds in the test. The sort adds O(n log n) per query.

6. The vectorised version

def pairwise_sq_dists(A, B):
    """(m, d), (n, d) -> (m, n) squared Euclidean distances via ||a||² - 2a·b + ||b||²."""
    d = (A * A).sum(1)[:, None] - 2 * A @ B.T + (B * B).sum(1)[None, :]
    return np.maximum(d, 0.0)                 # rounding can make a zero distance slightly negative

class KNNClassifier:
    def __init__(self, k=5, weighted=False):
        self.k, self.weighted = k, weighted

    def fit(self, X, y):
        X = np.asarray(X, float)
        self.mu = X.mean(0)                                  # centre: protects the expansion
        self.X = X - self.mu
        self.classes, self.y = np.unique(y, return_inverse=True)   # any label type -> 0..C-1
        return self

    def kneighbors(self, Xq, batch=1024):
        """Indices and squared distances of the k nearest training points, nearest first."""
        Xq = np.asarray(Xq, float) - self.mu
        k = min(self.k, len(self.X))
        idx_all, d_all = [], []
        for s in range(0, len(Xq), batch):                   # memory is batch × n, not m × n
            d = pairwise_sq_dists(Xq[s:s + batch], self.X)
            idx = np.argpartition(d, k - 1, axis=1)[:, :k]   # k smallest per row in O(n), unordered
            dk = np.take_along_axis(d, idx, 1)
            o = np.argsort(dk, axis=1, kind="stable")        # order only those k
            idx_all.append(np.take_along_axis(idx, o, 1)); d_all.append(np.take_along_axis(dk, o, 1))
        return np.vstack(idx_all), np.vstack(d_all)

    def predict(self, Xq, batch=1024):
        idx, d = self.kneighbors(Xq, batch)
        w = 1.0 / (np.sqrt(d) + 1e-12) if self.weighted else np.ones(idx.shape)
        votes = np.zeros((len(idx), len(self.classes)))
        rows = np.broadcast_to(np.arange(len(idx))[:, None], idx.shape)
        np.add.at(votes, (rows, self.y[idx]), w)             # accumulates repeated (row, class) pairs
        return self.classes[votes.argmax(1)]

Walk through the decisions as you would in the interview.

Centring at fit time. The class subtracts the training mean from the training points and, at query time, from the queries. Distances do not change when you shift every point by the same amount, so this is free, and it keeps the norms small, which protects the expansion from cancellation (section 7).

The clamp. np.maximum(d, 0.0) fixes the tiny negative values that rounding can produce when a query coincides with a training point. Without it, any later square root returns NaN.

argpartition, then sort only k. argpartition finds the k nearest in linear time; take_along_axis pulls out their distances, and a sort of just those k entries orders them (useful for kneighbors, and costing O(k log k)). A stable sort keeps tied neighbours in index order, which makes results reproducible.

The vote with np.add.at. To count votes, you want votes[row, label] += weight for every neighbour. The obvious vectorised form, votes[rows, labels] += w, is wrong: when the same (row, label) pair appears several times, which is exactly when several neighbours share a class, NumPy's fancy-index assignment computes all the sums from the old values and writes them back, so the last write wins and a three-vote class gets one vote. np.add.at is the unbuffered version that applies every index, including repeats. The test shows it on three elements: v[[0, 0, 1]] += 1 gives [1, 1], while np.add.at(v, [0, 0, 1], 1) gives [2, 1].

Labels of any type. np.unique(y, return_inverse=True) maps labels such as "cat" and "dog" to integers 0..C−1 for counting, and self.classes maps the winning integer back.

Batching. The loop over query batches is what keeps memory at batch × n floats. With batch = 1024 and a million training points, each block is about 8 GB in float64, so in practice you would use float32 and a smaller batch; the point is that memory no longer grows with the number of queries.

7. Memory arithmetic you should do out loud

Interviewers often ask for the numbers, so practise the arithmetic. A float64 is 8 bytes, a float32 is 4. The full distance table for 100,000 queries against 1,000,000 training points has 10¹¹ entries: 800 GB in float64, 400 GB in float32. No machine you will be offered has that, so batching is not optional. With a batch of 1,024 queries the block is 1,024 × 10⁶ = about 10⁹ entries, 8 GB in float64 or 4 GB in float32, still large; a batch of 256 in float32 is 1 GB, comfortable on a laptop. Add the temporaries: argpartition returns an integer index array of the same shape (another 8 bytes per entry for int64), so the real peak is two to three times the distance block. The training set itself is n × d: a million 128-dimensional points are 1 GB in float64. Compute, unlike memory, does not shrink with batching: 10¹¹ distances in 128 dimensions is about 2.6 × 10¹³ floating-point operations, roughly a minute at a few hundred gigaflops on a good CPU. That is the moment to say "beyond this, approximate search".

8. Numerical stability traps

Trap 1: catastrophic cancellation. The expansion computes a small number (a distance) as the difference of large numbers (the norms). When two nearly equal floating-point numbers are subtracted, the leading digits cancel and only the rounding error is left; this is catastrophic cancellation. In the test, the same 200 points were shifted by 10,000 in each coordinate and stored in float32 (about 7 significant digits). Their squared norms are about 2 × 10⁸, so float32 can only represent them to within about 16, while the true squared distances lie between 0 and 63. The computed distances were off by up to 39.4: several distinct distances came out as exactly 0 or 16. After centring, the maximum error was 0.013, and what remains is the rounding of the shifted inputs themselves when they were stored in float32. Centring costs one subtraction and removes the problem; using float64 makes it 10⁹ times smaller but does not remove it.

Trap 2: negative squared distances. Even with centring, a query identical to a training point can come out as −2 × 10⁻¹⁶. Clamp at zero.

Trap 3: zero distance in weighted votes. Weights of 1/distance divide by zero when the query is a training point. The + 1e-12 in the code keeps the weight finite and enormous, which correctly lets an exact match dominate. Some implementations instead return the matching point's label directly.

Trap 4: dtype of the distance table. float32 halves memory and doubles BLAS speed, which matters at scale, but makes traps 1 and 2 much worse. A common production compromise is float32 storage with centred, normalised vectors, plus an exact float64 re-ranking of the top candidates when exact order matters.

The test block checks, in order: the class reproduces the hand-worked votes for k = 1, 3 and 5 and the weighted vote, and kneighbors returns B, C, A, D, E with squared distances 1, 2, 5, 8, 9; the vectorised distance table equals the two-loop one; the classifier equals the naive sort-based one on 50 queries; changing the batch size from 16 to 1,000 changes nothing; string labels round-trip; the fancy-indexing pitfall behaves as described; the cancellation trap and its fix; and, if PyTorch is installed, torch.cdist plus torch.topk picks the same neighbour sets. Why compare neighbour sets with PyTorch rather than ordered lists? Because when two training points are at exactly the same distance, different libraries may order them differently, and both answers are correct. A test that demands one order is testing the tie-break, not the algorithm.

Edge cases worth naming

A few inputs break careless implementations, and mentioning them unprompted is a cheap way to show care. If k exceeds the number of training points, argpartition raises an error; the class clamps k to n, which turns the prediction into the overall majority class, and a stricter design would raise a clear error instead. k = 0 or an empty training set should be rejected at fit time. Duplicate training points are legal and simply vote twice, which is correct if they are genuine repeated observations and a data bug if they are copies. A NaN in any feature makes every distance involving that point NaN, and NaN compares as neither smaller nor larger than anything, so partition results become arbitrary; validate inputs, or impute missing values, before fitting. A query with a different number of features than the training data fails inside the matrix product with a shape error, which is the behaviour you want, but a clear message is kinder.

10. The one-loop middle ground

Between two loops and none there is a version that loops over queries but vectorises over training points: for each query, ((X − q) ** 2).sum(1) computes its distances to all training points in one array operation. It does not need the expansion, so it has no cancellation problem, and its memory is only n × d per query. It is a sensible answer when the interviewer is worried about numerical accuracy, and a useful stepping stone when explaining the no-loop version. It is slower than the matrix-product version because it does m separate passes over the training data instead of one cache-friendly BLAS call, and because subtracting and squaring creates an n × d temporary per query. In the lab you time all three; the usual order is two loops slowest by a wide margin, one loop in between, no loops fastest.

11. Complexity

Fitting is O(nd) for the centring and O(nd) memory to store the data. Predicting m queries costs O(mnd) for the distance matrix (one BLAS matrix product, fast in practice), O(mn) for the partition, and O(mk log k) to order the selected neighbours. Memory is O(batch × n) per block. A full argsort would make the selection O(mn log n). Tree indexes (KD-trees, ball trees) reduce query time toward O(log n) in low dimensions, but scikit-learn's own documentation switches to brute force automatically when d > 15, when k ≥ n/2, or for sparse input, because trees stop pruning anything in high dimensions.

12. Choosing k without refitting anything

The drill below implements leave-one-out cross-validation for every odd k from 1 to 25 using a single training-to-training distance matrix and a single sort per row: set the diagonal to infinity so no point votes for itself, sort once, and the first k columns serve every k. On two overlapping Gaussian classes it scored 0.750 at k = 1, rising to about 0.80-0.82 for larger k. Those numbers have a ceiling: the two classes have means about 2.83 apart and a spread of 1.5, so even the ideal classifier makes about 17% errors (accuracy about 0.83). That irreducible error is the Bayes error, and it is the yardstick the next section uses.

13. Talking through the design

A strong answer runs: the algorithm in one sentence; squared Euclidean distance after standardising features; the expansion with shapes, and the clamp; argpartition for top-k with the complexity difference; add.at for votes with the reason; batching for memory with a back-of-envelope number; then tests. If the interviewer pushes on scale, move to approximate indexes; if on accuracy, to choosing k and to distance concentration in high dimensions. Every step is short, and every one is a place where a weaker answer has a bug.

What nearest neighbours can achieve: Cover and Hart

In 1967 Thomas Cover and Peter Hart proved the result that made the nearest-neighbour rule respectable. With unlimited data, the error rate R of the 1-NN rule satisfies R* ≤ R ≤ R*(2 − M·R*/(M − 1)), where R* is the Bayes error and M the number of classes. For two classes that is R ≤ 2R*(1 − R*), and in every case it is less than twice the Bayes error. Their summary: half the classification information in an infinite sample set is contained in the nearest neighbour. Plugging in this lesson's overlapping Gaussians, with R* ≈ 0.173, the bound is 2 × 0.173 × 0.827 ≈ 0.286, and leave-one-out 1-NN measured 0.25. Larger k closes the gap toward R* as data grows, provided k grows too but more slowly than n. The caution is that this is a large-sample result: it says nothing about how much data you need, and in high dimensions the answer is "a great deal".

Distance concentration and the curse of dimensionality

Draw 1,000 points uniformly in a unit cube and measure their distances from a random query. In 2 dimensions the nearest was 0.01 away and the farthest 1.33, a ratio of 0.007. In 10 dimensions the ratio was 0.22, in 100 it was 0.72, and in 1,000 it was 0.90 (nearest 12.2, farthest 13.6). As dimensions are added, each one contributes an independent random amount to the squared distance, and sums of many independent terms concentrate around their mean. When the nearest point is 90% as far away as the farthest, "nearest" is close to arbitrary, small amounts of noise reorder neighbours, and k-NN's predictions degrade toward the base rate. Real data escapes this when it lies near a low-dimensional structure: images of faces, or sentence embeddings trained so that paraphrases are close. That is why k-NN over learned embeddings is the backbone of retrieval while k-NN over raw 1,000-dimensional sparse features usually disappoints.

Cosine similarity and normalised vectors

Embedding search usually ranks by cosine similarity, the dot product of two vectors divided by their lengths. If every vector is first scaled to length 1, then ‖a − b‖² = 2 − 2 cos(a, b), so Euclidean and cosine give the same ranking and the distance code above works unchanged; even simpler, the top-k by dot product is just argpartition on −A Bᵀ. Normalising once at indexing time is cheaper than dividing by norms on every query.

Trees, and why they fail in high dimensions

A KD-tree recursively splits space on one coordinate at a time; a query descends to its leaf, then backtracks into neighbouring cells only if they could contain something closer than the best found so far. In 2 or 3 dimensions that pruning removes almost everything and queries take roughly logarithmic time. In 50 dimensions the sphere around the current best neighbour intersects almost every cell, nothing is pruned, and the tree is slower than brute force because of its overhead. Ball trees prune with hyperspheres instead of boxes and last a little longer. Beyond a few tens of dimensions, exact search means brute force, and speed means giving up exactness.

Approximate nearest neighbours

Vector databases trade a little recall for orders of magnitude in speed. IVF (inverted file) indexes cluster the vectors with k-means (mc4) and, for each query, search only the few clusters whose centres are nearest; the number searched, nprobe, sets the recall-speed trade-off. HNSW builds a layered graph in which each vector links to near neighbours, and a query walks greedily through it. Product quantisation compresses each vector into a few bytes by quantising sub-vectors separately, so that a billion vectors fit in memory. The engineering habit that matters is measurement: compute exact top-k on a sample of queries with the brute-force code from this lesson, and report recall@k of the approximate index against it.

Weighted k-NN as a kernel smoother

Distance weighting generalises into kernel regression: instead of the k nearest with weights 1/distance, give every training point a weight K(distance / h) from a smooth kernel such as a Gaussian, with bandwidth h. The prediction is the weighted average of the targets (the Nadaraya-Watson estimator). k-NN is the special case of a kernel whose width adapts to the local density: in crowded regions the k nearest are close, in sparse regions they are far. That adaptivity is why k-NN often behaves better than a fixed-bandwidth kernel, and it gives you a precise way to talk about k: it is a bandwidth, and like every bandwidth it trades bias for variance.

Learned distances

Euclidean distance on standardised features treats every feature as equally important, which is rarely true. Metric learning methods learn a linear transform L so that distances ‖L(a − b)‖ put same-class points close together and different-class points far apart; contrastive training of neural encoders does the same thing non-linearly and is how modern embeddings are made. Once the transform is learned, the k-NN code is unchanged: transform the vectors, then run it.

Where k-NN runs in production

Exact k-NN is common at small scale: anomaly scoring (distance to the k-th neighbour), deduplication, "similar items" lists precomputed nightly, and evaluation ground truth for approximate indexes. At large scale the same idea runs as approximate search inside vector databases and retrieval-augmented generation: embed the query, find the nearest passages, hand them to a language model. The code in this lesson is the reference those systems are tested against.

Failure modes and symptoms

Unscaled features. Symptom: neighbours that agree on one feature and differ wildly on all others; accuracy no better than a single-feature rule. Cause: a feature in large units dominating the distance. Fix: standardise with statistics computed on the training set and stored with the model, so queries are scaled identically.

Memory blow-ups. Symptom: a prediction job that worked in testing is killed by the operating system in production. Cause: the full m × n distance matrix; 50,000 queries against 2 million points is 800 GB in float64. Fix: batch queries, keep only the running top-k, use float32, and size batches from available memory rather than hard-coding them.

Latency growing with the data. Symptom: p99 latency rises steadily as the catalogue grows. Cause: brute force is linear in n. Fix: an approximate index, re-tuned as the collection grows, with recall monitored.

Stale or poisoned neighbours. Because the model is the data, a bad or duplicated training point affects every query near it, immediately. Deduplicate, keep data lineage, and be able to remove a point and have it disappear from results at once, which is easy for brute force and harder for some indexes.

Ties and determinism. Results that change between runs or machines usually come from unstable sorting of tied distances or multi-threaded BLAS summing in different orders. Use a stable sort and break ties by index when reproducibility matters, for example in tests or audits.

A cost estimate for brute force at scale

Suppose a support chatbot retrieves from 5 million passage embeddings of dimension 768 in float32. The index is 5 × 10⁶ × 768 × 4 bytes, about 15 GB, so it fits in one large server's memory. One query is 5 × 10⁶ × 768 ≈ 3.8 × 10⁹ multiply-adds; at an effective 100 gigaflops on a CPU that is about 40-80 ms, acceptable for a few queries a second and hopeless for a thousand. On a GPU with a matrix-multiply throughput in the tens of teraflops, batches of queries become cheap, which is why exact search on GPUs remains practical into the tens of millions of vectors. Past that, or at high query rates on CPUs, approximate indexes are the answer, and the brute-force code becomes the evaluation harness.

What to monitor

Query latency percentiles and throughput; memory per batch; the distribution of the distance to the k-th neighbour (if it rises, queries are drifting away from the training data, an early warning of distribution shift); and, for approximate search, recall@k against exact search on a fixed sample, measured after every index rebuild.

The reference for this problem

The original paper is Cover and Hart's "Nearest Neighbor Pattern Classification", IEEE Transactions on Information Theory, volume IT-13, number 1, January 1967, pages 21-27, hosted on Stanford's Information Systems Laboratory site. Its abstract states the bound used in the deep section: in the M-category case R* ≤ R ≤ R*(2 − MR*/(M − 1)), these bounds are the tightest possible, and "for any number of categories, the probability of error of the nearest neighbor rule is bounded above by twice the Bayes probability of error", which the authors summarise as half the classification information in an infinite sample set being contained in the nearest neighbour. For the engineering side, scikit-learn's nearest-neighbours guide documents when its 'auto' setting gives up on trees and uses brute force: sparse input, a precomputed metric, more than 15 dimensions, or k of at least half the training set.

Work it out

Choose k by leave-one-out cross-validation without refitting anything. Evaluate every k in [1, 3, 5, …, 25] using a single distance computation and a single sort.

Answer

Compute the training-to-training distance matrix, set the diagonal to infinity so a point cannot vote for itself, and sort each row once. The first k columns of that order are the neighbours for every k at once.

def loo_accuracy(X, y, ks):
    d = pairwise_sq_dists(X, X)
    np.fill_diagonal(d, np.inf)                   # leave one out: never your own neighbour
    order = np.argsort(d, axis=1)                  # one sort serves every k
    C = y.max() + 1
    acc = {}
    for k in ks:
        nbr = y[order[:, :k]]                      # (n, k) neighbour labels
        votes = np.stack([(nbr == c).sum(1) for c in range(C)], axis=1)
        acc[k] = float((votes.argmax(1) == y).mean())
    return acc

Xn = np.vstack([rng.normal(0, 1.5, (150, 2)), rng.normal(2, 1.5, (150, 2))])   # overlapping classes
yn = np.array([0] * 150 + [1] * 150)
acc = loo_accuracy(Xn, yn, range(1, 26, 2))
assert max(acc, key=acc.get) > 1                  # with overlapping classes, k = 1 overfits
if __name__ == "__main__":
    print({k: round(v, 3) for k, v in acc.items()})

On these overlapping classes accuracy rises from 0.75 at k = 1 to about 0.80-0.82 by k in the twenties, approaching the Bayes ceiling of about 0.83; with more separated or more complex classes it peaks and then falls as neighbourhoods start to include the other class, the bias-variance trade-off in one curve. The cost is one O(n²d) distance matrix and one O(n² log n) sort, instead of a model fit per fold per k.
