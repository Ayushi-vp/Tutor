A streaming service knows which of its million users watched or rated which of its fifty thousand titles. Almost all of that table is empty: a typical user has rated a few dozen titles. The business question is how to fill in the blanks — which unseen titles would this user rate highly? — and in 2006 Netflix offered a million dollars for a 10% improvement on its own system's error. The techniques that won, and that still sit inside many recommender systems, came from treating the user-item matrix as approximately low-rank: each user and each item is a short vector of hidden "taste" coordinates, and a rating is roughly the dot product of the two.

Koren, Bell and Volinsky — members of the winning team — summarised the approach in a 2009 article: fit user and item factor vectors to the observed ratings only, regularise them so they do not memorise, add biases for generous users and popular items, and optimise either by stochastic gradient descent, as popularised by Simon Funk's blog post during the competition, or by alternating least squares, which turns the non-convex problem into a sequence of small exact ridge regressions. A year earlier, Hu, Koren and Volinsky had adapted the method to implicit feedback — clicks, plays, purchases — where there are no ratings, only behaviour with varying confidence, and showed how to fit over every user-item pair without paying for every pair.

The interview problem asks you to build this from scratch: the model with biases, an SGD trainer, an ALS trainer, held-out evaluation, and an explanation of regularisation and implicit feedback. This lesson works one SGD step and one ALS solve by hand; implements both optimisers on synthetic ratings generated from a known rank-3 model with biases and noise; shows test RMSE falling from 1.104 for the global mean to 0.937 with biases and 0.509-0.511 with factors, while the ALS objective decreases every sweep; shows that 20 factors with no regularisation reach a training RMSE of 0.001 and a test RMSE worse than the mean; and checks the implicit-feedback ALS shortcut against a direct weighted solve.

The analogy: a taste profile and a flavour profile

A wine shop wants to predict how much each customer will like each bottle. The owner describes every bottle by a few flavour scores — how fruity, how oaky, how tannic — and every customer by how much they like each of those flavours. A customer's predicted enjoyment is the sum over flavours of (how much the bottle has it) × (how much the customer likes it): a dot product. Some customers are simply enthusiastic and score everything high; some bottles are just good and everyone likes them more; so the owner also notes a personal offset for each customer and each bottle. Nobody hands the owner the flavour scores. Instead the owner guesses them, compares predictions with the ratings customers actually gave, and adjusts: when a customer liked a bottle more than predicted, nudge the customer's profile towards that bottle's flavours and the bottle's flavours towards that customer's tastes.

The flavour scores are item factors qi, the tastes are user factors pu, the offsets are biases bu and bi, the shop's average rating is μ, and nudging after each rating is SGD. An alternative routine — fix every bottle's flavours, then work out each customer's best tastes exactly; then fix the tastes and work out each bottle's flavours exactly — is alternating least squares.

The analogy breaks where it matters most: the owner chose the flavour dimensions, but the model does not. Its factors are whatever directions best explain the ratings, and they rarely correspond neatly to named attributes. They are useful because they predict, not because they explain.

Where this sits in a recommender

A production recommender usually has stages: candidate generation narrows millions of items to a few hundred per user, ranking scores those candidates with a richer model, and re-ranking adds business rules and diversity. Matrix factorisation is a classic candidate generator: once user and item factors exist, the best candidates for a user are the items with the largest dot products, which approximate-nearest-neighbour search can find quickly. The learned factors are also reused as features — user and item embeddings — by the ranking model. That is why the method survives in systems whose final ranking is done by much larger models: it is cheap, it captures collaborative signal well, and its output is a vector space.

What the interviewer is checking

That you fit observed entries only (not an SVD of a filled matrix); that you include biases and regularisation and can say why; that the SGD update uses old values and the right signs; that you can derive the ALS step as ridge regression; that you evaluate on held-out ratings against sensible baselines; and that you know how implicit feedback changes the objective and how ALS stays affordable on it.

2. The maths in plain words

Predict each rating as the overall average, plus how much this user tends to rate above or below average, plus how much this item tends to be rated above or below average, plus the dot product of the user's and the item's factor vectors. Choose all these numbers to make predictions close to the observed ratings, while penalising large values so they generalise.

SGD goes through the observed ratings in random order. For each, compute the error (actual minus predicted). If positive, the prediction was too low, so raise the user's and item's biases a little, move the user's factors a little in the direction of the item's factors, and the item's towards the user's — each step shrunk slightly towards zero by the regularisation.

ALS notices that if all item factors and item biases were known, each user's parameters would be an ordinary ridge regression: the "features" are the factor vectors of the items the user rated (plus a constant for the bias), and the "targets" are the ratings minus the global mean and the item biases. Ridge regression has a closed-form solution, so solve it for every user; then swap roles and solve for every item; repeat.

objective:  min Σ_(u,i)∈observed (r_ui − r̂_ui)² + λ(‖p_u‖² + ‖q_i‖² + b_u² + b_i²)

            p_u ← p_u + γ(e q_i − λ p_u)      q_i ← q_i + γ(e p_u_old − λ q_i)

4. A worked example by hand

Biases. Koren et al.'s example: the average rating over all movies is μ = 3.7; Titanic is rated 0.5 above average; Joe rates 0.3 below average. Baseline estimate for Joe on Titanic: 3.7 + 0.5 − 0.3 = 3.9, before any interaction term.

One SGD step. k = 1, μ = 3, bu = bi = 0, pu = 0.5, qi = 0.4, observed r = 4, learning rate γ = 0.1, λ = 0.1.

  • Prediction 3 + 0 + 0 + 0.5 × 0.4 = 3.2; error e = 0.8.
  • Biases: bu = 0 + 0.1(0.8 − 0) = 0.08, bi = 0.08.
  • Factors, both from the old values: pu = 0.5 + 0.1(0.8 × 0.4 − 0.1 × 0.5) = 0.5 + 0.027 = 0.527; qi = 0.4 + 0.1(0.8 × 0.5 − 0.1 × 0.4) = 0.4 + 0.036 = 0.436.
  • New prediction 3 + 0.16 + 0.527 × 0.436 = 3.390: the error fell from 0.8 to 0.61 in one step.

One ALS solve. A user rated two items whose (fixed) factors are q1 = 1 and q2 = 2 (k = 1, no bias to keep it short), with targets r − μ − bi of 1 and 3, and λ = 1. Ridge: p = (q1² + q2² + λ)⁻¹(q1·1 + q2·3) = (1 + 4 + 1)⁻¹(1 + 6) = 7/6 ≈ 1.167. Without regularisation it would be 7/5 = 1.4; λ shrinks it towards zero.

import numpy as np

# Hand-worked numbers. Baseline predictor from the paper's example: mu + b_i + b_u.
assert round(3.7 + 0.5 + (-0.3), 1) == 3.9                     # Joe rating Titanic

# One SGD step on one rating, k = 1: mu = 3, b_u = b_i = 0, p_u = 0.5, q_i = 0.4, r = 4.
mu, bu, bi, pu, qi, r, g, lam = 3.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.5, 0.4, 4.0, 0.1, 0.1
pred = mu + bu + bi + pu * qi                                  # 3.2
e = r - pred                                                   # 0.8
bu, bi = bu + g * (e - lam * bu), bi + g * (e - lam * bi)      # 0.08 each
pu, qi = pu + g * (e * qi - lam * pu), qi + g * (e * pu - lam * qi)   # both from the OLD values
assert np.isclose(e, 0.8) and np.allclose([bu, bi, pu, qi], [0.08, 0.08, 0.527, 0.436])
assert np.isclose(mu + bu + bi + pu * qi, 3.389772)            # error falls from 0.8 to 0.61

# One ALS user update by hand: item factors fixed (k = 1), targets are r - mu - b_i.
Q_u = np.array([[1.0], [2.0]]); y = np.array([1.0, 3.0]); lam_als = 1.0
p_new = np.linalg.solve(Q_u.T @ Q_u + lam_als * np.eye(1), Q_u.T @ y)
assert np.isclose(p_new[0], 7 / 6)                             # (1*1 + 2*3) / (1 + 4 + 1)

5. The naive implementation: data and SGD

make_ratings creates 80 users and 60 items with true rank-3 factors, user and item biases and a mean of 3.5, observes 30% of cells at random and adds noise of standard deviation 0.3 — so the best achievable test RMSE is about 0.3, and we know the right k. sgd_epoch is the textbook loop: visit observed ratings in random order, compute the error, apply the four updates, copying pu first so qi is updated with the old value. It is "naive" in the sense of one rating at a time in Python, which is exactly how the method is usually written; vectorising SGD changes the algorithm.

def make_ratings(n_users=80, n_items=60, k=3, density=0.3, noise=0.3, seed=0):
    """Synthetic explicit ratings: mu + user bias + item bias + rank-k interaction + noise,
    observed for a random 30% of (user, item) cells."""
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    P, Q = r.normal(scale=0.7, size=(n_users, k)), r.normal(scale=0.7, size=(n_items, k))
    bu, bi = r.normal(scale=0.4, size=n_users), r.normal(scale=0.4, size=n_items)
    full = 3.5 + bu[:, None] + bi[None, :] + P @ Q.T
    mask = r.random((n_users, n_items)) < density
    users, items = np.nonzero(mask)
    ratings = full[users, items] + r.normal(scale=noise, size=len(users))
    return users, items, ratings, n_users, n_items

def rmse(u, i, r, model):
    return float(np.sqrt(np.mean((r - predict(model, u, i)) ** 2)))

def predict(m, u, i):
    return m["mu"] + m["bu"][u] + m["bi"][i] + (m["P"][u] * m["Q"][i]).sum(axis=-1)

def init_model(nu, ni, k, mu, seed=1):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    return {"mu": mu, "bu": np.zeros(nu), "bi": np.zeros(ni),
            "P": r.normal(scale=0.1, size=(nu, k)), "Q": r.normal(scale=0.1, size=(ni, k))}

def sgd_epoch(m, users, items, ratings, lr=0.02, lam=0.05, rng=None):
    """Funk-style SGD, one rating at a time, in a random order (the Koren et al. update rules)."""
    order = rng.permutation(len(ratings)) if rng is not None else range(len(ratings))
    for t in order:
        u, i, r = users[t], items[t], ratings[t]
        e = r - (m["mu"] + m["bu"][u] + m["bi"][i] + m["P"][u] @ m["Q"][i])
        m["bu"][u] += lr * (e - lam * m["bu"][u])
        m["bi"][i] += lr * (e - lam * m["bi"][i])
        pu = m["P"][u].copy()
        m["P"][u] += lr * (e * m["Q"][i] - lam * pu)
        m["Q"][i] += lr * (e * pu - lam * m["Q"][i])

6. ALS and the implicit variant

als_sweep does one user half-step and one item half-step. For each user it gathers the indices of their ratings (via a sort and searchsorted so each user's slice is found in O(1) after one O(n log n) sort), builds a design matrix of the rated items' factors plus a column of ones for the bias, forms the targets r − μ − bi, and solves the (k + 1) × (k + 1) ridge system with np.linalg.solve (never an explicit inverse). Items are handled symmetrically. Every solve is independent of the others in its half-step — the property that makes ALS parallel. objective computes the full regularised loss so tests can confirm it never increases.

implicit_user_factor implements the Hu-Koren-Volinsky user step for one user over all items: preferences are 1 for touched items and 0 otherwise, confidences 1 + α·count. Instead of forming the n × n diagonal Cu, it computes YᵀY (shared by every user) and adds Yᵀ(Cu − I)Y using only the nu touched rows; the right-hand side YᵀCup(u) likewise only involves touched items, because p is zero elsewhere.

def als_sweep(m, users, items, ratings, lam=1.0):
    """One ALS sweep with biases. Fix items: each user's [p_u, b_u] is a ridge regression on the items
    they rated, targets r - mu - b_i. Then fix users and solve each item the same way."""
    k = m["P"].shape[1]
    for side in ("user", "item"):
        own, other = (users, items) if side == "user" else (items, users)
        F_other = m["Q"] if side == "user" else m["P"]
        b_other = m["bi"] if side == "user" else m["bu"]
        n_own = m["P"].shape[0] if side == "user" else m["Q"].shape[0]
        order = np.argsort(own, kind="stable")
        bounds = np.searchsorted(own[order], np.arange(n_own + 1))
        for a in range(n_own):
            idx = order[bounds[a]:bounds[a + 1]]
            if len(idx) == 0:
                continue
            X = np.hstack([F_other[other[idx]], np.ones((len(idx), 1))])      # (n_a, k + 1)
            y = ratings[idx] - m["mu"] - b_other[other[idx]]
            w = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X + lam * np.eye(k + 1), X.T @ y)       # closed-form ridge
            if side == "user":
                m["P"][a], m["bu"][a] = w[:k], w[k]
            else:
                m["Q"][a], m["bi"][a] = w[:k], w[k]

def objective(m, users, items, ratings, lam):
    e = ratings - predict(m, users, items)
    return float((e ** 2).sum() + lam * ((m["P"] ** 2).sum() + (m["Q"] ** 2).sum()
                                         + (m["bu"] ** 2).sum() + (m["bi"] ** 2).sum()))

def implicit_user_factor(Y, counts_u, alpha=40.0, lam=0.1):
    """Implicit-feedback ALS for one user (Hu, Koren & Volinsky): preference p = 1[count > 0],
    confidence c = 1 + alpha * count, over ALL items. Uses Y^T C Y = Y^T Y + Y^T (C - I) Y, so only
    the items the user touched cost anything beyond the shared Y^T Y."""
    nz = np.nonzero(counts_u)[0]
    YtY = Y.T @ Y
    Ynz, c = Y[nz], 1.0 + alpha * counts_u[nz]
    A = YtY + (Ynz.T * (c - 1.0)) @ Ynz + lam * np.eye(Y.shape[1])
    b = (Ynz.T * c) @ np.ones(len(nz))                      # Y^T C p, and p = 1 only where count > 0
    return np.linalg.solve(A, b)

7. Testing it

Six checks on 1,441 observed ratings split 80/20 (1,152 train, 289 test). (1) Predicting the training mean gives a test RMSE of 1.104; ALS with zero factors (biases only) gives 0.937. (2) SGD with biases and k = 3 for 60 epochs (γ = 0.02, λ = 0.05) reaches 0.509. (3) ALS with λ = 2 decreases the regularised objective at every one of 15 sweeps and reaches 0.511 — within a few thousandths of SGD on this data. (4) With k = 20 and λ = 0.001, ALS drives training RMSE to 0.001 while test RMSE rises to 1.118, worse than the global mean: the model memorised the training ratings. (5) For a user who touched 3 of 30 items, the YᵀY shortcut gives exactly the same factor vector as solving the full weighted ridge problem with the 30 × 30 confidence matrix. (6) PyTorch autograd on the per-rating loss ½e² + ½λ(‖p‖² + ‖q‖² + bu² + bi²) gives gradients whose negatives are exactly the SGD update directions.

# 1. Baselines: global mean, then mean plus user and item biases (from an ALS with k = 0 factors).

rmse_mean = rmse(*te, base)
bias_only = {k_: (v.copy() if isinstance(v, np.ndarray) else v) for k_, v in base.items()}

rmse_bias = rmse(*te, bias_only)
assert rmse_bias < rmse_mean

# 2. SGD with biases and k = 3 factors beats the bias baseline on held-out ratings.
m_sgd = init_model(nu, ni, 3, mu)
for _ in range(60):
    sgd_epoch(m_sgd, *tr, lr=0.02, lam=0.05, rng=rng)

assert rmse_sgd < rmse_bias - 0.1

# 3. ALS: the regularised objective never increases between sweeps, and it reaches similar test RMSE.
m_als = init_model(nu, ni, 3, mu)
objs = []
for _ in range(15):
    als_sweep(m_als, *tr, lam=2.0)
    objs.append(objective(m_als, *tr, lam=2.0))
assert all(b <= a + 1e-9 for a, b in zip(objs, objs[1:]))

assert rmse_als < rmse_bias - 0.1 and abs(rmse_als - rmse_sgd) < 0.15

# 4. Regularisation matters: with lambda near 0 and many factors, train RMSE drops and test RMSE rises.
m_over = init_model(nu, ni, 20, mu)

r_imp = np.random.default_rng(5)

8. Numerical and practical traps

Updating qi with the new pu: a subtle inconsistency; copy first. Learning rate: too large and SGD diverges on users or items with many ratings; decay it over epochs. Zero initialisation of factors: with p = q = 0, the factor gradients are e·0 = 0 and the factors never move; initialise small random values. Forgetting to subtract μ: the factors then waste capacity on the mean. Solving with inv: use solve for stability. Data leakage: compute μ and any normalisation on the training split only.

9. Edge cases worth naming

Users or items with no training ratings: ALS skips them, leaving initial values; predict with μ (+ the other side's bias). A user with one rating: the ridge solve is still well defined because of λ. Ratings on a bounded scale: clip predictions to the scale for evaluation. Duplicate (user, item) pairs: keep the latest or average before training. Very popular items: dominate SGD updates; per-item learning rates or ALS help.

10. Complexity

SGD: O(N·k) per epoch for N observed ratings. ALS: for each user, building XᵀX costs O(nu·k²) and solving O(k³), so a sweep is O(N·k² + (users + items)·k³) — linear in data for small k, and parallel across users and items. Implicit ALS with the YᵀY trick: O(f²·N + f³·users) for the user half-step, as Hu et al. state, instead of O(f²·n) per user. Memory: (users + items)·(k + 1) parameters.

11. Talking through the design

Write the model with biases and the loss on observed entries with λ, give the SGD update and the ALS ridge step, compare them (learning rate versus closed form, sequential versus parallel, sparse explicit versus dense implicit), evaluate on held-out data against the mean and bias baselines, show what regularisation prevents, and describe implicit feedback with confidence weights and the YᵀY trick. Close with cold start and why RMSE is not the whole story.

Why fitting only the observed entries is the key move

Early approaches imputed the missing ratings (with zeros or averages) and decomposed the completed matrix with SVD. Two problems follow. Computationally, the completed matrix is dense and enormous. Statistically, the imputed values dominate, because they vastly outnumber real ratings, so the factorisation mostly fits guesses. Fitting only the observed cells with regularisation — what Koren et al. describe as modelling directly the observed ratings while avoiding overfitting through a regularised model — avoids both. It also makes the problem non-convex (the product p·q is bilinear), which is why the choice of optimiser matters and why regularisation and initialisation influence the solution found.

The probabilistic view

The regularised squared loss is the negative log posterior of a simple probabilistic model: ratings are Gaussian around μ + bu + bi + pu·qi, and all parameters have zero-mean Gaussian priors. λ is the ratio of the noise variance to the prior variance. This is Salakhutdinov and Mnih's probabilistic matrix factorisation, which Koren et al. cite as a probabilistic foundation for regularisation. It explains why larger k needs larger λ (more parameters, each needs a tighter prior to avoid fitting noise), and it opens Bayesian variants that average over factors instead of picking one point estimate.

ALS as block coordinate descent

ALS is block coordinate descent on the bilinear objective: holding one block fixed makes the problem in the other block a sum of independent ridge regressions, each solved exactly. Exact minimisation over a block can only lower (or keep) the objective, which is why the lesson's test of monotone decrease is a real correctness check — a bug in the design matrix or the targets usually breaks it. ALS converges to a stationary point, not necessarily the global minimum, and typically needs only 10-20 sweeps (Hu et al. report about 10).

Implicit feedback: why all pairs, and how it stays cheap

With only positive signals, a model fitted on observed pairs could predict 1 everywhere and be perfect. Implicit models therefore treat every unobserved pair as a weak negative (preference 0 with confidence 1) and every observed pair as a positive with confidence growing with the amount of interaction (Hu et al. used c = 1 + α·r with α = 40, and also suggest c = 1 + α·log(1 + r/ε)). The objective now has users × items terms — billions — so SGD over it is impractical. ALS rescues it: each user's normal equations involve YᵀCuY, which splits into YᵀY (identical for everyone, computed once) plus a correction involving only the items the user touched. The work becomes linear in the number of interactions, the same as explicit ALS. The same paper notes that the solution can be rewritten as a weighted sum of item-item similarities, giving explanations ("recommended because you watched…").

Beyond the basic model

Koren et al. extend the model with implicit signals inside an explicit model (a user represented also by the normalised sum of factor vectors of items they interacted with, |N(u)|−1/2 Σ xi, which helps users with few ratings), user attributes, temporal dynamics (biases and user factors as functions of time, because tastes and item popularity drift), and confidence weights for explicit ratings. Modern systems often replace the dot-product model with neural two-tower models trained on implicit data with sampled negatives, but the bias terms, the regularisation logic and the treatment of implicit feedback carry over directly.

Initialisation and symmetry

The factor model is symmetric under rotations: replacing pu by Rpu and qi by Rqi for any orthogonal R leaves every prediction unchanged, so individual factor dimensions have no fixed meaning and differ between training runs. Initialising at exactly zero is a saddle point where the factor gradients vanish; small random values break the symmetry and let both optimisers start moving. This is also why comparing factor vectors across separately trained models requires aligning them first.

Ranking versus rating prediction

The Netflix Prize measured RMSE on ratings, but a recommender shows a ranked list of items the user has not seen. Optimising squared error on observed ratings does not directly optimise that ranking, and missing ratings are not random — people rate what they chose to watch, so observed ratings skew positive. For production, evaluate with ranking metrics on held-out interactions over the full catalogue (or carefully sampled candidates), and consider objectives such as implicit ALS or pairwise ranking losses that train on the decision the system actually makes.

Where it runs

Matrix factorisation — especially implicit-feedback ALS — remains a standard component of recommender systems: as a candidate generator producing a few hundred items per user from millions, as a source of user and item embeddings for downstream ranking models, and as a strong baseline that newer models must beat. Distributed ALS implementations (for example in Spark MLlib) scale it to billions of interactions by solving users and items in parallel.

Failure modes and symptoms

Popularity bias. Symptom: everyone gets the same popular items. Cause: biases and factors dominated by popular items, implicit confidence too high for heavy items. Fix: tune α and λ, down-weight popular items, add diversity in re-ranking.

Cold start. Symptom: new users get generic recommendations, new items are never shown. Cause: no factors. Fix: fold-in with one ALS step after a few interactions, side information, exploration slots for new items.

Overfitting. Symptom: offline training metrics excellent, online engagement flat. Cause: λ too small or k too large; leakage between train and evaluation (random splits that put a user's future in training). Fix: time-based splits, validation-tuned λ.

Stale factors. Symptom: recommendations ignore recent behaviour. Cause: factors retrained infrequently. Fix: frequent retraining, fold-in updates for users between full retrains.

Feedback loops. Symptom: the system keeps recommending what it recommended. Cause: implicit training data are produced by the recommender's own exposure. Fix: log exposures, weight or correct for position and exposure, reserve exploration traffic.

What to monitor and tune

Held-out RMSE (explicit) or recall@k and NDCG (implicit) with time-based splits; catalogue coverage and the share of recommendations going to the top 1% of items; performance for new and light users separately; training objective per sweep (it must fall monotonically for ALS). Tune k, λ, α (implicit), the number of sweeps or epochs and learning-rate schedules.

A cost estimate

Implicit ALS on 10 million users, 1 million items and 1 billion interactions with f = 100: each user half-step costs about f²·N = 104 × 109 = 1013 operations to accumulate the corrections plus f³·users = 106 × 107 = 1013 for the solves; the item half-step is similar. Around 4 × 1013 operations per sweep and ten sweeps per training run — minutes to an hour on a modest cluster. Storing the factors takes 11 million × 100 × 4 bytes ≈ 4.4 GB.

The reference for this problem

Koren, Bell and Volinsky (IEEE Computer, 2009) give the regularised objective over the known ratings, the SGD updates qi ← qi + γ(euipu − λqi) and pu ← pu + γ(euiqi − λpu), describe ALS as rotating between fixing the q's and solving least squares for the p's and vice versa — favourable when parallelising and for implicit data — add biases with the Joe/Titanic example, and discuss implicit feedback and confidence. Hu, Koren and Volinsky (ICDM 2008) define pui = 1[rui > 0], cui = 1 + αrui with α = 40, the all-pairs weighted objective, and the YᵀCuY = YᵀY + Yᵀ(Cu − I)Y speed-up that makes each sweep linear in the data.

Work it out

(a) A user has rated three items whose fixed factors (k = 2) are q1 = [1, 0], q2 = [0, 1], q3 = [1, 1], with targets r − μ − bi of 1, 2 and 2. Solve for pu with λ = 1 and no user bias. (b) Implicit ALS with 50 million users, 200,000 items, 2 billion interactions and f = 64: estimate the cost of one user half-step with and without the YᵀY trick.

Answer 
Qd = np.array([[1.0, 0.0], [0.0, 1.0], [1.0, 1.0]]); yd = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 2.0])
pd_ = np.linalg.solve(Qd.T @ Qd + np.eye(2), Qd.T @ yd)
assert np.allclose(pd_, [0.625, 1.125])
naive = 50e6 * 64 ** 2 * 200_000                                  # f^2 n per user
trick = 64 ** 2 * 2e9 + 64 ** 3 * 50e6                            # f^2 N + f^3 m
assert round(naive / trick) == 1923

(a) QᵀQ = q1q1ᵀ + q2q2ᵀ + q3q3ᵀ = [[2, 1], [1, 2]]; adding λI gives [[3, 1], [1, 3]]. Qᵀy = 1·[1, 0] + 2·[0, 1] + 2·[1, 1] = [3, 4]. The determinant is 3·3 − 1·1 = 8, so p = (1/8)[[3, −1], [−1, 3]]·[3, 4] = (1/8)[5, 9] = [0.625, 1.125]. Without λ the unregularised least-squares answer would be [2/3, 5/3]; the penalty shrinks both components. (b) Naively each user's YᵀCuY costs f²·n = 4,096 × 200,000 ≈ 8.2 × 108, times 50 million users ≈ 4.1 × 1016. With the trick: f²·N + f³·m = 4,096 × 2 × 109 + 262,144 × 5 × 107 ≈ 8.2 × 1012 + 1.3 × 1013 ≈ 2.1 × 1013, about 1,900 times less — the difference between impossible and routine.
