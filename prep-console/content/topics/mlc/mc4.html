A clothing retailer has 2 million customers and wants to send five different styles of marketing email. Nobody has labelled the customers; there is no "right answer" column. What the retailer has is behaviour: how often each customer buys, how much they spend, what share is on sale, which categories. The question is whether those customers fall into a handful of natural groups, and if so, what each group looks like. k-means is the algorithm most teams reach for first: tell it "five groups", and it returns five centre points (the typical customer of each group) and a group label for every customer.

The same algorithm hides inside much of modern AI infrastructure. Vector databases cluster millions of embeddings with k-means so that a search only scans the nearest few clusters. Image compression replaces each pixel's colour with the nearest of 256 cluster centres. Speech and image tokenisers learn codebooks with k-means-like updates. Data teams cluster training-set embeddings to find near-duplicates and balance topics. So when an interviewer says "implement k-means", they are asking for something that ships.

The algorithm fits in five lines, and that is the trap. Typed naively it starts from random centres, and random centres regularly land two in the same natural group and none in another. The algorithm then converges confidently to a bad answer, and running it longer does not help. In this lesson's 25-cluster test, random starts finished with errors between 1,600 and 4,000 times worse than the true clustering, every time. The fix is a seeding rule from a 2007 paper, k-means++, that takes about ten lines and makes those failures rare. A naive update step also divides by zero the moment a centre attracts no points, and a naive "choose k by lowest error" always answers "as many clusters as points".

This lesson builds k-means from scratch, carefully. It explains the objective and the two alternating steps in plain words, writes them as equations, then runs two full iterations by hand on six points from a deliberately bad start and confirms with brute force that the answer is optimal. It works the k-means++ seeding probabilities by hand (98% chance of a good second seed, against 60% for uniform seeding), writes the naive loop version and the vectorised version, handles empty clusters and restarts, tests everything including monotone decrease of the objective, reproduces the paper's headline effect on synthetic data built like its Norm25 dataset, and finishes with choosing k, complexity, and what changes at the scale of billions of points.

Imagine placing k post offices in a town so that the total walking distance (strictly, the total squared walking distance) from every house to its nearest post office is as small as possible. You do not know the best positions, so you guess k positions, then repeat two moves. First, every household registers at whichever post office is nearest. Second, every post office moves to the middle of its registered households (the average of their coordinates). Repeat until nobody switches office. That is Lloyd's algorithm, the procedure everyone means by "k-means".

The quantity being minimised is called the inertia (also the within-cluster sum of squares, or in the original paper the "potential"): for every point, the squared distance to the centre it belongs to, all added up. Each of the two moves can only make it smaller or leave it the same. Registering at the nearest office cannot increase anyone's distance. Moving an office to the average of its households cannot increase their total squared distance, because the average is precisely the point that minimises that total; that fact is why the algorithm is called k-means. Because the total only ever goes down, and there are only finitely many ways to register households, the process must stop.

The catch is where it stops. It stops at the first arrangement that neither move can improve, which is a local minimum, and which arrangement that is depends entirely on the starting guess. If two of your initial post offices are both in the north-west suburb and none is in the town centre, the town centre's households all register at the nearest suburb office, that office moves partway toward the centre, and the configuration can settle with one office serving two neighbourhoods badly while two offices split one neighbourhood. Neither move ever fixes it, because fixing it requires moving an office across town, and neither move does that.

k-means++ fixes the start rather than the iterations. Pick the first office location at a random household. For the second, pick a household at random, but weight each household by the square of its distance to the nearest office so far. Households far from every existing office are very likely to be picked, so the second office probably goes somewhere poorly served. Repeat until there are k. The offices end up spread across the town, roughly one per neighbourhood, and the ordinary two-move iterations polish the positions from there.

Where the post-office picture breaks: real data has dozens of dimensions, not two, so you cannot look at it; "squared distance" makes far-away outliers pull centres strongly toward themselves; and k-means can only draw straight boundaries halfway between centres, so a long, thin or ring-shaped group gets chopped up. The lesson returns to each of these.

1. The problem as it is usually asked

"Implement k-means with k-means++ initialisation. Return the centres, labels and inertia." Clarify: is k given or must you choose it? What should happen when a cluster becomes empty? Is there a fixed iteration budget or a convergence tolerance? Should the result be reproducible (a seed)? How large are n, d and k? Then state the plan: k-means++ seeding, vectorised Lloyd iterations, empty-cluster handling, several restarts keeping the lowest inertia, and tests.

2. The maths in plain words

We have n points, each a list of d numbers, and want k centres. Every point belongs to whichever centre is nearest. The score of a set of centres is the inertia: add up, over all points, the squared distance from the point to its nearest centre. Lower is better. Lloyd's algorithm improves the score by improving one thing at a time: holding the centres fixed, the best assignment is "each point to its nearest centre"; holding the assignment fixed, the best centre for each group is the group's average. Alternating two exact improvements is a pattern called coordinate descent (or alternating minimisation), and it is the same idea as the E and M steps of a Gaussian mixture model, which k-means is a hard-assignment special case of.

k-means++ adds a rule for the start. Write D(x) for the distance from point x to the nearest centre chosen so far. Choose the first centre uniformly at random from the points; choose each subsequent centre from the points with probability proportional to D(x)². Points that are already well served (small D) are rarely picked; points far from every centre are picked often.

3. The equations

inertia (the objective):   φ(C) = Σᵢ  min_j ‖xᵢ − c_j‖²

assignment step:   labelᵢ = argmin_j ‖xᵢ − c_j‖²
update step:       c_j    = (1 / |S_j|) Σ_{i ∈ S_j} xᵢ        S_j = points with label j

k-means++ seeding: c₁ ~ uniform over points
                   c_t ~ P(x) = D(x)² / Σ_y D(y)²            for t = 2..k

guarantee (Arthur & Vassilvitskii 2007):  E[φ] ≤ 8(ln k + 2) · φ_OPT  after seeding alone

Why the mean minimises the update: for fixed points S, the derivative of Σ‖x − c‖² with respect to c is −2Σ(x − c), which is zero exactly when c is the average of the points. Why the guarantee matters: the optimum is NP-hard to find, yet this ten-line seeding is provably within a logarithmic factor of it in expectation, on every dataset, before a single Lloyd iteration runs.

With the same labels, the inertia is now 0.25 + 0.25 for centre 1 and 14.5625 + 0.5625 + 4.0625 + 1.5625 for centre 2, a total of 21.25. The update step alone cut it from 80 to 21.25.

Iteration 2. P2 at (1,2) is now 1.25 from centre 1 and 14.56 from centre 2, so it switches. Every other point keeps its label. The new means are (4/3, 4/3) for the bottom-left trio and (16/3, 14/3) for the top-right trio, and the inertia is 8/3 ≈ 2.667. A third iteration changes nothing: converged.

Is 2.667 the best possible? With six points there are only 2⁶ = 64 ways to split them into two labelled groups (62 with both groups non-empty), so the test simply tries them all and confirms the minimum inertia is 8/3. On real data that brute force is hopeless, which is why the hand example is valuable as a known answer.

5. A worked example by hand: k-means++ seeding

Suppose the first seed, chosen uniformly, is P1 = (1,1). The squared distances of all six points to it are the first column above: 0, 1, 1, 25, 41, 32, summing to 100. k-means++ picks the second seed with probabilities equal to those values divided by 100: P1 0%, P2 1%, P3 1%, P4 25%, P5 41%, P6 32%. The chance the second seed lands in the far group is 25 + 41 + 32 = 98%. Uniform seeding would pick among the other five points with equal probability, landing in the far group 3 times in 5, 60%; overall, two uniform picks from six points land in the same group 40% of the time. That gap, between "almost always one seed per group" and "often two in one group", is the whole value of k-means++, and it widens as k grows because uniform seeding's chance of hitting every one of k groups shrinks fast.

The first solution block asserts every number in both worked examples.

import itertools
import time
import numpy as np

# The hand-worked example: six points, k = 2, a deliberately bad start.
P = np.array([[1.0, 1.0], [1.0, 2.0], [2.0, 1.0], [5.0, 4.0], [6.0, 5.0], [5.0, 5.0]])
C0 = np.array([[1.0, 1.0], [1.0, 2.0]])                      # both seeds in the bottom-left group

D = ((P[:, None, :] - C0[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(-1)           # (6, 2) squared distances
assert D[:, 0].tolist() == [0, 1, 1, 25, 41, 32]
assert D[:, 1].tolist() == [1, 0, 2, 20, 34, 25]
lab = D.argmin(1)
assert lab.tolist() == [0, 1, 0, 1, 1, 1]                     # P2 grabs the far group
assert D[np.arange(6), lab].sum() == 80                       # inertia after the assignment step

C1 = np.array([P[lab == j].mean(0) for j in range(2)])
assert np.allclose(C1, [[1.5, 1.0], [4.25, 4.0]])             # update step
inertia1 = ((P - C1[lab]) ** 2).sum()
assert np.isclose(inertia1, 21.25)

lab2 = ((P[:, None] - C1[None]) ** 2).sum(-1).argmin(1)
assert lab2.tolist() == [0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1]                    # second iteration fixes P2
C2 = np.array([P[lab2 == j].mean(0) for j in range(2)])
assert np.allclose(C2, [[4 / 3, 4 / 3], [16 / 3, 14 / 3]])
assert np.isclose(((P - C2[lab2]) ** 2).sum(), 8 / 3)         # 2.667, and nothing changes after

d2 = D[:, 0]
probs = d2 / d2.sum()
assert d2.sum() == 100 and np.allclose(probs, [0, 0.01, 0.01, 0.25, 0.41, 0.32])
assert np.isclose(probs[3:].sum(), 0.98)                       # 98% chance to seed the far group

6. The naive loop implementation

The literal version loops over points, then centres, then coordinates for the assignment, and accumulates sums and counts per cluster for the update. It handles an empty cluster by keeping its old centre, which avoids the crash but, as section 9 explains, can leave a centre stranded for ever.

def kmeans_naive(X, centres, n_iter=100):
    """Lloyd's algorithm with explicit loops over points, centres and coordinates."""
    X = X.tolist(); centres = [list(c) for c in centres]
    k, d = len(centres), len(X[0])
    labels = [0] * len(X)
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        for i, x in enumerate(X):                              # assignment step
            best, best_d = 0, float("inf")
            for j, c in enumerate(centres):
                dist = sum((x[t] - c[t]) ** 2 for t in range(d))
                if dist < best_d:
                    best, best_d = j, dist
            labels[i] = best
        new = [[0.0] * d for _ in range(k)]; counts = [0] * k   # update step
        for x, l in zip(X, labels):
            counts[l] += 1
            for t in range(d):
                new[l][t] += x[t]
        new = [[v / counts[j] for v in new[j]] if counts[j] else centres[j] for j in range(k)]
        if new == centres:
            break
        centres = new
    return np.array(centres), np.array(labels)

Each iteration is O(nkd) interpreted operations for the assignment and O(nd) for the update. It is a useful reference because every line maps to one symbol in the equations.

7. The vectorised version

def sq_dists(A, B):
    """(m, d), (n, d) -> (m, n) squared distances, clamped at zero."""
    return np.maximum((A * A).sum(1)[:, None] - 2 * A @ B.T + (B * B).sum(1)[None, :], 0.0)

def kmeans_pp_init(X, k, rng):
    """D² seeding (Arthur & Vassilvitskii, 2007): each new centre is sampled with
    probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest centre so far."""
    n = len(X)
    centres = [X[rng.integers(n)]]
    closest = sq_dists(X, centres[0][None]).ravel()             # D(x)² for every point
    for _ in range(1, k):
        total = closest.sum()
        if total == 0:                                          # fewer distinct points than k
            idx = rng.integers(n)
        else:
            idx = rng.choice(n, p=closest / total)
        centres.append(X[idx])
        closest = np.minimum(closest, sq_dists(X, X[idx][None]).ravel())   # O(n) update
    return np.array(centres)

def lloyd(X, centres, max_iter=300, tol=1e-6):
    centres = centres.astype(float).copy()
    k = len(centres)
    for it in range(1, max_iter + 1):
        d = sq_dists(X, centres)                                 # (n, k)
        labels = d.argmin(1)
        counts = np.bincount(labels, minlength=k)
        sums = np.zeros_like(centres)
        np.add.at(sums, labels, X)                              # per-cluster sums
        new = centres.copy()
        nz = counts > 0
        new[nz] = sums[nz] / counts[nz, None]
        if (~nz).any():                                         # empty cluster: reseed it on the
            far = d[np.arange(len(X)), labels].argsort()[::-1]  # points worst served right now
            new[~nz] = X[far[: (~nz).sum()]]
        shift = ((new - centres) ** 2).sum()
        centres = new
        if shift <= tol:
            break
    d = sq_dists(X, centres)
    labels = d.argmin(1)
    return centres, labels, d[np.arange(len(X)), labels].sum(), it

def kmeans(X, k, n_init=4, init="k-means++", seed=0, **kw):
    """Best of n_init runs by inertia. Returns centres, labels, inertia."""
    X = np.asarray(X, float)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    best = None
    for _ in range(n_init):
        c0 = kmeans_pp_init(X, k, rng) if init == "k-means++" else X[rng.choice(len(X), k, replace=False)]
        run = lloyd(X, c0, **kw)
        if best is None or run[2] < best[2]:
            best = run
    return best[:3]

The pieces, one by one.

Assignment. One call to sq_dists(X, centres) gives the (n, k) table of squared distances using the expansion from mc3, clamped at zero; argmin(1) picks each point's nearest centre.

Update. np.bincount(labels, minlength=k) counts points per cluster. np.add.at(sums, labels, X) adds each point's row into its cluster's row of sums; as in mc3, the plain sums[labels] += X would be wrong, because each label appears many times and fancy-index assignment applies each repeated index only once. Dividing sums by counts gives the means. (A one-hot matrix M of shape (k, n) with M @ X computes the same sums with BLAS, and is faster for small k.)

Empty clusters. If a count is zero, the mean is 0/0. The code moves each empty centre onto one of the points currently worst served, those with the largest distance to their assigned centre. That both fixes the NaN and puts the centre where it can do the most good.

Convergence. The loop stops when the centres move by less than a tolerance (total squared shift), or after max_iter iterations. Stopping when labels stop changing is equivalent and also common.

k-means++ seeding. kmeans_pp_init keeps an array closest holding D(x)² for every point. After choosing each new seed, it updates closest with the minimum of the old value and the distance to the new seed, an O(nd) step, so seeding costs O(nkd) in total, the same as one Lloyd iteration. If every remaining distance is zero (fewer distinct points than k), it falls back to a uniform choice rather than dividing by zero.

Restarts. kmeans runs the whole thing n_init times from different seeds and keeps the result with the lowest inertia. Because the result depends on the start, this is the cheapest insurance there is.

The experiment copies the design of the paper's synthetic Norm25 dataset at a smaller size: 25 true centres drawn uniformly from a 15-dimensional cube of side 500, and 80 points around each from a Gaussian with variance 1, so 2,000 points in well-separated tight blobs. Running k-means with k = 25 and a single start, five times each: uniform random seeding ended at inertias between 48.7 and 117.9 million, while k-means++ reached 29,358 every time, which is below the 29,737 you get by measuring each point against its true centre (the fitted means fit the sample slightly better than the true ones). Random seeding was 1,600-4,000 times worse. The paper reported the same kind of gap on real data: on its Spam and Intrusion datasets, potentials 20 to 1,000 times smaller with k-means++.

Tracing the vectorised update on the hand example

It helps to watch the array operations produce the hand-worked numbers. After the first assignment, labels = [0, 1, 0, 1, 1, 1]. np.bincount(labels, minlength=2) returns [2, 4]: two points in cluster 0, four in cluster 1. np.add.at(sums, labels, P) adds rows 1 and 3 (zero-based 0 and 2) into sums[0], giving (1 + 2, 1 + 1) = (3, 2), and the other four rows into sums[1], giving (1 + 5 + 6 + 5, 2 + 4 + 5 + 5) = (17, 16). Dividing by the counts gives (1.5, 1) and (4.25, 4), the centres computed by hand. Had the code written sums[labels] += P, each cluster's row would have received only the last point assigned to it, (2, 1) and (5, 5), and dividing by the counts would give the nonsense centres (1, 0.5) and (1.25, 1.25), a bug that converges quietly to the wrong answer. Printing these intermediate arrays for a tiny example is the fastest way to debug a vectorised update.

Edge cases worth naming

With k = 1 the answer is the mean of all the points and the inertia is the total sum of squares, a useful sanity check. With k = n every point is its own centre and the inertia is zero, the reason inertia cannot choose k. With k larger than the number of distinct points, some centres must coincide or be empty; the seeding's zero-total fallback and the empty-cluster reseeding keep the code from crashing, but the right behaviour is usually to raise a clear error. Duplicate points are fine and simply carry more weight, which is also how weighted k-means is implemented (a point with weight 3 behaves like three copies; use np.add.at with the weights times the points and bincount with weights for the counts). And ties in the assignment step, a point exactly equidistant from two centres, go to the lower index with argmin, which is harmless but should be deterministic.

9. Numerical and practical traps

Trap 1: empty clusters produce NaN. Dividing a zero sum by a zero count gives NaN, and a NaN centre is never nearest to anything (all comparisons with NaN are false, and argmin treats NaN as the minimum in NumPy, which is worse: every point could be assigned to it). Detect zero counts and reseed. The test's far-away seed at (100, 100) exercises exactly this.

Trap 2: zero total in D² sampling. If there are fewer distinct points than k, at some step every D(x)² is zero and the probabilities are 0/0. Fall back to uniform choice, or reduce k and warn.

Trap 3: cancellation in the distance expansion. As in mc3, the expansion loses digits when the data is far from the origin. Centring the data once before clustering (and adding the mean back to the centres afterwards) is free, because k-means is translation-invariant.

Trap 4: scale. Squared distance is dominated by the features with the largest numeric range. Annual spend in pounds next to number of visits will cluster on spend alone. Standardise features, or deliberately weight them, before clustering; this is a modelling decision you should state.

Trap 5: float32 sums. Accumulating millions of points into a cluster sum in float32 loses precision (each addition rounds to about 7 digits relative to the running total). Accumulate sums in float64 even if the data is float32.

Trap 6: randomness and reproducibility. Both the seeding and any tie-breaking are random. Take a seed (or a Generator) as a parameter so that tests and experiments are reproducible, as the solution does.

10. Complexity

One Lloyd iteration costs O(nkd) for the distance table (a BLAS matrix product plus broadcasts), O(nk) for the argmin and O(nd) for the update, with O(nk) memory for the table; batch the points if n × k floats do not fit. k-means++ seeding costs O(nkd) as well, done as k sequential passes. With I iterations and R restarts, the total is O(R · I · nkd). Typical I is tens; the theoretical worst case is exponential in n but essentially never seen. For a million points, k = 1,000 and d = 128, one iteration is 1.3 × 10¹¹ multiply-adds, a few seconds on a CPU and well under a second on a GPU.

11. Choosing k

Inertia always decreases when k increases (more centres can only bring each point closer), and it is zero when every point is its own centre, so "the k with the lowest inertia" is always the largest k. Three practical alternatives. The elbow method plots inertia against k and looks for the bend after which extra clusters buy little. The silhouette score (the drill) compares each point's average distance to its own cluster, a, with its average distance to the nearest other cluster, b, as (b − a)/max(a, b), between −1 and 1; on the three-blob test data it peaks at k = 3 (0.711, against 0.509 at k = 2 and 0.574 at k = 4). And the honest answer in most products: choose k by what the clusters are for, such as the number of email campaigns the marketing team can actually write.

12. Talking through the design

In the interview: state the objective, the two steps and why each lowers it; give the k-means++ rule and why it helps, with the 98% example if you have a whiteboard; vectorise the assignment with the distance expansion and the update with bincount and add.at; name empty clusters, restarts and seeds; then tests, including monotone inertia and a tiny brute-force check. If pushed on scale, go to mini-batch k-means and k-means||; if pushed on quality, to choosing k and to cluster shapes k-means cannot represent.

Outliers, and k-medoids

Squared distance makes k-means sensitive to outliers in two places. In the update step, one point a hundred units away pulls its cluster's mean as hard as a hundred points one unit away. In D² seeding, the outlier has the largest D² of all and is very likely to become a seed, wasting one of the k centres on a single point. Practical defences are to remove or clip extreme points first, to run seeding on a sample with the most extreme points excluded, or to change the objective. k-medoids restricts centres to be actual data points and minimises the sum of (unsquared) distances, so an outlier can pull a cluster's representative only as far as the most central real point; it also works with any distance, including ones without a meaningful mean such as edit distance between strings. The price is cost: the classic PAM algorithm is quadratic in the cluster size per update, so it is used on thousands of points, not millions.

Why D² sampling works, intuitively

The proof in Arthur and Vassilvitskii has two halves. First, if a seed is drawn from some cluster of the optimal solution, its expected cost for that cluster is within a constant factor of optimal: a uniformly chosen point of a cluster has expected squared distance to the others of twice the cluster's optimal cost, and D² sampling within a cluster is at most four times worse, giving the factor 8. Second, D² sampling makes it likely that each new seed lands in an optimal cluster not yet covered, because uncovered clusters contribute most of the remaining Σ D(x)². Clusters that do get missed contribute an amount the authors bound by an induction that produces the harmonic-number factor, hence the ln k + 2. The authors also show the analysis is tight: there are datasets where k-means++ really is Ω(log k) worse than optimal in expectation.

Greedy k-means++

scikit-learn's default is a variant its documentation calls "greedy k-means++": at each seeding step it draws several candidates by D² sampling and keeps the one that most reduces the potential. It costs a few times more seeding work and noticeably reduces variance between runs, which is why scikit-learn's n_init='auto' runs k-means only once when the initialisation is k-means++ but ten times when it is random. That is a good, concrete answer to "how many restarts do you need?": with good seeding, few.

k-means|| for distributed data

k-means++ needs k sequential passes over the data, one per seed, which is fine for a million points in memory and painful for a billion on a cluster. Bahmani and colleagues' k-means|| oversamples instead: in each round, every point is selected independently with probability proportional to ℓ · D(x)² / φ, so about ℓ candidates are added per round in parallel (the paper tested ℓ from 0.1k to 10k), for a small number of rounds: theory asks for O(log ψ) rounds, but the authors found that after as few as five the result was consistently as good as any other method's. The few hundred or thousand candidates are weighted by how many points are closest to them and reclustered with ordinary k-means++ on one machine. The candidate set alone is a constant-factor approximation, so reclustering it with k-means++ keeps an O(log k) guarantee overall, with far fewer passes over the data; it is the initialisation Spark's MLlib uses.

Mini-batch k-means

When even one full Lloyd iteration is expensive, mini-batch k-means updates centres from a random batch of, say, 1,024 points at a time: assign the batch, then move each centre a little toward its batch points with a per-centre learning rate of 1 over the number of points it has seen so far. Each centre is then a running average. It converges to slightly worse inertia than full Lloyd but orders of magnitude faster on large data, and it works on streams.

Elkan's algorithm: skipping distances with the triangle inequality

Most points do not change cluster in later iterations, yet Lloyd recomputes all k distances for each. Elkan's variant keeps, for each point, an upper bound on the distance to its own centre and lower bounds on the distances to the others, updated cheaply by how far each centre moved. If the upper bound is below every lower bound, the assignment cannot have changed and no distances are computed. On well-clustered data it skips the large majority of distance computations, at the cost of O(nk) memory for the bounds; scikit-learn offers it as algorithm="elkan".

k-means as a Gaussian mixture in the limit

A Gaussian mixture model with equal, spherical covariances σ²I and equal weights, fitted by EM, gives each point soft responsibilities proportional to exp(−‖x − c‖²/2σ²). As σ shrinks to zero, the largest responsibility goes to 1 and the rest to 0, so the E-step becomes the hard assignment step and the M-step becomes the mean update: k-means. That view explains its biases (it prefers round clusters of similar size and spread) and tells you what to use when they do not hold: a full mixture model with per-cluster covariances.

Vector quantisation in modern systems

Seen differently, k-means compresses: replace each vector with the index of its nearest centre and you need only log₂(k) bits, at a distortion equal to the inertia. That is the basis of IVF search (centres as coarse buckets), product quantisation (k-means on sub-vectors, so 8 sub-quantisers of 256 centres each encode a vector in 8 bytes), and learned discrete tokenisers. The failure mode carries over too: "dead" codebook entries that no input maps to are empty clusters, and the fix is the same reseeding used here.

Where k-means runs

Customer and content segmentation, colour quantisation, initialising Gaussian mixtures and other models, the coarse quantiser of IVF vector indexes, codebook learning for product quantisation, and dataset curation (cluster embeddings, then sample evenly across clusters or drop near-duplicates within one). In most of these the clustering is rebuilt periodically as data changes, so stability between runs matters as much as quality.

Failure modes and symptoms

One giant cluster plus several tiny ones. Usually unscaled features or heavy outliers: a handful of extreme points each capture a centre, and everything else shares one. Look at cluster sizes and the per-feature spread within clusters; standardise, transform skewed features (log of spend), clip or remove outliers.

Clusters that change completely between weekly runs. Different random seeds find different local minima, and cluster numbering is arbitrary. Fix the seed, warm-start from last week's centres, match clusters across runs by nearest centres, and track an agreement score such as the adjusted Rand index between runs. Downstream systems should never depend on raw cluster IDs being stable.

Dead or empty centres in a quantiser. In an IVF index this shows as some lists empty and others huge, which hurts both recall and latency because queries probing a huge list scan many vectors. Monitor the list-size distribution; retrain with reseeding and enough training points (a common rule of thumb is at least 30-100 training vectors per centre).

Silent quality loss as data drifts. New kinds of items arrive and are forced into the nearest old cluster. Monitor the average distance of new points to their nearest centre; when it rises, re-cluster.

What to monitor

For a clustering that is rebuilt on a schedule, log the inertia per point (so it is comparable across data sizes), the cluster-size distribution (smallest, largest, and how many are below a minimum useful size), the number of iterations to converge and whether the iteration cap was hit, the number of empty clusters reseeded, and the agreement with the previous run. For clusters used as an index, also log the recall of searches against exact search. A sudden change in any of these is more informative than the absolute values.

Knobs and what they trade

k (granularity versus stability and interpretability), the initialisation (k-means++ by default), n_init (cost versus robustness; one or a few with k-means++), max_iter and tolerance (cost versus polish; most improvement happens in the first few iterations), batch size for mini-batch k-means, and feature scaling, which matters more than all the others.

The reference for this problem

David Arthur and Sergei Vassilvitskii's "k-means++: The Advantages of Careful Seeding" (SODA 2007, PDF hosted on Stanford's theory site) defines the seeding rule implemented here and proves Theorem 3.1: if the centres are chosen with k-means++, the potential satisfies E[φ] ≤ 8(ln k + 2)·φ_OPT, so the seeding is O(log k)-competitive on all datasets, and the analysis is tight up to a constant factor. Its experiments use a synthetic Norm25 dataset (25 true centres drawn uniformly from a 15-dimensional hypercube of side 500, with variance-1 Gaussians around each, n = 10,000) and three UCI datasets: Cloud (1,024 points, 10 dimensions), Intrusion (494,019 points, 35 dimensions) and Spam (4,601 points, 58 dimensions). The authors report that k-means++ beat standard k-means on every dataset by at least 10% in accuracy, achieved potentials 20 to 1,000 times smaller on Spam and Intrusion, and completed each trial two to three times faster because better seeds need fewer Lloyd iterations. This lesson's test reproduces the Norm25 design at 2,000 points and sees the same qualitative gap.

Work it out

Implement the silhouette score and use it to choose k between 2 and 6 on the three-blob data from the tests. For point i, a(i) is the mean distance to the other points in its cluster and b(i) the mean distance to the points of the nearest other cluster; s(i) = (b − a) / max(a, b).

Answer 
def silhouette(X, labels):
    D = np.sqrt(sq_dists(X, X))
    ks = np.unique(labels)
    s = np.zeros(len(X))
    for i in range(len(X)):
        own = labels == labels[i]
        if own.sum() == 1:
            continue                                    # singleton: silhouette defined as 0
        a = D[i, own].sum() / (own.sum() - 1)           # exclude the point itself (distance 0)
        b = min(D[i, labels == c].mean() for c in ks if c != labels[i])
        s[i] = (b - a) / max(a, b)
    return s.mean()

scores = {k: silhouette(X3, kmeans(X3, k)[1]) for k in range(2, 7)}
assert max(scores, key=scores.get) == 3
if __name__ == "__main__":
    print({k: round(v, 3) for k, v in scores.items()})

The scores were 0.509, 0.711, 0.574, 0.450 and 0.452 for k = 2 to 6, a clear peak at the true k = 3. The silhouette rewards tight, well-separated clusters and is bounded in [−1, 1], so unlike inertia it does not keep improving as k grows. Its cost is O(n²) distances, so sample a few thousand points on large data. The elbow on inertia would be visible here too, but on real data it is rarely this clear.
