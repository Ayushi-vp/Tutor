A bank's loan team wants a model that approves or declines small-business loans, and the regulator wants every decision explained in plain language. "Declined because annual revenue is below £120,000 and the business is less than two years old" is an explanation a loan officer, a customer and a regulator can all read. A decision tree produces exactly that kind of rule: a short chain of yes/no questions about single features, ending in a verdict. It is also the building block of the two model families that still win most competitions on tabular data, random forests and gradient-boosted trees, which is why "implement a decision tree" remains a staple of ML coding rounds. Everything you write here, the impurity, the split search and the stopping rules, reappears unchanged inside those ensembles.

The interviewer is not testing whether you know what a tree looks like. They are testing three things. Can you score a split, which means defining an impurity measure and weighting the children correctly? Can you find the best split efficiently, without the O(n²) recount that makes a naive tree crawl? (In this lesson's test, the naive scan took 535 ms and the cumulative-count scan 1.2 ms on the same 400 rows, a factor of 450.) And do you know when to stop, because a tree allowed to grow until every leaf is pure will memorise anything, including random labels: here a fully grown tree fitted 500 coin-flip labels perfectly and then scored 49% on fresh coin flips.

There are also small traps that only show up when you write the code: thresholds between equal values that cannot separate anything, a midpoint between two adjacent floating-point numbers that rounds onto one of them, Python's recursion limit on deep trees, missing values that silently all go right, and a prediction loop that walks rows one at a time.

The lesson builds the classifier step by step. It explains impurity and splitting in plain words, writes the equations, and then scores every threshold of a seven-point example by hand, finding the best split and its Gini gain (and the entropy version). It writes the naive split scan, replaces it with sorting and cumulative counts, builds the tree iteratively into flat arrays like scikit-learn's, predicts all rows in lock-step, and tests against a brute-force scan, known shapes and the hand example. It ends with complexity, pruning, categorical and missing features, why single trees are unstable, and how the same split search powers gradient boosting in the next problem.

Think of the game "twenty questions", played on a spreadsheet. Each question names one column and one cut-off: "Is revenue at most £120,000?" Every row answers yes or no and goes left or right. Then each group gets its own next question, and so on. The rows that end up together at the bottom, in a leaf, get a prediction: the most common class among the training rows that landed there, or the class proportions if you want probabilities. That chain of questions is a decision tree. The first question is the root; questions in the middle are internal nodes.

The art is in choosing the questions. A good question splits a mixed group into groups that are each more one-sided. Asking "is revenue at most £120,000?" is good if nearly everyone below the line defaulted and nearly everyone above repaid; it is useless if both sides have the same default rate as before. So we need a number that measures how mixed a group is, called impurity. The standard one, Gini impurity, has a friendly meaning: pick two rows from the group at random (with replacement); Gini is the chance they have different labels. A group of all one class scores 0. A 50/50 group of two classes scores 0.5, the maximum for two classes.

A question's quality is how much it reduces impurity. Compute the impurity of each side, weight each by how many rows went there (a pure group of two rows should count less than a mixed group of two hundred), add them up, and compare with the impurity before the split. The difference is the gain. The tree-building algorithm, at every node, tries every column and every sensible cut-off, keeps the question with the largest gain, splits, and repeats on each side. That is a greedy algorithm: it takes the best next step without looking further ahead.

Two properties follow from the "one column, one cut-off" form. Because a question only compares a value with a threshold, scaling or log-transforming a feature never changes which rows go left, so trees need no feature scaling. And because each question cuts along one axis, a tree carves the feature space into rectangles, as the figure shows. That makes diagonal boundaries expensive (a staircase of many small rectangles) and means a tree cannot extrapolate: beyond the training range, it predicts whatever the edge leaf says.

The last ingredient is knowing when to stop. Left alone, the greedy builder keeps splitting until every leaf is pure, which on noisy data means building a separate leaf for nearly every row: a perfect memory of the training set and a poor guide to new data. Stopping rules (maximum depth, minimum rows per leaf) or pruning afterwards keep the tree general. Where the twenty-questions picture breaks: a human chooses questions with foresight, while the algorithm only ever looks one question ahead, so it can miss pairs of questions that only work together.

1. The problem as it is usually asked

"Implement a decision tree classifier from scratch: fit, predict, predict_proba, with max_depth and min_samples_split." Clarify: numeric features only, or categorical too? Missing values? How many classes, and are labels integers or strings? Is Gini acceptable or do they want entropy? How large is the data (the split search's complexity matters)? Then outline: impurity, the split search, recursive growth with stopping rules, prediction by walking the tree, tests.

2. The maths in plain words

For a node holding some training rows, count the classes and turn the counts into proportions p_k. Gini impurity is one minus the sum of the squared proportions. Why that formula? The chance two random draws are both class k is p_k²; summing over classes gives the chance they match; one minus that is the chance they differ. Entropy, the main alternative, is −Σ p_k log₂ p_k, the average number of bits needed to name a random row's class; it is also zero for a pure node and largest for an even mix. Both are concave in the proportions, which is the property that guarantees splitting a node never increases the weighted impurity.

A split on feature f at threshold t sends rows with value at most t left and the rest right. Its score is the weighted average of the children's impurities, weights being the fraction of rows in each child. The best split is the one with the lowest score, equivalently the largest impurity decrease. Only thresholds between consecutive distinct sorted values matter, because any two thresholds in the same gap produce the same partition.

3. The equations

Gini(S)     = 1 − Σ_k p_k²                 p_k = (rows of class k in S) / |S|
Entropy(S)  = − Σ_k p_k log₂ p_k

split (f, t):   L = {x : x_f ≤ t},   R = {x : x_f > t}
weighted child impurity:   I(f, t) = (|L|·G(L) + |R|·G(R)) / |S|
gain:                      Δ(f, t) = G(S) − I(f, t)            choose the (f, t) with the largest Δ

candidate thresholds for feature f:  midpoints between consecutive distinct sorted values

The fast scan rests on one observation. Sort the rows by feature f. A cut after sorted position i puts the first i + 1 rows on the left. So the left child's class counts for every possible cut are the cumulative counts of the sorted labels, C[i] = Σ_{j ≤ i} onehot(y_j), and the right child's counts are the parent's counts minus C[i]. One sort, one cumulative sum, and every candidate's impurity is a few array operations.

4. A worked example by hand: one split's Gini gain

Seven rows with one feature, x = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and labels 0, 0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1. The parent has four 0s and three 1s: Gini = 1 − (4/7)² − (3/7)² = 1 − 16/49 − 9/49 = 24/49 = 0.490.

The candidate thresholds are the six midpoints 1.5 to 6.5. For each, count each side, compute each side's Gini, weight and add:

The best threshold is 3.5: three pure 0s on the left, and 1, 0, 1, 1 on the right with Gini 0.375, giving a weighted impurity of 4/7 × 0.375 = 0.214. The Gini gain is 0.490 − 0.214 = 0.276. Notice that 5.5, which isolates two pure 1s on the right, comes a close second (0.229): pure children are attractive, but the weighting by size decides.

The same split scored with entropy: the parent has −(4/7)log₂(4/7) − (3/7)log₂(3/7) = 0.985 bits; the right child (three 1s, one 0) has 0.811 bits; the left is pure. Information gain = 0.985 − 4/7 × 0.811 = 0.522 bits, also the best of the six, as is typical: Gini and entropy rarely disagree.

The first solution block asserts every one of these numbers.

import time
import numpy as np

# The hand-worked example: one feature, seven points, scan every threshold for Gini gain.
x_hand = np.array([1.0, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7])
y_hand = np.array([0, 0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1])

def gini(labels):
    p = np.bincount(labels, minlength=2) / len(labels)
    return 1 - (p ** 2).sum()

parent = gini(y_hand)
assert np.isclose(parent, 24 / 49)                         # 0.4898: four 0s, three 1s
table = {}
for t in (x_hand[:-1] + x_hand[1:]) / 2:                   # 1.5, 2.5, ..., 6.5
    L, R = y_hand[x_hand <= t], y_hand[x_hand > t]
    table[t] = (len(L) * gini(L) + len(R) * gini(R)) / len(y_hand)
assert np.isclose(table[3.5], 4 / 7 * 0.375)               # 0.2143: left pure, right 1,0,1,1
assert np.isclose(table[4.5], 4 / 7 * 0.375 + 3 / 7 * 4 / 9)
assert min(table, key=table.get) == 3.5
assert np.isclose(parent - table[3.5], 0.2755, atol=1e-4)  # the Gini gain of the best split

# The same split scored by entropy (information gain, in bits).
def entropy(labels):
    p = np.bincount(labels, minlength=2) / len(labels)
    p = p[p > 0]
    return -(p * np.log2(p)).sum()
ig = entropy(y_hand) - 4 / 7 * entropy(y_hand[x_hand > 3.5])
assert np.isclose(entropy(y_hand), 0.9852, atol=1e-4) and np.isclose(ig, 0.5216, atol=1e-4)

5. The naive split scan

The literal approach: for each feature, for each candidate threshold, loop over all rows to count the classes on each side, then compute the weighted Gini. With n rows that is up to n − 1 thresholds times an n-row pass, so O(n²) per feature and O(d·n²) per node.

def best_split_naive(X, y, K):
    """Try every feature and every midpoint, recounting the classes each time: O(d · n²)."""
    n, d = X.shape
    best = (gini_counts(np.bincount(y, minlength=K)), None, None)
    for f in range(d):
        vals = np.unique(X[:, f])
        for t in (vals[:-1] + vals[1:]) / 2:
            left = [0] * K; right = [0] * K
            for i in range(n):                              # a full pass per threshold
                (left if X[i, f] <= t else right)[y[i]] += 1
            nl, nr = sum(left), sum(right)
            imp = (nl * gini_counts(np.array(left)) + nr * gini_counts(np.array(right))) / n
            if imp < best[0] - 1e-12:
                best = (imp, f, t)
    return best[1], best[2]

def gini_counts(counts):
    counts = np.asarray(counts, float)
    return 1.0 - ((counts / counts.sum()) ** 2).sum()

It is correct, and it is a perfect reference for testing the fast version, because it is too simple to be wrong in the same way. On 400 rows and 4 features it took 535 ms for a single node.

6. The vectorised split search and the tree

def gini_of_counts(counts):
    """Gini impurity for every row of a (..., K) array of class counts."""
    n = counts.sum(-1, keepdims=True)
    return 1.0 - ((counts / n) ** 2).sum(-1)

class DecisionTree:
    """CART-style classifier. Nodes live in flat arrays, like scikit-learn's tree_."""

    def __init__(self, max_depth=5, min_samples_split=2, min_samples_leaf=1):
        self.max_depth, self.min_samples_split, self.min_samples_leaf = max_depth, min_samples_split, min_samples_leaf

    def fit(self, X, y):
        X = np.asarray(X, float)
        if np.isnan(X).any():
            raise ValueError("NaN in X: impute or encode missing values first")
        self.classes, y = np.unique(y, return_inverse=True)
        self.K = len(self.classes)
        self.feature, self.threshold, self.left, self.right, self.value = [], [], [], [], []
        stack = [(np.arange(len(y)), 0, None, None)]        # iterative: no recursion limit
        while stack:
            idx, depth, parent, side = stack.pop()
            node = self._new_node(np.bincount(y[idx], minlength=self.K))
            if parent is not None:
                (self.left if side == "L" else self.right)[parent] = node
            counts = self.value[node]
            if depth == self.max_depth or len(idx) < self.min_samples_split or counts.max() == len(idx):
                continue
            f, t = self.best_split(X[idx], y[idx])
            if f is None:                                   # no split reduces impurity
                continue
            self.feature[node], self.threshold[node] = f, t
            m = X[idx, f] <= t
            stack.append((idx[~m], depth + 1, node, "R"))
            stack.append((idx[m], depth + 1, node, "L"))
        self.feature, self.threshold = np.array(self.feature), np.array(self.threshold)
        self.left, self.right, self.value = np.array(self.left), np.array(self.right), np.array(self.value, float)
        return self

    def _new_node(self, counts):
        for a, v in ((self.feature, -1), (self.threshold, np.nan), (self.left, -1), (self.right, -1), (self.value, counts)):
            a.append(v)
        return len(self.value) - 1

    def best_split(self, X, y):
        n = len(y)
        parent = np.bincount(y, minlength=self.K)
        best_imp, best = gini_of_counts(parent), (None, None)   # a split must beat the parent
        n_left = np.arange(1, n)
        for f in range(X.shape[1]):
            order = np.argsort(X[:, f], kind="stable")
            xs, ys = X[order, f], y[order]
            left = np.cumsum(np.eye(self.K)[ys], axis=0)[:-1]    # counts left of each cut, (n-1, K)
            right = parent - left
            imp = (n_left * gini_of_counts(left) + (n - n_left) * gini_of_counts(right)) / n
            bad = (xs[1:] == xs[:-1]) | (n_left < self.min_samples_leaf) | (n - n_left < self.min_samples_leaf)
            imp[bad] = np.inf                                       # cannot cut between equal values
            i = int(imp.argmin())
            if imp[i] < best_imp - 1e-12:
                t = (xs[i] + xs[i + 1]) / 2
                if t == xs[i + 1]:                                  # midpoint of adjacent floats rounded up
                    t = xs[i]
                best_imp, best = imp[i], (f, t)
        return best

    def apply(self, X):
        """Leaf index for every row, walking all rows down the tree in lock-step."""
        X = np.asarray(X, float)
        node = np.zeros(len(X), dtype=int)
        while True:
            internal = self.feature[node] >= 0
            if not internal.any():
                return node
            rows, nd = np.nonzero(internal)[0], node[internal]
            go_left = X[rows, self.feature[nd]] <= self.threshold[nd]
            node[rows] = np.where(go_left, self.left[nd], self.right[nd])

    def predict_proba(self, X):
        v = self.value[self.apply(X)]
        return v / v.sum(1, keepdims=True)

    def predict(self, X):
        return self.classes[self.predict_proba(X).argmax(1)]

    @property
    def depth(self):
        d = np.zeros(len(self.value), int)
        for i in range(len(self.value)):                       # children are created after parents
            for c in (self.left[i], self.right[i]):
                if c >= 0:
                    d[c] = d[i] + 1
        return d.max()

The split search. For each feature: argsort with a stable sort; reorder the labels; turn them into one-hot rows with np.eye(K)[ys] (shape (n, K)) and take cumsum down the rows. Dropping the last row leaves n − 1 rows of left-child counts, one per possible cut; subtracting from the parent's counts gives the right-child counts. gini_of_counts works on whole arrays of counts, so the weighted impurity of every cut is computed at once. Three kinds of cut are disallowed by setting their impurity to infinity: cuts between equal values (both would go the same way), and cuts that would leave fewer than min_samples_leaf rows on either side. The best cut must beat the parent's impurity by a small tolerance, 1e-12, so that floating-point noise never produces a "split" that achieves nothing.

The midpoint guard. The threshold is the midpoint of the two values either side of the cut. But if those values are adjacent floating-point numbers, there is no number strictly between them, and the computed midpoint rounds to one of them. If it rounds to the upper one, the rule "go left if value ≤ t" sends both rows left and the split separates nothing. The code checks for that and uses the lower value instead. The test builds exactly this case with 1.0 and np.nextafter(1.0, 2.0). scikit-learn's splitter contains an equivalent guard.

Building without recursion. The textbook builder is recursive: split, then build the left subtree, then the right. A tree on adversarial or noisy data can be hundreds of levels deep, and Python's default recursion limit is 1,000 frames, so the builder here uses an explicit stack of (row indices, depth, parent, side) entries instead. It also stores nodes in flat arrays, the same layout as scikit-learn's tree_ object: feature[i] (−1 for a leaf), threshold[i], left[i], right[i], and value[i], the class counts of the training rows that reached node i. Flat arrays are compact, easy to serialise, and make vectorised prediction possible.

Stopping rules. A node becomes a leaf if it is at max_depth, has fewer than min_samples_split rows, is pure, or has no split that reduces impurity. max_depth=None means no depth limit (in Python, depth == None is never true).

Lock-step prediction. apply starts every row at node 0. In each pass it finds the rows still at internal nodes, compares each one's value of its node's feature with its node's threshold, and moves it to the left or right child with np.where. The loop runs once per level of the tree, so predicting a million rows on a depth-10 tree is ten rounds of array operations rather than a million Python walks. predict_proba then normalises each leaf's counts.

Tracing the build to depth 2

Let the builder continue on the hand example. The root splits at 3.5. The left child (rows 1-3, all class 0) is pure, so it becomes a leaf. The right child holds rows 4-7 with labels 1, 0, 1, 1 and Gini 0.375. Its candidates are 4.5 (left 1, right 0, 1, 1: weighted 3/4 × 0.444 = 0.333), 5.5 (left 1, 0 with Gini 0.5, right 1, 1 pure: weighted 2/4 × 0.5 = 0.25) and 6.5 (left 1, 0, 1, right 1: weighted 3/4 × 0.444 = 0.333). The best is 5.5, a gain of 0.375 − 0.25 = 0.125. Its left child, rows 4 and 5 with labels 1, 0, could be split once more at 4.5 into two pure leaves; with max_depth=2 it stays a leaf predicting 50/50, and the tree gets six of the seven training rows right. Allowing depth 3 gets all seven, by building a leaf for the single row that broke the pattern, which is exactly the kind of split that memorises noise. On this explicit stack, the order of work is: pop the root, push its right then left child; pop the left (a pure leaf), pop the right, split it, push its children; and so on. Pushing the right child first means the left subtree is finished first, matching the usual recursive order and giving the same node numbering.

Edge cases worth naming

A dataset with a single class produces a one-node tree whose leaf predicts that class with probability 1; the code handles it through the "pure node" rule. Duplicate rows with conflicting labels can never be separated, so the leaf holding them keeps a mixed distribution, which is the honest answer. A feature that is constant at a node offers no candidate thresholds (all cuts are between equal values) and is skipped. Labels that are strings or non-contiguous integers are mapped to 0..K−1 with np.unique, and the leaf counts always have length K, so probability columns line up with classes even if a leaf never saw some class. And a query row with a value exactly equal to a threshold goes left, because the rule is "≤"; that convention must be the same at training and prediction time, which flat arrays make easy to guarantee.

7. Testing it

The test block checks: a depth-1 tree on the hand example picks feature 0 at 3.5 and predicts probabilities [1, 0] on the left and [0.25, 0.75] on the right; on twenty random datasets with deliberately many tied values and three classes, the vectorised best_split returns exactly the same feature and threshold as the naive scan; a rectangle-shaped class is learned perfectly with max_depth=2; a fully grown tree memorises 500 random labels (347 nodes, depth 23) and then scores 49% on new random labels, the overfitting lesson in two lines; lock-step prediction matches a row-by-row walk; the adjacent-floats case splits correctly; string labels work; and min_samples_leaf=20 leaves no leaf smaller than 20 rows. Finally it times one node's split search both ways: 535 ms against 1.17 ms.

def walk(t, x):

    while t.feature[n] >= 0:

    return n

Why test with many ties? Ties are where split search goes wrong: thresholds between equal values, cumulative counts at the edge of a run of equal values, and the tie-break between equally good splits. Integer features drawn from 0 to 5 create long runs of equal values in 40 rows, which exercises all of that; continuous random features almost never would. Comparing against the naive scan, rather than against fixed expected values, turns every random dataset into a test case.

Switching the criterion to entropy

Only gini_of_counts changes. Entropy of a row of counts is −Σ p log₂ p, where the term for a class with p = 0 must be treated as 0 (its limit), not computed as 0 × log 0 = 0 × −inf = NaN. In vectorised form that means either masking zero proportions or using np.where(p > 0, p * np.log2(p), 0), with errstate silencing the harmless warning from evaluating the unused branch. The weighting, the cumulative counts, the threshold rules and the stopping rules are identical. In practice the choice rarely changes the tree; Gini avoids a logarithm per class per candidate, which is a measurable saving on large data, and that is why it is the usual default.

8. Numerical and practical traps

Trap 1: floating-point ties between splits. Two different cuts can have weighted impurities that are mathematically equal but differ in the last bit because of the order of operations. Comparing with a small tolerance and a deterministic tie-break (first feature, first threshold) keeps the vectorised and naive versions in agreement. Without it, the test above fails occasionally for reasons that have nothing to do with correctness.

Trap 2: the "no real improvement" split. If every candidate's weighted impurity equals the parent's, splitting adds a node and changes nothing. Requiring the best split to beat the parent by more than a tolerance prevents useless nodes and infinite growth on duplicated rows with conflicting labels (which no threshold can separate).

Trap 3: the midpoint that does not split. Described above: adjacent floats have no midpoint, so guard it.

Trap 4: NaN. NaN ≤ t is false for every t, so NaNs silently go right at every node, and argsort puts them at the end, so cumulative counts treat them as the largest values. The class refuses NaN at fit time. Real libraries either impute or learn a default direction for missing values.

Trap 5: recursion depth. A recursive builder on a deep tree raises RecursionError. The explicit stack avoids it.

Trap 6: integer division in probabilities. Leaf counts are integers; dividing them in Python 3 gives floats, but storing value in an integer array and normalising in place would not. The code stores counts as floats.

9. Complexity

At a node with m rows, the split search sorts each of d features (O(m log m)) and scans the cumulative counts (O(mK) for K classes), so O(d·m(log m + K)). Across one level of the tree the nodes partition the n rows, so each level costs about O(d·n log n), and a balanced tree of depth log n costs O(d·n log² n). Implementations that sort once at the root and maintain each feature's order when partitioning (by stable filtering) save the repeated sorts; scikit-learn's documentation gives O(n_features · n_samples · log n_samples) for such implementations. A degenerate tree that peels off one row per split has depth n and is much worse, one reason to cap depth. Memory is O(n) for the row indices plus the nodes. Prediction is O(depth) per row; in lock-step form, depth rounds of array operations over all rows.

10. Talking through the design

In the interview: define Gini with its meaning; define the weighted child impurity and the gain; show the cumulative-count scan and its complexity, contrasting it with the naive recount; name the threshold rules (midpoints between distinct values, the adjacent-float guard); list stopping rules and what each controls; mention the explicit stack and flat arrays; then tests against a brute-force scan and known shapes. Then volunteer the senior points: trees overfit when grown to purity, are unstable, cannot extrapolate, and are mostly used inside ensembles.

Pruning: grow big, then cut back

Stopping rules decide early, using only local information, and can stop too soon: a split with little gain may enable very useful splits below it (the XOR case). Cost-complexity pruning, from the CART book, grows the full tree first and then cuts it back. For a penalty α per leaf, it minimises training error plus α times the number of leaves. As α increases, the optimal subtree shrinks through a nested sequence, and each subtree is reached by collapsing the internal node whose "effective α" (the impurity it saves divided by the leaves it adds, minus one) is smallest. Cross-validation picks α. scikit-learn exposes this as ccp_alpha and cost_complexity_pruning_path.

Regression trees and the variance-reduction criterion

For a numeric target, the impurity of a node is the sum of squared errors around its mean, and a leaf predicts the mean. The SSE of a group equals Σy² − (Σy)²/n, so cumulative sums of y and y² along the sorted order give every cut's left and right SSE in one pass: the same trick with different running totals. The drill implements it, and it is exactly the inner loop of gradient boosting (mc6), where the targets are residuals.

Histogram-based split finding

Sorting at every node is the expensive part. LightGBM, XGBoost's hist mode and scikit-learn's HistGradientBoosting instead bucket each feature into at most 256 bins once, up front. At each node they build a histogram of class counts (or gradient sums) per bin in O(m) and scan the bins, so split finding costs O(m + bins) per feature with no sorting. A further trick: a child's histogram equals the parent's minus its sibling's, so only the smaller child needs to be counted. The precision lost by binning rarely matters, and the speed-up on millions of rows is large.

Categorical features done properly

A categorical feature with q levels has 2^(q−1) − 1 ways to split its levels into two groups, too many to try for large q. For binary classification (and for squared-error regression) there is a classic shortcut: sort the levels by their positive rate (or mean target) and only consider splits along that order, which is guaranteed to include the optimal partition, reducing 2^(q−1) candidates to q − 1. LightGBM uses a version of this. One-hot encoding instead turns one strong categorical split into many weak binary ones, which greedy search handles poorly.

Missing values: default directions and surrogates

XGBoost's sparsity-aware split finding evaluates each candidate twice, once sending all rows with a missing value left and once right, and stores whichever direction gave the larger gain as the node's default. At prediction time, missing values follow the default. CART's older approach finds surrogate splits, questions on other features that best mimic the primary split, and uses them in order when the primary feature is missing. Both beat imputing a constant, because they learn where "missing" belongs, and missingness is often informative in itself.

Why trees are unstable, and why that is useful

Because every split depends on the one above it, a small perturbation that changes the root split changes the entire tree. As a single model that is a weakness (high variance). For ensembles it is a strength: random forests grow many deep trees on bootstrap samples, choosing each split among a random subset of features, so the trees differ and their errors partly cancel when averaged. The variance of an average of B trees with pairwise correlation ρ and individual variance σ² is ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ²/B, which is why decorrelating the trees (the random feature subsets) matters as much as the number of trees.

Beyond axis-aligned splits

Because every split looks at one feature, a boundary along the diagonal x₁ = x₂ needs a staircase of many small rectangles, and the tree spends depth and data approximating something a single linear question could express. Oblique trees allow questions of the form w·x ≤ t, found by a small linear model at each node; they are more compact on such data but far more expensive to train and harder to read. In practice, people either add the useful combination as a feature (x₁ − x₂) or let an ensemble of many axis-aligned trees approximate the diagonal, which boosting does well.

Impurity versus permutation importance

Impurity importance adds up each feature's weighted impurity decreases over all nodes. It is computed on training data and favours features with many distinct values, which offer more thresholds to overfit with; a random ID column can rank highly in a deep tree. Permutation importance shuffles one feature in held-out data and measures the drop in score, which is honest about generalisation but shares credit unpredictably between correlated features. Knowing both biases is a common senior follow-up.

Where single trees run

Single trees ship when people must read the model: credit and insurance decision rules, triage protocols, eligibility checks, and as a "distillation" of a complex model into a few readable rules. Most of the time, though, trees run inside random forests and gradient-boosted ensembles (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost), which dominate tabular problems such as fraud, ranking features, demand forecasting and churn. The split search in this lesson is the core of all of them.

Failure modes and symptoms

Overfitting. Training accuracy near 100% with much lower validation accuracy, and leaves holding one or two rows. Fix with min_samples_leaf (often the most effective single knob), max_depth, pruning, or an ensemble.

Rules that change every retrain. A different root split after a small data change rewrites the model; a compliance team that reviewed last month's rules now sees different ones. Fix the random seed, prefer shallower trees, or freeze the top of the tree. If stability matters more than accuracy, say so explicitly when choosing the model.

Flat predictions outside the training range. A demand model trained on prices between £5 and £20 predicts the same as at £20 for a price of £40. Trees cannot extrapolate. Use a linear model for the trend and a tree on the residuals, or add monotonic constraints and features that encode the trend.

Leakage found by the tree. Trees are excellent at finding a single feature that perfectly predicts the label, including leaked ones (an "account closed" flag in a churn model). A root split with near-perfect gain is a red flag worth investigating before celebrating.

Missing values going one way by accident. In hand-written or older code, NaN comparisons send every missing value right. If a feature's missing rate changes in production (an upstream service starts failing), predictions shift silently. Monitor missing rates per feature.

Class imbalance in practice

With 1% positives, the parent Gini is only about 0.02, and most splits barely reduce it, so an unweighted tree often stops early and predicts the majority class everywhere. Class weights scale each row's contribution to the counts (a positive with weight 50 counts as 50 rows), which makes the impurity sensitive to the rare class again; in this lesson's code that means accumulating weights instead of one-hot ones in the cumulative sum. Balanced sampling per tree does the same job in forests. Either way, evaluate with precision-recall metrics, not accuracy.

Serving trees fast

A tree's prediction is a handful of comparisons, so serving cost is dominated by overhead: feature lookups, Python calls, and branch mispredictions in the CPU. Production systems compile trees into flat arrays (as here), into generated code with nested if-statements, or into specialised formats such as Treelite, and evaluate ensembles of thousands of trees for millions of rows per second. The flat-array layout in this lesson is the same idea in miniature, and it is also how a trained tree is serialised and versioned alongside its feature preprocessing.

What to monitor and tune

Track depth and leaf count across retrains, the minimum leaf size actually reached, the per-feature split counts (a feature suddenly used everywhere suggests leakage or a broken pipeline), and the distribution of predicted probabilities. Tune min_samples_leaf, max_depth and ccp_alpha by cross-validation, with class weights if classes are imbalanced, because Gini on imbalanced data happily builds trees that mostly predict the majority class.

Work it out

Turn the classifier into a regression tree: the split criterion becomes the reduction in squared error, and a leaf predicts the mean. Keep the split search vectorised: what replaces the cumulative class counts?

Answer

The sum of squared errors of a group is Σy² − (Σy)²/n, so cumulative sums of y and y² along the sorted order give the SSE of every left and right child in one pass.

def best_regression_split(X, y, min_leaf=1):
    n, best = len(y), (((y - y.mean()) ** 2).sum(), None, None)
    for f in range(X.shape[1]):
        o = np.argsort(X[:, f], kind="stable")
        xs, ys = X[o, f], y[o]
        s, s2 = np.cumsum(ys), np.cumsum(ys * ys)
        nl = np.arange(1, n); nr = n - nl
        sse = (s2[:-1] - s[:-1] ** 2 / nl) + ((s2[-1] - s2[:-1]) - (s[-1] - s[:-1]) ** 2 / nr)
        sse[(xs[1:] == xs[:-1]) | (nl < min_leaf) | (nr < min_leaf)] = np.inf
        i = int(sse.argmin())
        if sse[i] < best[0] - 1e-9:
            best = (sse[i], f, (xs[i] + xs[i + 1]) / 2)
    return best[1], best[2]

Xr = rng.random((300, 2))
yr2 = np.where(Xr[:, 1] > 0.7, 5.0, 1.0) + 0.1 * rng.normal(size=300)
f, t = best_regression_split(Xr, yr2)
assert f == 1 and abs(t - 0.7) < 0.05          # finds the step in feature 1

The rest of the tree is unchanged: leaves store the mean of their targets, and the prediction walk is identical. This vectorised SSE scan is exactly the inner loop of gradient boosting, where the targets are residuals, which is the next problem. One numerical caution: Σy² − (Σy)²/n cancels badly when the targets are large and nearly equal; centring y at the node first keeps it accurate.
