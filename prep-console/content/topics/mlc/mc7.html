In the early 2000s, email was drowning in spam, and one of the most effective early filters was not a clever rule set but a few dozen lines of probability. Count how often each word appears in spam and in legitimate mail; for a new message, ask which kind of mail is more likely to have produced its words. Words like "winner" and "unsubscribe" push toward spam, "meeting" and "attached" toward legitimate mail, and the filter combines all of them. That is Naive Bayes, and it is still the first thing many teams try for a new text-classification problem: support-ticket routing, review sentiment, language identification, filtering toxic text out of a training corpus. It trains in one pass over the data, needs no tuning beyond one smoothing number, and works with surprisingly few labelled examples. When a team has two hundred labelled tickets and a deadline, it is often the model that ships first and the baseline every later model must beat.

In an ML coding round it is a favourite because the algorithm is short but the implementation has three classic traps, each of which turns a working-looking classifier into a broken one. Multiply hundreds of small probabilities and the result underflows to exactly zero for every class, so the classifier can no longer choose: in this lesson's test, a 2,000-word document had log probabilities of −12,300 and −21,585, numbers that cannot exist outside log space. Estimate word probabilities by raw counting and any word unseen in a class makes the whole document impossible for that class. And loop over words in Python dictionaries and scoring is thousands of times slower than it needs to be: 537 ms against 0.11 ms for 200 documents here.

There is also a deeper interview question hiding in it. The "naive" assumption, that words are independent once you know the class, is obviously false, so why does the classifier work at all? And what is it, really? The answer (it is a linear classifier whose weights are set by counting) connects it to logistic regression from mc2 and explains both its strengths and its overconfident probabilities.

The lesson starts from Bayes' rule in plain words, adds the independence assumption and smoothing, and then reproduces, by hand and in code, the worked example from Jurafsky and Martin's textbook: five tiny movie reviews and the test sentence "predictable with no fun". It writes the dictionary version that follows the textbook's pseudocode, then the matrix version in which training and prediction are one matrix product each, demonstrates underflow and the log-space fix, proves the linear-classifier form numerically, and checks the estimates against maximum likelihood. It ends with complement Naive Bayes, the Gaussian variant, calibration and what changes at the scale of a billion documents.

Imagine two bags of words. One was filled by tearing up every negative movie review you have, the other by tearing up the positive ones. Someone hands you a scrap of paper with the words "predictable", "no" and "fun" and asks which bag it came from. A sensible way to decide: for each bag, ask how likely you would be to pull those three words out of it, and also how big the bag is to begin with (if negative reviews are more common, a random scrap is more likely to be negative). Multiply those together and pick the bag with the larger answer. That is the whole Naive Bayes classifier.

In probability language: the prior P(c) is how common class c is before you look at the document. The likelihood P(d | c) is how probable the document is if it came from class c. Bayes' rule says the probability of the class given the document, the posterior, is proportional to prior times likelihood: P(c | d) ∝ P(c) · P(d | c). The constant of proportionality, 1/P(d), is the same for every class, so to pick the best class you can ignore it.

The hard part is the likelihood of a whole document. There are far too many possible documents to count each one. The "naive" step is to pretend each word is drawn from the bag independently of the others, once the class is fixed. Then the probability of a document is just the product of its words' probabilities: P(d | c) = P(w₁ | c) × P(w₂ | c) × …. Each per-word probability is easy to estimate: count how often the word appears in that class's training text and divide by the total number of words in that class. A document is treated as a bag of words: only the counts matter, not the order.

Estimating those per-word probabilities is the only "training" there is. If negative reviews contain 14 words in total and "predictable" is one of them, the raw estimate of P(predictable | negative) is 1/14. If positive reviews contain 9 words and "fun" is one, P(fun | positive) is 1/9. Nothing is optimised, there is no learning rate and no iteration: one pass of counting over the training data produces the whole model, which is why Naive Bayes can be retrained in seconds on millions of documents and why adding a new labelled document just means incrementing some counts.

Two practical problems appear at once. First, a word that never appeared in one class's training text would get probability zero, and a single zero wipes out the whole product. The fix, smoothing, adds a small pseudo-count (usually 1) to every word's count, so nothing is ever impossible. Second, multiplying hundreds of numbers smaller than one produces a number too small for the computer to store, which becomes exactly zero. The fix is to add logarithms instead of multiplying probabilities, which changes nothing about which class wins.

Where the bag picture breaks: real words are not independent. "New" and "York" travel together, and a review that says "boring" once tends to say it again. Naive Bayes counts that correlated evidence as if it were independent, which makes its probabilities far too confident. Remarkably, the ranking of classes often survives, which is why the classifier works better than its assumption deserves.

1. The problem as it is usually asked

"Implement a Naive Bayes text classifier: fit(documents, labels) and predict(documents)." Clarify: multinomial (word counts) or Bernoulli (word present or absent)? How are documents tokenised (whitespace and lower-casing is fine for an interview)? What smoothing (add-one unless told otherwise)? How should unknown words be handled? Do they want probabilities or just labels? Then outline: build the vocabulary, count words per class, smooth and take logs, score in log space, normalise with log-sum-exp if probabilities are needed, and test.

2. The maths in plain words

Training is counting. For each class, count how many training documents it has (that gives the prior) and how many times each vocabulary word appears across all of its documents (that gives the word probabilities). To turn a class's word counts into probabilities, add α to every count and divide by the class's total word count plus α times the vocabulary size, so the probabilities still add up to one.

Prediction is adding. For a new document, start each class's score at the log of its prior, then for every word occurrence add the log of that word's probability in the class (skip words outside the vocabulary). The class with the highest score wins. If you want probabilities, convert the scores with the softmax, carefully.

3. The equations

prior:              P(c) = N_docs(c) / N_docs
word probability:   θ_{c,w} = P(w | c) = (count(w, c) + α) / (Σ_v count(v, c) + α·|V|)
decision:           ĉ = argmax_c  P(c) · Π_{i} θ_{c, wᵢ}
in log space:       ĉ = argmax_c  [ log P(c) + Σ_w x_w · log θ_{c,w} ]        x_w = count of w in the document
posterior:          P(c | d) = exp(s_c − logsumexp(s))                        s_c = the bracket above

Why is the word-probability formula the right estimate? With α = 0 it is the fraction of class c's tokens that are w, which is the maximum-likelihood estimate: of all word distributions for class c, it is the one that makes the observed training text most probable. The solution's tests check that numerically by perturbing the distribution and confirming the training log-likelihood always drops. Adding α is equivalent to assuming, before seeing any data, that every word has already been seen α times in every class (a Dirichlet prior in Bayesian terms), which pulls rare-word estimates away from zero.

Look at the log-space decision once more. Σ_w x_w · log θ_{c,w} is a dot product between the document's count vector and the class's vector of log word probabilities. Stack the classes' vectors into a matrix and every class's score for every document is a single matrix product. That is the whole vectorisation story, and it also shows that the decision is linear in the counts.

4. A worked example by hand

This is the example in Jurafsky and Martin's textbook (Appendix B of the third-edition draft), reproduced exactly. Five training documents:

classdocumenttokens
−just plain boring3
−entirely predictable and lacks energy5
−no surprises and very few laughs6
+very powerful2
+the most fun film of the summer7

Priors. Three of five documents are negative: P(−) = 3/5, P(+) = 2/5.

Vocabulary. The distinct words across all training documents: 13 in the negative documents ("and" appears twice) plus 7 new ones in the positive documents ("very" was already counted), so |V| = 20. Negative has 14 tokens in total, positive 9.

Test document "predictable with no fun". The word "with" is not in the vocabulary, so it is dropped for both classes. For the remaining three words, with add-one smoothing:

  • P(predictable | −) = (1 + 1)/(14 + 20) = 2/34; P(no | −) = (1 + 1)/34 = 2/34; P(fun | −) = (0 + 1)/34 = 1/34.
  • P(predictable | +) = (0 + 1)/(9 + 20) = 1/29; P(no | +) = 1/29; P(fun | +) = (1 + 1)/29 = 2/29.

Scores. Negative: 3/5 × (2 × 2 × 1)/34³ = 0.6 × 4/39,304 = 6.1 × 10⁻⁵. Positive: 2/5 × (1 × 1 × 2)/29³ = 0.4 × 2/24,389 = 3.28 × 10⁻⁵. Negative wins. (The textbook prints the positive score as 3.2 × 10⁻⁵, truncating rather than rounding 3.28; the decision is the same.) Normalising, the posterior probability of negative is 6.11/(6.11 + 3.28) = 0.65.

Notice what smoothing did. Without it, P(fun | −) would be 0/14 and P(predictable | +) 0/9, both classes would score exactly zero, and the classifier could not decide. With it, each unseen word costs its class a factor of about two relative to a seen word, a penalty rather than a veto. The first block asserts every number.

import math
import time
from collections import Counter
import numpy as np

# The hand-worked example: Jurafsky & Martin's five-review sentiment corpus, add-one smoothing.
train = [("-", "just plain boring"),
         ("-", "entirely predictable and lacks energy"),
         ("-", "no surprises and very few laughs"),
         ("+", "very powerful"),
         ("+", "the most fun film of the summer")]
test = "predictable with no fun"

vocab = sorted({w for _, d in train for w in d.split()})
assert len(vocab) == 20
tokens = {c: [w for cc, d in train if cc == c for w in d.split()] for c in "-+"}
assert len(tokens["-"]) == 14 and len(tokens["+"]) == 9
prior = {c: sum(cc == c for cc, _ in train) / len(train) for c in "-+"}      # 3/5 and 2/5

def p_word(w, c):
    return (tokens[c].count(w) + 1) / (len(tokens[c]) + len(vocab))         # (count + 1) / (N_c + |V|)
assert p_word("predictable", "-") == 2 / 34 and p_word("fun", "+") == 2 / 29

kept = [w for w in test.split() if w in vocab]                               # "with" is unseen: dropped
assert kept == ["predictable", "no", "fun"]
score = {c: prior[c] * math.prod(p_word(w, c) for w in kept) for c in "-+"}
assert math.isclose(score["-"], 3 / 5 * 2 * 2 * 1 / 34 ** 3)                 # 6.1 × 10⁻⁵
assert math.isclose(score["+"], 2 / 5 * 1 * 1 * 2 / 29 ** 3)                 # 3.2 × 10⁻⁵
assert f"{score['-']:.1e}" == "6.1e-05" and f"{score['+']:.1e}" == "3.3e-05"
assert max(score, key=score.get) == "-"
posterior_neg = score["-"] / (score["-"] + score["+"])
assert abs(posterior_neg - 0.651) < 1e-3

It is worth writing out the full count table once, because it shows how sparse the evidence is even in a toy corpus. In the negative class, "and" appears twice and every other negative word once (just, plain, boring, entirely, predictable, lacks, energy, no, surprises, very, few, laughs); in the positive class, "the" appears twice and very, powerful, most, fun, film, of and summer once each. Of the 20 vocabulary words, only "very" appears in both classes. Every other word has a zero count in one class, so without smoothing almost any new sentence would be impossible for one class or the other. Smoothing turns each of those zeros into a probability of 1/34 (negative) or 1/29 (positive). Notice the asymmetry: the positive class has fewer tokens, so its smoothed probabilities are all a little larger, one of the class-size effects Rennie and colleagues later corrected for.

Edge cases worth naming

A test document with no vocabulary words at all scores only its log prior, so it is assigned to the most common class, which is the right fallback but should be logged, since it means the model saw nothing. An empty class (a label in the list of classes with no training documents) has prior zero and log prior minus infinity; reject it at fit time. Duplicate training documents are legitimate evidence and simply count twice. Very long documents are handled fine in log space, but they make posteriors more extreme, because more independent-looking evidence accumulates, so long and short documents' probabilities are not comparable; length normalisation (dividing counts by document length) is a common fix when that matters. And labels can be any type: np.unique sorts them, so remember that column order follows sorted labels, which is why the test compares the positive class first.

5. The naive implementation

The first version follows the textbook's TRAIN NAIVE BAYES and TEST NAIVE BAYES pseudocode almost line for line: a log prior per class, a Counter of words per class, a dictionary of log likelihoods keyed by (word, class), and a scoring loop that adds the log prior and then each known word's log likelihood.

def train_nb_naive(docs, alpha=1.0):
    """Dictionary-and-loop Naive Bayes, the shape of the textbook pseudocode."""
    classes = sorted({c for c, _ in docs})
    V = sorted({w for _, d in docs for w in d.split()})
    logprior, loglik = {}, {}
    for c in classes:
        logprior[c] = math.log(sum(cc == c for cc, _ in docs) / len(docs))
        counts = Counter(w for cc, d in docs if cc == c for w in d.split())
        total = sum(counts.values())
        for w in V:
            loglik[w, c] = math.log((counts[w] + alpha) / (total + alpha * len(V)))
    return classes, set(V), logprior, loglik

def predict_nb_naive(model, doc):
    classes, V, logprior, loglik = model
    s = {c: logprior[c] + sum(loglik[w, c] for w in doc.split() if w in V) for c in classes}
    return max(s, key=s.get), s

It is already in log space, which is the important part, and it is perfectly fine for small data. Its cost is a Python dictionary lookup and addition per word occurrence per class: for 200 documents of 2,000 words and three classes, 1.2 million interpreted operations, 537 ms in the test.

6. The vectorised version

Represent the corpus as a count matrix X of shape (n_docs, |V|), row i holding document i's word counts, and the labels as a one-hot matrix Y of shape (n_docs, C).

class MultinomialNB:
    """Bag-of-words Naive Bayes on a (n_docs, |V|) count matrix, all in log space."""

    def __init__(self, alpha=1.0):
        self.alpha = alpha

    def fit(self, X, y):
        X = np.asarray(X, float)
        self.classes, yi = np.unique(y, return_inverse=True)
        Y = np.eye(len(self.classes))[yi]                          # (n, C) one-hot
        counts = Y.T @ X                                           # (C, |V|) word counts per class
        self.class_log_prior = np.log(Y.sum(0) / len(yi))
        smoothed = counts + self.alpha
        self.feature_log_prob = np.log(smoothed) - np.log(smoothed.sum(1, keepdims=True))
        return self

    def joint_log_likelihood(self, X):
        return np.asarray(X, float) @ self.feature_log_prob.T + self.class_log_prior   # (n, C)

    def predict_log_proba(self, X):
        jll = self.joint_log_likelihood(X)
        m = jll.max(1, keepdims=True)                              # log-sum-exp normaliser
        return jll - (m + np.log(np.exp(jll - m).sum(1, keepdims=True)))

    def predict_proba(self, X):
        return np.exp(self.predict_log_proba(X))

    def predict(self, X):
        return self.classes[self.joint_log_likelihood(X).argmax(1)]

def count_matrix(docs, vocab):
    index = {w: j for j, w in enumerate(vocab)}
    X = np.zeros((len(docs), len(vocab)))
    for i, d in enumerate(docs):
        for w in d.split():
            if w in index:                                         # unseen words are dropped
                X[i, index[w]] += 1
    return X

Training is one matrix product. Y.T @ X has shape (C, |V|): row c sums the count vectors of class c's documents, which is exactly the per-class word counts. Adding α and dividing each row by its sum gives the smoothed probabilities; the code subtracts logs instead of dividing, so feature_log_prob is computed directly in log space. Priors are the column sums of Y divided by the number of documents.

Prediction is one matrix product. X_test @ feature_log_prob.T has shape (n_test, C): entry (i, c) is Σ_w x_{i,w} log θ_{c,w}. Adding the log priors (broadcast across rows) gives the joint log-likelihood, and argmax over columns gives the labels.

Posteriors with log-sum-exp. To turn joint scores into probabilities, subtract the normaliser log Σ_c exp(s_c). Computing it naively would exponentiate numbers like −12,300, which underflow to zero, giving log(0) = −inf. Subtracting the row maximum first makes the largest exponent exactly 0, so the sum is at least 1 and its log is finite; then add the maximum back. That is the same log-sum-exp trick as mc2's logaddexp, generalised to C classes, and it is what scipy.special.logsumexp and torch.logsumexp do.

The count matrix. count_matrix builds X with a dictionary from word to column, dropping out-of-vocabulary words. In production X would be a sparse matrix (most documents use a tiny fraction of the vocabulary), and the same two matrix products work unchanged with a sparse library. Scoring the same 200 documents took 0.11 ms once the count matrix existed; building the matrix is itself a loop over tokens, so the honest end-to-end comparison is smaller, but scoring many classes or many models against one matrix is where the matrix form shines.

7. Underflow, demonstrated

The test generates 400 synthetic documents of 2,000 words each over a 3,000-word vocabulary, then computes one document's likelihood as a plain product of probabilities. Both classes come out as exactly 0.0. The same document's joint log-likelihoods are −12,300 and −21,585. To see why, recall that float64 can represent positive numbers down to about 2.2 × 10⁻³⁰⁸ normally and 4.9 × 10⁻³²⁴ with reduced precision (subnormal numbers); e^−12,300 is about 10⁻⁵³⁴², thousands of orders of magnitude smaller. In log space the numbers are ordinary, the difference between classes (about 9,300 nats) is exact, and the argmax is trivially correct. Underflow does not need long documents to bite: 100 words at probability 10⁻⁴ each already give 10⁻⁴⁰⁰.

8. Testing it

The test block checks: both implementations reproduce the textbook scores (the matrix version's classes are sorted, so positive comes first) and the 0.65 posterior; the underflow demonstration; the dictionary and matrix versions give identical joint log-likelihoods on 30 random documents with three classes; with α near zero the estimated word probabilities equal the count ratios, and random perturbations of them always lower the training log-likelihood, a direct check that the estimate is the maximum-likelihood one; the difference of the two classes' scores equals X·w + b with w the difference of log word probabilities (the linear-classifier form); posteriors sum to one and match PyTorch's log_softmax applied to the joint scores; and the timing comparison.

# 1. Both implementations reproduce the textbook numbers.
model = train_nb_naive(train)
label, s = predict_nb_naive(model, test)
assert label == "-" and math.isclose(math.exp(s["-"]), score["-"]) and math.isclose(math.exp(s["+"]), score["+"])

Xtr = count_matrix([d for _, d in train], vocab)
nb = MultinomialNB().fit(Xtr, [c for c, _ in train])
Xte = count_matrix([test], vocab)
assert nb.predict(Xte)[0] == "-"
assert np.allclose(np.exp(nb.joint_log_likelihood(Xte)[0]), [score["+"], score["-"]])    # classes sorted: + then -
assert abs(nb.predict_proba(Xte)[0, 1] - posterior_neg) < 1e-12

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
V, n_docs = 3000, 400
topic = rng.dirichlet(np.full(V, 0.1), size=2)                     # two "classes" of word usage
y = rng.integers(0, 2, n_docs)
X = np.array([rng.multinomial(2000, topic[c]) for c in y], float)

jll = MultinomialNB().fit(X, y).joint_log_likelihood(X[:1])[0]
assert np.isfinite(jll).all() and jll.max() < -5000                 # fine in log space

# 3. The naive and vectorised versions agree on a random corpus.
words = [f"w{j}" for j in range(50)]

# 4. Reference check: with alpha → 0 the estimates are the maximum-likelihood counts ratio,
#    and any perturbation of a class's word distribution lowers the training log-likelihood.
mle = MultinomialNB(alpha=1e-12).fit(X, y)

assert np.allclose(np.exp(mle.feature_log_prob[0]), counts0 / counts0.sum())
seen = counts0 > 0                                                  # words class 0 actually used

    return counts0[seen] @ np.log(theta[seen])
theta = counts0 / counts0.sum()
for _ in range(5):

    assert class_ll(q) < class_ll(theta)

w = nb2.feature_log_prob[1] - nb2.feature_log_prob[0]

assert np.allclose(jll2[:, 1] - jll2[:, 0], Xd @ w + b)

There is no gradient to check here, because nothing is optimised by gradient descent: the parameters are closed-form counts. The perturbation test plays the same role. It checks the claim the whole model rests on, "these estimates maximise the likelihood", against the definition of the likelihood itself.

Reading the model: which words matter

Because the model is a table of log probabilities, explaining it is easy, and interviewers like to ask. For two classes, the weight of word w in the linear form is log θ_{+,w} − log θ_{−,w}, the log of how many times more likely the word is under the positive class. In the textbook example, "fun" has weight log((2/29)/(1/34)) = log 2.34 = 0.85 toward positive, while "predictable" and "no" each have log((1/29)/(2/34)) = log 0.59 = −0.53, toward negative. The prior adds log(2/3) = −0.41. Summing, 0.85 − 0.53 − 0.53 − 0.41 = −0.62, and σ(−0.62) = 0.35: the posterior for positive, matching the 0.65 for negative from the full calculation. Sorting all words by weight gives the model's most indicative words for each class, which is the first thing to show a product team and the quickest way to spot a leaked label (a word like "refund_issued" at the top of a churn model).

9. Numerical and practical traps

Trap 1: multiplying probabilities. Underflow, as shown. Always sum logs, from the first line of code, rather than adding them later as a fix.

Trap 2: zero counts without smoothing. log(0) = −inf, and 0 × −inf = NaN in the matrix product when a document does not contain a word whose probability is zero. So even words absent from the document poison the score through NaN. With α > 0 nothing is ever zero. (scikit-learn's MultinomialNB has a force_alpha switch for this reason: with it turned off, any α below 10⁻¹⁰ is raised to 10⁻¹⁰.)

Trap 3: naive normalisation. exp(s) / exp(s).sum() with scores of −12,300 is 0/0 = NaN. Use log-sum-exp. The same applies to any code that averages probabilities across documents or models: average in probability space only after normalising each one in log space.

Trap 4: unknown words. Drop them at prediction time; do not give them a smoothed probability, which would penalise each class by a different amount depending on its total word count.

Trap 5: vocabulary mismatch. The count matrix at prediction time must use the training vocabulary's column order. Building a new vocabulary from the test documents silently scrambles every column. Store the vocabulary with the model.

Trap 6: floating-point counts. Counts are integers; accumulating them in float32 over a huge corpus loses exactness above about 16.7 million. Keep counts as integers or float64.

10. Complexity

Training reads every token once: O(T) for T training tokens, plus O(C·|V|) to smooth and take logs. As a dense matrix product, Yᵀ X costs O(n·C·|V|), but with a sparse X it is O(C · nnz), proportional to the number of non-zero counts. Prediction costs O(C) per token, or O(C · nnz) as a sparse matrix product. Memory is the C × |V| log-probability table: 20 classes and a million-word vocabulary is 160 MB in float64. Nothing is iterative, so training on a billion documents is a single pass of counting.

11. Talking through the design

In the interview: write Bayes' rule and drop the denominator; state the independence assumption and what it buys; give the smoothed estimate and why α; move to log space before anyone asks; then the two matrix products and log-sum-exp for posteriors; mention unknown words and the stored vocabulary; then tests. Finish with the linear-classifier view and the calibration caveat, which is where follow-ups usually go.

Why it works despite the false assumption

Classification needs the correct class to have the highest score, not correct probabilities. Domingos and Pazzani showed that Naive Bayes can be optimal under zero-one loss in many situations where its independence assumption is badly violated, because violations often inflate all classes' scores in similar ways, leaving the ranking intact. What correlated features do break is the probability scale: if "New" and "York" always appear together, their shared evidence is counted twice, and a dozen correlated words push posteriors to 0.99999. This is why scikit-learn's documentation calls Naive Bayes "a decent classifier" but "a bad estimator", and why its probabilities should be recalibrated before being used as probabilities.

Generative versus discriminative: Ng and Jordan

Naive Bayes and logistic regression produce the same family of decision boundaries (linear in the features), but estimate them differently. Naive Bayes fits each class's feature distribution separately (a generative model), and each weight is estimated from its own counts. Logistic regression fits the weights jointly to maximise the conditional likelihood of the labels (a discriminative model). Ng and Jordan showed that logistic regression has the lower asymptotic error, because it does not depend on the independence assumption, but Naive Bayes approaches its own asymptotic error with a number of training examples only logarithmic in the number of features, against linear for logistic regression. So with few labels Naive Bayes often wins, and with many, logistic regression does: a crossover worth drawing on a whiteboard.

Complement Naive Bayes and Rennie's fixes

Rennie and colleagues identified systematic problems with multinomial Naive Bayes on text. Classes with more training text get sharper, less smoothed estimates, and the classifier is biased toward them. Complement Naive Bayes fixes this by estimating each class's word distribution from all the other classes' documents (which is far more text for small classes) and choosing the class whose complement fits worst. They also showed that word burstiness (a word that appears once in a document is likely to appear again) makes raw counts overweight repeated words, and that transforming counts with log(1 + count), applying inverse document frequency, and length-normalising each document closes much of the gap to support vector machines. scikit-learn implements Complement NB.

Smoothing as a prior, and choosing α

Add-α smoothing has a precise Bayesian meaning. Put a symmetric Dirichlet prior with parameter α + 1 on each class's word distribution (a distribution over distributions that, for α > 0, favours spreading probability across all words); the most probable distribution after seeing the counts, the MAP estimate, is exactly (count + α)/(N + α|V|). So α says how many imaginary occurrences of every word you believe in before seeing data. With a 20-word vocabulary and 14 real tokens, α = 1 adds 20 imaginary tokens, more than the real ones, which is why the textbook example's probabilities are so flat. With a 100,000-word vocabulary and a million tokens per class, α = 1 adds a tenth as many imaginary tokens as real ones, still a lot for rare words. That is why tuned values for large corpora are often well below 1, chosen by validation accuracy or log-loss.

Bernoulli versus multinomial

Bernoulli Naive Bayes models each vocabulary word as present or absent, and, unlike the multinomial model, it also counts the absence of words as evidence: a class whose documents usually contain "invoice" is penalised when a document lacks it. That works well for short texts and small vocabularies; for long documents and big vocabularies, the multinomial model, which only looks at the words present and their counts, is usually better. The vectorised code changes only in the likelihood: x log θ + (1 − x) log(1 − θ) with binary x.

Gaussian Naive Bayes

For continuous features, model each feature within each class as a normal distribution with its own mean and variance. The log-likelihood of a feature value is −½ log(2πσ²) − (x − μ)²/(2σ²), summed over features. A feature that is constant within a class has zero variance, which divides by zero; scikit-learn adds var_smoothing (default 10⁻⁹) times the largest feature variance to every variance, the continuous analogue of add-α smoothing. The drill implements it. With per-class variances the boundary is quadratic, not linear.

Feature selection with the same statistics

Very large vocabularies dilute Naive Bayes with millions of rare, noisy words. The same counts give cheap feature-selection scores: the absolute log-count ratio above, mutual information between a word's presence and the class, or a chi-squared statistic from the word-by-class count table. Keeping the top few thousand words by such a score often improves accuracy and always shrinks the model, and because all the statistics come from one pass of counting, selection costs almost nothing extra.

NB features inside discriminative models

Wang and Manning's NB-SVM uses the Naive Bayes log-count ratios log θ_{1,w} − log θ_{0,w} as feature scalings for a linear SVM or logistic regression, combining Naive Bayes's good behaviour on rare words with the discriminative model's handling of correlations; for years it was a hard baseline to beat on sentiment tasks. It is a good example of the senior instinct to use a generative model's statistics as features rather than as the final classifier.

Where Naive Bayes runs

Spam and abuse pre-filters, ticket and email routing, language identification, quick baselines for any new text classification task, and large-scale data filtering for language-model training corpora, where a cheap classifier must score billions of documents. Its advantages in production are operational: training is counting, so it retrains in minutes and updates online; the model is a table of numbers that is easy to inspect ("which words push hardest toward spam?"); and inference is a sparse dot product.

Failure modes and symptoms

Overconfident probabilities. Downstream code that thresholds or multiplies probabilities behaves badly because everything is near 0 or 1. Fix: recalibrate on held-out data, or use the scores only for ranking.

Class-size bias. A routing model sends too many tickets to the biggest queue. Large classes have better-estimated word probabilities and larger priors. Fix: Complement NB, balanced priors, or threshold adjustment per class.

Adversarial word salad. Spammers append many innocent-looking words ("good word attacks") to push the score toward the legitimate class; because every word is independent evidence, enough of them will overwhelm the spam words. Fix: cap the number of tokens considered, use the most informative tokens only, combine with non-text features.

Vocabulary drift. New products, slang or campaigns introduce words the model has never seen; they are silently dropped, so the model is blind to exactly what changed. Monitor the out-of-vocabulary rate and retrain with an updated vocabulary.

Prior shift. The class mix in production differs from training: spam was 30% of the labelled set but is 80% of live traffic during a campaign, or a support queue that was rare becomes common after a product launch. Naive Bayes makes this easy to fix, because the prior is a separate term: replace the training log prior with an estimate of the current class mix, and every posterior updates correctly without retraining the word probabilities. Discriminative models have no such clean handle. Monitor the predicted class distribution against periodic labelled samples to know when to do it.

Tokenisation mismatch. Training lower-cased and stripped punctuation, serving did not: every word becomes unknown. Version the tokeniser with the model and test that a known training document scores identically in both paths.

A cost estimate: filtering a training corpus

Suppose a data team wants to drop low-quality web pages from a 2-billion-document crawl before language-model training, and a Naive Bayes quality classifier is the first stage. Scoring is one sparse dot product per document: with an average of 500 distinct words, that is 500 multiply-adds per class, a microsecond or so, so the whole crawl takes on the order of an hour of CPU time across a modest cluster, far less than tokenising the text in the first place. Training on a few hundred thousand labelled pages takes seconds. That economics, not accuracy, is why simple linear text classifiers remain the first filter in such pipelines, with more expensive models applied only to what survives.

What to monitor and tune

α (tune on validation data; values from 0.01 to 1 are common, smaller for large corpora), the vocabulary size (drop very rare and very common words), count transforms (binary, log, TF-IDF), and per-class thresholds. Monitor out-of-vocabulary rate, class distribution of predictions against labelled samples, and calibration if probabilities are used.

Work it out

Implement Gaussian Naive Bayes for continuous features: per class, fit a mean and variance for each feature, and classify with the sum of per-feature log densities. Why does scikit-learn add a small var_smoothing term?

Answer 
class GaussianNB:
    def __init__(self, var_smoothing=1e-9):
        self.var_smoothing = var_smoothing

    def fit(self, X, y):
        self.classes = np.unique(y)
        eps = self.var_smoothing * X.var(0).max()          # guards against zero variance
        self.mu = np.array([X[y == c].mean(0) for c in self.classes])
        self.var = np.array([X[y == c].var(0) + eps for c in self.classes])
        self.log_prior = np.log(np.array([(y == c).mean() for c in self.classes]))
        return self

    def predict_log_proba(self, X):
        ll = -0.5 * (np.log(2 * np.pi * self.var)[None] + (X[:, None, :] - self.mu[None]) ** 2 / self.var[None]).sum(-1)
        joint = ll + self.log_prior
        return joint - np.logaddexp.reduce(joint, axis=1, keepdims=True)

Xg = np.vstack([rng.normal([0, 0], 1, (200, 2)), rng.normal([3, 3], 1, (200, 2))])
yg = np.repeat([0, 1], 200)
g = GaussianNB().fit(Xg, yg)
assert (g.predict_log_proba(Xg).argmax(1) == yg).mean() > 0.95

Xz = np.c_[Xg, np.ones(400)]                    # a constant feature has variance exactly 0
assert np.isfinite(GaussianNB().fit(Xz, yg).predict_log_proba(Xz)).all()

A feature that is constant within a class has zero variance, and the Gaussian log-density then divides by zero. var_smoothing adds a tiny fraction (10⁻⁹ by default) of the largest feature variance to every variance, the continuous-feature analogue of Laplace smoothing. The normalisation uses np.logaddexp.reduce, which is log-sum-exp along an axis.
