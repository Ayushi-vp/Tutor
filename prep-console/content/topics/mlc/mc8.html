A search team has 50 million product embeddings, each a list of 1,024 numbers, and the vector index that serves them needs 200 GB of memory. Someone asks: do we really need all 1,024 numbers per product? Perhaps most of the variation between products lives in a few hundred directions, and the rest is noise. If we could find those directions and keep only the coordinates along them, the index would shrink four-fold and searches would speed up, with little loss in quality. Finding the directions along which data varies most, and re-expressing every point by its coordinates along them, is principal component analysis (PCA).

The same tool shows up everywhere data has more columns than it needs: compressing images and sensor readings, decorrelating features before a regression, visualising a high-dimensional dataset in two dimensions, finding the handful of directions in a language model's activations that carry most of the signal, and measuring the effective rank of a weight matrix. It is also the linear baseline that every fancier dimensionality-reduction method is compared against, and, through the low-rank idea at its heart, the intuition behind low-rank fine-tuning methods such as LoRA (mc21).

In an ML coding round, "implement PCA" checks whether you understand the linear algebra well enough to choose the right computation. The textbook recipe, compute the covariance matrix and take its eigenvectors, is correct mathematically and can be badly wrong numerically: forming the covariance squares the condition number of the data. In this lesson's float32 test, the covariance route reported a variance of −0.023 for a direction whose true variance is 9.7 × 10⁻⁷, a negative variance, which is impossible. The SVD of the centred data got it right to four digits. Interviewers also listen for centring (forgetting it is the most common bug), the arbitrary sign of components, how to compute explained variance, how to choose the number of components, and what to do when the matrix has ten million rows.

This lesson works PCA by hand on five points with a covariance chosen so that every number is clean, then implements it three ways: a naive version that builds the covariance with loops and finds eigenvectors by power iteration, the standard SVD version, and randomized SVD for large data. The tests check the hand example, agreement between all methods and PyTorch, the conditioning trap, the centring trap, and the identity that ties reconstruction error to the discarded variance. Along the way it shows which of the three is the right choice for which shape of data, the question a senior interviewer actually cares about.

Picture a cloud of points shaped like a rugby ball floating in a room. You want to describe each point with fewer numbers. The most informative single number would be each point's position along the ball's long axis, because that is where the points differ most. The second most informative is the position along the widest direction perpendicular to that, and so on. Those axes, ordered by how spread out the data is along each, are the principal components, and PCA is the procedure that finds them.

Three steps do it. First, centre the cloud: subtract the average point from every point, so the cloud sits around the origin. Second, find the directions of greatest spread. "Spread along a direction" means the variance of the points' projections onto it, and the direction with the largest variance is the first component; the second component is the direction of largest variance among those perpendicular to the first, and so on. Third, describe each point by its coordinates along the first few components, its scores. Keeping k of d components reduces each point from d numbers to k, and the variance along the dropped components is exactly the information thrown away.

How do you find the directions? There are two standard routes, and choosing between them is the core of this round. The first builds the covariance matrix, a d × d table whose entry (i, j) says how features i and j vary together, and finds its eigenvectors: directions the matrix only stretches, never rotates. The eigenvectors of the covariance are exactly the principal components, and their stretch factors, the eigenvalues, are the variances along them. The second route skips the covariance and applies the singular value decomposition (SVD) directly to the centred data, which writes any matrix as a rotation, a stretch along perpendicular axes, and another rotation. The second rotation's axes are the same principal components, and the stretches, squared and divided by n − 1, are the same variances.

The two routes agree mathematically and differ numerically. Building the covariance multiplies the data by itself, which squares the ratio between the largest and smallest spreads. If the largest spread is a thousand and the smallest a thousandth, that ratio goes from a million to a trillion, beyond what float32's seven digits can hold, and the small directions are lost in rounding. The SVD works on the data as it is. That is why the SVD is the default in every serious library, and why "PCA with the SVD" is the title of this problem.

A second way to see the same thing is compression. Keeping k components means storing k numbers per point plus the k × d component matrix and the mean, instead of d numbers per point. Reconstructing multiplies the k numbers back out. PCA chooses the k directions so that this round trip loses as little as possible, measured as total squared error, and the loss is exactly the variance along the directions you threw away. So "how many components do I need?" and "how much error can I accept?" are the same question, answered by the same table of variances.

Where the rugby-ball picture breaks: PCA only finds straight axes. If the data lies on a curved surface (a spiral, a sphere), PCA describes the straight-line shadow of it and needs many components for what is really a one- or two-dimensional shape. And "most variance" is not "most useful": the direction that separates two classes may have small variance.

1. The problem as it is usually asked

"Implement PCA: fit(X), transform(X), inverse_transform(Z), and report the explained variance ratio." Clarify: should features be standardised as well as centred? How should k be chosen, fixed or by a variance threshold? How big is X (it decides between full SVD, covariance and randomized SVD)? Is the data dense? Then outline: centre and store the mean, SVD of the centred data, fix component signs, compute variances and ratios, project and reconstruct, test.

2. The maths in plain words

Take a unit vector v (a direction). Project every centred point onto it: the projection is the dot product x·v, one number per point. The variance of those numbers is the spread along v. Written as a matrix expression, that variance is vᵀ C v, where C = XcᵀXc/(n − 1) is the covariance matrix. PCA asks for the v that makes vᵀCv as large as possible among unit vectors. The answer from linear algebra: the top eigenvector of C, and the maximum value is the top eigenvalue. Repeat the question among directions perpendicular to the first and you get the second eigenvector, and so on. Because C is symmetric, its eigenvectors are perpendicular and its eigenvalues are real and non-negative.

The SVD connects to this through one line of algebra. Write the centred data as Xc = U S Vᵀ, where U (n × r) and V (d × r) have orthonormal columns and S is a diagonal of non-negative singular values in decreasing order. Then XcᵀXc = V S Uᵀ U S Vᵀ = V S² Vᵀ, because UᵀU = I. That is exactly an eigendecomposition of XcᵀXc: eigenvectors V, eigenvalues S². Divide by n − 1 and you have the covariance's eigenvalues. So the rows of Vᵀ are the principal components and S²/(n − 1) are their variances, obtained without forming the covariance.

3. The equations

centre:            Xc = X − mean(X)                                  shape (n, d)
covariance:        C = XcᵀXc / (n − 1)                               shape (d, d)
PCA via eigh:      C = V Λ Vᵀ,   Λ = diag(λ₁ ≥ λ₂ ≥ … ≥ 0)
PCA via SVD:       Xc = U S Vᵀ   ⟹   C = V (S²/(n − 1)) Vᵀ,   λ_j = s_j² / (n − 1)

components:        rows of Vₖᵀ (the first k right singular vectors)    shape (k, d)
scores:            Z = Xc Vₖ = Uₖ Sₖ                                  shape (n, k)
reconstruction:    X̂ = Z Vₖᵀ + mean
explained ratio:   λ_j / Σ_i λ_i
reconstruction error:  ‖Xc − Z Vₖᵀ‖² = Σ_{j > k} s_j² = (n − 1) Σ_{j > k} λ_j

Two shapes deserve saying aloud. full_matrices=False in NumPy returns the reduced SVD, with U of shape (n, r) where r = min(n, d); the full SVD would make U an n × n matrix, which for a million rows is 8 TB. And the scores can be computed either as Xc @ Vₖ or as Uₖ * Sₖ; for new data only the first works, because new data has no U.

4. A worked example by hand

Five points: (5, 4), (−1, −2), (3, 0), (1, 2), (2, 1).

  • Centre. The mean is ((5 − 1 + 3 + 1 + 2)/5, (4 − 2 + 0 + 2 + 1)/5) = (2, 1). Centred: (3, 3), (−3, −3), (1, −1), (−1, 1), (0, 0).
  • Covariance. Sum of x²: 9 + 9 + 1 + 1 + 0 = 20. Sum of y²: also 20. Sum of xy: 9 + 9 − 1 − 1 + 0 = 16. Divide by n − 1 = 4: C = [[5, 4], [4, 5]].
  • Eigen-decomposition. For a 2 × 2 matrix [[a, b], [b, a]] the eigenvalues are a + b and a − b, with eigenvectors (1, 1)/√2 and (1, −1)/√2. Check the first: C·(1, 1) = (5 + 4, 4 + 5) = 9·(1, 1). So λ₁ = 9 along the diagonal, λ₂ = 1 across it.
  • Explained variance. PC1 explains 9/(9 + 1) = 90%.
  • Scores on PC1. (3, 3)·(1, 1)/√2 = 6/√2 = 4.243; (−3, −3) gives −4.243; the other three give 0. Their variance is (18 + 18)/4 = 9, the eigenvalue, as it must be.
  • Reconstruction with one component. Each point becomes its score times PC1, plus the mean. The first two points are reconstructed exactly; (1, −1) and (−1, 1) both collapse to the origin (i.e. back to the mean (2, 1) in original coordinates), each with squared error 2. Total error 4, which equals (n − 1) × λ₂ = 4 × 1.
  • Singular values. s_j = √((n − 1)λ_j): √36 = 6 and √4 = 2.

The first block asserts every number, including the singular values from NumPy's SVD.

import time
import numpy as np

# The hand-worked example: five 2-D points whose sample covariance is [[5, 4], [4, 5]].
P = np.array([[5.0, 4.0], [-1.0, -2.0], [3.0, 0.0], [1.0, 2.0], [2.0, 1.0]])
mu = P.mean(0)
assert mu.tolist() == [2.0, 1.0]
C = P - mu                                                 # (3,3) (-3,-3) (1,-1) (-1,1) (0,0)
cov = C.T @ C / (len(P) - 1)
assert cov.tolist() == [[5.0, 4.0], [4.0, 5.0]]

# Eigenvalues of [[a, b], [b, a]] are a ± b, with eigenvectors (1, 1)/√2 and (1, -1)/√2.
evals, evecs = np.linalg.eigh(cov)
assert np.allclose(evals, [1.0, 9.0])
pc1 = np.array([1.0, 1.0]) / np.sqrt(2)
assert np.isclose(abs(evecs[:, 1] @ pc1), 1.0)
assert np.isclose(9 / (9 + 1), 0.9)                       # explained-variance ratio of PC1

scores = C @ pc1                                           # projections onto PC1
assert np.allclose(scores, [6 / np.sqrt(2), -6 / np.sqrt(2), 0, 0, 0])
assert np.isclose(scores.var(ddof=1), 9.0)
recon = np.outer(scores, pc1) + mu                          # back to 2-D with one component
assert np.isclose(((P - recon) ** 2).sum(), 4.0)           # = (n − 1) × discarded eigenvalue 1

# The SVD of the centred data: singular values are sqrt((n − 1) λ) = 6 and 2.
S = np.linalg.svd(C, compute_uv=False)
assert np.allclose(S, [6.0, 2.0])

5. The naive implementation: covariance with loops, power iteration

The most literal implementation computes the mean and covariance with nested loops, then finds eigenvectors one at a time by power iteration: start from almost any vector, multiply by C, normalise, repeat. Each multiplication stretches the component along the top eigenvector by λ₁ and the others by smaller eigenvalues, so after enough steps only the top direction survives. Its eigenvalue is then vᵀCv. To find the next one, deflate: subtract λ₁ v vᵀ from C, which zeroes the found direction, and repeat.

def pca_naive(X, k, iters=500):
    """Covariance with explicit loops, then power iteration with deflation."""
    n, d = len(X), len(X[0])
    mean = [sum(row[j] for row in X) / n for j in range(d)]
    cov = [[sum((row[a] - mean[a]) * (row[b] - mean[b]) for row in X) / (n - 1) for b in range(d)]
           for a in range(d)]
    comps, variances = [], []
    for _ in range(k):
        v = [1.0 / (j + 1) for j in range(d)]               # any start not orthogonal to the answer
        for _ in range(iters):
            w = [sum(cov[a][b] * v[b] for b in range(d)) for a in range(d)]
            norm = sum(x * x for x in w) ** 0.5
            v = [x / norm for x in w]
        lam = sum(v[a] * sum(cov[a][b] * v[b] for b in range(d)) for a in range(d))
        comps.append(v); variances.append(lam)
        cov = [[cov[a][b] - lam * v[a] * v[b] for b in range(d)] for a in range(d)]   # deflate
    return np.array(comps), np.array(variances), np.array(mean)

Power iteration converges at a rate set by the ratio of the second eigenvalue to the first: the unwanted component shrinks by λ₂/λ₁ each step. For the hand example that is 1/9, so a dozen steps give full precision; if two eigenvalues are nearly equal, it can take thousands. Deflation accumulates error with each component found. It is a fine teaching implementation and the basis of methods like PageRank, but not how you would compute a full PCA.

Trace it on the hand example. Start from v₀ = (1, 0.5). In the eigenbasis, its PC1 part is (1 + 0.5)/√2 = 1.061 and its PC2 part (1 − 0.5)/√2 = 0.354, a ratio of 1/3. Multiplying by C gives (5 + 2, 4 + 2.5) = (7, 6.5): the PC1 part was multiplied by 9 and the PC2 part by 1, so the ratio is now 1/27. Another multiplication gives (61, 60.5), ratio 1/243, already within half a degree of the diagonal. After normalising, vᵀCv approaches 9. Then deflation subtracts 9 · (1, 1)(1, 1)ᵀ/2 = [[4.5, 4.5], [4.5, 4.5]] from C, leaving [[0.5, −0.5], [−0.5, 0.5]], whose only non-zero eigenvalue is 1 along (1, −1)/√2: PC2. The naive function reproduces both, as the tests check.

6. The vectorised version with the SVD

class PCA:
    def __init__(self, n_components, solver="svd", seed=0):
        self.k, self.solver, self.seed = n_components, solver, seed

    def fit(self, X):
        X = np.asarray(X, float)
        self.mean = X.mean(0)
        Xc = X - self.mean
        n = len(X)
        if self.solver == "svd":
            U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(Xc, full_matrices=False)
        elif self.solver == "randomized":
            S, Vt = randomized_svd(Xc, self.k, seed=self.seed)
        else:                                                   # "covariance": eigh of XᵀX/(n−1)
            ev, V = np.linalg.eigh(Xc.T @ Xc / (n - 1))
            order = ev.argsort()[::-1]
            S, Vt = np.sqrt(np.clip(ev[order], 0, None) * (n - 1)), V[:, order].T
        # singular vectors have an arbitrary sign; fix it so results are reproducible
        signs = np.sign(Vt[np.arange(len(Vt)), np.abs(Vt).argmax(1)])
        Vt = Vt * signs[:, None]
        var = S ** 2 / (n - 1)                                   # variance along each component
        total = (Xc ** 2).sum() / (n - 1)                        # total variance, from the data itself
        self.components = Vt[:self.k]                            # (k, d), orthonormal rows
        self.explained_variance = var[:self.k]
        self.explained_variance_ratio = var[:self.k] / total
        self.singular_values = S[:self.k]
        return self

    def transform(self, X):
        return (np.asarray(X, float) - self.mean) @ self.components.T     # (n, k) scores

    def inverse_transform(self, Z):
        return Z @ self.components + self.mean

def randomized_svd(A, k, oversample=10, n_iter=4, seed=0):
    """Halko, Martinsson & Tropp: sketch the range of A with a random projection, then do a
    small exact SVD. Power iterations sharpen the spectrum when singular values decay slowly."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    Q = np.linalg.qr(A @ rng.normal(size=(A.shape[1], k + oversample)))[0]   # (n, k + p)
    for _ in range(n_iter):
        Q = np.linalg.qr(A.T @ Q)[0]
        Q = np.linalg.qr(A @ Q)[0]                               # re-orthonormalise each pass
    Ub, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(Q.T @ A, full_matrices=False)       # (k + p, d): small
    return S[:k], Vt[:k]

Fit. Store the mean, centre, and call np.linalg.svd(Xc, full_matrices=False). The rows of Vt are the components in order of decreasing singular value.

Sign fixing. An eigenvector is only defined up to sign: if v works, so does −v, and the SVD may return either. Different LAPACK builds, thread counts and NumPy versions can differ, so stored models and tests would flip. The code flips each component so that its largest-magnitude entry is positive, the same convention scikit-learn's svd_flip follows in spirit.

Variances. S²/(n − 1) gives each component's variance. The ratio divides by the total variance, computed here directly from the centred data (Σ Xc²/(n − 1)) rather than as the sum of the returned singular values, so it stays correct when a truncated or randomized solver returns only the top k.

Transform and inverse. transform subtracts the stored training mean (never the new data's mean) and multiplies by componentsᵀ; inverse_transform multiplies scores by the components and adds the mean back.

Other solvers. The covariance option forms XcᵀXc/(n − 1) and calls eigh (ascending eigenvalues, so it reverses them, and clips tiny negative values to zero before the square root), which is how scikit-learn's covariance_eigh works. The randomized option calls randomized_svd, described in the deep section.

7. The conditioning trap, measured

The test builds 2,000 points in 3-D whose spreads along three perpendicular (but rotated, so not axis-aligned) directions are 1,000, 1 and 0.001, stores them in float32, and asks both routes for the smallest variance. The true value, computed in float64, is 9.74 × 10⁻⁷. The SVD of the float32 data returns 9.74 × 10⁻⁷. The covariance route returns −0.023: a negative variance, which is impossible, and more than ten thousand times larger in magnitude than the true value.

Why? The covariance's eigenvalues span from about 10⁶ (1,000²) to 10⁻⁶ (0.001²), a ratio of 10¹². Float32 represents numbers to about 7 significant digits, so in a matrix whose largest entries are around 10⁶, rounding errors in each entry are around 0.1, and anything smaller than that, including the entire smallest eigenvalue, is noise. The SVD's singular values span 10³ to 10⁻³ (the square roots), a ratio of 10⁶, which float32 can just about resolve. The rotation matters: if the directions were exactly aligned with the axes, the covariance would be diagonal and rounding would not mix the large and small parts, which is why naive tests on axis-aligned toy data never reveal the problem. scikit-learn's documentation states the same caveat for its covariance solver: compared with the full SVD it "effectively doubles the condition number and is therefore less numerical stable" (doubling it in digits, i.e. squaring it).

8. Testing it

The test block checks: the SVD class and the naive loop version reproduce the hand example (PC1, variances 9 and 1, ratio 0.9); on 500 points that lie near a 2-D plane in 5-D, two components are orthonormal, explain over 99.9% of the variance, and reconstruct every point to within 0.1, and the variance of the scores equals the explained variance; the covariance, randomized and naive solvers agree with the SVD (variances to 10⁻⁶ relative, components up to sign); PyTorch's torch.linalg.svd gives the same singular values; the float32 conditioning trap behaves as described; and data centred at (50, 0) shows the uncentred SVD pointing at the mean while the class (which centres) finds the true direction.

Components are compared with |A Bᵀ| ≈ I rather than A ≈ B, because the sign of each component is arbitrary between methods even after fixing a convention (two solvers may disagree on which entry is largest when entries are close). Comparing absolute dot products tests what matters, that the directions are the same.

Choosing k from the explained-variance curve

The drill turns the reconstruction identity into a rule for k: compute all components once, take the cumulative sum of the explained-variance ratios, and find the first index that crosses the threshold with np.searchsorted. On the drill's data, with spreads 6, 4, 2, 1, 0.5 and 0.1, the cumulative ratios were 0.633, 0.898, 0.978 and 0.996, so k = 3 for 95%. Two cautions. A threshold on variance is a threshold on squared reconstruction error, not on what a downstream model needs, so for features feeding a classifier, choose k by the classifier's validation score. And on standardised data every feature starts with variance 1, so "95% of the variance" means something different than on raw data; report which you used.

Edge cases worth naming

More features than rows (d > n, common for gene expression or embeddings of a small set) gives at most n − 1 non-zero components after centring, because n centred points span at most n − 1 dimensions; the reduced SVD handles it, and asking for more components should raise an error or return zeros explicitly. A constant feature has zero variance and contributes nothing, but if you standardise you divide by zero, so drop it or guard the division. Duplicate rows are fine; they weight those points more. A single row cannot be centred meaningfully (every centred value is zero), so require at least two. And two equal eigenvalues make the corresponding components ill-defined: any rotation within their plane is equally valid, so tests and downstream code must compare the subspace, not individual vectors.

9. Numerical and practical traps

Trap 1: forming the covariance in low precision. Shown above. Use the SVD, or form the covariance in float64 from centred data.

Trap 2: not centring, or centring with the wrong mean. Fit must subtract the training mean; transform must subtract the same stored mean, not the mean of the batch being transformed (a batch of one would always map to zero).

Trap 3: negative eigenvalues from eigh. Rounding can make a true zero eigenvalue slightly negative; taking its square root gives NaN. Clip at zero, as the covariance solver does.

Trap 4: eigh's ascending order. np.linalg.eigh returns eigenvalues in ascending order, the opposite of what PCA wants. Reverse both values and vectors together.

Trap 5: the sign. Fix it, or downstream code that relies on "positive score means more of X" breaks after a library upgrade.

Trap 6: the full SVD. full_matrices=True (NumPy's default) builds an n × n U; on 100,000 rows that is 80 GB. Always pass full_matrices=False.

Trap 7: n − 1 versus n. Sample variance divides by n − 1; NumPy's var divides by n unless ddof=1. The ratio is unaffected, but explained variances compared with another library will be off by n/(n − 1).

10. Complexity

The thin SVD of an n × d matrix costs O(n·d·min(n, d)); for tall data (n ≫ d) that is O(nd²), with memory O(nd). The covariance route costs O(nd²) to form XcᵀXc plus O(d³) for eigh, with a smaller constant and only O(d²) extra memory, which is why it is faster for tall, narrow data. Randomized SVD for k components costs O(ndk) per pass over the data, with a handful of passes. Power iteration costs O(d²) per step on the covariance (or O(nd) per step if applied as Xcᵀ(Xc v) without forming it). Transforming m new points costs O(mdk). For 10 million rows and 1,000 features, the full SVD is about 10¹³ operations; the covariance route the same order for forming XcᵀXc, but in one streaming pass; randomized SVD with k = 100 about 10¹² per pass.

11. Talking through the design

In the interview: say the three steps (centre, decompose, project); write Xc = USVᵀ and derive C = V(S²/(n − 1))Vᵀ in one line, which is the key insight; explain why the SVD beats the covariance numerically and when the covariance is still used; fix the sign; compute ratios; mention full_matrices=False; test with low-rank data and against eigh. Then offer randomized SVD for scale and the reconstruction-error identity for choosing k.

Randomized SVD

Halko, Martinsson and Tropp's randomized SVD rests on a simple idea. If X is close to rank k, then multiplying it by a random d × (k + p) matrix Ω gives n × (k + p) columns that, with high probability, span almost exactly the same space as X's top k left singular vectors, because a random combination of columns is unlikely to miss any dominant direction. Orthonormalise those columns (a QR decomposition) to get Q; then X ≈ Q(QᵀX), and the SVD of the small (k + p) × d matrix QᵀX gives the top singular values and right singular vectors. The oversampling p (5 to 10 extra columns) buys accuracy cheaply. When singular values decay slowly, the random sketch also picks up the tail, so the method applies a few power iterations, replacing X with (XXᵀ)^q X, which raises singular values to the power 2q + 1 and makes the top ones dominate; re-orthonormalising between multiplications (as the solution does) prevents the small directions from being lost to rounding. scikit-learn's 'auto' policy uses this method when the data is larger than 500 × 500 and fewer than 80% of the components are requested.

Why the top-k SVD is the best rank-k approximation

The Eckart-Young theorem says that truncating the SVD to the top k singular values gives the matrix of rank k closest to X, in both the Frobenius norm (sum of squared entries) and the spectral norm, with errors √(Σ_{j>k} s_j²) and s_{k+1}. That is the precise sense in which PCA is optimal: no other k-dimensional linear projection reconstructs the centred data with smaller squared error. It is also the theoretical basis for low-rank compression of weight matrices and, in spirit, for LoRA's assumption that fine-tuning updates have low rank.

Whitening, and PCA as a preprocessing step

Dividing each component's scores by the square root of its variance gives whitened data: uncorrelated features with unit variance. That helps models sensitive to feature scale and correlation, and it is used to make sentence embeddings more isotropic so that cosine similarities spread out. The cost is that whitening amplifies the small-variance directions, which are often mostly noise, so it is usually combined with dropping those directions first. For a regression, PCA followed by least squares on the top components is principal component regression, a cousin of ridge (mc1's deep section showed ridge shrinks each SVD direction by s²/(s² + nα); PCR keeps the top directions fully and drops the rest).

Incremental and streaming PCA

When the data does not fit in memory there are two routes. Accumulate the sufficient statistics (column sums and XᵀX) in one pass, in float64, and eigendecompose the d × d result: exact up to the conditioning caveat, fine for d up to tens of thousands. Or update an SVD incrementally from mini-batches (scikit-learn's IncrementalPCA), which keeps memory at O(batch × d + k × d) and avoids forming XᵀX. For streams with drift, Oja's rule, a stochastic version of power iteration, updates the components a little with each new point.

Kernel PCA and the limits of linearity

PCA can only find linear structure. Kernel PCA replaces the covariance with a kernel matrix of pairwise similarities (for instance Gaussian), centred in feature space, and eigendecomposes that n × n matrix, finding non-linear directions such as the angle around a ring. It costs O(n³), so it is limited to tens of thousands of points. Methods like t-SNE and UMAP go further for visualisation, but they preserve neighbourhoods rather than variance and should not be used for compression or as features without care.

PCA on embeddings: anisotropy and outlier dimensions

Sentence and token embeddings from transformers are typically anisotropic: a few directions carry a large share of the variance, often shared by all inputs (a common offset or a frequency-related direction), and individual coordinates can have outlier magnitudes. Two consequences matter in practice. Cosine similarities between unrelated texts come out high because they share the dominant direction, which compresses the useful range of scores; centring and removing the top one or two components, or whitening, spreads them out. And quantising embeddings without accounting for outlier dimensions wastes precision; a PCA rotation spreads energy more evenly before quantisation. Both are cheap uses of exactly the code in this lesson.

Comparing subspaces: principal angles

When two runs, two libraries or two months of data give slightly different components, comparing them vector by vector is misleading: signs flip, and when eigenvalues are close, the vectors rotate within their shared plane while the plane itself barely moves. The right comparison is between subspaces. Take the two k × d component matrices A and B with orthonormal rows, compute the singular values of A Bᵀ, and their arc-cosines are the principal angles between the subspaces. All angles near zero mean the same subspace whatever the individual vectors did; one large angle means a genuinely new direction of variation has appeared. This is the metric to alert on when PCA is refitted on fresh data.

Probabilistic PCA

Tipping and Bishop showed PCA is the maximum-likelihood solution of a latent-variable model in which each point is a linear map of a k-dimensional Gaussian latent variable plus isotropic noise. That view gives PCA a likelihood (useful for choosing k on held-out data), handles missing values through EM, and is the linear special case of the variational autoencoders in dl10.

Where PCA runs

Embedding compression for vector search (often PCA to a few hundred dimensions, then quantisation), feature decorrelation and denoising in tabular pipelines, exploratory analysis and dashboards, anomaly detection by reconstruction error (points poorly explained by the top components are unusual), sensor and image compression, and analysis of model internals (activation directions, weight-matrix rank, training dynamics).

Failure modes and symptoms

A component that is just one feature. PC1 is almost entirely "revenue in pence" because that column's variance dwarfs the others. Cause: unscaled features. Fix: standardise, or deliberately choose units.

Results that flip between runs. Downstream dashboards show a component's meaning reversing after a library upgrade. Cause: sign ambiguity. Fix: a sign convention, stored with the model.

Silent quality loss after compression. Retrieval recall drops after reducing embeddings from 1,024 to 128 dimensions, even though 128 components "explain 90% of the variance". Variance kept is not task performance kept; measure recall@k on a labelled sample for several k before choosing.

Drift. Components fitted on last year's data describe last year's variation. New behaviour falls into the discarded directions and shows up as rising reconstruction error, which is also the metric to monitor.

Memory blow-up. A job that calls the default full SVD on a tall matrix allocates an n × n matrix. Pass full_matrices=False, or use randomized or incremental PCA.

Anomaly detection by reconstruction error

A PCA fitted on normal traffic, transactions or sensor readings reconstructs normal points well and unusual points badly, because unusual points have energy in the discarded directions. The squared reconstruction error of each new point is then an anomaly score, cheap to compute (two small matrix products) and easy to explain ("this transaction is unusual along directions the model never needed"). Set the alert threshold from the distribution of errors on held-out normal data, for example its 99.9th percentile, and refit periodically so that slow, legitimate drift does not turn into a flood of alerts.

A cost estimate: compressing 50 million embeddings

Return to the opening example: 50 million vectors of 1,024 float32 numbers is 50 × 10⁶ × 1,024 × 4 bytes, about 205 GB. Fitting PCA does not need all of them; a random sample of one million (4 GB) is plenty to estimate a 1,024 × 1,024 covariance. Forming it costs 10⁶ × 1,024² ≈ 10¹² multiply-adds, under a minute on a multicore CPU in float64, and eigh on a 1,024 × 1,024 matrix takes well under a second; here the covariance route is the sensible choice, with float64 accumulation. Projecting all 50 million vectors to 256 dimensions costs 50 × 10⁶ × 1,024 × 256 ≈ 1.3 × 10¹³ multiply-adds, minutes on a GPU, and the stored index shrinks to about 51 GB. Then measure recall@10 against the full vectors on a few thousand labelled queries before shipping, because the 256 components that keep, say, 90% of the variance may keep more or less than 90% of the retrieval quality.

What to monitor and tune

The cumulative explained-variance curve (choose k from it or from downstream metrics), reconstruction error on fresh data over time, the stability of the top components between refits (principal angles between old and new subspaces), and, for compressed embeddings, retrieval recall against the uncompressed baseline.

The reference for this problem

scikit-learn's PCA documentation is the official reference for the solver choices discussed here. It describes the default 'auto' policy: "if the input data has fewer than 1000 features and more than 10 times as many samples, then the 'covariance_eigh' solver is used. Otherwise, if the input data is larger than 500x500 and the number of components to extract is lower than 80% of the smallest dimension of the data, then the more efficient 'randomized' method is selected. Otherwise the exact 'full' SVD is computed and optionally truncated afterwards." Of the covariance solver (added in version 1.5) it says it is "very efficient for n_samples >> n_features and small n_features", but that "compared to the 'full' solver, this solver effectively doubles the condition number and is therefore less numerical stable", which is the trade-off this lesson measured. The randomized solver implements "the method of Halko et al.", the second reference.

Work it out

Show that the reconstruction error of a k-component PCA equals the sum of the variances of the discarded components. Then write a function that picks the smallest k keeping at least 95% of the variance.

Answer

Because the components are orthonormal, the centred data splits into two orthogonal parts: the projection onto the kept components and the residual in the discarded ones. The squared error of the reconstruction is exactly the residual's squared norm, which is the sum of the discarded s². Divide by n − 1 and it is the sum of the discarded variances (the discarded eigenvalues).

def k_for_variance(X, keep=0.95):
    full = PCA(min(X.shape)).fit(X)
    return int(np.searchsorted(np.cumsum(full.explained_variance_ratio), keep) + 1)

Xw = rng.normal(size=(300, 6)) * [6, 4, 2, 1, 0.5, 0.1]
full = PCA(6).fit(Xw)
for k in range(1, 6):
    pk = PCA(k).fit(Xw)
    err = ((Xw - pk.inverse_transform(pk.transform(Xw))) ** 2).sum() / (len(Xw) - 1)
    assert np.isclose(err, full.explained_variance[k:].sum())

k = k_for_variance(Xw)
assert np.cumsum(full.explained_variance_ratio)[k - 1] >= 0.95 > np.cumsum(full.explained_variance_ratio)[k - 2]

The identity is also why the explained-variance curve is the right tool for choosing k: the variance you drop is the squared reconstruction error you accept. With per-feature spreads 6, 4, 2, 1, 0.5 and 0.1, the variances are roughly 36, 16, 4, 1, 0.25 and 0.01, so the first three components keep about 98% and k = 3.
