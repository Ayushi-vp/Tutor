Imagine you have a language model that reads text well, and you want it to read a photo of a receipt, a chart in a report, or a screenshot of an error dialog. The language model only knows how to process a sequence of vectors, one per token. A photo is not a sequence: it is a grid of about 150,000 pixel positions, each with three colour values. Somebody has to decide how that grid becomes a sequence of vectors the transformer can consume, and that decision fixes almost everything downstream: how much detail survives, how many tokens the image costs, how slow it is, and whether the model can read the small print at all.

For most of the 2010s the answer to "how should a network look at images" was the convolutional neural network (CNN, dl6): slide small learned filters across the image, pool, repeat. CNNs work because they bake in two assumptions about images that are true: nearby pixels belong together, and a pattern means the same thing wherever it appears. But those assumptions also make CNNs a separate world from the transformers that took over language in 2017. Different architecture, different tricks, different scaling behaviour, and no easy way to put image features and word tokens in one sequence.

In 2020 a Google team asked a blunt question: what if you just cut the image into squares, treat each square like a word, and feed the result to a standard transformer with no image-specific machinery at all? Their Vision Transformer (ViT) lost to CNNs on the standard 1.3-million-image ImageNet dataset, and beat them when pretrained on 14 to 300 million images, while costing less compute to train. Within three years ViT encoders were inside almost every vision-language model, image generator and multimodal LLM. When an interviewer asks how GPT-style models "see", the honest first answer is: a ViT turns the image into patch tokens.

This lesson builds that pipeline from the pixels up. You will patchify a 224 × 224 image by hand and count its tokens, see why the patch projection is secretly a convolution, add position embeddings, run the standard transformer encoder, and calculate what the whole thing costs in FLOPs. Then the trade-offs: why ViT needs so much more data than a CNN, how DeiT and MAE fixed that, how to change resolution after training, why big ViTs grow strange "artefact" tokens, and what all of this means when the ViT is the eyes of a multimodal model. The public case is the original ViT paper, with its numbers checked against the source.

The idea in one picture: a mosaic of words

Think of a mosaic made of square tiles. From across the room you see a face; up close, each tile is a small block of colour. A Vision Transformer reads an image as a mosaic. It cuts the image into a grid of equal, non-overlapping square patches, each P pixels on a side (P is the patch size, typically 16 or 14). Each patch is then treated the way a language model treats a word: it becomes one token, one vector in a sequence.

Concretely, take a colour image of 224 × 224 pixels, the classic ImageNet size. With P = 16 the image is 224 / 16 = 14 patches wide and 14 patches tall, so the grid holds 14 × 14 = 196 patches. Each patch is a 16 × 16 block of pixels with 3 colour channels (red, green, blue), so it contains 16 × 16 × 3 = 768 numbers. Flatten those 768 numbers into a single list, multiply by one learned matrix, and you have the patch's token vector, of whatever length the transformer uses (its width D; 768 in the base-sized model). Do it for all 196 patches and the image is now a sequence of 196 vectors: exactly the kind of input a transformer encoder (lm3) takes.

Two more ingredients complete the input. First, attention has no sense of order (lm2, lm4): shuffle the tokens and it computes the same thing, shuffled. So each token gets a learned position embedding added to it, one per grid position, so the model can know that a patch came from the top-left corner. Second, for classification, ViT borrows a trick from the BERT language model: it prepends one extra learned vector called the [CLS] token (for "classification"). It holds no image content, but because it attends to every patch through all the layers, its final output works as a summary of the whole image. That makes 197 tokens. From here on there is nothing visual about the model at all: it is the same stack of attention and MLP blocks used for text.

The analogy, and where it breaks

The mosaic analogy is right about the tokenisation and wrong about the reading. When you look at a mosaic, your eyes naturally group neighbouring tiles. ViT does not have that habit built in. In the very first layer, the patch in the top-left corner can attend just as easily to the patch in the bottom-right as to the one beside it, and nothing in the architecture says "neighbours matter more". It is more like being handed 196 tiles in a bag, each labelled with its grid coordinates, and having to learn from experience which combinations make a face. That is the crucial difference from a CNN, and it explains ViT's whole personality: it is hungry for data (it has to learn what a CNN gets for free) and it scales well (it is not stuck with the CNN's assumptions when the data says otherwise).

The two numbers to carry around

The first is the token count, N = (H / P) × (W / P). It grows with the square of the image side: double the side from 224 to 448 pixels and the patches go from 196 to 784. The second is attention's cost, which grows with the square of N (lm2). So doubling the side multiplies attention-score work by about sixteen. In a multimodal model, N is also the number of tokens the image occupies in the language model's context, which is why "what resolution do we feed the encoder?" is a cost and latency question as much as an accuracy one.

If you stop reading here you have a correct picture: patches become tokens through one learned matrix, positions are added, a stock transformer does the rest, and resolution is paid for quadratically.

1. What an image is, as numbers

A digital colour image is a three-dimensional array of shape H × W × C: height in pixels, width in pixels, and C = 3 channels for red, green and blue. Each entry is an intensity, stored as an integer from 0 to 255 and, before entering a network, converted to a floating-point number and normalised: divide by 255, then subtract a per-channel mean and divide by a per-channel standard deviation, so each channel is centred near zero with spread near one. A 224 × 224 image therefore holds 224 × 224 × 3 = 150,528 numbers. Libraries differ in the order of the axes: NumPy and PIL usually use H × W × C ("channels last"), PyTorch uses C × H × W ("channels first"). The arithmetic below uses channels last.

A transformer wants a matrix of shape N × D: N tokens, each a vector of length D. The job of the front end is to go from H × W × C to N × D. Using each pixel as a token would give N = 50,176 for a 224 × 224 image, and attention over 50,176 tokens needs 50,176² ≈ 2.5 billion scores per head per layer: hopeless. Grouping pixels into patches cuts N by a factor of P², which for P = 16 is 256.

2. Patchify: from a grid to a sequence

Choose P so that it divides H and W exactly (images are resized or padded to make this true). The grid has H/P rows and W/P columns of patches. Read them in raster order: left to right along the top row, then the next row, like reading a page. Patch number k sits at grid row k // (W/P) and column k % (W/P), and covers pixel rows from P·row to P·row + P − 1 and pixel columns from P·col to P·col + P − 1.

Worked example, 224 × 224 × 3 image, P = 16:

grid          = (224/16) × (224/16) = 14 × 14 = 196 patches
numbers/patch = 16 × 16 × 3        = 768
patch 0       = pixels rows 0–15,    cols 0–15     (top-left)
patch 15      = grid row 1, col 1   → pixels rows 16–31, cols 16–31
patch 195     = grid row 13, col 13 → pixels rows 208–223, cols 208–223 (bottom-right)
check         : 196 × 768 = 150,528 = 224 × 224 × 3   (nothing lost, nothing duplicated)

In NumPy this is a reshape and a transpose, with no copying of pixel values beyond the final reshape:

x = img.reshape(14, 16, 14, 16, 3)   # (grid row, row-in-patch, grid col, col-in-patch, channel)
x = x.transpose(0, 2, 1, 3, 4)        # (grid row, grid col, 16, 16, 3)
patches = x.reshape(196, 768)         # one flattened patch per row

The transpose is the step people get wrong: reshaping straight from (224, 224, 3) to (196, 768) would take 768 consecutive numbers, which is 256 pixels from one image row, not a 16 × 16 square. The lab checks this by comparing patch 15 with the slice img[16:32, 16:32].

3. Patch embedding: one learned matrix

Each flattened patch x_k (768 numbers) is mapped to a token by a learned weight matrix E of shape 768 × D and a bias b of length D:

z_k = x_k E + b        (a row vector of length D)

This is a plain linear layer, shared by every patch: the same E is applied at every grid position, exactly as one convolution filter is applied at every location. For ViT-B, D = 768, so E holds 768 × 768 = 589,824 weights plus 768 biases: 590,592 parameters, under 1% of the model's 86 million. Each of the D output numbers is a dot product between the patch and one column of E, so each column acts as a learned 16 × 16 × 3 "template" and the output says how strongly the patch matches it. The ViT paper visualised these columns for a trained model (its Figure 7, left): they look like the colour blobs and edge-like stripes you see in the first layer of a CNN.

That observation leads to an identity worth being able to prove in an interview: the patch embedding is a convolution. A 2-D convolution with D output channels, kernel size P × P and stride P (so its windows tile the image without overlap) computes, at each window, D dot products between the window's P × P × 3 pixels and D filters. Reshape column j of E into a P × P × 3 filter and that is exactly z_k[j]. That is why every implementation writes the patch embedding as Conv2d(3, D, kernel_size=P, stride=P): same numbers, and the library's convolution avoids materialising the patch array. The lab verifies the equality numerically.

4. The [CLS] token and position embeddings

After embedding, we have a 196 × D matrix. ViT then makes two additions.

The [CLS] token. A learned vector of length D is placed in front of the 196 patch tokens, giving 197. It starts identical for every image; it becomes image-specific only because, layer after layer, it attends to the patches. At the end, its output vector goes through a small classifier head (a linear layer, after pretraining) to produce class scores. The alternative is global average pooling: average the 196 patch outputs. The ViT paper's appendix reports both work equally well once each gets its own learning rate; the [CLS] choice was inherited from BERT.

Position embeddings. Self-attention is permutation-equivariant (lm4): it treats the tokens as a set. Without position information, a scrambled image and the original produce the same set of outputs, just reordered, so the model cannot learn "eyes are above the mouth". ViT's fix is the simplest one: a learned matrix of shape 197 × D, one row per sequence position, added element-wise to the tokens before the first layer. They start random and are trained with everything else. ViT-B's position table has 197 × 768 = 151,296 parameters.

Notice that these embeddings are indexed by position in the 1-D sequence, with no notion of rows and columns. The ViT authors tried 2-D-aware variants (separate row and column embeddings, concatenated) and relative position schemes. In their ablation (Table 8, ViT-B/16, 5-shot ImageNet linear accuracy) the results were: no position embedding 0.614, 1-D learned 0.642, 2-D 0.640, relative 0.640. Having positions matters (about 3 points); how you encode them barely does. And when they plotted the cosine similarity between the learned embeddings, the 2-D grid had emerged on its own: each position's embedding was most similar to its neighbours, with visible row and column structure. The model learned the geometry from data.

5. The encoder: a transformer you already know

The 197 × D sequence now runs through L identical pre-norm transformer blocks (lm3). Each block does:

h = x + MultiHeadAttention(LayerNorm(x))      # every token looks at every token
x = h + MLP(LayerNorm(h))                      # per-token: D → 4D → D with GELU

A final LayerNorm follows the last block. The three standard sizes come straight from BERT plus one larger model (ViT paper, Table 1):

ModelLayers LWidth DMLP sizeHeadsParams
ViT-Base127683,0721286M
ViT-Large241,0244,09616307M
ViT-Huge321,2805,12016632M

The name adds the patch size: ViT-L/16 is the Large model with 16 × 16 patches, ViT-H/14 the Huge model with 14 × 14. You can check the Base count yourself. Each block has four D × D attention matrices (query, key, value, output) and two MLP matrices of D × 4D and 4D × D: 12D² weights, plus biases and LayerNorm parameters of about 13D. With D = 768 that is 7.09M per block, 85.1M for 12 blocks, plus 0.59M for the patch embedding and 0.15M for positions: 85.8M, which the paper rounds to 86M. The patch size barely changes the parameter count (only E's input size changes); it changes the compute, through N.

6. What it costs, worked out

Two kinds of work happen in each layer. The matrix multiplications with weights (projections and MLP) cost 2 FLOPs (one multiply, one add) per weight per token: 2 × 12D² × N = 24·N·D². The attention itself (scores QKᵀ and the weighted sum with V) costs about 2·N²·D each, so 4·N²·D, independent of the weights. For ViT-B/16 at 224 px, N = 197:

weights   : 24 × 197 × 768²      = 2.79 GFLOPs per layer
attention :  4 × 197² × 768      = 0.12 GFLOPs per layer   (4% of the layer)
12 layers : ≈ 35 GFLOPs per image   (≈ 17.5 G multiply-accumulates, the figure often quoted)

Now change only the resolution, keeping P = 16:

InputGridTokens NWeights/layerAttention/layerAttention share
224 px14 × 141972.79 GF0.12 GF4%
384 px24 × 245778.17 GF1.02 GF11%
512 px32 × 321,02514.5 GF3.23 GF18%

Read the table carefully, because it corrects a common belief. At normal resolutions a ViT's cost is dominated by its weights, which scale linearly in N, and attention is a minor term. But both grow with resolution: going from 224 to 512 px (2.3× the side) multiplies tokens by 5.2, weight FLOPs by 5.2 and attention FLOPs by 27. Attention's share keeps rising, and at document-scale resolutions with thousands of tokens it becomes the main cost. Memory follows the same pattern if the attention matrix is materialised: 197² is 38,809 scores per head, 1,025² is over a million.

7. Inductive bias: what ViT gives up, and why it still wins

An inductive bias is an assumption built into a model's structure that makes some solutions easier to find. A CNN has two strong ones (dl6). Locality: a 3 × 3 filter only sees a 3 × 3 neighbourhood, so early features are local by construction. Translation equivariance: the same filter is applied everywhere, so a cat's ear detector works in any corner, and shifting the input shifts the feature map. These assumptions are correct for natural images, so they act like free training data.

ViT keeps almost none. Inside a patch the projection is local, and the MLP acts on each token separately, so those parts are local and translation-equivariant. But attention is global from layer 1, and the position embeddings start random. The paper's own summary: the 2-D structure is used only when cutting patches and when resizing position embeddings for a new resolution. Everything else about images must be learned.

The paper measured the consequence directly. Pretrained on ImageNet (1.3M images, 1k classes) and fine-tuned, ViT-L/16 scored 76.5% on ImageNet, worse than the smaller ViT-B/16 (77.9%) because it overfit. On ImageNet-21k (14M images) the two were about equal (85.2% and 84.0%). On JFT-300M (303M images) the bigger model pulled ahead, 87.1% against 84.2% (all fine-tuned at 384 px, Table 5). A second experiment trained on random subsets of JFT of 9M, 30M, 90M and 300M images: ViT-B/32 did much worse than a similarly fast ResNet-50 on 9M images and better from 90M up. The conclusion the authors drew, "large scale training trumps inductive bias", is the sentence to remember: priors help when data is scarce and get in the way when it is plentiful.

Inside the trained model you can watch the bias being learned. The paper measured each attention head's mean attention distance: the average pixel distance between a query patch and the patches it attends to, weighted by attention. In the lowest layers some heads already attend across most of the image while others attend locally, behaving like a CNN's early filters; with depth, all heads become global. ViT has learned locality where it is useful without being forced into it everywhere.

8. Training recipes that fixed the data hunger

JFT-300M is private, so the obvious next question was whether ViTs could be trained on public data. Three lines of work answered it, and each produced checkpoints you will meet in multimodal models.

Supervised with heavy regularisation (DeiT). Touvron et al. trained ViT-B on ImageNet-1k alone, on a single 8-GPU machine in about two to three days, by using strong data augmentation (random transformations that create new training images: RandAugment, Mixup and CutMix blend images and labels), stochastic depth (randomly skipping blocks during training), and many epochs. DeiT-B reached 81.8% at 224 px and 83.1% fine-tuned at 384 px. They also added distillation (training a student to match a teacher's predictions) through an extra "distillation token" that attends like [CLS] but is trained to match a CNN teacher's predicted labels; that pushed the best model to 85.2%.

Masked autoencoding (MAE). He et al. borrowed BERT's idea of predicting hidden input, but with a twist that suits images: hide a very large fraction, 75% of patches, chosen at random. The encoder runs only on the visible 25% (so pretraining is cheap: the paper reports 3× or more speed-up), and a small decoder gets the encoded visible tokens plus placeholder "mask tokens" and reconstructs the missing pixels. Images are highly redundant, which is why the mask ratio can be so much higher than BERT's 15%: with less hidden, the task can be solved by copying neighbours. A ViT-H trained this way reached 87.8% on ImageNet-1k with no extra data.

Self-distillation (DINO, DINOv2) and image–text contrastive learning (CLIP, SigLIP) are the other two families. The second is the subject of the next topic (mm2), and its encoders are the ones inside most vision-language models.

9. Changing resolution after training

Fine-tuning at a higher resolution than pretraining is standard: more pixels means more detail, and the expensive pretraining is done at low resolution. The ViT paper pretrained at 224 px and fine-tuned at 384 px for most experiments and at 512 (ViT-L/16) or 518 (ViT-H/14) px for its headline results. Two things change.

First, the grid. The patch size stays the same (the projection E only knows about P × P patches), so a larger image simply gives more patches: at 384 px with P = 16, a 24 × 24 grid of 576. The transformer itself does not care about N; attention works for any sequence length.

Second, the position embeddings. There are 196 learned patch positions, but now 576 slots. The fix is to treat the 196 embeddings as what they really are, a 14 × 14 grid of D-dimensional vectors, and resize that grid to 24 × 24 with 2-D interpolation: each new position's embedding is a weighted blend of the nearest old ones (bilinear uses the 4 nearest, bicubic the 16 nearest). The [CLS] embedding is set aside and reattached. Doing this in 2-D matters: if you interpolate the 1-D list of 196 vectors, the end of one row blends into the start of the next, which are on opposite sides of the image. After resizing, a short fine-tune lets the model adapt. The ViT paper notes that this resizing and the patch cutting are the only two places where it injects knowledge of 2-D structure by hand.

10. How ViT features are used downstream

A trained ViT gives two kinds of output. The [CLS] (or pooled) vector is a single summary, good for classification, retrieval and similarity search. The patch outputs are a grid of D-dimensional features, one per patch, that keep spatial layout; detectors and segmenters (mm10) and vision-language models (mm3) use these. A vision-language model typically takes the patch tokens from the second-to-last layer rather than the last, because the last layer is the most specialised to the pretraining objective (for CLIP, producing a single vector that matches a caption) and has discarded local detail. Those patch tokens, after a small projection, enter the language model as if they were words. So the arithmetic of section 6 is also the arithmetic of the LLM's context: a 336 px image through a /14 encoder is 24 × 24 = 576 tokens of context before any text is added.

Hierarchical ViTs: Swin and the return of locality

Detection and segmentation need features at several scales (small objects in fine maps, large ones in coarse maps) and high input resolutions, where global attention becomes the dominant cost. The Swin Transformer reintroduces two CNN ideas into the transformer. Attention is computed only within local windows of 7 × 7 patches, so the cost per token is constant and total cost is linear in image size rather than quadratic; and alternate layers shift the windows by half a window so information flows between neighbouring windows. Between stages, "patch merging" concatenates each 2 × 2 group of tokens and projects them, halving the grid side and doubling the width, like a CNN's downsampling. The result is a pyramid of feature maps that plugs into detectors designed for CNNs. The trade-off is that Swin gives up the plain, uniform token sequence that makes ViT easy to connect to language models, which is why VLMs mostly use plain ViTs and dense-prediction systems often use hierarchical ones.

Native resolution and aspect ratio

Squashing every image to 224 × 224 distorts wide images (a 16:9 screenshot becomes squeezed horizontally) and destroys small text. NaViT ("Patch n' Pack") trains on images at their native resolution and aspect ratio: each image yields a different number of patches, and several images' patch sequences are packed into one long training sequence with an attention mask so tokens attend only within their own image. This is the same packing trick used for variable-length text. It needs position encodings that handle any grid shape; NaViT uses factorised row and column embeddings, and many recent VLM encoders use 2-D rotary embeddings (RoPE applied separately to the row and column coordinates, lm4), which extrapolate to new grid sizes far more gracefully than an interpolated lookup table. Native resolution also gives a natural cost dial at inference: you can choose how many patches to spend on an image.

Artefact tokens and registers

Darcet et al. noticed that large ViTs trained long enough (DINOv2, OpenCLIP, DeiT-III, but not the original DINO) produce attention maps with bright speckles on background regions. Measuring the output norms of patch tokens in DINOv2 ViT-g showed a bimodal distribution: about 2.4% of tokens had norms above 150, roughly ten times the rest. These high-norm tokens appear around the middle of the 40-layer network, only after about a third of training, and only in models of ViT-Large size or bigger. They sit on patches that are nearly identical to their neighbours (sky, plain walls), and linear probes show they have lost their local information (position and pixels are poorly recoverable) but hold more global information than normal tokens. The interpretation: the model needs scratch space for global computation and recycles redundant patches for it. The fix is to give it scratch space deliberately: append a few learned register tokens to the input that carry no image content and are discarded at the output. With registers the outlier tokens disappear, attention maps become smooth, dense-prediction scores rise, and object-discovery methods that read attention maps work again. If you build anything that reads ViT attention maps or uses individual patch features for localisation, check whether your checkpoint has registers.

Patch size as a dial, and token reduction

Patch size trades detail against tokens, and that trade is normally frozen at training time. FlexiViT trains with a randomly chosen patch size at each step, resizing the embedding weights to match, so one model can run at several token budgets. Downstream, the more common approach is to reduce tokens after the encoder: average-pool the patch grid 2 × 2 (4× fewer tokens), "pixel-shuffle" (concatenate each 2 × 2 group of D-vectors into one 4D-vector, then project; also 4× fewer, but without averaging away information), or merge similar tokens progressively inside the encoder (ToMe merges the most similar token pairs in each layer). These keep most quality for scene understanding and lose noticeable quality for small text, which is spread across few patches and suffers when neighbours are merged.

Why compute at high resolution is not just "more tokens"

Two second-order effects matter in practice. First, a model pretrained at 224 px has only ever seen patch statistics at that scale: at 448 px each patch covers a quarter of the area, so an object spans four times as many patches and edges look softer per patch. This scale shift, not only the interpolated positions, is why a fine-tune is needed after a resolution change. Second, at long sequences the attention kernels' memory traffic, not their FLOPs, dominate unless a fused attention kernel such as FlashAttention (which avoids writing the N × N matrix to memory) is used. A ViT that runs fine at 576 tokens can run out of memory at 4,096 if a custom attention mask silently disables the fused path.

Inductive bias versus scale: the framing interviewers want

The deep lesson of ViT is not "transformers beat CNNs". It is that architecture priors are a form of data: they substitute for examples you do not have. When data is limited, a CNN's priors win; when data is abundant, a more general model learns a better solution than the prior would allow, and its uniform structure makes it easy to scale and to join with other modalities. Modern practice sits in between: ViTs with convolutional stems, local windows, or 2-D relative positions where the data or resolution calls for it, and plain ViTs where the goal is a token sequence for a language model. A strong answer names the regime (data size, resolution, downstream use) before naming the architecture.

Penultimate layers and feature choice

Different layers of a ViT hold different information. Early layers are local and close to pixels; late layers are global and shaped by the training objective. For a contrastive encoder like CLIP, whose objective only scores a single pooled vector against a caption, the final layer's patch tokens are under little pressure to keep local detail. LLaVA and many successors take the penultimate layer's patch tokens for this reason and report better results, especially for counting, OCR (optical character recognition: reading text in images) and grounding. Some systems concatenate features from several layers. This is an empirical choice to re-test when you change encoders: a self-supervised encoder like DINOv2, whose objective works on patch tokens too, keeps local detail in its last layer.

The public case: "An Image is Worth 16x16 Words" (Dosovitskiy et al., 2020)

The ViT paper (ICLR 2021) is short and its claims are concrete, which makes it a good reference to be precise about. The model is exactly the pipeline in this lesson: non-overlapping patches, a linear projection, a learned [CLS] token, learned 1-D position embeddings, and a standard pre-norm transformer encoder. The variants are Base (12 layers, D = 768, 86M parameters), Large (24 layers, D = 1,024, 307M) and Huge (32 layers, D = 1,280, 632M). Models were pretrained with Adam at batch size 4,096 and weight decay 0.1, then fine-tuned with SGD at higher resolution.

Three datasets frame the result: ImageNet (1.3M images, 1k classes), ImageNet-21k (14M images, 21k classes) and JFT-300M (303M images, 18k classes; Google-internal). The headline comparison (Table 2) is against the best CNNs of the day, BiT-L (a large ResNet pretrained on JFT) and Noisy Student (a large EfficientNet trained semi-supervised on ImageNet and unlabelled JFT). ViT-H/14 pretrained on JFT reached 88.55% top-1 on ImageNet (fine-tuned at 518 px), against 87.54% for BiT-L and 88.4–88.5% for Noisy Student, and led on CIFAR-100 (94.55%) and the 19-task VTAB transfer suite (77.63%). The compute column is the striking part: ViT-H/14 took 2.5k TPUv3-core-days to pretrain, against 9.9k for BiT-L and 12.3k for Noisy Student; the smaller ViT-L/16 matched or beat BiT-L on every task with 0.68k. ViT-L/16 pretrained only on public ImageNet-21k scored 85.30% with 0.23k core-days.

The data-scale experiment is the paper's real lesson and the source of the "data hungry" reputation. Fine-tuned at 384 px (Table 5), ViT-L/16 scored 76.53% on ImageNet when pretrained on ImageNet, 85.15% from ImageNet-21k and 87.12% from JFT-300M, while ViT-B/16 went 77.91%, 83.97%, 84.15%: on the small dataset the bigger model was worse. The JFT-subset study showed the same crossover against ResNets around 90M images. Read in 2026, the paper's lasting contributions are the tokenisation recipe (still the front end of most VLMs), the evidence that a general architecture plus data beats a specialised one, and the resolution fine-tuning trick with interpolated position embeddings.

Failure modes you will actually see

Small text and fine detail vanish. A VLM that cannot read a receipt is very often a resolution problem, not a model problem: the encoder runs at 224–384 px, and the preprocessing step downsampled a 3,000-pixel photo before it ever reached the model. Symptoms: good scene descriptions, wrong numbers and names. Check the actual tensor shape going into the encoder, then try tiling (mm3) before blaming the language model.

Aspect-ratio distortion and centre crops. Many preprocessing pipelines resize the short side and centre-crop to a square, silently cutting off the edges of wide images, which is exactly where a screenshot's menu bar or a document's margin notes are. Others squash without cropping and distort shapes. Log the preprocessed image in your evaluation harness and look at it.

Wrong normalisation constants. Each checkpoint expects specific per-channel means and standard deviations (ImageNet's, CLIP's and SigLIP's differ). Using the wrong ones does not crash; it degrades accuracy by a few points, which is easy to miss. Load the processor configuration that ships with the checkpoint.

Channel and layout mix-ups. BGR versus RGB (OpenCV loads BGR), channels-first versus channels-last, and integer 0–255 versus float 0–1 inputs all produce plausible-looking garbage. A one-image smoke test against the model card's expected output catches them.

Resolution changes without position resizing. Feeding a 448 px image to a model with a fixed 16 × 16 position table either errors out or, in hand-written code, silently reuses the wrong positions. Libraries such as timm resample position embeddings when you set a new image size; custom code must do it explicitly, in 2-D.

What to measure and tune

For an encoder in production, track images per second and latency per image at your real resolution distribution, not at 224 px. Batch images of the same size together, since padding wastes compute. Use bfloat16 and a fused attention kernel. If the encoder feeds an LLM, record image tokens per request: it is the number that drives prefill time and cost, and it changes whenever someone changes the resize policy. Finally, keep a small "acuity" evaluation set (receipts, charts, screenshots with small text) separate from your general set, because aggregate scores barely move when fine detail is lost.

Goal: patchify an image array with reshape and transpose, embed it the ViT-B way, prove the embedding equals a stride-16 convolution, tabulate tokens and FLOPs against resolution, and resize the position grid for 384 px. NumPy only; runs in under a second with the existing gpt_env (Python 3.12, NumPy 2.x).

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def patchify(img, P):
    """(H, W, C) image -> (N, P*P*C) patches in raster order."""
    H, W, C = img.shape
    assert H % P == 0 and W % P == 0, "side must be a multiple of P"
    x = img.reshape(H // P, P, W // P, P, C)      # split rows and columns into blocks
    x = x.transpose(0, 2, 1, 3, 4)                # (rows, cols, P, P, C)
    return x.reshape(-1, P * P * C)

# 1. patchify a 224x224 RGB image with 16x16 patches
img = rng.random((224, 224, 3)).astype(np.float32)
X = patchify(img, 16)
print("patches:", X.shape)                         # (196, 768)
assert np.array_equal(X[15], img[16:32, 16:32].reshape(-1))  # patch 15 = row 1, col 1

# 2. linear patch embedding + [CLS] + learned position embeddings (ViT-B width)
D = 768
E = rng.normal(0, 0.02, (X.shape[1], D)); b = np.zeros(D)
cls = rng.normal(0, 0.02, (1, D)); pos = rng.normal(0, 0.02, (197, D))
tokens = np.vstack([cls, X @ E + b]) + pos
print("tokens:", tokens.shape, "| embedding params:", E.size + b.size)

# 3. same thing as a stride-16 convolution: one output pixel per patch
k = E.T.reshape(D, 16, 16, 3)                     # D filters of size 16x16x3
conv_out = np.einsum("dpqc,pqc->d", k, img[16:32, 16:32])
print("conv == linear for patch 15:", np.allclose(conv_out, X[15] @ E))

# 4. token count and per-layer FLOPs versus resolution
for side, P in [(224, 16), (384, 16), (336, 14), (448, 14)]:
    N = (side // P) ** 2 + 1
    lin, att = 24 * N * D * D, 4 * N * N * D
    print(f"{side}px /{P}: {N:5d} tokens  linear {lin/1e9:5.2f} GF  attention {att/1e9:5.2f} GF  ({att/(lin+att):.0%})")

# 5. resize the 14x14 position grid to 24x24 (bilinear) for 384px fine-tuning
def resize_grid(g, new):
    old = g.shape[0]
    t = np.linspace(0, old - 1, new); i0 = np.floor(t).astype(int); i1 = np.minimum(i0 + 1, old - 1); w = t - i0
    g = g[i0] * (1 - w)[:, None, None] + g[i1] * w[:, None, None]                  # rows
    return g[:, i0] * (1 - w)[None, :, None] + g[:, i1] * w[None, :, None]         # columns
grid = pos[1:].reshape(14, 14, D)
new = resize_grid(grid, 24)
print("pos grid", grid.shape, "->", new.shape, "| corners kept:", np.allclose(new[0, 0], grid[0, 0]), np.allclose(new[-1, -1], grid[-1, -1]))

Work it out

A ViT-L/14 encoder (24 layers, D = 1024) processes an image at 224, 336 and 448 px. For each, how many patch tokens are there, and by what factor do the linear-layer FLOPs and the attention-score FLOPs grow relative to 224 px?

Answer

Tokens: (224/14)² = 256, (336/14)² = 576, (448/14)² = 1,024.

Linear layers scale with the number of tokens: 576/256 = 2.25× and 1024/256 = 4×. Attention scores scale with tokens squared: 2.25² ≈ 5.1× and 4² = 16×.

At 256 tokens, per layer the linear layers cost about 24·N·D² ≈ 6.4 GFLOPs against 4·N²·D ≈ 0.27 GFLOPs for attention — attention is 4%. At 1,024 tokens attention is 4.3 GFLOPs against 25.8 — about 14%, and rising. The practical conclusion: going from 336 to 448 px nearly doubles the encoder cost and nearly doubles the image's token cost inside the LLM, so raise resolution only for tasks that need it (documents, charts, small objects) — or tile the image and pay per tile.

Diagnose it

A team fine-tunes a ViT-B/16 checkpoint at 384 px for chart reading. Training loss falls but validation accuracy is worse than at 224 px. Their code resizes the position embeddings with np.interp applied to the 197 × 768 table as a 1-D sequence (stretching it to 577 rows). What is wrong, and what should they do?

Answer

Two mistakes. The [CLS] embedding was included in the interpolation, so it gets blended with patch positions; it must be set aside and reattached unchanged. And the 196 patch embeddings were stretched as a flat list: a raster sequence wraps at the end of every row, so new positions near row ends receive blends of the far-right patch of one row and the far-left patch of the next, giving the model wrong spatial information across the whole grid. The fix is to reshape to 14 × 14 × 768, resize in 2-D (bilinear or bicubic) to 24 × 24, flatten back to 576 rows and prepend the [CLS] row. Then compare against the library implementation (timm's position-embedding resampling) on one image to confirm.
