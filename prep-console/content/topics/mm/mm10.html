A classifier looks at a street photo and says "car". That is useless to a self-driving system, which needs to know where every car, cyclist and pedestrian is, how big, and which pixels belong to which. It is useless to a warehouse robot that must pick one box from a pile, to a radiologist who wants the tumour outlined, to a retail auditor counting products on a shelf, and to a VLM user who asks "which of these jackets is cheapest?" and wants the answer pointed out. Most practical vision problems are not "what is in the image" but "what is where".

The family of answers to "where" has grown over a decade. Object detection draws a box and label around each object. Semantic segmentation labels every pixel by class; instance segmentation separates individual objects with masks. Grounding links a phrase ("the man in the red jacket") to its region. Each has standard architectures, standard post-processing and standard metrics, and each has been reshaped by the same forces as the rest of this track: transformers, which turned detection into set prediction (DETR); massive model-assisted datasets, which produced a segmenter that works on almost anything (Segment Anything); and image–text models, which let detectors find objects described by any phrase, and let VLMs answer with boxes.

For an AI engineer these models appear constantly: as components in document pipelines (layout detection, mm9), as tools that a VLM-based agent calls when it needs precise locations or counts, as annotation accelerators, and as the backbone of any product that measures, counts or tracks things in images. Interviews test whether you can compute IoU by hand, explain non-maximum suppression and why DETR removed it, describe how SAM is fast enough for interactive use, and decide when a detector beats a VLM.

There is also a reason these models matter more now, not less, in the era of large multimodal models. When an agent needs to click a button in a screenshot, crop a receipt, count items for an inventory, or highlight the clause it is citing, it needs coordinates it can trust. Detection, segmentation and grounding are the parts of the stack that turn "the model understood the image" into "the system can act on a precise region of it".

This lesson builds the "where" toolbox: the output types, IoU computed by hand, non-maximum suppression on three boxes, two-stage and one-stage detectors, DETR's set prediction with a matching worked example, segmentation and Segment Anything, open-vocabulary detection and VLM grounding, and how all of these are evaluated. The public cases are DETR and SAM, with their numbers checked against the papers.

Four questions about one photo

Take a photo of two dogs on a lawn next to a bench. Four tasks ask four different questions of it. Classification: what is in this image? "Dog." Detection: where is each object, and what is it? Two boxes labelled "dog" and one labelled "bench", each a rectangle given by its corners and a confidence score. Semantic segmentation: what class is every pixel? A map where dog pixels are one colour, bench pixels another, grass a third, but the two dogs share one colour, so if they touch you cannot tell where one ends. Instance segmentation: which pixels belong to each individual object? A separate mask for dog 1, dog 2 and the bench. And grounding asks a question in words: "the dog on the left" → this box.

The analogy: a team of spotters

Think of detection as a team of spotters looking at a picture. Each spotter is responsible for one area (in classic detectors, a grid location and a few box shapes) or for one "slot" (in DETR, an object query). Each shouts out "I see a dog here, 90% sure" with a rectangle. Because several spotters often see the same dog, you get duplicate shouts, and someone has to merge them: that is non-maximum suppression. DETR's idea was to train the spotters to coordinate, so exactly one of them claims each dog and the others say "nothing here". The analogy breaks for segmentation, which is less like shouting and more like colouring in: every pixel must be assigned, and boundaries matter as much as identities.

What each output costs to label, and why that matters

The tasks differ sharply in labelling cost, and that shapes which models exist. A classification label takes a second. A box takes several seconds per object, and a crowded street scene can have dozens of objects. A precise mask takes far longer: tracing a bicycle's outline, wheel spokes included, is minutes of careful work. That is why detection datasets (COCO has about 330,000 images) are large while dense segmentation datasets were historically small, and why the breakthrough of Segment Anything was as much about building a dataset of a billion masks with a model-assisted engine as about architecture. When you scope a project, the label type you need often decides the budget more than the model does: if boxes are enough for the decision you must make (count, locate, alert), do not pay for masks.

The two numbers to carry

The first is IoU, intersection over union: how much two regions overlap, from 0 (disjoint) to 1 (identical). It is used everywhere: to decide whether a detection matches the truth, to decide which duplicates to suppress, to score masks. The second is the NMS threshold: the IoU above which two detections are assumed to be the same object. Together they explain most of the behaviour, and most of the bugs, of real detection systems.

If you stop here: detection gives boxes, semantic segmentation gives class maps, instance segmentation gives per-object masks, grounding links words to regions; IoU measures overlap; and classic detectors need NMS to remove duplicates, which set-based detectors learn to avoid.

1. Representing boxes and masks

A bounding box is an axis-aligned rectangle, written either as corners (x₁, y₁, x₂, y₂), with (x₁, y₁) the top-left and (x₂, y₂) the bottom-right in pixels, or as centre and size (cx, cy, w, h), often normalised by the image width and height to lie between 0 and 1. Converting between them is a frequent source of bugs (off-by-one pixel conventions, swapped x and y, normalised versus absolute coordinates), so pipelines should fix one convention and test conversions. A mask is a 2-D array of the image's size with 1 for pixels inside the object and 0 elsewhere; it can be stored compactly with run-length encoding. Detectors output a box, a class and a confidence score per object; instance segmenters add a mask.

2. IoU, worked by hand

Intersection over union compares two regions: the area they share divided by the area they cover together. For boxes A = (10, 10, 110, 110) and B = (20, 20, 120, 120):

intersection: x from max(10,20)=20 to min(110,120)=110 → 90
              y from max(10,20)=20 to min(110,120)=110 → 90     → 90 × 90 = 8,100
areas       : A = 100 × 100 = 10,000      B = 10,000
union       : 10,000 + 10,000 − 8,100 = 11,900
IoU(A, B)   = 8,100 / 11,900 = 0.681

Shifting B by just 10 pixels in each direction relative to a 100-pixel box already drops IoU to 0.68: IoU is strict. A third box C = (100, 20, 180, 100) touches A along a thin strip: the intersection is x from 100 to 110 (10) by y from 20 to 100 (80) = 800; C's area is 80 × 80 = 6,400; the union is 10,000 + 6,400 − 800 = 15,600; IoU(A, C) = 800 / 15,600 = 0.051. If the two boxes do not overlap at all, the width or height of the intersection is negative, which code must clamp to zero. Mask IoU is the same idea with pixel counts: the number of pixels in both masks divided by the number in either.

3. Non-maximum suppression, one pass

Detectors produce many candidate boxes per object, because neighbouring locations and box shapes all respond to it. Non-maximum suppression (NMS) cleans this up greedily: sort candidates by score; take the highest; delete every remaining candidate whose IoU with it exceeds a threshold; repeat with the highest survivor. Take the three boxes above, all predicted as "dog", with scores A 0.90, B 0.80, C 0.70, and threshold 0.5:

sorted: A (0.90), B (0.80), C (0.70)
keep A. compare: IoU(A,B) = 0.681 > 0.5 → delete B;  IoU(A,C) = 0.051 ≤ 0.5 → keep C in the list
next highest remaining: C. keep C. nothing left.
result: A and C

Was that right? If B was a duplicate of A's dog, yes. If B was a second dog standing very close, NMS just deleted a real object. With threshold 0.7 all three survive (the lab shows both). That is the central weakness of NMS: a single threshold cannot distinguish "same object, slightly different box" from "different objects that overlap". Crowded scenes (people in a queue, products on a shelf) suffer most. NMS is applied per class, so a "dog" box does not suppress an overlapping "bench" box.

4. Two-stage detectors

The canonical two-stage design is Faster R-CNN. A CNN backbone (dl6), usually with a feature pyramid (feature maps at several resolutions, so small objects are found on fine maps and large ones on coarse maps), computes features for the whole image once. Stage one, the region proposal network, slides over the feature map and at each location scores a few anchor boxes (predefined shapes, for example three sizes × three aspect ratios) for "object or not" and regresses offsets to fit them; after NMS, a few hundred to a thousand proposals remain. Stage two crops features for each proposal from the shared map (an operation called RoI pooling or RoI align), classifies it into the object classes or background, and refines its box. Mask R-CNN adds a small branch that predicts a mask inside each box, giving instance segmentation. Two-stage detectors are accurate and flexible but run the second stage hundreds of times per image.

5. One-stage detectors

One-stage detectors skip proposals: at every location of every pyramid level they directly predict class scores and box offsets. The YOLO family and RetinaNet are the classic examples. The challenge is imbalance: a feature map has tens of thousands of locations, almost all background, so a plain classification loss is dominated by easy negatives. RetinaNet's focal loss multiplies the cross-entropy by (1 − p)^γ, where p is the probability of the correct class, so well-classified background contributes almost nothing and training focuses on hard examples. One-stage detectors are fast and simple to deploy, which is why they dominate real-time applications. Anchor-free variants predict, at each location, distances to the four sides of the object instead of offsets from anchor shapes. All of them still produce overlapping candidates and need NMS.

6. DETR: detection as set prediction

DETR (Carion et al., 2020) asked why detection needs anchors and NMS at all. Its pipeline: a CNN backbone produces a feature map; a transformer encoder (lm3) processes the flattened feature map with positional encodings so every location can see every other; a transformer decoder takes N = 100 learned object queries, vectors that cross-attend (lm2) to the encoded image; and each query's output goes through small heads that predict a class (including a special "no object" class) and a box (cx, cy, w, h, normalised). The output is always a set of 100 predictions, most of which should say "no object".

The key is the training loss. The model's predictions are an unordered set, and so are the ground-truth objects; to compute a loss you must decide which prediction is responsible for which object. DETR finds the optimal one-to-one assignment with the Hungarian algorithm, using a matching cost of (negative probability of the true class) plus a box distance (L1 plus a generalised-IoU term). Matched predictions are trained toward their objects; all unmatched predictions are trained toward "no object". A duplicate prediction of an already-matched object is therefore pushed to "no object", so the model learns to produce exactly one prediction per object, and NMS is unnecessary.

A worked matching with 3 predictions and 2 ground-truth objects (a cat at (0.32, 0.42, 0.20, 0.28) and a dog at (0.68, 0.52, 0.30, 0.40)), using cost = −P(true class) + L1 box distance:

prediction 0: P(cat)=0.7, box (0.30, 0.40, 0.20, 0.30)
  cost to cat = −0.7 + (0.02 + 0.02 + 0.00 + 0.02) = −0.64
prediction 1: P(dog)=0.8, box (0.70, 0.50, 0.25, 0.40)
  cost to dog = −0.8 + (0.02 + 0.02 + 0.05 + 0.00) = −0.71
prediction 2: P(cat)=0.2, box (0.50, 0.50, 0.10, 0.10)
  cost to cat = −0.2 + (0.18 + 0.08 + 0.10 + 0.18) = +0.34
best assignment: cat ← prediction 0, dog ← prediction 1 (total −1.35)
prediction 2 is unmatched → trained to predict "no object"

DETR's results (COCO, ResNet-50 backbone, 6 encoder and 6 decoder layers, width 256, 41.3M parameters): 40.6 AP on its shorter schedule and 42.0 AP on a long 500-epoch schedule, at about 28 frames per second, comparable to a heavily tuned Faster R-CNN with a feature pyramid. It was much better on large objects and worse on small ones, and it converged slowly. Successors (Deformable DETR, DINO-DETR and others) fixed both with multi-scale features, attention restricted to a few sampling points per query, and better query initialisation, and DETR-style detectors now lead many benchmarks. The architectural idea, learned queries that each claim one output and a matching loss, reappears in segmentation models and in the resamplers of mm3.

7. Segmentation

Semantic segmentation classifies every pixel. The classic architecture is an encoder–decoder: a backbone downsamples to coarse, semantically rich features; a decoder upsamples back to full resolution with skip connections from earlier layers (the U-Net pattern of mm5), and a per-pixel classifier outputs class scores, trained with per-pixel cross-entropy. Instance segmentation additionally separates objects: Mask R-CNN predicts a mask inside each detected box; query-based models in the DETR lineage predict a set of (class, mask) pairs directly, each query producing a mask by dot-producting its embedding with per-pixel features. Panoptic segmentation unifies both: every pixel gets a class, and pixels of countable "things" (cars, people) also get an instance identity, while "stuff" (sky, road) does not. Mask quality is measured with mask IoU; the lab computes 0.769 for a square guess against a disc.

Semantic segmentation is scored per class with IoU over pixels and then averaged: mean IoU (mIoU). A worked example on a small street image with three classes, counting pixels (true positives are pixels correctly labelled with the class; false positives are pixels wrongly given it; false negatives are pixels of the class labelled as something else):

class    TP    FP   FN    IoU = TP / (TP + FP + FN)
road    900    50  100    900 / 1,050 = 0.857
car     200    60   40    200 /   300 = 0.667
person   30    20   30     30 /    80 = 0.375
mean IoU = (0.857 + 0.667 + 0.375) / 3 = 0.633
pixel accuracy = (900 + 200 + 30) / (1,000 + 240 + 60) = 1,130 / 1,300 = 86.9%

Pixel accuracy looks good because road dominates the image; mean IoU exposes that people, the class that matters most for safety, are segmented poorly. This is the same lesson as class imbalance in classification (ml8): choose metrics that weight the classes you care about, and report per-class numbers alongside the mean.

8. Segment Anything: promptable segmentation

Segment Anything (Kirillov et al., 2023) reframed segmentation as a promptable task: given an image and a prompt (a point, a box, a rough mask; text was explored in early form), return a valid mask for what the prompt indicates. The architecture is split for interactivity. A heavy image encoder, an MAE-pretrained ViT-H (mm1), runs once per image on a 1,024 × 1,024 input and produces a 64 × 64 grid of image embeddings. A light prompt encoder embeds points and boxes (with positional encodings) and masks (with convolutions). A light mask decoder, a two-way transformer between prompt tokens and image embeddings, outputs masks. Because the expensive encoder is amortised, each new prompt takes about 50 ms, fast enough to run in a web browser while a user clicks.

SAM's second design point is ambiguity. A click on a person's shirt could mean the shirt or the whole person. Rather than averaging (which would produce a mask that is neither), SAM outputs three masks, intended as whole, part and subpart, each with a predicted IoU score used to rank them. Training backpropagates only the loss of the best of the three for each example, so each output can specialise.

Its third design point is data. Masks, unlike captions, are not lying around the web. The authors built a data engine in three stages: assisted-manual (annotators click, SAM proposes, annotators correct), semi-automatic (SAM fills in confident masks, annotators add what it missed), and fully automatic (SAM is prompted with a grid of points on each image, keeping confident, stable masks after NMS). The model was retrained six times as data grew, and the encoder scaled from ViT-B to ViT-H. The result, SA-1B, has 1.1 billion masks on 11 million licensed, privacy-protecting images: 400 times more masks than any previous segmentation dataset. SAM transferred zero-shot to a suite of 23 segmentation datasets with competitive quality from a single point prompt.

SAM does not name what it segments. To get "segment the red car", combine it with something that knows about cars: an open-vocabulary detector (next section) supplies a box for "red car", and SAM turns the box into a precise mask.

9. Open-vocabulary detection and grounding

Classic detectors know a fixed label set (COCO has 80 classes). Open-vocabulary detectors accept any text. The trick, borrowed from CLIP (mm2), is to replace the final classification layer, a learned weight vector per class, with text embeddings: for each candidate region, compute a region feature, and score it against the embedding of each requested phrase ("forklift", "person without a helmet"). Training combines detection datasets with large grounding and image–caption data so region features align with text. Grounding DINO pushes text deeper, fusing text and image features inside a DETR-style detector so queries are conditioned on the phrase. These models are invaluable for rare categories and for building datasets, but they are less accurate than a detector trained on your specific classes, especially for fine-grained or unusual phrases.

Phrase grounding is the same capability phrased as a question: given "the man holding the umbrella", return his box. It is evaluated by the fraction of phrases whose predicted box has IoU above 0.5 with the true one.

10. VLMs that answer with boxes

Vision-language models (mm3) can be trained to output locations as text. Qwen2-VL, for example, normalises box coordinates to [0, 1000) and writes them as "(x_top-left, y_top-left), (x_bottom-right, y_bottom-right)" between special box tokens, with object-reference tokens marking which phrase the box belongs to; GUI agents in the same paper output tap points on the same 0–1000 scale. The application parses the text and converts: a box (250, 100, 480, 900) on a 1,280 × 720 image is (250 × 1.28, 100 × 0.72, 480 × 1.28, 900 × 0.72) = (320, 72, 614, 648) in pixels. Normalising makes the numbers independent of image size and of any resizing the preprocessor did.

VLM grounding is flexible (any description, including relational ones like "the cup to the left of the laptop") and composes with reasoning, but its boxes are coarser than a dedicated detector's, it can miss or duplicate objects in crowds, and it is slower. A useful pattern combines them: a detector or SAM provides precise candidates with IDs drawn on the image (or listed with coordinates), and the VLM reasons about which candidates answer the question.

11. Evaluation: average precision

Detection is scored with average precision (AP). For one class: sort all detections by score; walk down the list, marking each as a true positive if its IoU with a not-yet-matched ground-truth box exceeds the threshold, otherwise a false positive; compute precision and recall at each point; AP is the area under the resulting precision–recall curve (ml7). COCO's primary metric averages AP over all classes and over ten IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95 in steps of 0.05, so a detector must both find objects and place tight boxes; AP₅₀ (threshold 0.5 only) is more forgiving. AP_S, AP_M and AP_L report small, medium and large objects separately, which is where DETR's trade-off shows. Segmentation uses mean IoU over classes (semantic) or mask AP (instance).

Why small objects are hard

A 20 × 20-pixel object in a 640-pixel image occupies one cell of a stride-32 feature map, or less than one patch of a ViT/16, so its signal is mixed with everything around it. Feature pyramids help by detecting small objects on high-resolution maps, at a cost in compute. Original DETR used only the coarsest map, because attention over a high-resolution map was too expensive, hence its weak AP_S; its dilated variant (DC5) doubled resolution at 16× the encoder self-attention cost. Deformable attention fixed this by letting each query attend to a handful of sampled points across several scales instead of every location. In practice, tiling large images (detect on overlapping crops, then merge with NMS across tiles) is the simplest fix for small objects in high-resolution inputs such as aerial or shelf images.

Label assignment is the real design decision

Every detector must decide during training which predictions are responsible for which ground-truth objects. Anchor-based detectors assign an anchor to an object if their IoU exceeds a threshold, so several anchors match one object (which is why duplicates, and NMS, follow). DETR assigns exactly one prediction per object by global matching, which removes duplicates but gives each object only one positive training signal per image, a cause of slow convergence. Later DETR variants add auxiliary one-to-many matching during training to speed it up, while keeping one-to-one at inference. When an interviewer asks "why does DETR train slowly?", the answer is sparse supervision from one-to-one assignment plus global attention that must learn to localise from scratch.

Promptable models as annotation engines

SAM's data engine is a general pattern: a model proposes labels, humans correct them, the model is retrained on the corrected data, and the share of human effort falls each round. The SAM paper reports that in its assisted-manual stage the average annotation time per mask fell from 34 to 14 seconds as the model was retrained, before the semi-automatic and fully automatic stages took over. The same pattern builds detection and segmentation datasets for new domains at a fraction of manual cost: an open-vocabulary detector proposes boxes, SAM converts them to masks, annotators fix errors, and a domain-specific model is trained on the result. Quality control matters: automatic labels carry the proposer's biases (missing rare objects, splitting thin structures), which the trained model will inherit.

Ambiguity and multiple valid outputs

SAM's three-mask output is an instance of a general problem: when a prompt has several valid answers, training a single output with a regression loss produces the average, which is no valid answer at all (the same mean-collapse seen with one-step flow sampling in mm5). Predicting several candidates and training only the best one (a "minimum over outputs" loss) lets each output specialise; a learned score then ranks them. VLM grounding faces the same issue with referring expressions ("the cup" when there are three), and good systems either ask a clarifying question or return all candidates.

Tracking: detection over time

Video applications need the same object identified across frames: count people entering a store, follow a forklift through a warehouse. The common approach is tracking by detection: run a detector on each frame, then associate detections across frames using predicted motion (a Kalman filter that predicts where each track's box will be) and appearance embeddings, solving an assignment problem each frame, again with the Hungarian algorithm and an IoU-based cost. Identity switches when tracks cross and fragmented tracks after occlusion are the characteristic errors. SAM 2 extends promptable segmentation to video with a memory of previous frames, so one click can segment an object through a whole clip.

Tracking quality is measured with metrics that count both detection errors and identity errors: a tracker that finds every person in every frame but swaps two people's identities when they cross has perfect detection and a broken count of who entered the store. For counting applications, test specifically on crossings, occlusions behind shelves or vehicles, and people leaving and re-entering the frame.

Calibration and thresholds per class

A detector's confidence score is not a probability you can compare across classes. Rare classes typically get lower scores than common ones for equally correct detections, because the model has seen fewer examples; a single global threshold therefore under-detects rare classes and over-detects common ones. Production systems tune a threshold per class on validation data, at the operating point the product needs (high recall for safety alerts, high precision for automatic actions), and re-check those thresholds when the camera, lighting or population changes. Temperature scaling and similar calibration methods (ml15) can make scores more comparable, but per-class validation remains the safeguard.

Detectors versus VLMs in production

The choice is usually not either-or. Dedicated detectors are fast (milliseconds per image on a GPU), precise, calibrated per class, and cheap at high volume, but only know their training classes. VLMs are slow, expensive per image, imprecise at localisation and counting in crowds, but understand open-ended descriptions and can reason ("is the forklift in the pedestrian lane?"). Architectures that combine them, a detector for recall and geometry and a VLM for semantics and decisions, get the strengths of both. The VLM can also be given the detector's output as text ("objects: forklift at (…), person at (…)") or as numbered boxes drawn on the image, which is called visual prompting or set-of-marks prompting and makes the VLM's references checkable.

The public cases: DETR (2020) and Segment Anything (2023)

DETR (Carion et al., "End-to-End Object Detection with Transformers") is the paper that turned detection into a set prediction problem. Its claims are concrete: a CNN backbone, a transformer encoder–decoder with N = 100 learned object queries, a "no object" class for empty slots, and a set loss based on Hungarian matching, with no anchors and no NMS. The base model with a ResNet-50 backbone (6 encoder and 6 decoder layers, width 256, 41.3M parameters) achieved 40.6 AP on COCO with its shorter schedule and 42.0 AP with a 500-epoch schedule, at about 28 FPS, matching a well-tuned Faster R-CNN with feature pyramids; it improved large-object AP substantially and was behind on small objects. Its significance was architectural: it removed hand-designed components and made detection a direct application of transformers, opening the line of query-based detectors and segmenters that followed.

Segment Anything (Kirillov et al.) introduced the promptable segmentation task, the SAM model and the SA-1B dataset. The image encoder is an MAE-pretrained ViT-H producing a 64 × 64 embedding of a 1,024-pixel input; the prompt encoder and mask decoder return masks in about 50 ms in a browser; ambiguous prompts get three masks with predicted IoU scores. The data engine moved from assisted-manual to semi-automatic to fully automatic annotation, retraining the model six times, and produced 1.1 billion masks on 11 million images, 400 times more masks than prior datasets. Zero-shot evaluation on 23 segmentation datasets showed strong single-point performance. In practice SAM became infrastructure: annotation tools, the mask stage behind open-vocabulary detectors, and a component in document, medical and video pipelines.

Failure modes in production

Coordinate bugs. Boxes drawn in the wrong place because of (x, y) versus (row, column) confusion, normalised versus pixel coordinates, or forgetting that the preprocessor resized and padded the image. Always map predictions back through the exact preprocessing transform, and visualise outputs in tests.

Threshold drift. A confidence threshold tuned on validation data behaves differently on new cameras or lighting, silently changing recall. Monitor detection counts per image over time and recalibrate per deployment.

Crowds and occlusion. NMS removes real neighbours; partially visible objects get low scores. Use set-based detectors, higher NMS thresholds or soft-NMS for crowded domains, and evaluate with crowd-specific test sets.

Domain shift. A detector trained on street photos fails on thermal images, drone views or medical scans. Fine-tune with domain data (bootstrapped with SAM and open-vocabulary detectors), and evaluate AP per domain.

VLM counting errors. Asking a VLM "how many?" on dense scenes gives confident wrong numbers. Count with a detector, or require one box per counted object.

Label noise in the evaluation set. Missing annotations make correct detections count as false positives, and loose boxes make tight predictions fail at high IoU thresholds. Before trusting AP differences of a point or two, audit a sample of the ground truth, especially for small and occluded objects.

Latency at the edge. Models benchmarked on server GPUs miss frame-rate targets on cameras and phones. Measure end-to-end latency (decode, resize, detect, NMS, track) on the deployment hardware, and consider smaller backbones or lower input resolution, at a known cost in small-object recall.

What to measure

AP at the IoU thresholds that matter for your use (AP₅₀ for "is it there", AP₇₅ or COCO AP for tight boxes), AP by object size, precision and recall at the deployed threshold, counting error, mask IoU for segmentation, grounding accuracy at IoU 0.5 for phrase grounding, and latency per image at your resolution. For tracking: identity switches and track fragmentation.

import numpy as np
from itertools import permutations

def iou(a, b):
    """boxes as (x1, y1, x2, y2) in pixels"""
    ix = max(0, min(a[2], b[2]) - max(a[0], b[0]))
    iy = max(0, min(a[3], b[3]) - max(a[1], b[1]))
    inter = ix * iy
    area = lambda r: (r[2] - r[0]) * (r[3] - r[1])
    return inter / (area(a) + area(b) - inter)

# 1. three candidate 'dog' boxes from a detector, with confidence scores
boxes = {"A": (10, 10, 110, 110), "B": (20, 20, 120, 120), "C": (100, 20, 180, 100)}
scores = {"A": 0.90, "B": 0.80, "C": 0.70}
print("IoU(A,B) =", round(iou(boxes["A"], boxes["B"]), 3), " IoU(A,C) =", round(iou(boxes["A"], boxes["C"]), 3),
      " IoU(B,C) =", round(iou(boxes["B"], boxes["C"]), 3))

# 2. one pass of greedy non-maximum suppression at IoU threshold 0.5
def nms(boxes, scores, thr=0.5):
    order = sorted(boxes, key=lambda k: -scores[k]); keep = []
    while order:
        best = order.pop(0); keep.append(best)
        order = [k for k in order if iou(boxes[best], boxes[k]) <= thr]
    return keep
print("NMS keeps:", nms(boxes, scores, 0.5), "| with threshold 0.7:", nms(boxes, scores, 0.7))

# 3. DETR-style bipartite matching: 3 predictions vs 2 ground-truth objects (+ 'no object')
pred_prob = np.array([[0.7, 0.2, 0.1],     # columns: P(cat), P(dog), P(no object)
                      [0.1, 0.8, 0.1],
                      [0.2, 0.1, 0.7]])
pred_box = np.array([[0.30, 0.40, 0.20, 0.30], [0.70, 0.50, 0.25, 0.40], [0.50, 0.50, 0.10, 0.10]])   # cx, cy, w, h
gt = [(0, np.array([0.32, 0.42, 0.20, 0.28])), (1, np.array([0.68, 0.52, 0.30, 0.40]))]
def cost(i, j):                                   # -prob of the right class + L1 box distance
    c, b = gt[j]; return -pred_prob[i, c] + np.abs(pred_box[i] - b).sum()

Compute it

Ground truth: one person at (100, 50, 200, 250). A detector outputs P1 = (110, 60, 210, 260) score 0.95, P2 = (90, 40, 190, 240) score 0.85, P3 = (300, 50, 380, 220) score 0.60. Compute IoU(P1, P2), run NMS at 0.5, and say whether each surviving box is a true or false positive at AP₅₀ and at AP₇₅.

Answer

P1 and P2: intersection x 110–190 = 80, y 60–240 = 180 → 14,400. Each box is 100 × 200 = 20,000; union = 40,000 − 14,400 = 25,600; IoU = 0.5625 > 0.5, so NMS keeps P1 (higher score) and suppresses P2. P3 does not overlap P1 and is kept.

P1 against the ground truth: intersection x 110–200 = 90, y 60–250 = 190 → 17,100; union = 20,000 + 20,000 − 17,100 = 22,900; IoU = 0.747. At AP₅₀ (threshold 0.5) P1 is a true positive; at AP₇₅ (threshold 0.75) it narrowly fails, 0.747 < 0.75, so it is a false positive and the person counts as missed. P3 matches no ground truth: a false positive at both thresholds. The near-miss at 0.75 shows why COCO averages over thresholds: tightness matters.

Design it

A construction company wants alerts when a worker without a hard hat enters a zone, from 50 fixed cameras at 5 frames per second. Sketch a pipeline and say where a VLM helps.

Answer

Per frame: a fast person detector (one-stage, fine-tuned on site footage), then a head-region classifier or a detector for "helmet" and "head", associated per person; track people across frames (tracking by detection) so alerts are per person and debounced over a second or two rather than per frame; check zone membership with polygon tests on box footpoints. 50 cameras × 5 fps = 250 frames per second, comfortably handled by detectors on one or two GPUs; a VLM on every frame would be far too slow and costly. Use a VLM only on candidate alerts, to double-check ambiguous cases ("is this person wearing a hard hat, a cap or a hood?") before notifying, and to describe the event in the alert. Bootstrap labels with an open-vocabulary detector and SAM, and evaluate recall on night, rain and occlusion footage.
