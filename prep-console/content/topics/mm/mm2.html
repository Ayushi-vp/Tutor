Suppose you run a photo library with 50 million images and someone types "a golden retriever catching a frisbee on a beach". Before 2021 your options were bad. You could train a classifier, but only for labels you had chosen in advance: if "frisbee" was not one of your 1,000 classes, the model has never heard of it. You could search the captions and alt text people wrote, but most images have none, and the ones that do say "IMG_4031". You could pay people to label images, at cents per image and weeks per million. Every new concept meant new labels and a new training run.

Meanwhile the web was full of free, messy supervision: billions of images posted next to text that describes them, product photos with titles, news photos with captions, memes with words. The text is noisy and unstructured, but it covers far more concepts than any label set. The question was how to learn from it efficiently. Training a model to write the caption for each image turns out to be slow, because there are a thousand valid ways to describe a photo and the model wastes effort predicting the exact words.

OpenAI's CLIP (2021) took an easier route: do not ask the model to write the caption, ask it to recognise the caption. Show it a batch of images and their captions, shuffled, and train it to match each image with its own caption. To do that it has to learn what a golden retriever, a frisbee and a beach look like, and what the words mean, and put both into one shared space where matching things are close. Once trained, that space does image search from free text, classifies images into any set of categories you can name, filters datasets, and gives vision-language models their eyes. SigLIP (2023) changed one piece of the loss and made the whole thing cheaper to train and better at small scale.

This lesson builds contrastive image–text learning from the ground up: the dual-encoder architecture, cosine similarity and the shared space, the similarity matrix for a batch, the CLIP loss computed by hand on a 3 × 3 matrix, temperature, zero-shot classification and prompts, then SigLIP's sigmoid loss on the same matrix and why it distributes better. After that come the known weaknesses (the modality gap, bag-of-words behaviour) and the production patterns. The public case is the CLIP paper, with its numbers checked.

The matching game

Picture a party game. Eight photos are laid face up on a table and eight caption cards are shuffled beside them. Your job is to pair them. To win, you do not need to be able to write a caption yourself; you need to look at a photo of a dog on a skateboard and recognise that "dog skateboarding down a hill" fits it better than "bowl of ramen" or "dog asleep on a sofa". The more cards on the table, and the more similar they are, the finer the distinctions you must learn to make. CLIP plays this game millions of times, with 32,768 cards per round.

Two encoders, one space

CLIP has two networks. The image encoder (a ViT from mm1, or a ResNet) turns an image into a single vector. The text encoder (a transformer like the ones in lm3) turns a caption into a single vector. Each output goes through a small learned linear layer, the projection head, so both land in vectors of the same length, for example 512 or 768 numbers. Then both vectors are scaled to length 1. This design is called a dual encoder or two-tower model: the two inputs never meet inside a network; they are only compared at the very end.

The comparison is cosine similarity (lm10): for unit-length vectors, just the dot product, which is 1 when they point the same way, 0 when they are unrelated and −1 when opposite. Training aims for one property: a matching image and caption should have a high cosine; a non-matching image and caption, low. When that holds for hundreds of millions of examples, the space becomes meaningful: the vector for a photo of a beach sits near the vector for the word "beach", near other beach photos, and away from photos of offices.

Where the analogy breaks

In the party game you look at each photo and caption together. CLIP never does: each image and each caption is encoded alone, without seeing the other side. That is a weakness (the model cannot check fine details of one caption against one image, which hurts composition) and a huge strength: you can encode 50 million images once, store the vectors, and answer every future text query with a single fast vector search (lm10). Keep both halves of that trade-off in mind; it decides when to use a dual encoder and when to use a full vision-language model.

The punchline

Once trained, the text encoder can turn any phrase into a point in the shared space. To classify an image into "cat", "dog" or "fox", embed "a photo of a cat.", "a photo of a dog.", "a photo of a fox.", and pick the nearest to the image. No labelled training examples, any label set you like. That is zero-shot classification, and it is why CLIP changed how people build vision systems.

1. The data: image–text pairs

The training set is a list of (image, text) pairs. CLIP's authors built their own: 400 million pairs collected from public internet sources, using 500,000 search queries (words and phrases common on Wikipedia and elsewhere) to cover as many visual concepts as possible, and including up to about 20,000 pairs per query so that common queries did not swamp rare ones. The text is whatever accompanied the image: a title, a caption, alt text. It is noisy (some captions do not describe the image at all) and that is accepted: the scale and breadth matter more than cleanliness. Later open efforts (LAION, DataComp) built similar sets of billions of pairs, and a lot of modern work is about filtering these sets well, for example keeping only pairs that an existing model scores as well matched.

2. Two encoders and a projection

For each pair, the image goes through the image encoder and the text through the text encoder. In CLIP the image encoder was either a modified ResNet or a ViT (ViT-B/32, ViT-B/16, ViT-L/14), and the image's vector was the pooled output (the [CLS]-style summary, mm1). The text encoder was a 12-layer transformer (lm3) over byte-pair-encoded tokens (lm1) with a 76-token context, and the text's vector was its output at the end-of-text token. Each vector is multiplied by a learned projection matrix into the shared embedding dimension, then divided by its length (L2 normalisation) so it has length 1. Call the image vectors I₁ … I_B and the text vectors T₁ … T_B for a batch of B pairs.

3. The similarity matrix

Because every vector has length 1, the dot product I_i · T_j is the cosine of the angle between them. Computing it for every image against every text is one matrix multiplication: stack the I's as rows of a B × d matrix and the T's as rows of another, and the product I Tᵀ is a B × B matrix. Entry (i, j) says how well image i matches text j. The true pairs are on the diagonal, (1, 1), (2, 2), …; everything off the diagonal is a pairing that is (almost certainly) wrong. A batch of B pairs therefore gives B positives and B² − B negatives for free, with no extra labelling: every other caption in the batch is a negative for this image. At CLIP's batch size of 32,768, that is over a billion similarity scores per step.

4. Temperature: making cosines confident

Cosines lie between −1 and 1. A softmax over values that close together is nearly uniform: even if the right caption scores 0.9 and every other one 0.1, among 32,768 candidates the right one would get only a tiny probability. So the similarities are multiplied by a logit scale s before the softmax; equivalently divided by a temperature τ = 1/s. CLIP learns s as a parameter (stored as its logarithm so it stays positive), initialised to the equivalent of τ = 0.07, a scale of about 14.3, and clips it so the scale never exceeds 100, which the authors found necessary to prevent training instability. In trained CLIP models the scale sits at that cap of 100. For the hand example we will use s = 10, so the numbers stay readable.

5. The CLIP loss, worked by hand

The CLIP loss is cross-entropy (ml5) applied twice. For each row i, softmax the scaled similarities to get a probability distribution over the B texts, and take −log of the probability on the true text i: the image is classifying which caption is its own. For each column j, do the same over the B images: the caption classifies which image is its own. Average the row losses, average the column losses, and take the mean of the two. This symmetric softmax loss is often called InfoNCE.

logits  L = s · I Tᵀ
loss_i2t = mean_i [ −log( exp(L_ii) / Σ_j exp(L_ij) ) ]     # image i picks its caption
loss_t2i = mean_j [ −log( exp(L_jj) / Σ_i exp(L_ij) ) ]     # caption j picks its image
loss     = (loss_i2t + loss_t2i) / 2

Take three pairs (a dog on a skateboard, a bowl of ramen, a red bicycle) with these cosine similarities after some training:

             T₁    T₂    T₃
     I₁  [  0.8   0.1   0.2 ]
     I₂  [  0.3   0.7   0.0 ]
     I₃  [  0.1   0.4   0.6 ]
scale s = 10  →  logits [[8, 1, 2], [3, 7, 0], [1, 4, 6]]

Row 1 (image 1 picks a caption). exp(8) = 2,981.0, exp(1) = 2.718, exp(2) = 7.389. Sum = 2,991.1. Probability on the true caption = 2,981.0 / 2,991.1 = 0.9966. Loss = −ln 0.9966 = 0.0034. Almost nothing to learn here.

Row 2. exp(3) = 20.09, exp(7) = 1,096.6, exp(0) = 1. Sum = 1,117.7. Probability = 0.9811, loss = 0.019.

Row 3. exp(1) = 2.718, exp(4) = 54.60, exp(6) = 403.4. Sum = 460.7. Probability on the true caption = 403.4 / 460.7 = 0.8756, loss = 0.133. Image 3 (the bicycle) is somewhat confused with caption 2 (ramen), which gets probability 54.6 / 460.7 = 0.119.

Mean over rows: (0.0034 + 0.019 + 0.133) / 3 = 0.052.

Columns (each caption picks an image). Column 1 holds [8, 3, 1]: probability on image 1 is exp(8) / (2,981.0 + 20.09 + 2.718) = 0.9924, loss 0.0076. Column 2 holds [1, 7, 4]: 1,096.6 / (2.718 + 1,096.6 + 54.60) = 0.9503, loss 0.051. Column 3 holds [2, 0, 6]: 403.4 / (7.389 + 1 + 403.4) = 0.9796, loss 0.021. Mean over columns: 0.026.

CLIP loss = (0.052 + 0.026) / 2 = 0.039.

What does training do with this? The gradient of a softmax cross-entropy with respect to each logit is (probability − target). In row 3 that is −0.124 for the true caption (push its similarity up), +0.119 for caption 2 (push down) and +0.006 for caption 1 (barely touched). So the loss automatically spends its effort on the hard negatives, the wrong pairings the model currently finds plausible, and ignores easy ones. That is why big batches help: the more captions in each row, the more likely some are genuinely confusing, which forces the encoders to learn fine distinctions (two dogs of different breeds, "on" versus "under").

6. Why matching beats generating

The CLIP paper reports its route to this design. The first attempt jointly trained an image CNN and a text transformer to predict each image's caption word by word. A 63-million-parameter text model learned to recognise ImageNet classes three times more slowly than a simpler baseline that only predicted a bag-of-words version of the caption (which words appear, not their order), and the contrastive objective improved efficiency a further large step. The reason is the size of the target: predicting the exact caption means modelling every stylistic choice of whoever wrote it, while matching only requires the representation to carry enough information to tell this caption apart from the others in the batch. The contrastive task asks precisely for what we want from an embedding.

7. Zero-shot classification and prompts

After training, classification needs no classifier training. For a dataset with classes c₁ … c_K, embed one text per class, normalise, and stack them into a K × d matrix W. For an image with embedding I, the scores are W I: one cosine per class. Pick the largest (or softmax them for probabilities). The CLIP paper points out what this really is: a linear classifier whose weights were written by the text encoder from the class names. The text vectors can be computed once and cached, so classification costs one image forward pass and one small matrix product.

The wording of the class text matters. Single words are rare as captions in web data, so "dog" is out of distribution for the text encoder. The template "A photo of a {label}." improved ImageNet accuracy by 1.3 points on its own. Task-specific templates help further ("a photo of a {label}, a type of pet." for a pet dataset; "a satellite photo of a {label}." for satellite images), and so does prompt ensembling: embed the class with many templates ("a photo of a big {label}", "a photo of a small {label}", …), average the embeddings, and renormalise. For ImageNet, ensembling 80 templates added 3.5 points over the single default, and prompt engineering plus ensembling together gave almost 5 points, which the paper notes is similar to the gain from four times more training compute. Ensembling is done in embedding space, so it costs nothing at inference.

8. SigLIP: one sigmoid per pair

The softmax loss has a structural property that becomes a cost at scale: the denominator of row i sums over every caption in the batch. One pair's loss depends on all others, so the full B × B matrix of similarities must be computed in one place, which in data-parallel training (dl9) means every device must all-gather every other device's embeddings. Memory grows as B², and the loss behaves differently at different batch sizes.

SigLIP (Zhai et al., 2023) replaces the row and column softmaxes with an independent yes/no question for every cell of the matrix: "do image i and text j belong together?" Each cell gets a logit t · (I_i · T_j) + b, with a learnable scale t and a learnable bias b, and a binary cross-entropy (ml6) with label z_ij = +1 on the diagonal and −1 off it:

loss = −(1/B) Σ_i Σ_j log σ( z_ij · (t · I_i·T_j + b) )      σ(x) = 1 / (1 + e^−x)

The batch has B positives and B² − B negatives, a heavy imbalance. With b = 0 at the start, every negative pair would be predicted "match" with probability about 0.5, and the huge negative loss would dominate the first updates. SigLIP initialises t to 10 (stored as log 10) and b to −10, so that at the start every pair is predicted "probably not a match", close to the true base rate.

Apply it to the same 3 × 3 matrix with t = 10:

b = −10:  cell logits = 10·cos − 10
  positives (diag): 8−10 = −2 → loss ln(1+e²)  = 2.127
                    7−10 = −3 → ln(1+e³)       = 3.049
                    6−10 = −4 → ln(1+e⁴)       = 4.018
  negatives: e.g. I₃·T₂: 4−10 = −6, label −1 → ln(1+e^−6) = 0.0025
  total / B = (2.127 + 3.049 + 4.018 + ≈0.004) / 3 = 3.066

b = −5:   positives 3, 2, 1      → losses 0.049, 0.127, 0.313
          hardest negative I₃·T₂: 4−5 = −1 → ln(1+e^−1) = 0.313
          total / B = 0.340

Three things are visible. First, each cell's loss depends only on that cell: the I₃·T₂ negative costs 0.313 at b = −5 no matter what else is in row 3. Second, the bias sets the operating point: at b = −10 the positives are under-confident and all the loss is on pulling them up; training will raise b and t together until positives sit above zero and negatives below. Third, the sigmoid view yields a calibrated-looking per-pair probability: at b = −5, σ(3) = 0.95 that I₁ and T₁ match and σ(−1) = 0.27 that I₃ and T₂ do. These are not exact probabilities in practice, but unlike CLIP's softmax they do not depend on which other items were in the batch.

9. The chunked implementation and the batch-size result

Because the sigmoid loss is a plain sum over cells, it can be computed block by block. Each of D devices holds b = B/D images and texts. Each device first computes its local b × b block (its own images against its own texts, which contains the positives). Then devices pass their text embeddings one step around a ring (a "collective permute") and compute the next block of negatives, D times in all. Losses are summed at the end. Memory per device at any moment is b² instead of B², and the paper notes D permutes are typically faster than the two all-gathers the softmax version needs.

That efficiency let the SigLIP authors study batch size directly. They trained a locked-image variant (SigLiT: a frozen pretrained image encoder, only the text side learning) at batch sizes up to one million, and found that both sigmoid and softmax losses saturate at around 32k: beyond it, gains are negligible. Below it, sigmoid beats softmax, notably at small batches. The headline efficiency number: with four TPUv4 chips, a SigLiT model using a frozen ViT-g/14 image encoder reached 84.5% zero-shot ImageNet accuracy after two days of training. The released SigLIP checkpoints (notably the 400M-parameter "So400m" vision tower) became the default image encoder of many open vision-language models.

10. How to use a trained contrastive model

There are four standard uses. Retrieval: embed a corpus of images once, store them in a vector index, embed each text query and search (lm10); the reverse (image query, text corpus) also works. Zero-shot classification and tagging, as above, including filtering datasets ("remove images that look like screenshots"). Linear probes and fine-tuning: freeze the image encoder and train a small classifier on its features, which the CLIP paper found strongly competitive. And as the eyes of a VLM (mm3): the image encoder's patch tokens, not its pooled vector, are passed into a language model. In all four, remember the dual-encoder trade: fast and scalable, but each side is encoded without seeing the other.

11. Measuring a contrastive model: recall@k by hand

Retrieval quality is reported as recall@k: for each query, is the correct item among the top k results? Averaged over queries, it is the fraction of queries that "hit" within k. It matches how retrieval is used: a re-ranker or a human only ever looks at the first few results, so being in the top 5 matters and the exact position beyond that matters less.

Use the 3 × 3 matrix again as a tiny benchmark. For text-to-image retrieval, each caption is a query and we rank the images by the column of similarities. Caption 1's column is [0.8, 0.3, 0.1]: image 1 is ranked first, a hit at k = 1. Caption 2's column is [0.1, 0.7, 0.4]: image 2 first, a hit. Caption 3's column is [0.2, 0.0, 0.6]: image 3 first, a hit. Text-to-image recall@1 = 3/3 = 1.0. For image-to-text, rank captions by each row: row 3 is [0.1, 0.4, 0.6], so caption 3 is still first. Recall@1 is also 1.0, even though the CLIP loss was not zero. This is a useful sanity check about what the loss measures: the loss keeps rewarding larger margins after the ranking is already correct, which is what makes the model robust when the next batch brings harder negatives.

Now imagine the real evaluation: the 5,000-image test split of the MS-COCO captions dataset, each image with about five human captions, so 25,000 text queries against 5,000 images. Random ranking would give text-to-image recall@1 of 1/5,000 = 0.02%. A strong contrastive model scores in the tens of percent at recall@1 and much higher at recall@5 and recall@10; the size of the step from k = 1 to k = 10 tells you how often the right image is "nearly there", which is exactly what a re-ranking stage can fix. Two practical cautions. Benchmarks with several correct captions per image make image-to-text recall look easier than text-to-image, so always report both directions. And recall on a public benchmark says little about your own data: build a few hundred labelled in-domain queries before choosing a model, because the ranking of models frequently changes between general web photos and, say, medical scans or retail catalogues.

The modality gap

Train a contrastive model and plot the embeddings: images and texts do not intermingle. They occupy two separate narrow cones of the unit sphere, and the gap between them is consistent. Liang et al. traced this to two effects. At initialisation, a deep network's outputs already occupy a narrow cone (random networks map random inputs to similar directions), and the two encoders start in different cones. The contrastive loss with a low temperature then preserves the separation, because the loss only cares about the relative ranking within each row and column, not about closing the gap. Practical consequences: a perfectly matched image–caption pair may have cosine 0.3 while two loosely related images have 0.8; a score threshold tuned for text-to-image search is meaningless for image-to-image search; and a single index mixing text and image items is biased toward the query's own modality. Calibrate thresholds separately per direction and query type, and compare scores only within one query.

Bags of words and compositionality

Benchmarks such as ARO test whether a model can pick "the horse is eating the grass" over "the grass is eating the horse", or "a red car and a blue house" over the swapped version. CLIP-style models perform near chance on many of these. Yuksekgonul et al. explain why: in random web batches, almost no negative caption shares the exact same words in a different order, so the loss never needed word order or attribute binding; a bag-of-concepts encoder scores perfectly on the training task. The fix they proposed is hard negatives built on purpose: for each caption, add a scrambled or attribute-swapped version as an extra negative, so the loss now punishes order-blindness. This improves composition benchmarks noticeably at little cost to retrieval. The interview takeaway: a contrastive objective learns only the distinctions its negatives require.

What the temperature is really doing

The temperature controls how much the loss focuses on hard negatives. The gradient on a negative logit equals its softmax probability; at a high scale (low temperature) the softmax is sharp, so nearly all the gradient goes to the one or two most similar wrong captions; at a low scale it spreads across many. Too sharp and training chases noisy near-duplicates (a different photo of the same product, with a slightly different caption, is labelled a negative but is really a positive). Too flat and fine distinctions are never learned. Learning the temperature lets the model choose; capping it at 100 prevents the runaway sharpening that caused CLIP's instability. The same reasoning explains why false negatives (duplicates in a batch) hurt more with large batches and low temperatures, and why deduplication of training data matters.

Why batch size mattered, and why it stopped mattering

With the softmax loss, the batch defines the classification problem: a batch of 32,768 is a 32,768-way classification per image. More candidates mean more chances of a genuinely hard negative and a tighter estimate of the denominator (InfoNCE is a bound on mutual information that tightens as log B grows). That argued for ever larger batches, which are expensive because of the all-gather and the B² matrix. SigLIP's sigmoid decouples the loss from the batch: each pair is judged on its own. The empirical result, saturation at around 32k for both losses, says the hard-negative benefit runs out: random web data simply does not contain enough useful extra negatives beyond that scale, and curated hard negatives are a better use of compute than a bigger random batch.

Robustness under distribution shift

A model trained on ImageNet's training set and one using CLIP zero-shot can have the same ImageNet accuracy (76.2%) but behave very differently on related datasets: sketches, renditions, unusual viewpoints. The CLIP paper compared its best model with a ResNet-101 of equal ImageNet accuracy: on ImageNet-R (renditions such as cartoons and sculptures) 88.9% against 37.7%, on ObjectNet 72.3% against 32.6%, on ImageNet Sketch 60.2% against 25.2%, closing up to 75% of the "robustness gap". The interpretation: a model fitted to one dataset learns that dataset's shortcuts, while a zero-shot model never saw that distribution's labels. When the authors adapted CLIP to ImageNet with a supervised classifier, ImageNet accuracy rose 9.2 points but robustness on the shifted sets slightly fell. The lesson for practitioners is that fine-tuning for in-distribution accuracy can cost out-of-distribution robustness; methods that interpolate between zero-shot and fine-tuned weights exist to keep both.

Variants worth being able to explain

LiT (locked-image tuning) freezes a strong pretrained image encoder and trains only the text tower, which is cheap and keeps the image features; it works well when the image encoder is already excellent. Multilingual contrastive models train the text tower on many languages so one image index serves queries in all of them. Captioning plus contrastive hybrids (such as CoCa) add a text decoder alongside the contrastive loss, recovering some of the fine-grained, ordered information a pure matching loss ignores. Data curation approaches (DataComp, filtering with an existing CLIP score) showed that which pairs you train on matters as much as model size: a well-filtered subset can beat the full noisy set at the same compute.

The public case: CLIP (Radford et al., 2021)

CLIP, "Learning Transferable Visual Models From Natural Language Supervision", is the paper that made this approach mainstream, and its numbers are worth knowing precisely. The training set was 400 million (image, text) pairs collected from the public internet, built around 500,000 search queries. The authors trained eight models, five ResNets and three ViTs (B/32, B/16 and L/14), each for 32 epochs, with Adam with decoupled weight decay, a cosine learning-rate schedule and a batch size of 32,768. The learnable temperature started at the equivalent of 0.07 and the logit scale was clipped at 100 for stability. The largest ResNet (RN50x64) took 18 days on 592 V100 GPUs; the largest ViT, 12 days on 256 V100s. ViT-L/14 was then trained one more epoch at 336 px, and this ViT-L/14@336px model is what the paper means by "CLIP".

The headline: zero-shot CLIP reached 76.2% top-1 on ImageNet, matching the original fully supervised ResNet-50 without using any of ImageNet's 1.28 million labelled training images, with 95% top-5. Across a 27-dataset suite, zero-shot CLIP beat a fully supervised logistic-regression classifier on ResNet-50 features on 16 datasets. It did very well on action recognition in video frames (Kinetics-700: 14.5 points above the ResNet-50 baseline) and poorly on specialised tasks such as satellite imagery and tumour detection, and on counting. The paper's own limitations section is candid: zero-shot CLIP was well below the state of the art on many tasks, and the authors estimated about a 1,000× increase in compute would be needed for zero-shot CLIP to reach overall state-of-the-art performance. It also reported the robustness results above and studied data overlap between its training set and evaluation sets.

The architectural lessons from the paper are the ones to quote in interviews: contrastive matching was far more compute-efficient than caption generation; CLIP ViTs were about 3× more compute-efficient than CLIP ResNets, replicating the ViT paper's finding at another scale; prompt engineering and ensembling gave about 5 points for free; and performance improved smoothly and predictably with compute across the eight models.

Failure modes in production

Threshold drift across models. Teams set "similarity > 0.28 means relevant" for one checkpoint, then upgrade the encoder and everything breaks, because each model's cosine distribution and modality gap differ. Recalibrate thresholds on a labelled sample for every model change, and store the model version with every embedding.

Mixed embedding versions in one index. Re-embedding 50 million images takes time, so indexes end up half old, half new; vectors from different models are not comparable at all. Re-index fully into a new index and switch over atomically.

Typographic and text-in-image effects. Because CLIP learned to read text in images (it can do some OCR), an image containing written words is pulled toward captions with those words. A product photo with a large brand name can match queries for the brand over the product type; a sign reading "dog" can make a picture of a cat score as a dog. For content filtering and moderation this is an attack surface (mm11 returns to image-borne attacks).

Compositional queries. "Man without a hat" returns men with hats; "two dogs" returns one or three. If your users write such queries, re-rank the top-k with a model that reads image and text together.

Bias inherited from web data. Zero-shot classifiers inherit associations in web captions. The CLIP paper documented harmful misclassifications in its bias probes. Any deployment that classifies people needs its own audit, and some category sets should not be offered at all.

What to measure

For retrieval, track recall@k on a labelled in-domain set, separately for text-to-image and image-to-image, plus latency of the embedding service and the vector index. For zero-shot tagging, track precision and recall per tag at the operating threshold, and re-check after any prompt or model change. Monitor the distribution of top-1 similarity scores in production; a sudden shift usually means a preprocessing change (resize, normalisation constants) or a model mix-up rather than a change in users.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def l2norm(x):
    return x / np.linalg.norm(x, axis=1, keepdims=True)

def logsumexp(L, axis):
    m = L.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True)
    return (m + np.log(np.exp(L - m).sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True))).squeeze(axis)

def clip_loss(img, txt, scale):
    L = scale * img @ txt.T                       # (B, B) logits, positives on the diagonal
    i2t = logsumexp(L, 1) - np.diag(L)            # each image picks its caption among B
    t2i = logsumexp(L, 0) - np.diag(L)            # each caption picks its image among B
    return (i2t.mean() + t2i.mean()) / 2

def siglip_loss(img, txt, t, b):
    L = t * img @ txt.T + b
    z = 2 * np.eye(len(L)) - 1                    # +1 on the diagonal, -1 elsewhere
    return np.logaddexp(0, -z * L).sum() / len(L) # sum of B*B independent binary losses, / B

print("CLIP loss, 3x3 example :", round(float((((logsumexp(L,1)-np.diag(L)).mean()) + ((logsumexp(L,0)-np.diag(L)).mean()))/2), 4))
for b in (-10, -5):
    z = 2 * np.eye(3) - 1
    print(f"SigLIP loss, t=10 b={b:3d}:", round(float(np.logaddexp(0, -z * (10 * S + b)).sum() / 3), 4))

# 2. random 'encoders': captions are noisy copies of their images in a 64-d space
d, B = 64, 256
for noise in (6.0, 3.0, 1.5):
    img = l2norm(rng.normal(size=(B, d)))
    txt = l2norm(img + noise * rng.normal(size=(B, d)) / np.sqrt(d))
    acc = (np.argmax(img @ txt.T, axis=1) == np.arange(B)).mean()
    print(f"noise {noise}: CLIP {clip_loss(img, txt, 100):.3f}  SigLIP {siglip_loss(img, txt, 10, -10):.3f}  "
          f"retrieval top-1 {acc:.2f}  mean cos(pos) {np.mean(np.sum(img*txt,1)):.2f}")

# 3. CLIP loss grows with batch size even for the same quality: more negatives per row
img = l2norm(rng.normal(size=(4096, d))); txt = l2norm(img + 4 * rng.normal(size=(4096, d)) / np.sqrt(d))
for B in (32, 256, 4096):
    print(f"batch {B:5d}: CLIP loss {clip_loss(img[:B], txt[:B], 100):.3f}   uniform-guess loss log(B) = {np.log(B):.2f}")

# 4. zero-shot classification: class 'prompts' are text embeddings; pick the nearest
protos = l2norm(rng.normal(size=(10, d)))                       # 10 classes
labels = rng.integers(0, 10, 500)
queries = l2norm(protos[labels] + 4 * rng.normal(size=(500, d)) / np.sqrt(d))
pred = np.argmax(queries @ protos.T, axis=1)
print("zero-shot accuracy over 10 classes:", (pred == labels).mean())

Compute it

A batch has two pairs. Cosines: I₁·T₁ = 0.5, I₁·T₂ = 0.4, I₂·T₁ = 0.1, I₂·T₂ = 0.6. Using a logit scale of 10, compute the image-to-text CLIP loss for each row, and the SigLIP loss for the pair (I₁, T₂) with t = 10 and b = −5. Which loss treats (I₁, T₂) as a bigger problem, and why?

Answer

Row 1 logits are 5 and 4: probability on the true caption = e⁵/(e⁵ + e⁴) = 1/(1 + e⁻¹) = 0.731, loss = −ln 0.731 = 0.313. Row 2 logits are 1 and 6: probability = e⁶/(e¹ + e⁶) = 1/(1 + e⁻⁵) = 0.9933, loss = 0.0067.

SigLIP for (I₁, T₂): logit 10 × 0.4 − 5 = −1, label −1, loss = ln(1 + e^{−1}) = 0.313; the model gives it σ(−1) = 0.27 probability of matching.

Here the numbers happen to coincide, but they mean different things. CLIP penalises (I₁, T₂) only relative to (I₁, T₁): if T₁ rose to 0.9, the row loss would almost vanish while 0.4 stayed. SigLIP penalises the 0.4 cosine on its own: with b = −5 any negative above 0.5 cosine is predicted as a match, regardless of the rest of the row. In a two-item batch the softmax has only one negative to learn from, which is the situation where the sigmoid loss is reported to work better.

Design it

An e-commerce team wants "search by photo" across 20 million product images and text search with phrases like "black leather boots without laces". Outline a design using contrastive encoders and say where it will fail.

Answer

Embed all 20M images once with a SigLIP or CLIP image tower (re-embed only new products), store vectors with their model version in an approximate-nearest-neighbour index, and embed queries online: photo queries with the image tower, text queries with the text tower. Retrieve the top 200. Fine-tune on catalogue image–title pairs with hard negatives mined from near-duplicate products. Calibrate separate score thresholds for image and text queries (modality gap). Failure points: negation and attributes ("without laces", "black not brown") because the text embedding is bag-like; near-identical products whose differences are tiny; brand text in images dominating matches. Mitigation: re-rank the top 200 with a VLM or cross-encoder that reads the query and each candidate together, plus structured filters (colour, category) from catalogue metadata. Evaluate recall@10 per query type before and after fine-tuning.
