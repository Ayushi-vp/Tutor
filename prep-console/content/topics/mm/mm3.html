You have a strong language model that can reason, follow instructions and write. You also have a strong vision encoder, a ViT trained with CLIP or SigLIP, that turns an image into a grid of feature vectors. Neither can answer "what is the total on this receipt?" alone: the language model cannot see, and the encoder cannot talk. Training one giant model from scratch on images and text together would cost as much as training the LLM in the first place, and throw away everything both pretrained models already know. The practical question is how to connect them.

That connection turns out to decide most of what matters about a vision-language model (VLM) in production. It decides how many tokens each image costs, which sets the price per request, the time before the first word appears, and how much GPU memory each conversation needs. It decides how much of the image's detail reaches the LLM, which decides whether the model can read a serial number or only say "a photo of a label". It decides whether you must retrain the language model, possibly changing the text behaviour your users rely on, or can keep it frozen. And it decides whether ten images in one request are fine or ruinous.

Three families of answers emerged between 2022 and 2024. Projector designs (LLaVA and most open VLMs since) translate every patch feature into a vector that looks like a word embedding and put the image in the prompt. Cross-attention designs (Flamingo, Llama 3.2 Vision) keep the image outside the prompt and let the LLM read it through new attention layers. Early-fusion designs (Chameleon) turn images into discrete tokens and train one model on everything. Inside the projector family, a second set of choices (fixed resolution, tiling, native resolution, token compression) sets the token budget.

These are not academic distinctions. When an interviewer asks you to "design a system that answers questions about uploaded invoices" or "add image input to our chatbot", the first follow-up is nearly always about cost and latency, and the honest answer starts from the architecture: how many tokens per image, whether the LLM must be retrained, and what happens to the context window when a user uploads ten pictures. Engineers who can do that arithmetic on a whiteboard, and explain why a model that is excellent at describing holiday photos may be useless on a scanned contract, stand out quickly.

This lesson builds each design from the parts you already know, with shapes and numbers: what goes into the connector, what comes out, how many tokens an image becomes, and what those tokens cost in FLOPs and KV-cache memory. The worked example prices one 1344 × 896 screenshot under four designs. The public case is Flamingo and LLaVA-1.5, read side by side, because together they define the two main branches. How these models are trained (alignment, instruction tuning, hallucination) is the next topic, mm4.

The interpreter analogy

Imagine a brilliant writer who reads only English, and a camera operator who describes scenes in a private shorthand only they understand. To get the writer to answer questions about what the camera sees, you hire an interpreter. There are three ways to run the arrangement. The interpreter can translate every line of shorthand into English and paste it into the writer's notes, so the writer reads it like any other text (projector). The writer can keep their own notes and, at certain points while writing, glance over at a separate sheet the interpreter has prepared (cross-attention). Or you can raise a new writer from childhood who learns both English and the camera shorthand as native languages (early fusion). The analogy captures the cost trade-offs well: pasting everything into the notes uses up the writer's limited page space; glancing at a separate sheet does not, but the writer has to be taught where to glance; raising a new writer takes years.

The three parts every VLM has

Almost every VLM in use is three components in a row. A vision encoder, usually a ViT pretrained with CLIP or SigLIP (mm1, mm2), turns the image into a grid of patch feature vectors: at 336 px with 14-pixel patches, 576 vectors of width 1,024. A connector converts those features into something the language model can use. A language model, a pretrained decoder-only LLM (lm3), produces the answer one token at a time. The encoder and LLM are usually reused from separate pretraining; the connector is the new piece, and the architectures differ mainly in what it does.

What "training a VLM" changes

It helps to know which parts are new and which are reused, because that decides the cost of building one. The vision encoder and the LLM each took enormous compute to pretrain and are downloaded, not trained from scratch. The connector is tiny by comparison: about 21 million parameters for LLaVA-1.5's MLP, against 7 billion in the LLM. Training a projector-style VLM is therefore mostly about teaching the connector to translate and then nudging the LLM to use the translations well, which can take a day on a single 8-GPU machine. Training an early-fusion model, by contrast, means pretraining a whole new model on trillions of mixed tokens. That difference in cost, more than any difference in quality, explains why almost every open VLM you will meet is a reused encoder plus a reused LLM plus a small new connector.

The one number to watch

For the projector family, which covers most models you will deploy, every image becomes a number of visual tokens in the LLM's input, and each costs exactly what a text token costs at prefill: it passes through every layer and occupies the KV cache (lm5). Image tokens therefore set price, latency to the first output token, and memory per conversation, and they also cap detail, because the token budget limits how many pixels effectively reach the LLM. Remember the primer's warning from mm1: a 336 px image is 576 tokens, and that resolution cannot show a receipt's small print however clever the LLM is.

If you stop here: a VLM is encoder → connector → LLM; the connector either inserts image tokens into the prompt or lets the LLM attend to them from the side; and the image-token budget is the lever that controls both cost and what the model can see.

1. The vision encoder's output, precisely

Start with the object the connector receives. LLaVA-1.5 uses CLIP ViT-L/14 at 336 × 336 px (mm1): a 24 × 24 grid of 576 patch tokens, each of width 1,024, plus a [CLS] token. VLMs typically discard the [CLS] token and take the 576 patch tokens from the penultimate layer, because the last layer is shaped most strongly by CLIP's caption-matching objective and has lost local detail. So the connector's input is a matrix V of shape 576 × 1,024, one row per patch, in raster order (top-left to bottom-right). The language model on the other side, a 7B Llama-family model, has hidden width 4,096: every text token enters as a 4,096-number embedding vector looked up from its vocabulary table. The connector's job is to get from the first object to something the second understands.

2. The projector design: image features as "visual words"

The simplest connector is a learned map applied to each patch feature separately. The original LLaVA used a single linear layer (a 1,024 × 4,096 matrix). LLaVA-1.5 found a two-layer MLP works better: a linear layer to 4,096, a GELU nonlinearity, and another linear layer 4,096 → 4,096. Its parameters are 1,024 × 4,096 + 4,096 × 4,096 + two bias vectors ≈ 21.0 million, about 0.3% of the 7B LLM. Applied to V it gives a 576 × 4,096 matrix: 576 vectors of exactly the shape of word embeddings.

These vectors are then spliced into the prompt. The chat template contains a placeholder such as <image>; the tokenizer turns the text into token IDs, the embedding table turns IDs into vectors, and at the placeholder the 576 image vectors are inserted. The LLM then runs as usual over the combined sequence, for example:

[system prompt: 35 tokens] [USER:] [576 image vectors] [What is the total? : 6 tokens] [ASSISTANT:]
→ sequence of about 620 positions; causal self-attention over all of them

From the LLM's point of view nothing special happens: it attends from the question tokens back to the image vectors exactly as it would attend to earlier words. Two consequences follow. First, everything the LLM knows about the image must be in those 576 vectors, so the encoder's resolution caps the detail. Second, the LLM must learn to interpret these unusual "words", which is why projector VLMs train the connector first (to align the spaces) and then fine-tune the LLM too (mm4).

3. Worked example: what one image costs in the LLM

Take an 8B-class LLM with 32 layers and grouped-query attention with 8 KV heads of size 128 (lm5), in bfloat16 (2 bytes per number), and an image of 576 tokens.

prefill FLOPs  ≈ 2 × params × tokens = 2 × 7e9 × 576       ≈ 8.1 TFLOPs
KV per token   = 2 (K and V) × 32 layers × 8 heads × 128 × 2 bytes = 131,072 B = 128 KiB
KV for image   = 576 × 128 KiB                              = 72 MiB
time on a GPU sustaining 300 TFLOP/s in prefill            ≈ 27 ms

Now the same image tiled into thirteen crops (section 6) is 7,488 tokens: 105 TFLOPs of prefill (about 0.35 s at that rate) and 936 MiB of KV cache, for one image in one conversation. A batch of 32 concurrent users each sending one such image needs about 29 GiB of KV just for the images. This is why image-token budgets are designed, not left to defaults. The lab reproduces these numbers for four designs.

4. Compressing the tokens: pooling, pixel shuffle and merging

Projector designs can reduce tokens before the LLM. Three methods are common, all applied to the 24 × 24 grid:

Average pooling 2 × 2: average each 2 × 2 block of neighbouring features into one, giving 12 × 12 = 144 tokens of width 1,024. Cheap, but averaging blurs features together, which hurts small text.

Pixel shuffle (used by InternVL and others): instead of averaging the 2 × 2 block, concatenate its four 1,024-vectors into one 4,096-vector, then let the MLP project it. Again 144 tokens, but no information is thrown away before the learned layer; the MLP decides what to keep.

2 × 2 merging with an MLP (Qwen2-VL): the same idea applied to a native-resolution grid; after merging, each token covers 28 × 28 pixels with 14-pixel patches. Qwen2-VL's paper gives the check: a 224 × 224 image with patch size 14 is 16 × 16 = 256 patches, merged to 64, plus two special tokens marking the start and end of the image: 66 tokens.

A 4× reduction cuts prefill and KV cost 4× and usually loses little on natural-image questions; the loss shows up on dense text and fine detail. Choosing the factor is an empirical, task-dependent decision.

5. Learned-query compressors: the Q-Former and the Perceiver Resampler

A more aggressive approach decouples the token count from the image entirely. Create a small, fixed set of learned vectors, the queries, say 64 of them. Run a few transformer layers in which the queries cross-attend (lm2) to the image features: each query computes attention weights over all 576 patches and pulls in a weighted mixture. The output is always 64 vectors, whether the input was 576 patches, 2,304 patches, or the patches of eight video frames. Flamingo's Perceiver Resampler does this with 64 outputs; BLIP-2's Q-Former uses 32 queries, producing a 32 × 768 output from, for example, 257 × 1,024 ViT-L/14 features. The BLIP-2 paper describes it as an information bottleneck that passes the most useful visual information to the LLM.

The appeal is a small, predictable cost: 64 tokens is a ninth of LLaVA's 576 and gives 1/9 the prefill and KV. The weakness is that word: bottleneck. Sixty-four vectors cannot hold the full content of a dense page, and the queries were trained to keep what helped the training tasks, which were mostly captions and short questions. Idefics2's ablations add a nuance: with its perceiver pooling, going beyond 64 visual tokens gave no gains on their benchmarks, while splitting the image into crops (more pixels in) did. The general rule is that resamplers suit natural images, video and many-image prompts; per-patch projectors with high resolution suit documents.

6. Getting more pixels in: tiling versus native resolution

CLIP-family encoders are trained at fixed small sizes (224–384 px). Two families of methods feed them larger images.

Tiling (LLaVA-1.5-HD, LLaVA-NeXT's "AnyRes", InternVL's dynamic tiling): choose a grid of crops that fits the image's aspect ratio, resize the image to fill it, encode each crop separately at the encoder's native size, and concatenate their tokens. Add a downscaled thumbnail of the whole image, encoded the same way, so the LLM keeps the global layout; LLaVA-1.5's paper notes the thumbnail reduces artefacts from splitting and merging. Tokens multiply by the number of crops plus one.

Native dynamic resolution (NaViT-style, mm1; Qwen2-VL): change the encoder itself so it accepts any grid of patches, using 2-D rotary positions instead of a fixed position table, then merge 2 × 2. Tokens then scale with pixel area. Qwen2-VL bounds the count with minimum and maximum pixel settings (its paper's evaluation used min_pixels = 100 × 28 × 28 and max_pixels = 16,384 × 28 × 28), upscaling tiny images and downscaling huge ones.

7. Positions for image tokens

Once image tokens are in the sequence, the LLM's position scheme treats them as a 1-D run: token 37 of the image is "after" token 36. But tokens 36 and 60 are vertical neighbours in a 24-wide grid, 24 positions apart. Three fixes are used. Separator tokens: insert a learned "newline" vector after each row of image tokens and between tiles, so the model can tell where rows and crops wrap; LLaVA-NeXT does this. 2-D positions in the encoder: the ViT's own 2-D rotary or learned positions already mixed geometry into each feature before the connector, so some spatial information survives flattening. M-RoPE (Qwen2-VL): split the LLM's rotary dimensions into three parts, for time, height and width. Text tokens use the same ID in all three, so for pure text it is ordinary RoPE; image tokens get their row and column; video tokens also a frame index. The next text token after the image starts from the largest position used plus one. The Qwen2-VL paper reports better downstream results than 1-D RoPE, especially on video, and robust behaviour beyond the trained length.

8. The cross-attention design: Flamingo's gated layers

Flamingo (DeepMind, 2022) starts from two frozen models: a contrastively pretrained vision encoder (an NFNet-F6, a convolutional network) and the 70B Chinchilla language model. Images go through the encoder and the Perceiver Resampler to 64 visual tokens per image. Those tokens are not put in the text sequence. Instead, new gated cross-attention blocks are inserted between the frozen LM layers. In each, the text tokens' hidden states provide the queries, and the visual tokens provide the keys and values; a dense feed-forward layer follows. The output is multiplied by tanh(α), where α is a learned scalar per layer initialised to 0, and added to the residual stream:

y = x + tanh(α_attn) · CrossAttn(query = x, key/value = visual tokens)
y = y + tanh(α_ffw)  · FFW(y)
then the frozen LM layer runs on y as before

At initialisation tanh(0) = 0, so y = x: the combined model computes exactly the frozen LM's output and its language ability is untouched. Training (only the resampler and the new layers; the LM and encoder stay frozen) gradually opens the gates. Flamingo's ablations quantify the design. Removing the zero-initialised tanh gating cost 4.2% of the overall score and caused instabilities. Inserting cross-attention every layer is best but expensive; every fourth layer trained 66% faster for a 1.9% lower score, so the 9B model inserts one every fourth layer and the 80B model every seventh. Replacing the resampler with a plain MLP or transformer of the same parameter budget did worse.

Two more Flamingo details matter in practice. It was trained on interleaved data: 43 million web pages with images and text in their original order (the M3W dataset), plus image–text and video–text pairs. Each text token attends only to the visual tokens of the most recent preceding image, which lets the model handle sequences like "image, caption, image, caption, image, ?" and gives it few-shot in-context ability: it was trained on sequences of up to 5 images but benefited from up to 32 at inference. And because visual tokens are outside the sequence, many images do not consume the context window.

9. Early fusion: one model, one vocabulary

The third family abandons the separate encoder. A learned image tokenizer (a vector-quantised autoencoder, related to the VAEs of dl10) compresses an image into a grid of discrete codes, each an integer from a fixed codebook; Chameleon maps a 512 × 512 image to 1,024 codes from a codebook of 8,192. These codes are added to the text vocabulary, and one transformer is trained from scratch on documents of mixed text and image tokens, predicting the next token whatever its type. Because image codes are ordinary output tokens, the model can generate images as naturally as text, and interleave them. The costs: full pretraining rather than adaptation, discrete codes that lose fine detail compared with continuous features (bad for OCR), and training that proved unstable at scale; the Chameleon team needed changes such as QK-norm (normalising queries and keys before the dot product) to keep the logits from diverging.

10. Which design for which job

Idefics2's controlled experiments are the best evidence on projector versus cross-attention. With both backbones frozen, the cross-attention architecture performed better. When the backbones were adapted (with low-rank adapters), the fully autoregressive (projector) architecture improved by 12.9 points against 0.6 for cross-attention, and came out ahead, although it needed care for stable training. That matches the field's drift: open VLMs since 2023 are overwhelmingly projector-style with trained LLMs. Cross-attention remains the choice when the LLM must stay frozen or when images are numerous: Meta's Llama 3.2 Vision models added cross-attention adapters to a frozen Llama 3.1 text model, so text-only behaviour is unchanged. Early fusion is chosen when image generation and free interleaving matter more than training cost.

Why projector models beat cross-attention once the LLM is trained

In a projector model image tokens are ordinary positions in the sequence, so every LLM layer's self-attention, every head, can read them, and the LLM's own MLPs process them. The image is processed with the full capacity of the language model. In a cross-attention model, image information enters only through the inserted layers, a small fraction of the network, and the LLM's layers never transform the visual tokens themselves. When the LLM is frozen this is an advantage (the inserted layers are the only trainable path and are designed for it); once the LLM can train, the projector design simply has more capacity devoted to vision. The price is context length, which is why cross-attention survives for many-image inputs.

The token-count versus detail curve

Empirical results across model families show a consistent shape. For natural-image questions (what is happening, what colour, what object), accuracy rises quickly up to a few hundred image tokens and then flattens; even 64 tokens from a good resampler do well. For text-rich images (documents, charts, screenshots, infographics) accuracy keeps rising with tokens into the thousands, because each character needs a minimum number of pixels and a token can only summarise so many characters. This is why VLM evaluations should report the image-token budget alongside scores, and why products often route requests: low-budget encoding for photos, high-budget for documents.

Freezing, unfreezing and the encoder's learning rate

The vision encoder can be frozen throughout training (cheap, stable, keeps CLIP's general features) or unfrozen in later stages. Unfreezing tends to help OCR and fine-grained tasks because CLIP's features were never optimised for reading small text, but it risks damaging the encoder's general representations if the learning rate is as large as the LLM's. Common practice is to unfreeze late, with a learning rate several times smaller than the LLM's, or to train only the encoder's last few layers. Several model families also combine two encoders: a CLIP or SigLIP encoder for semantics plus a self-supervised one such as DINOv2 for spatial detail, concatenating their features before the connector.

Separate encoders for understanding and generation

A model that both reads and generates images faces a representational conflict. Understanding wants semantic features (this is a dog, it is jumping), which CLIP-like encoders give. Generation wants features that can be decoded back to exact pixels, which discrete autoencoder codes give. Using one representation for both compromises each. Designs such as Janus keep a semantic encoder for the input path and a pixel-level tokenizer for the output path, sharing the transformer between them. This is the same lesson as early fusion's OCR weakness, from the other side: the right image representation depends on whether you are reading or writing.

KV cache and batching behaviour with images

Image tokens change the shape of a serving workload. They inflate prompt length without adding output, so a VLM endpoint looks like a long-prompt, short-answer workload: prefill-bound, with time-to-first-token dominated by image tokens. Continuous batching (lm13) still applies, but the scheduler must account for image tokens in its token budget, or a burst of tiled screenshots starves other requests. The vision encoder adds its own compute before the LLM sees anything; with large encoders and many crops it can be a noticeable share of latency, and it batches differently (by image size). Production stacks therefore run the encoder as a separate stage, cache its outputs by image hash, and let the LLM's prefix cache reuse the KV of an image across turns of the same conversation.

Positional subtleties in multi-image and video prompts

With several images in one prompt, the model must know which tokens belong to which image and in what order the images came. Projector models wrap each image's tokens in start and end markers and sometimes add an index ("Image 1:"); M-RoPE gives each image its own block of positions. Flamingo's rule, that text attends only to the most recent preceding image, is a built-in answer that makes "the second image" questions harder. When a model answers about the wrong image in a multi-image prompt, check how images are delimited before blaming the model's reasoning.

Hardware-aware choices: where the compute actually goes

It is worth doing the arithmetic for the vision encoder too, because people often assume it is negligible. A ViT-L/14 (about 300M parameters) on a 336 px image processes 577 tokens: roughly 2 × 0.3 × 10⁹ × 577 ≈ 0.35 TFLOPs, plus attention. The LLM's prefill of the resulting 576 image tokens in a 7B model is about 8 TFLOPs, more than twenty times larger. So for single images with a small encoder and a large LLM, the LLM's image prefill dominates and token reduction is the lever. The balance shifts with tiling (the encoder runs once per crop, 13 times for the screenshot above), with large encoders (InternViT-6B, a 6-billion-parameter vision tower, costs as much per token as a small LLM), and with video, where dozens of frames go through the encoder. The engineering rule: profile encoder time and LLM prefill time separately before optimising, because the right fix (smaller encoder, fewer crops, token merging, or encoder caching) depends on which one dominates.

How the connector is actually trained

The connector cannot be trained by inspection: it learns from the LLM's next-token loss, backpropagated through the frozen LLM into the connector. In stage one of LLaVA-style training (mm4 covers the full pipeline), only the connector is trained on image–caption pairs with the prompt "describe this image"; the loss is the LLM's cross-entropy on the caption tokens. This aligns the connector's outputs with regions of the LLM's embedding space that the LLM already associates with the right words, without disturbing either pretrained model. The 21M-parameter MLP learns quickly: LLaVA-1.5's pretraining stage took about 6 hours on one 8-GPU node. Then stage two unfreezes the LLM for instruction tuning.

The public case: Flamingo (2022) and LLaVA-1.5 (2023), side by side

Flamingo (Alayrac et al., DeepMind) is the reference cross-attention design. It kept a contrastively pretrained NFNet-F6 vision encoder and the 70B Chinchilla LM frozen, and trained a Perceiver Resampler (64 output tokens per image) and gated cross-attention layers inserted every 7th LM layer in the 80B model (every 4th in the 9B model). It trained on 43 million interleaved web pages (M3W) plus 312 million image–text pairs (LTIP) and video–text pairs. The headline: on 16 multimodal benchmarks, prompting the single model with a few examples set the few-shot state of the art, and on 6 of the 16 it beat the best fine-tuned models while using only 32 task-specific examples, about 1,000 times less task data. The ablations are the useful part for engineers: zero-initialised tanh gating mattered (−4.2% without it), cross-attention frequency is a speed–quality dial (every 4th layer: 66% faster training, −1.9%), and its NFNet-F6 encoder beat a CLIP ViT-L/14 at 224 px by 5.8% in its setup, showing that the encoder choice moves results substantially.

LLaVA-1.5 (Liu et al.) is the reference projector design and the template most open VLMs followed. Architecture: CLIP ViT-L/14 at 336 px (the highest-resolution CLIP available), a two-layer MLP connector, and a Vicuna LLM (7B or 13B). 576 image tokens per image. The paper's point was how far simple choices go: with the MLP connector and academic VQA data formatted with short-answer prompts, the 13B model reached state of the art on 11 of 12 benchmarks at the time, using only 1.2 million publicly available training examples and completing full training in about one day on a single node of 8 A100 GPUs. It also introduced a tiling variant, LLaVA-1.5-HD, which splits the image into 224 × 224 crops encoded separately and concatenates a downsampled whole-image feature for global context.

Read together, the two papers show the branch point. Flamingo needed no LM training and handled many images, at the cost of new layers and a fixed 64-token view. LLaVA-1.5 was simpler, cheaper to train and stronger on single-image benchmarks, at the cost of 576 context tokens per image and a trained LLM. Almost every later design is one of these two plus a resolution strategy.

Failure modes in production

Silent downscaling. The processor resizes every input to 336 px or caps native resolution with a low max_pixels; OCR quality collapses on high-resolution uploads while photos look fine. Log the actual tensor shape and image-token count per request.

Token blow-ups. A tiling configuration that allows 12 crops turns a batch of screenshots into a wall of prefill: p99 time-to-first-token jumps, KV memory runs out, other requests queue. Cap crops, downscale images whose text is not needed, and include image tokens in the scheduler's per-batch token budget.

Mismatched preprocessing. Using the encoder's default processor with a fine-tuned checkpoint that expects a different resolution or crop grid degrades quality without errors. Version the processor configuration with the weights.

Multi-image confusion. The model answers about the wrong image or blends two. Check image delimiters and ordering in the template, and evaluate multi-image prompts explicitly.

Encoder–LLM version drift. Teams sometimes swap in a newer vision encoder or a newer LLM checkpoint while keeping the old connector, assuming the interfaces match because the shapes do. The connector was trained to translate one specific encoder's feature space into one specific LLM's embedding space; with either side changed, it produces vectors the LLM misreads, and the model confidently describes things that are not in the image. Treat encoder, connector and LLM as one versioned artefact, and rerun alignment training after changing either end.

Context overflow with many images. A projector model with an 8K context can hold about a dozen 576-token images plus text; a user who uploads 30 product photos either gets an error or, worse, silently truncated history. Set an explicit images-per-request limit derived from the token budget, and fall back to fewer tokens per image (downscaling or merging) when the limit is approached.

What to measure

Per request: image count, image tokens, encoder latency, prefill latency, time-to-first-token. Per model: accuracy on a text-rich set and a natural-image set separately, at the token budget you will actually deploy. When comparing vendors or checkpoints, normalise by image tokens; a model that "wins" may simply be spending four times the tokens.

for alpha in (0.0, 0.5):
    out = h_text + np.tanh(alpha) * xattn
    print(f"gate alpha={alpha}: max change to text states = {np.abs(out - h_text).max():.4f}")

# 5. image-token budget for a 1344 x 896 screenshot, and what it costs in a 7B LLM
def tokens(design, w, h):
    if design == "fixed 336/14":         return 576
    if design == "tiles 336 + thumb":    return (int(np.ceil(w/336)) * int(np.ceil(h/336)) + 1) * 576
    if design == "native /14 + 2x2 merge": return (w // 28) * (h // 28)
    if design == "resampler 64":         return 64
kv_per_token = 2 * 32 * 8 * 128 * 2                     # K and V, 32 layers, 8 KV heads x 128, bf16 bytes
for d in ["fixed 336/14", "tiles 336 + thumb", "native /14 + 2x2 merge", "resampler 64"]:
    n = tokens(d, 1344, 896)
    print(f"{d:24s} {n:6d} tokens  prefill {2*7e9*n/1e12:6.1f} TFLOP  KV {n*kv_per_token/2**20:6.1f} MiB")

What to look for. The projector keeps one token per patch and adds about 21M parameters. Pixel shuffle reaches 144 tokens by concatenation; the transpose in its reshape is the same raster trap as mm1's patchify. The resampler's queries are untrained here, so each spreads attention over about 180 of the 576 patches; a trained one learns to focus, but 64 vectors are still all the LLM gets. The gate line is the Flamingo initialisation argument in one number. The table is the body's worked example; change the image to 4032 × 3024 (a phone photo) and watch which designs explode.

Work it out

A 1344 × 896 screenshot goes into three VLMs: (a) LLaVA-1.5 (resize to 336², ViT-L/14); (b) tiling with 336-px tiles plus a thumbnail, 576 tokens each; (c) native resolution with 14-px patches and 2×2 merging. How many image tokens does each use, and what does 10,000 such images cost at $2.50 per million input tokens?

Answer

(a) 576 tokens, whatever the input size. Cost: 5.76M tokens → $14.40. The screenshot has been squeezed to 336 × 336, so its text is unreadable.

(b) 1344/336 = 4 tiles across, 896/336 = 2.67 → 3 tiles down: 12 tiles + 1 thumbnail = 13 × 576 = 7,488 tokens. Cost: 74.9M tokens → $187. (Real implementations cap the grid, often at 4–6 tiles, and resize to fit.)

(c) (1344/28) × (896/28) = 48 × 32 = 1,536 tokens. Cost: 15.4M tokens → $38. Native resolution keeps full detail at a fifth of the tiled cost here, because tiles pad partial rows and duplicate the thumbnail. The general lesson: price and latency scale with image tokens, so choose the encoding strategy per task — and downscale screenshots whose text you do not need.

Choose it

A video-analytics team needs a model to answer questions over 16 frames sampled from a clip, on an 8B LLM with an 8K context. Frames are 448 × 448. Compare a LLaVA-style projector at native resolution with 2 × 2 merging against a Flamingo-style resampler with 64 tokens per frame.

Answer

Projector: each frame is (448/28)² = 16 × 16 = 256 tokens, so 16 frames = 4,096 image tokens, half the 8K context, 512 MiB of KV per request at 128 KiB per token, and about 57 TFLOPs of prefill. Resampler: 16 × 64 = 1,024 tokens (or zero context tokens with cross-attention), 128 MiB of KV, about 14 TFLOPs. For questions about what happens across the clip (actions, sequence of events) the resampler's coarse summaries are usually enough and far cheaper; for questions about text or small objects in specific frames, the projector's per-patch tokens win. A practical design: resampler-style budget for all frames, plus the option to re-encode a few selected frames at full token budget when the question needs detail (mm8 covers frame sampling).
