Picture a team that has just wired a CLIP encoder to a 7B LLM through a fresh MLP connector, exactly as in mm3. They upload a photo of a kitchen and ask "what is on the counter?". The model replies with grammatical nonsense, or ignores the image and talks about kitchens in general. Nothing is broken: the connector's weights are random, so the 576 image vectors it produces are noise in the LLM's embedding space. The architecture is only half of a VLM. The other half is training: teaching the connector to translate, and teaching the LLM to answer questions about images in the way users expect.

Months later the same team has a model that chats fluently about photos and tops a leaderboard. A user uploads a picture of a dining table with plates and glasses and asks for a description. The model writes a lovely paragraph that mentions the forks, the knives and the wine bottle. There are no forks, knives or bottle in the photo. This is object hallucination, the most common and most damaging VLM failure, and it is not random: the model invents exactly the objects that usually appear on dining tables. Its language model knows what dining tables tend to have, and when the visual evidence is thin, that knowledge wins.

For an AI engineer, both halves matter. You will be asked how a VLM is trained (which parts are frozen when, what data, what loss), because it decides what you can fine-tune cheaply and what you will break. And you will be asked how you know a VLM is telling the truth about an image, because a product that describes insurance photos, reads medical images or writes product listings is only as useful as it is faithful.

There is a practical reason to understand training even if you never train a VLM from scratch: you will almost certainly fine-tune one. Domain adaptation (your product photos, your forms, your medical images) uses the same stages, the same loss masking and the same data-generation shortcuts, and it is where teams most often make hallucination worse without noticing.

This lesson covers the standard two-stage recipe, using LLaVA's published numbers: alignment, instruction tuning, the loss masking that makes them work, and how instruction data is generated and mixed. Then it turns to hallucination: why it happens, how it is measured (CHAIR for captions, POPE for yes/no polling, with a worked confusion matrix) and what reduces it. The public case is LLaVA's training recipe read together with POPE's findings about the models trained that way.

Teaching a translator, then a guide

Think of hiring someone to give museum tours in a language they speak fluently about paintings they have never seen. First you need a translator: someone who can turn the curator's technical notes on each painting into the guide's language. At this stage you do not want the guide changing how they speak; you want the translator to produce sentences the guide already understands. That is stage one of VLM training, alignment: the vision encoder (the curator's notes) and the LLM (the guide) are frozen, and only the connector (the translator) learns, on simple image–caption pairs. Once the translation works, you train the guide on the job: walk through real tours, answer real visitor questions, learn when to describe and when to explain. That is stage two, visual instruction tuning: the connector and the LLM are trained together on conversations about images.

The analogy also shows where hallucination comes from. A guide who knows a lot about art but gets thin notes on a painting will fill in from general knowledge: "and in the corner, as is typical for this period, a small dog". Visitors cannot tell the difference between what is on the canvas and what the guide expects to be there. A VLM's LLM is that knowledgeable guide; when the image evidence is weak, its language prior, what text usually says in this context, fills the gap.

Why not train everything at once?

A natural question is why the stages are separate at all. Imagine unfreezing everything on day one. The connector's outputs are random, so for the first thousands of steps the LLM receives 576 vectors of noise in front of every caption. The fastest way for the LLM to lower its loss is to learn to ignore those positions and predict captions from the text prompt alone, and to adjust its weights in whatever direction fits the noise. Both are harmful: the first teaches the model that images are irrelevant, the second erodes its pretrained knowledge. Freezing the LLM in stage 1 removes both options: the only way to reduce the loss is to make the image vectors informative. Staging is a way of controlling which part of the system is allowed to adapt to which signal, and you will see the same idea in later recipes that unfreeze the vision encoder only at the end.

Two numbers to keep in mind

The first is how cheap this is relative to pretraining: LLaVA's whole two-stage recipe took about 14 hours on one machine with 8 A100 GPUs, because the expensive models are reused. That is why there are hundreds of fine-tuned VLMs and why your team can build one. The second is how unreliable early VLMs were on simple truth: in the POPE paper's evaluation, several popular instruction-tuned VLMs answered "yes" to nearly 99% of "Is there a [object] in the image?" questions, even though half the objects were absent. Training for helpful, detailed answers without training for faithfulness produces confident fiction.

If you stop here: VLMs are trained by aligning a connector with everything else frozen, then instruction-tuning the connector and LLM; and the main failure of the result is a language prior that invents plausible objects when evidence is weak, which you must measure explicitly.

1. The training objective: next-token prediction on the answer

Both stages use the same loss as LLM fine-tuning (lm6): next-token cross-entropy. Each example is turned into one sequence: the image's visual tokens, the user's text, and the assistant's response. The model predicts each next token; the loss at a position is −log of the probability it gave to the true next token. The crucial detail is which positions count. Image tokens are not words, so there is no "true next token" to predict there; the system prompt and the user's question are context written by someone else, and training the model to predict them would waste capacity and teach it to imitate users. So each position gets a weight: 1 for the assistant's answer tokens, 0 for everything else. This is loss masking.

A worked example. A sequence has 576 image tokens, an 8-token question and a 5-token answer. Suppose the model assigns probabilities 0.9, 0.7, 0.95, 0.6 and 0.98 to the five correct answer tokens. The losses are −ln 0.9 = 0.105, −ln 0.7 = 0.357, −ln 0.95 = 0.051, −ln 0.6 = 0.511 and −ln 0.98 = 0.020. The masked loss is their mean, 0.209. If instead you averaged over all 589 positions, the 584 context positions, where the model has no idea what comes next (an image position has nothing meaningful to predict), would dominate: the lab's simulation gives a mean around 5.2, with the answer contributing almost nothing to the gradient. The model would spend its training learning to predict pixels-as-tokens and questions instead of answers.

2. Stage 1: feature alignment

Goal: make the connector output vectors that the frozen LLM interprets correctly. Data: many image–caption pairs. LLaVA filtered the 3.3M-pair Conceptual Captions set (CC3M) to 595K pairs, balancing concept coverage against training cost. Each pair becomes a trivial instruction: the image, a randomly chosen request such as "Describe the image briefly", and the caption as the answer. Only the projection matrix is trained; the vision encoder and the LLM are frozen. LLaVA used a learning rate of 2e-3 and batch size 128 for one epoch, about 4 hours on 8 A100 GPUs.

Why does this work? The LLM already knows how to produce the caption "a brown dog catching a frisbee on a beach" from appropriate inputs. The gradient of the caption loss flows back through the frozen LLM into the connector and pushes its output vectors toward whatever input makes that caption likely, which in practice is a region of embedding space near the vectors for "dog", "frisbee" and "beach". After alignment, the image tokens act like a dense, soft description in the LLM's own vocabulary space. The LLM is frozen precisely because the connector starts random: if the LLM were also trainable, the early, meaningless gradients would change the LLM to fit noise, damaging its language skills. A 100× higher learning rate is safe for the 21M-parameter connector and would be destructive for the 7B LLM.

Stage 1 alone produces a model that can caption, but it only says caption-like things. Ask it a question and it describes the image anyway, because that is all it was trained to do.

3. Stage 2: visual instruction tuning

Goal: a model that follows arbitrary instructions about images: answer questions, reason, explain, follow formats, hold a multi-turn conversation. Now the connector and the LLM are trained; LLaVA used a learning rate of 2e-5 and batch size 32 for 3 epochs on 158K examples, about 10 hours on the same machine. The vision encoder stayed frozen. The format is a chat transcript: system prompt, user turn with the image and a question, assistant answer, possibly more turns, with loss on all assistant turns.

The data question is where the real work lies. Human-written multimodal conversations were scarce in 2023, so LLaVA generated them. The authors took COCO images, which have human captions and object bounding boxes, and gave text-only GPT-4 these symbolic descriptions (never the pixels), asking for three kinds of output: multi-turn conversations about the image (58K), detailed descriptions (23K) and complex reasoning questions with answers (77K), totalling the 158K examples of LLaVA-Instruct. A few hand-written examples in the prompt set the style. The trick is that captions plus boxes contain enough information for a strong LLM to write plausible questions and answers grounded in what is actually there.

4. Scaling the recipe: LLaVA-1.5's data mix

LLaVA-1.5 kept the two stages and changed the data. Alignment used 558K caption pairs; instruction tuning used a 665K mix that added academic task data (visual question answering sets such as VQAv2 and GQA, OCR-oriented sets, region-level data) and text-only ShareGPT conversations. Two lessons from it are widely reused.

Response formatting prompts. Academic VQA answers are one or two words. Mixed in naively, they teach the model to answer tersely everywhere, ruining conversation. LLaVA-1.5 appended "Answer the question using a single word or phrase." to those questions only. The model learns the format is conditional: brief when asked, detailed otherwise. This is a general pattern for mixing datasets with different answer styles: make the style explicit in the prompt.

Text-only data protects text skills. Fine-tuning the LLM only on image conversations erodes its text-only abilities. Including text-only conversations keeps them, and the LLaVA-1.5 authors observed that their model could follow instructions in several languages, which they attribute to the multilingual ShareGPT data, despite the visual data being English-only.

5. A compute budget for the recipe, by hand

It is worth checking that "a day on one machine" is plausible, because the same arithmetic tells you what your own fine-tune will cost. Use the standard estimates from lm7: a full training step costs about 6 FLOPs per parameter per token (2 for the forward pass, 4 for the backward pass, which computes gradients for both activations and weights). When the LLM is frozen, its weight gradients are skipped but gradients must still flow back through it to reach the connector, so the cost is about 4 FLOPs per parameter per token.

Take LLaVA-1.5 with a 13B LLM. Stage 1 has 558K examples of about 600 tokens each (576 image tokens plus a short prompt and caption): 3.3 × 10⁸ tokens. Cost ≈ 4 × 13 × 10⁹ × 3.3 × 10⁸ ≈ 1.7 × 10¹⁹ FLOPs. Eight A100 GPUs have a dense bfloat16 peak of 8 × 312 = 2,496 TFLOP/s; at a realistic 40% utilisation, about 10¹⁵ FLOP/s. Time ≈ 1.7 × 10¹⁹ / 10¹⁵ ≈ 17,000 s, under 5 hours. The paper reports 6 hours. Stage 2 has 665K examples averaging perhaps 800 tokens (multi-turn conversations are longer): 5.3 × 10⁸ tokens, now with full training, ≈ 6 × 13 × 10⁹ × 5.3 × 10⁸ ≈ 4.1 × 10¹⁹ FLOPs, about 11 hours at the same rate; the paper reports 20, which is within the usual factor of two for padding, data loading and lower utilisation on variable-length batches.

The lesson in the numbers: image tokens dominate the token count (576 of about 600 in stage 1), so the image-token budget from mm3 sets the training bill too. Doubling resolution to a tiled or native high-resolution design that averages 2,000 tokens per image roughly triples both stages. This is why many recipes run alignment at low resolution and switch to high resolution only for later stages, and why the LLM's size, not the encoder's, is the main cost driver: the 300M-parameter encoder is a rounding error next to 13 billion LLM parameters touched by every image token.

6. Where hallucination comes from

With a trained model in hand, the dominant failure appears. Object hallucination means describing objects, or attributes and relations, that are not in the image. Four causes compound.

The language prior. The LLM was pretrained on trillions of text tokens and knows that dining tables have forks, kitchens have knives and beaches have umbrellas. At each step of generation it combines this prior with the visual evidence in the image tokens. When the evidence is strong (a large, clear object) it wins; when it is weak (a small object, a blurry region, a 336 px image of a large scene) the prior can win. The POPE authors tested this directly: objects that appear frequently in the instruction data, and objects that co-occur frequently with the objects actually in the image, are hallucinated more often.

Low resolution. If the encoder sees a 2,000-pixel photo at 336 px, small objects are a few pixels in one patch. The model is answering from almost no evidence, so the prior dominates (mm3's token budget again).

Training data that rewards elaboration. Detailed-description data teaches the model that a good answer mentions many things. Generated data can include details that the caption implied but the image does not show, and the model learns to produce them. Few training examples teach the model to say "there is no X" or "I can't tell".

Generation dynamics. Hallucinations increase later in long descriptions: as the text grows, the model attends more to its own previous words and relatively less to the image tokens, so continuation by text association takes over. Short answers hallucinate less than long ones for this reason.

7. Measuring it in captions: CHAIR

CHAIR (Rohrbach et al., 2018) measures hallucination in generated captions against object annotations, originally on COCO's 80 object categories. Parse each caption for object words (with synonym lists, so "puppy" counts as dog), compare them with the image's annotated objects, and compute two numbers:

CHAIR_i = (# hallucinated object mentions) / (# object mentions)        per instance
CHAIR_s = (# captions with ≥ 1 hallucinated object) / (# captions)       per sentence

Worked example with four captions. Caption 1 mentions dog, frisbee, grass; the image has dog, frisbee, grass, tree: no hallucination. Caption 2 mentions man, laptop, cup; the image has man, laptop, desk: "cup" is hallucinated. Caption 3 mentions cat, sofa; the image has cat, sofa, pillow: fine (omitting the pillow is not a hallucination). Caption 4 mentions car, street, person, traffic light; the image has car, street, building: "person" and "traffic light" are hallucinated. Mentions total 3 + 3 + 2 + 4 = 12, of which 3 are hallucinated: CHAIR_i = 3/12 = 0.25. Two of four captions contain a hallucination: CHAIR_s = 2/4 = 0.50. Notice that the fourth, longest caption contributed two of the three errors: CHAIR rewards brevity, so always compare models at similar caption lengths.

CHAIR's weaknesses motivated the next metric. It depends on the prompt: the POPE authors found two near-identical instructions could double the CHAIR values for the same model. It depends on the parsing rules and synonym lists. And it needs object annotations for every evaluation image.

8. Measuring it by polling: POPE

POPE (Li et al., 2023) turns hallucination into a yes/no classification task. For each image, take objects that are present (from annotations, or from a segmentation model for unannotated images) and objects that are absent, and ask "Is there a {object} in the image?" for each. The answer should be yes for present objects and no for absent ones. Because the questions are short and closed, results are stable across prompt wording. The clever part is how absent objects are chosen, in three settings:

Random: any object category not in the image. Popular: the most frequent object categories in the whole dataset that are not in this image (person, car, chair...). Adversarial: objects ranked by how often they co-occur with the objects that are in the image, and not present (a fork for a table with plates). Each setting is a test of a different cause: random tests basic perception; popular tests the frequency prior; adversarial tests the co-occurrence prior. The paper's evaluation used 500 COCO validation images with more than three annotated objects and six questions per image, half present and half absent, 3,000 questions per setting.

Scoring treats "yes" as the positive class. With TP = present objects answered yes, FP = absent objects answered yes, FN = present objects answered no, TN = absent objects answered no: accuracy = (TP + TN)/total, precision = TP/(TP + FP), recall = TP/(TP + FN), F1 = 2PR/(P + R), and the yes-ratio = (TP + FP)/total, which on a balanced set should be near 50%.

A worked example on 3,000 questions (1,500 present, 1,500 absent). A yes-biased model says yes to 1,470 present objects and to 1,380 absent ones:

TP = 1,470   FN = 30   FP = 1,380   TN = 120
precision = 1,470 / 2,850 = 0.516     recall = 1,470 / 1,500 = 0.980
F1 = 2 × 0.516 × 0.980 / (0.516 + 0.980) = 0.676
accuracy = (1,470 + 120) / 3,000 = 0.530    yes-ratio = 2,850 / 3,000 = 95%

A calibrated model says yes to 1,320 present and 150 absent objects:

TP = 1,320   FN = 180   FP = 150   TN = 1,350
precision = 1,320 / 1,470 = 0.898     recall = 0.880
F1 = 0.889    accuracy = 0.890    yes-ratio = 49%

The first model looks better on recall and would impress anyone who only tested images where the object is present. The yes-ratio exposes it immediately. This is not hypothetical: the POPE paper reported that LLaVA, MultiModal-GPT and mPLUG-Owl answered yes close to 99% of the time, with F1 below 70, while InstructBLIP, trained on large amounts of short-answer VQA data, did best. And every model's score fell from random to popular to adversarial, exactly as the co-occurrence explanation predicts.

9. Beyond objects: attributes, relations and counting

Object existence is the easiest hallucination to measure and not the only one. Models also hallucinate attributes (a red car that is blue), relations (a cup "on" the laptop that is beside it), counts (three people when there are two) and text (misread or invented words in signs and documents). Benchmarks that extend polling to these (asking "Is the car red?", "Are there three people?") use the same logic: balanced yes/no questions, adversarial negatives drawn from plausible confusions, precision and recall reported separately. For text in images, exact-match accuracy against the true transcription is the honest metric, because a fluent but invented reading of a serial number is worse than "unreadable".

Preference tuning against hallucination

Supervised fine-tuning shows the model good answers but never shows it what a bad one looks like. Preference methods (lm6) do. Collect pairs of answers to the same image and question where one is faithful and the other contains a hallucinated object or attribute, either from human annotators correcting model outputs or by automatically corrupting faithful answers (insert a co-occurring object that is absent). Then train with DPO, which raises the probability of the preferred answer relative to the rejected one, or with RLHF using a reward model trained on such pairs. Because the rejected answer differs from the chosen one mainly in the hallucinated span, the gradient concentrates exactly on the tokens that introduced the false object. Sun et al.'s factually augmented RLHF added a twist relevant to reward hacking: the reward model was given extra ground-truth information (such as the image captions) so it could recognise hallucinations it would otherwise miss, because a reward model that cannot see the error will happily reward fluent fiction.

Decoding-time mitigations

Several methods change how tokens are chosen rather than retraining. Visual contrastive decoding (Leng et al.) runs the model twice per step: once with the real image and once with a distorted version (heavily noised). Tokens that are likely in both runs are being driven by the language prior, since the distorted image carries little information; tokens whose probability drops a lot without the real image are being driven by visual evidence. The method boosts the second kind by combining the two distributions, roughly (1 + α) × logits(real) − α × logits(distorted), and restricts sampling to tokens that were plausible under the real image. It costs a second forward pass per token. Other approaches penalise the tendency to attend to a few summary tokens in long generations, or re-inject image attention as generation proceeds. These help at the margin; they cannot create evidence that low resolution destroyed.

Teaching abstention

A model that has never seen "I can't tell from this image" or "there is no dog in this picture" in training will rarely say it. Adding negative and uncertain examples (questions about absent objects with "no" answers, unreadable text with "the text is not legible", ambiguous counts with ranges) changes the base rate the model learns. This links directly to POPE's yes-ratio: data with balanced yes and no answers produces models with balanced yes-ratios. LLaVA-1.5's inclusion of short-answer VQA data with natural "no" answers is part of why later models in that family behaved better on polling benchmarks than the original LLaVA. The trade-off is over-refusal: a model trained heavily on "I can't tell" may abstain on clear images, so measure both hallucination and unnecessary abstention.

There is also a measurement subtlety in abstention. A model that says "I'm not sure" on 20% of questions and is right on all the rest looks worse on accuracy than one that guesses everything and is right 85% of the time, yet for a product that routes uncertain cases to a human it is far more useful. Report selective accuracy, the accuracy on answered questions, together with coverage, the share answered, the same way you would for any classifier with a reject option.

Synthetic data: the hidden hallucination pipeline

Generated instruction data has a structural risk worth understanding. When a text-only LLM writes a "detailed description" from a caption and bounding boxes, it will naturally fill in plausible context ("the kitchen is well lit, with utensils neatly arranged") that the annotations do not support. Train on 23,000 such descriptions and you have trained the VLM to produce plausible unsupported context. Later data pipelines address this by generating with a strong VLM that sees the image (so claims are at least visually motivated), by filtering generated answers with a detector or a verifier VLM that checks each mentioned object, and by preferring dense human annotations for description data. The general rule: any detail in an answer that the training image does not show is a lesson in hallucination.

Catastrophic forgetting and data mixing

When stage 2 updates all 7B LLM parameters on image conversations, its text-only skills drift: coding, math, long-form writing and safety behaviour can degrade, because nothing in the visual data exercises them and the parameters move to fit the new distribution. Three defences are standard. Mix text-only instruction data into stage 2 (LLaVA-1.5's ShareGPT portion, and much larger proportions in later recipes), so the old skills keep receiving gradient. Use parameter-efficient fine-tuning such as LoRA (lm11), which leaves the base weights unchanged and constrains the update to a low-rank subspace. Or freeze the LLM entirely and use a cross-attention design (mm3). Whatever you choose, evaluate text benchmarks before and after visual training as a regression test; the drop is often invisible on multimodal leaderboards.

Stage design beyond two stages

Modern recipes often insert more steps. A large-scale pretraining stage on hundreds of millions of interleaved image–text documents (as in Flamingo) or high-quality dense captions, with the LLM partly unfrozen, builds broad visual knowledge before instruction tuning. A high-resolution or document stage unfreezes the vision encoder at a low learning rate and trains on OCR and chart data. A final preference stage targets hallucination and refusals. Each stage has a different data mix and set of trainable parameters, and the main engineering question at each is the same as in stage 1: which parameters are ready to move, and which would be damaged by gradients they cannot yet use.

Why metrics disagree

A model can improve on POPE and get worse on CHAIR, or the reverse. POPE measures whether the model answers a direct question correctly, which mostly reflects perception plus yes-bias. CHAIR measures what it volunteers in free-form description, which reflects generation dynamics and style: a model trained to write longer descriptions will score worse on CHAIR_s even with better perception. Neither measures attribute or relation hallucination, and both are tied to COCO's 80 object categories. Senior practice is to treat them as complementary, add domain-specific polling for the attributes your product depends on, and look at a sample of outputs by hand every time a number moves.

The public case: LLaVA's recipe and the POPE findings

LLaVA (Liu et al., "Visual Instruction Tuning", 2023) published the recipe in enough detail to reproduce. Architecture: a CLIP ViT-L/14 encoder, a single linear projection, and a Vicuna LLM. Stage 1: 595K image–caption pairs filtered from CC3M, encoder and LLM frozen, only the projection trained, learning rate 2e-3, batch 128, one epoch; under 4 hours on 8 A100s. Stage 2: LLaVA-Instruct-158K (58K conversations, 23K detailed descriptions, 77K complex reasoning), generated by text-only GPT-4 from COCO captions and bounding boxes, projection and LLM trained, learning rate 2e-5, batch 32, 3 epochs; about 10 hours. Results reported by the paper: an 85.1% relative score compared with GPT-4 on its synthetic instruction-following benchmark, and a new state-of-the-art 92.53% on ScienceQA when combined with GPT-4 as a judge. The recipe's cheapness, about 14 GPU-node hours, is why it became the template for most open VLMs.

POPE (Li et al., "Evaluating Object Hallucination in Large Vision-Language Models", 2023) examined models built that way. On CHAIR, LLaVA's captions hallucinated more than an older, smaller captioning model (CHAIR_s 32.7 against 13.0 for OSCAR-base with the same instruction), which suggested that instruction tuning itself was making hallucination worse. Their analysis of instruction data found that objects frequent in the instruction corpus, and objects co-occurring with the real ones, were hallucinated more. Their polling benchmark (500 COCO images, 6 questions each, random/popular/adversarial negatives) found LLaVA, MultiModal-GPT and mPLUG-Owl answering yes close to 99% of the time with F1 below 70, InstructBLIP best, and all models declining from random to popular to adversarial. The paper also showed CHAIR's instability: two similar prompts could double CHAIR values for the same model, while POPE's results were stable across prompt templates.

Together the two papers tell a cautionary story that is useful in interviews: a cheap, effective recipe for making helpful VLMs, measured on helpfulness, produced models that agreed with almost any existence question. The fixes that followed (short-answer VQA with natural "no" answers, formatting prompts, higher resolution, preference tuning) came from measuring the right thing.

Failure modes in production

Confident detail in low-quality images. Users upload dark, blurry or tiny photos; the model describes them as confidently as sharp ones. Detect image quality upstream (size, blur) and route to a "please upload a clearer photo" path or force short, hedged answers.

Leading questions. "What brand is the laptop?" presupposes a laptop. Yes-biased models answer with a brand even if there is no laptop. Test your model with presupposition-violating questions and train or prompt it to correct false premises.

Domain co-occurrence. In insurance photos, a damaged bumper "comes with" a broken headlight in the training data; the model reports both. Build adversarial polling sets from your own domain's co-occurrence statistics.

Regression after fine-tuning. A domain fine-tune improves task accuracy and silently raises the yes-ratio or degrades text-only skills. Keep POPE-style and text-only regression suites in CI for every checkpoint.

Benchmark-tuned but user-blind. A model fine-tuned heavily on short-answer VQA data posts excellent POPE numbers but gives curt, unhelpful answers in chat, or refuses to describe images in detail. Optimising one hallucination metric can trade away usefulness. Evaluate helpfulness (human ratings on real requests) alongside faithfulness, and treat a jump in one with a drop in the other as a regression, not a win.

What to monitor

Offline: POPE-style F1 and yes-ratio in all three settings, CHAIR on descriptions at a fixed length, domain-specific attribute polling, text-only benchmarks. Online: user corrections and thumbs-down rates by image size and category, the share of answers that mention objects a lightweight detector does not find (a cheap automatic hallucination signal), and abstention rate, which should be neither zero nor high.

Goal: compute the masked instruction-tuning loss, simulate POPE for a yes-biased model across the three negative-sampling settings and compare it with a calibrated model, and compute CHAIR on the lesson's four captions. NumPy only; existing gpt_env (Python 3.12).

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 1. instruction tuning loss: only the assistant's answer tokens count
#    sequence = [576 image tokens][question: 8 tokens][answer: 5 tokens]
p_correct = np.concatenate([rng.uniform(0.001, 0.01, 576),   # model can't predict image 'tokens' (no targets anyway)
                            rng.uniform(0.05, 0.3, 8),       # question tokens: hard to predict
                            [0.9, 0.7, 0.95, 0.6, 0.98]])    # answer tokens
mask = np.r_[np.zeros(576 + 8), np.ones(5)]
nll = -np.log(p_correct)
print(f"loss on answer only: {(nll * mask).sum() / mask.sum():.3f}   (unmasked mean would be {nll.mean():.3f})")

# 2. POPE-style evaluation: 500 images x 6 questions, half 'yes' (object present), half 'no'
def pope(answers_yes, truth_yes):
    tp = np.sum(answers_yes & truth_yes); fp = np.sum(answers_yes & ~truth_yes)
    fn = np.sum(~answers_yes & truth_yes); tn = np.sum(~answers_yes & ~truth_yes)
    prec = tp / (tp + fp); rec = tp / (tp + fn)
    return dict(acc=(tp + tn) / len(truth_yes), prec=prec, rec=rec,
                f1=2 * prec * rec / (prec + rec), yes=answers_yes.mean())

truth = np.r_[np.ones(1500, bool), np.zeros(1500, bool)]
# a 'yes-biased' model: sees present objects 98% of the time, but says yes to absent ones
# with a probability that grows with how often the absent object co-occurs with what is there
for setting, p_false_yes in [("random", 0.75), ("popular", 0.88), ("adversarial", 0.92)]:
    ans = np.where(truth, rng.random(3000) < 0.98, rng.random(3000) < p_false_yes)
    m = pope(ans, truth)
    print(f"{setting:11s} acc {m['acc']:.3f} prec {m['prec']:.3f} rec {m['rec']:.3f} F1 {m['f1']:.3f} yes {m['yes']:.0%}")
calibrated = np.where(truth, rng.random(3000) < 0.88, rng.random(3000) < 0.10)
m = pope(calibrated, truth)

Diagnose it

Your team fine-tuned an open VLM on 40,000 product-description examples written by a text-only LLM from catalogue metadata (title, category, attributes). Task accuracy went up, but customers report descriptions mentioning accessories that are not in the photo, and a POPE-style test on your catalogue shows yes-ratio rising from 52% to 81%. What happened and what do you do?

Answer

The generated descriptions were written from metadata, not pixels, so they mention what products in that category usually include (a phone "with charger and case") whether or not the photo shows it. Fine-tuning taught the model to produce category-typical details: a domain language prior, visible as the rising yes-ratio. Fixes: regenerate descriptions with a VLM that sees the image, or filter generated ones with a detector or verifier that checks each mentioned item against the photo; add examples that state absence ("charger not shown"); build adversarial polling from your catalogue's co-occurrence statistics (accessories that usually accompany each category) and track F1 and yes-ratio per category in CI; optionally run a DPO pass with rejected answers that add absent accessories. Re-check text-only and task metrics after each change.

Compute it
