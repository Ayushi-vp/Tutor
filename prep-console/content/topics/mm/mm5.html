By 2021 generative models could produce convincing faces, bedrooms and cats, each from a model trained on that one category. What people wanted was different: type "a watercolour of a lighthouse in a storm, seen from a small boat" and get that picture, for any sentence. Two obstacles stood in the way. The models that produced the best images, diffusion models, worked directly on pixels and needed hundreds of network passes per image; at 512 × 512 resolution, each pass over 786,432 numbers was expensive, so generating one image took minutes on a large GPU and training took hundreds of GPU-days. And there was no clean way to make an arbitrary sentence steer the result strongly enough: samples were either faithful to the prompt and ugly, or beautiful and off-topic.

Three ideas, published over about two years, removed both obstacles. Latent diffusion (Rombach et al., 2021) compressed images with an autoencoder first and ran diffusion on the compressed version, 48 times smaller; its open release as Stable Diffusion in 2022 put text-to-image on consumer GPUs. Cross-attention conditioning let every part of the image read every word of the prompt at every step. Classifier-free guidance gave a single dial to trade natural-looking images against prompt adherence. Then flow matching and distillation attacked the remaining cost, the number of steps, bringing generation from 50 steps to as few as one to four.

An AI engineer meets these models as products (image generation features, design tools, synthetic data pipelines) and as systems (GPU cost per image, latency, safety filtering). Interviews probe whether you can explain the mechanism beyond buzzwords: what exactly is being predicted, why the latent space, what guidance does to the numbers, why step count can drop, and what breaks.

The same machinery also underlies video generation (mm8), image editing, super-resolution and a growing share of audio generation, so the mechanism you learn here transfers. If you can explain one denoising step with real numbers, the guidance formula and the latent-space arithmetic, you can reason about all of them.

This lesson builds text-to-image generation from the ground up. First the forward noising process with DDPM's real schedule, computing one pixel's journey by hand; then the training objective and sampling; then the latent autoencoder and its compression arithmetic; text conditioning through cross-attention; classifier-free guidance with a worked mixing example; faster samplers; and flow matching, with a lab that shows why one step lands on a blurry average. The public case is the latent diffusion paper and the Stable Diffusion v1 release built on it.

The sculptor and the fog

Imagine a photograph slowly disappearing into fog. Each second a little more grey haze covers it, until after a thousand seconds there is only uniform fog and the photo is gone. Now imagine a sculptor trained to do the reverse: shown a foggy photo and told how foggy it is, they can guess what fog to remove to make it a little clearer. If they are good enough at that small task, you can hand them pure fog and ask them to clear it step by step. They will not recover any particular photo, since the fog has no memory of one, but they will produce a plausible photo, because every guess they make is shaped by the thousands of photos they practised on. That is a diffusion model: a fixed, easy process that turns data into noise, and a learned network that undoes it a little at a time.

The analogy is close to the mathematics. The "fog" is Gaussian noise; the "how foggy" is the timestep t, from 0 (clean) to T (pure noise); and the sculptor's guess is a neural network's prediction of the noise present in the image. Where it breaks: a real sculptor would clear the fog from the edges inward, but a diffusion model updates every pixel at every step. Early steps decide the overall composition and colours; later steps refine texture. And the network never sees the photo it is "recovering"; it only learned the statistics of photos in general.

Three additions that make it text-to-image

On its own, this process generates random images from the training distribution. Text-to-image systems add three things. First, they work in a latent space: an autoencoder compresses each 512 × 512 image into a 64 × 64 × 4 array, diffusion runs there, and a decoder turns the final latent into pixels. Second, the denoising network receives the prompt, encoded by a text encoder, and reads it through cross-attention, so every guess about the noise depends on the words. Third, at sampling time, guidance exaggerates the effect of the prompt: the network predicts the noise with and without the prompt, and the sampler moves further in the direction of the difference.

The cost picture

The network that predicts noise is the expensive part, and it runs once (twice with guidance) per step. Stable Diffusion v1 used an 860-million-parameter U-Net, 50 sampling steps by default and a guidance scale of 7.5: 100 network evaluations per image, on a latent of 16,384 numbers. Without the latent space, each evaluation would be on 786,432 pixels. Without guidance, half the evaluations. With a flow-matching or distilled model, four steps instead of fifty. Every speed-up in this field attacks one of those three factors.

If you stop here, you have the right picture: noise is removed step by step by a network that predicts it; the whole process runs in a compressed latent; the prompt is read through cross-attention and amplified by guidance; and the step count is the main cost.

1. The forward process: adding noise on a schedule

Start with a clean data sample x_0 (for now, a pixel value scaled to [−1, 1], or a whole image of them). The forward process produces progressively noisier versions x_1, x_2, …, x_T. At each step it shrinks the current value slightly and adds a little Gaussian noise:

x_t = √(1 − β_t) · x_{t−1} + √β_t · ε_t        ε_t ~ N(0, 1), fresh each step

β_t is the noise schedule: the variance of the noise added at step t. DDPM (Ho et al., 2020) used T = 1,000 steps with β rising linearly from β_1 = 10⁻⁴ to β_T = 0.02. The shrink factor √(1 − β_t) keeps the overall variance near 1: if x_{t−1} has variance 1, then x_t has variance (1 − β_t) + β_t = 1. So the process gradually swaps signal for noise without the values blowing up.

One step by hand, with x_0 = 0.8 and ε = −1.2 at t = 1: x_1 = √(1 − 0.0001) × 0.8 + √0.0001 × (−1.2) = 0.99995 × 0.8 + 0.01 × (−1.2) = 0.79996 − 0.012 = 0.788. A tiny change, as intended: the first steps barely touch the data.

2. Jumping straight to any step: the closed form

Running 500 single steps to get x_500 would be slow during training. Fortunately, adding Gaussian noise repeatedly gives Gaussian noise, so x_t can be written directly in terms of x_0. Define α_t = 1 − β_t and ᾱ_t (alpha-bar) as the product α_1 × α_2 × … × α_t. Then

x_t = √ᾱ_t · x_0 + √(1 − ᾱ_t) · ε        ε ~ N(0, 1)

√ᾱ_t is how much of the original signal survives; √(1 − ᾱ_t) is the standard deviation of the accumulated noise. With DDPM's linear schedule:

The pixel at t = 500, with the same x_0 = 0.8 and ε = −1.2: x_500 = 0.280 × 0.8 + 0.960 × (−1.2) = 0.224 − 1.152 = −0.928. The original value contributes less than a quarter of the result. By t = 1,000 the signal factor is 0.006: x_T is, for practical purposes, pure N(0, 1) noise, which is what lets sampling start from random noise. The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) column is the most useful way to think about a timestep: it falls from about 10,000 to 0.00004, and crosses 1 near t = 250. The lab checks the closed form against 500 single steps by sampling: both give mean 0.224 and standard deviation 0.960.

3. Training: predict the noise

The reverse direction, going from x_t to x_{t−1}, is what we want to learn. DDPM showed a convenient way to parameterise it: train a network ε_θ(x_t, t) to predict the noise ε that was mixed in. The training loop is short:

repeat:
    x_0 = a training image (latent)
    t   = random integer in 1..1000
    ε   = random Gaussian noise of the same shape
    x_t = √ᾱ_t · x_0 + √(1 − ᾱ_t) · ε          # closed form, one line
    loss = ‖ ε − ε_θ(x_t, t) ‖²                 # plain mean-squared error
    gradient step on loss

Each training example is an independent regression problem at a random noise level, which is why diffusion training is stable compared with GANs (dl10): there is no adversary, just a denoiser getting better at every noise level. DDPM derived this from a variational bound on the likelihood and found that the unweighted version (their "simplified" objective) gave better samples than the weighted bound, because it down-weights the smallest-noise steps, which only affect imperceptible detail. Their unconditional CIFAR-10 model reached an FID of 3.17, state of the art at the time.

Why is predicting the noise enough? Because given x_t and a noise estimate ε̂, you can estimate the clean data by rearranging the closed form: x̂_0 = (x_t − √(1 − ᾱ_t) · ε̂) / √ᾱ_t. At t = 500 our pixel was −0.928. If the network predicts ε̂ = −1.1 instead of the true −1.2, then x̂_0 = (−0.928 − 0.960 × (−1.1)) / 0.280 = (−0.928 + 1.056) / 0.280 = 0.458, not 0.8. An error of 0.1 in the noise becomes an error of 0.34 in the estimate of the clean pixel, amplified by 0.960 / 0.280. At high noise the network can only get the rough picture right; precise detail must be decided at later, low-noise steps, which is exactly how sampling proceeds.

4. Sampling: walking back from noise

To generate, draw x_T from N(0, 1) and repeatedly step toward t = 0. DDPM's ancestral sampler uses the network's ε̂ to compute the mean of x_{t−1} given x_t, then adds a small amount of fresh noise, for all 1,000 steps. That is slow. The key speed-up came from DDIM (Song et al., 2020), which showed that the same trained network defines a deterministic path: at each step, estimate x̂_0 from ε̂, then jump to a lower noise level by re-noising x̂_0 with the predicted noise instead of fresh noise. Because the path is deterministic, it can be followed with large jumps, so 50, 25 or even 10 steps give good images from a model trained with 1,000. A side benefit: the same starting noise gives the same image, which makes "seeds" reproducible and lets you interpolate between them.

Viewed abstractly, deterministic sampling solves an ordinary differential equation (ODE): dx/dt = v(x, t), where v is derived from the network's prediction. Sampling is numerical integration of that ODE from t = T to t = 0. Everything that follows about faster samplers and flow matching is about making this integration take fewer, more accurate steps.

5. Latent diffusion: the autoencoder

Running the denoiser on pixels at 512 × 512 means a network over 786,432 numbers, 50+ times per image. Most of those numbers encode fine texture that is perceptually redundant. Latent diffusion (Rombach et al.) splits the job in two. First, train an autoencoder once: an encoder E that maps an image to a smaller latent z = E(x), and a decoder D with D(z) ≈ x. It is trained with a reconstruction loss plus a perceptual loss and an adversarial loss (so the decoder produces sharp textures, not blur), and a light regulariser on the latent: a small KL penalty toward a standard normal as in a VAE ("KL-reg") or a vector-quantisation layer ("VQ-reg"). Second, train the diffusion model on latents instead of pixels.

The compression arithmetic for Stable Diffusion v1, which uses a downsampling factor f = 8 and 4 latent channels:

pixels : 512 × 512 × 3       = 786,432 numbers
latent : (512/8) × (512/8) × 4 = 64 × 64 × 4 = 16,384 numbers
ratio  : 786,432 / 16,384      = 48×

Every denoising step now works on 48× fewer numbers, and because a U-Net's cost scales with spatial area, the saving in FLOPs is similar. The LDM paper studied the factor directly: f = 1 (pixel space) trained slowly; f = 4 to 8 gave the best trade-off; f = 32 compressed so hard that sample quality stagnated, because the decoder could no longer reconstruct detail. Two consequences are worth remembering. The decoder sets a ceiling: details the autoencoder cannot reconstruct (small text, faces at a distance, fine hands) cannot be generated, whatever the diffusion model does. And the latent's statistics matter: SD v1 scales latents by a constant (0.18215) so their variance is close to 1, matching the noise process's assumption.

6. Conditioning on text: cross-attention

The denoiser must know the prompt. Stable Diffusion v1 encodes it with a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 text encoder (mm2), using all of its per-token output embeddings (77 tokens × 768), not just the pooled vector, so word-level information is available. Inside the U-Net, at several resolutions, cross-attention layers (lm2) let each spatial position of the latent feature map query the text tokens:

Q = latent features at each position × W_Q      (e.g. 32 × 32 = 1,024 positions)
K = text token embeddings × W_K                  (77 tokens)
V = text token embeddings × W_V
out = softmax(Q Kᵀ / √d) V                      (each position mixes in the words relevant to it)

A position in the region that will become the lighthouse can attend strongly to "lighthouse"; a position in the sky can attend to "storm". This happens at every step, so the prompt shapes composition early and texture late. The same mechanism accepts other conditions (layouts, depth maps, other images' embeddings), which is why the LDM paper describes cross-attention as a general conditioning interface.

The U-Net itself (dl6) is a convolutional encoder–decoder: downsampling stages that reduce the 64 × 64 latent to 8 × 8 while increasing channels, upsampling stages back to 64 × 64, and skip connections between matching resolutions so detail is not lost. The timestep t is embedded (with sinusoidal features, as for positions in lm4) and added inside every block, so the network knows how much noise to expect. Newer models replace the U-Net with a diffusion transformer (DiT): cut the latent into 2 × 2 patches, treat each as a token (exactly the ViT recipe of mm1), and run a transformer, conditioning on t and the text.

7. Classifier-free guidance

A conditional diffusion model trained as above follows the prompt only loosely: its predictions represent what images with that caption look like on average, including many that match it weakly. Ho and Salimans' classifier-free guidance (CFG) sharpens this with no extra model. During training, replace the prompt with an empty prompt some fraction of the time (their experiments found 10–20% worked better than 50%; Stable Diffusion's later checkpoints dropped the text 10% of the time). The same network then knows both ε_c = ε_θ(x_t, t, prompt) and ε_u = ε_θ(x_t, t, empty). At sampling, combine them:

ε_guided = ε_u + s · (ε_c − ε_u)

The difference ε_c − ε_u is the direction "what the prompt changes". With s = 1 you get the plain conditional prediction; with s > 1 you go past it, exaggerating the prompt's effect. (The CFG paper writes the same thing as (1 + w)ε_c − wε_u, so its w equals s − 1.) A worked example for two latent values:

ε_u = (0.20, −0.10)     ε_c = (0.35, 0.05)     ε_c − ε_u = (0.15, 0.15)
s = 1.0  → (0.35, 0.05)         plain conditional
s = 3.0  → (0.20 + 0.45, −0.10 + 0.45) = (0.65, 0.35)
s = 7.5  → (0.20 + 1.125, −0.10 + 1.125) = (1.325, 1.025)   SD v1's default

At s = 7.5 the guided prediction is several times larger than either branch: the sampler is told to remove noise strongly in the prompt's direction. That is why guidance improves adherence and why too much of it causes oversaturated colours, harsh contrast and repetitive images: the update extrapolates into regions the model never saw in training. The CFG paper showed that sweeping the weight trades off FID (diversity and realism) against Inception score (class distinctiveness), like classifier guidance before it but with no separate classifier. In the LDM paper, guidance improved the text-to-image model's FID on MS-COCO from 23.31 to 12.63.

A negative prompt uses the same machinery: put the negative text in the unconditional branch instead of the empty prompt. The update ε_neg + s(ε_c − ε_neg) then moves toward the prompt and away from the negative text. The cost of CFG is two network evaluations per step (usually batched together), which roughly doubles sampling compute.

8. Flow matching and rectified flow

Diffusion's forward process defines curved paths from data to noise: the signal coefficient √ᾱ_t and noise coefficient √(1 − ᾱ_t) change non-linearly. Curved paths need many small steps to follow accurately. Flow matching (Lipman et al., 2022) generalises the setup: choose any path from noise to data, and train a network to predict the velocity along it. The simplest choice is a straight line, called rectified flow, and it is the form SD3 uses:

x_t = (1 − t) · x_0 + t · ε        t ∈ [0, 1], t = 0 data, t = 1 noise
velocity  dx_t/dt = ε − x_0         (constant along each straight path)
loss = ‖ (ε − x_0) − v_θ(x_t, t) ‖²

Training is the same shape as diffusion: pick data, noise and a time, form x_t in one line, regress a target. Sampling integrates dx/dt = v_θ(x, t) from t = 1 to t = 0, for example with Euler steps x ← x + Δt · v_θ(x, t) with negative Δt.

Worked example: a pixel with x_0 = 0.8 and ε = −1.2. At t = 0.5, x_t = 0.5 × 0.8 + 0.5 × (−1.2) = −0.2, and the target velocity is ε − x_0 = −1.2 − 0.8 = −2.0. If the network predicted exactly −2.0, one Euler step from t = 0.5 straight to t = 0 would give x = −0.2 + (0 − 0.5) × (−2.0) = 0.8: the exact data point, in one step. On a straight path, Euler integration is exact.

So why not always use one step? Because the network does not know which x_0 a given noisy point came from. Many data points could have produced x_t, so the best possible prediction is the average of their velocities. At t = 1, pure noise, every data point is equally plausible, and one step lands on the mean of the dataset: a blurry average. The lab demonstrates it on one-dimensional data with two values, −2 and +2: one Euler step from noise puts every sample near 0 (mean |x| = 0.001), two steps get halfway, four steps put 99% of samples within 0.25 of ±2. As t decreases, the noisy point increasingly identifies which data point it belongs to, the averaged paths straighten, and steps become accurate. Rectified flow's paths are much straighter than diffusion's curved ones, which is why flow models sample well in fewer steps; distillation methods (deep section) then train a student to take the remaining shortcuts.

9. Putting it together: one image, step by step

A complete SD v1-style generation with 50 DDIM steps and guidance 7.5: tokenise and encode the prompt and the empty prompt once (two passes of the 123M-parameter text encoder). Draw z_T, a 64 × 64 × 4 array of Gaussian noise, from the seed. For each of 50 timesteps from high to low noise, run the 860M-parameter U-Net on a batch of two (with and without the prompt), combine with s = 7.5, and take a DDIM step. Then run the decoder once to produce 512 × 512 × 3 pixels and clip to valid colours. Of the total compute, the 100 U-Net evaluations dominate; the text encoder and decoder are a few percent. Cutting steps from 50 to 4 and folding guidance into the network cuts the dominant term by about 25×.

Parameterisations: ε, x_0, v and velocity

The network can be trained to output different but equivalent targets, and the choice matters for numerical behaviour. Predicting ε works well at most noise levels but becomes ill-conditioned near pure noise (where x_t is almost all ε, so the task is trivial but x̂_0 derived from it is unstable) and near clean data. Predicting x_0 directly has the opposite problems. "v-prediction", a combination of the two (v = √ᾱ·ε − √(1 − ᾱ)·x_0), keeps the target's scale stable across all noise levels and is used by several later models; flow matching's velocity ε − x_0 plays a similar role. They are linear transformations of each other given x_t and t, so a model trained one way can be sampled with formulas written for another; what changes is how errors are weighted across timesteps during training.

Noise schedules and timestep weighting

The linear schedule wastes effort: as the table shows, beyond t ≈ 600 the signal is almost gone, so many training steps are spent on near-pure-noise inputs where there is little to learn, and the SNR does not reach zero exactly, which leaves a small leak of signal at t = T that the model learns to rely on but never gets at sampling (a cause of images that cannot be very dark or very bright). Cosine schedules make the SNR fall more evenly. SD3's study treated the question as which noise levels to train on: sampling t from a logit-normal distribution concentrates training on intermediate times, where the denoising task is hardest and decides structure, and this beat uniform sampling for rectified flow in their experiments. The interview-level point: the schedule and timestep distribution decide where the model's capacity goes, and both are tuned, not fixed.

Why the autoencoder is a separate model

Training the autoencoder and the diffusion model separately is a deliberate division of labour. The autoencoder is trained once with losses designed for perceptual fidelity (perceptual and adversarial terms), producing a latent space that drops imperceptible detail. The diffusion model then spends all its capacity on the semantically meaningful structure. Joint training would let the diffusion model's difficulty leak into the representation. The cost is the reconstruction ceiling, and the improvements show it: SD3 moved from 4 to 16 latent channels, and its authors found the 16-channel autoencoder gave better reconstructions and better scaling of generated-image quality with model size. A bigger latent is harder for the diffusion model but raises the ceiling; as the diffusion model grows, the ceiling becomes the binding constraint.

Transformers replace U-Nets

U-Nets have strong image priors (convolution, multi-scale processing), which helps at small scale. Diffusion transformers, like ViTs (mm1), trade those priors for uniformity and scale better with compute: the backbone is a stack of identical transformer blocks over latent patches, with the timestep and conditioning injected by adaptive normalisation (scale and shift parameters predicted from the conditioning). SD3's MM-DiT goes further for text conditioning: instead of image tokens cross-attending to frozen text embeddings, text tokens and image tokens are concatenated into one sequence with separate weights per modality and joint self-attention, so text representations are also updated by the image. The SD3 paper's architecture comparison found MM-DiT outperforming cross-attention and plain DiT variants in validation loss, CLIP score and FID, with its largest model at 8 billion parameters.

Fewer steps: solvers, distillation and consistency

Three families cut sampling cost. Better ODE solvers (higher-order methods such as DPM-Solver) take larger steps along the same trained model's path with less error, giving good images in 10–25 steps with no retraining. Progressive and guidance distillation trains a student to match two teacher steps with one, repeatedly halving the step count, and can fold the two guidance passes into one. Consistency models and adversarial distillation train the student to map any point on a path straight to its end, using a consistency loss (outputs from neighbouring points on the same path must agree) and often a discriminator to keep single-step outputs sharp instead of averaged; models such as SDXL Turbo sample in 1–4 steps. The lab's lesson applies: a single step can only be sharp if training explicitly prevents it from regressing to the mean, which is what the adversarial or consistency terms do.

Guidance beyond CFG, and its failure modes

CFG has well-known side effects at high scales: oversaturation, loss of diversity (many seeds give similar compositions) and reduced realism. Remedies include guidance rescaling (normalising the guided prediction's standard deviation back toward the conditional one's), applying guidance only in an interval of middle timesteps (early steps decide layout, where strong guidance hurts diversity; late steps decide texture, where it causes artefacts), and dynamic thresholding of predicted x_0 values. Each is a way of keeping the extrapolation inside the region the model knows. Guidance can also be applied with other conditions (image prompts, layouts), with separate scales per condition.

Control and editing on a frozen model

Because the denoiser takes conditions through a general interface, many controls are added without retraining the base model. Image-to-image starts sampling from a partially noised version of an input image instead of pure noise (strength = how far along the schedule you start), so the output keeps the input's layout. Inpainting fixes the known region's latent at each step and generates only the masked region. Adapter networks add spatial conditions (edges, depth, pose) by injecting features into the U-Net's skip connections. LoRA fine-tunes (lm11) teach a new style or subject with a few megabytes of weights. All exploit the same fact: the sampling loop is a sequence of predictions you can intervene in at any step.

The public case: latent diffusion and Stable Diffusion v1

"High-Resolution Image Synthesis with Latent Diffusion Models" (Rombach, Blattmann, Lorenz, Esser and Ommer; CVPR 2022) is the paper behind Stable Diffusion. Its claims are concrete. It trains autoencoders with downsampling factors f from 1 to 32 and shows that diffusion in pixel space (f = 1) trains far more slowly, f = 4–8 hits the best balance, and f = 32 limits quality: a measured version of the "compress, then diffuse" argument. It introduces cross-attention conditioning as a general mechanism and demonstrates it on text, semantic layouts and class labels. For text-to-image it trains a 1.45-billion-parameter KL-regularised LDM on LAION-400M (a public dataset of 400 million web image–text pairs); on 256 × 256 MS-COCO with 250 DDIM steps it reports an FID of 23.31 without guidance and 12.63 with classifier-free guidance at scale 1.5, on par with much larger autoregressive and diffusion models of the time.

The open release that followed, Stable Diffusion v1 (CompVis with Stability AI and LAION, 2022), applied the same architecture at 512 × 512: a downsampling-factor-8 autoencoder, an 860M-parameter U-Net and a frozen 123M-parameter CLIP ViT-L/14 text encoder, trained on subsets of LAION-5B (later checkpoints on an aesthetics-filtered subset, with 10% of text conditioning dropped to support classifier-free guidance). The reference script defaults to 50 sampling steps and a guidance scale of 7.5, and the model card notes it runs on a GPU with at least 10 GB of memory. That last fact was the revolution: the 48× latent compression put text-to-image generation on consumer graphics cards, and an ecosystem of fine-tunes, LoRAs, control adapters and editors grew on top of the open weights.

SD3 (Esser et al., 2024) shows where the line went next: rectified flow with logit-normal timestep sampling, the MM-DiT transformer backbone, 16-channel latents, multiple text encoders including T5, and models up to 8B parameters, evaluated mainly by human preference.

Failure modes in production

Anatomy and counting errors. Hands with extra fingers, wrong numbers of objects, garbled text. Causes include the autoencoder's reconstruction ceiling for small structures, weak counting in text encoders and training captions that do not describe these details. Mitigations: higher resolution or two-stage upscaling, better text encoders, targeted fine-tuning, and automatic checks with a detector or VLM before returning images.

Over-guidance artefacts. Users who push guidance high get saturated, posterised images. Clamp the scale range in the product, or use guidance rescaling.

Memorisation and copying. Diffusion models can reproduce near-copies of training images that were heavily duplicated in the dataset, which creates copyright and privacy exposure. Deduplicate training data, and screen outputs against known protected content where it matters.

Safety filtering. Text-to-image services run prompt classifiers before generation and image classifiers after it; negative prompts are not a safety mechanism. Filters must be evaluated for both false negatives (harmful images that pass) and false positives (benign requests blocked).

Irreproducible "same seed" results. Users and QA teams expect a seed plus a prompt to reproduce an image exactly. It does, only if everything else is identical: sampler, step count, guidance scale, model and autoencoder versions, precision, and often the GPU type and library versions, since floating-point kernels differ. Store the full generation recipe with every image you may need to regenerate, and treat cross-hardware reproducibility as approximate.

Prompt drift after a model upgrade. Prompts tuned for one model (with its text encoder's quirks, preferred guidance scale and negative prompts) often perform worse on the next. A newer model is not a drop-in replacement for a library of curated prompts; re-evaluate the top prompts before switching.

Serving and cost

Cost per image ≈ steps × (2 with guidance) × denoiser FLOPs per evaluation, plus small text-encoder and decoder terms. Levers in order of impact: fewer steps (better solver, distilled model), no separate guidance pass (guidance distillation), lower precision (bfloat16 or 8-bit), batching requests, and caching text encodings of popular prompts. The VAE decoder at high resolution is memory-hungry; tiled decoding avoids out-of-memory errors at 2K–4K output sizes. Track latency per step, images per second per GPU, and a quality metric you trust on your own prompts (human preference samples plus automated prompt-adherence checks), because FID on COCO says little about a specific product.

    x = np.sqrt(1 - beta[t]) * x + np.sqrt(beta[t]) * rng.normal(size=x.shape)
a = abar[499]
print(f"500 steps: mean {x.mean():.4f} sd {x.std():.4f} | closed form: mean {np.sqrt(a)*0.8:.4f} sd {np.sqrt(1-a):.4f}")

# 3. classifier-free guidance on one latent 'pixel' pair
eps_u, eps_c = np.array([0.20, -0.10]), np.array([0.35, 0.05])
for s in (1.0, 3.0, 7.5):
    print(f"guidance s={s}: eps = {eps_u + s * (eps_c - eps_u)}")

# 4. flow matching on 1-D data {-2, +2}: x_t = (1-t) x0 + t * noise; exact velocity field
def velocity(x, t):
    x0s = np.array([-2.0, 2.0])
    logw = -(x[:, None] - (1 - t) * x0s) ** 2 / (2 * t * t)          # posterior over which x0
    w = np.exp(logw - logw.max(1, keepdims=True)); w /= w.sum(1, keepdims=True)
    v = (x[:, None] - (1 - t) * x0s) / t - x0s                       # E[noise - x0 | x_t, x0]
    return (w * v).sum(1)

for steps in (1, 2, 4, 50):
    x = rng.normal(size=20_000); ts = np.linspace(1.0, 1e-3, steps + 1)
    for t0, t1 in zip(ts[:-1], ts[1:]):
        x = x + (t1 - t0) * velocity(x, t0)                          # Euler step toward t = 0
    print(f"{steps:2d} Euler steps: mean |x| = {np.abs(x).mean():.3f} (target 2), "
          f"share within 0.25 of +-2: {np.mean(np.abs(np.abs(x) - 2) < 0.25):.2f}")

Compute it

Under DDPM's linear schedule, ᾱ_250 ≈ 0.524. A latent value x_0 = −0.5 is noised with ε = 0.9. Compute x_250. Then, if the network predicts ε̂ = 0.8, what x̂_0 does it imply? Finally, with ε_u = 0.10 and ε_c = 0.25 for this value, what guided prediction does s = 5 give?

Answer

√ᾱ = √0.524 = 0.724 and √(1 − ᾱ) = √0.476 = 0.690. x_250 = 0.724 × (−0.5) + 0.690 × 0.9 = −0.362 + 0.621 = 0.259.

x̂_0 = (x_t − 0.690 × 0.8) / 0.724 = (0.259 − 0.552) / 0.724 = −0.405, against the true −0.5: a 0.1 noise error becomes a 0.095 error in x_0 here, because at t = 250 signal and noise are about equal (compare 0.34 at t = 500).

Guidance: 0.10 + 5 × (0.25 − 0.10) = 0.10 + 0.75 = 0.85, more than three times the conditional prediction.

Estimate it

A product generates 1,024 × 1,024 images with an SDXL-class model: about 2.6B U-Net parameters, latent 128 × 128 × 4, 30 steps, guidance on. Assume one U-Net evaluation costs about 2 × parameters × latent positions / 4 FLOPs (a rough stand-in: the U-Net works mostly at reduced resolutions), so 2 × 2.6e9 × 4,096 ≈ 21 TFLOPs. How much compute per image, and what changes with a 4-step distilled model without separate guidance?

Answer

Evaluations: 30 steps × 2 (guidance) = 60, so about 60 × 21 ≈ 1,300 TFLOPs, plus a few percent for text encoders and the decoder. On a GPU sustaining 300 TFLOP/s that is about 4 seconds. The distilled model needs 4 evaluations: about 85 TFLOPs, under 0.3 seconds at the same rate, a 15× reduction. The FLOP formula is crude; the ratio is what matters, and it is why distilled few-step models dominate interactive products while many-step models remain for highest-quality offline generation. Verify with a real benchmark on your hardware before capacity planning.
