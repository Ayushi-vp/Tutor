A bank wants its support line to understand callers who say "I want to move five hundred dollars from checking to savings" in a noisy car, with an accent, over a phone line that cuts everything above 4 kHz. A media company wants searchable transcripts of 20 years of video in 40 languages. A product team wants its assistant to answer out loud in a natural voice, without sounding like a 2005 GPS. All three are speech problems, and in all three the hard part is not the language model; it is turning sound into something a model can use, and back again.

Sound is awkward data. A second of audio at telephone quality is 8,000 numbers; at the quality used for speech recognition, 16,000; for music, 44,100 per channel. Individually those numbers mean almost nothing: a single sample is an air-pressure reading. Meaning lives in patterns spread over tens of milliseconds (a vowel) to seconds (a sentence), mixed with noise, echo and other voices. And the mapping to text is not one-to-one in time: the word "the" may take 80 ms and "strengths" 600 ms, and no one labels where each word starts.

For years, speech systems were pipelines of hand-engineered parts. Then two developments reshaped the field. Large-scale weakly supervised models, Whisper above all, showed that one transformer trained on hundreds of thousands of hours of messy web audio could transcribe and translate robustly across languages and conditions, out of the box. And neural audio codecs showed how to turn sound into discrete tokens at a fixed rate, so the same language-modelling machinery that writes text can read and write audio, which is what powers modern voice cloning and speech-native assistants.

This lesson starts from the waveform: sampling, frames, the Fourier transform, the mel scale and the log-mel spectrogram, with the frame count for 10 seconds of audio worked out. Then speech recognition: CTC and its blank token, attention encoder–decoders, and how Whisper turns many tasks into one token format. Then measurement, with word error rate computed by hand. Then the reverse direction, text-to-speech, and neural codecs with residual vector quantisation and their bitrate arithmetic. The public case is Whisper, with its numbers checked against the paper. Along the way you will see why a transcript can be fluent and wrong, why a word error rate can hide the one error that matters, and why voice cloning needs only a few seconds of audio. Real-time voice agents, where latency rules, follow in mm7.

Sheet music for sound

A recording of a piano piece is a wiggly line: air pressure over time. Sheet music describes the same piece differently: which notes (frequencies) sound, when, and how loudly. A trained musician reads sheet music far more easily than a waveform. Speech models want the same change of view. A spectrogram is sheet music for any sound: time runs left to right, frequency bottom to top, and brightness shows how much energy is at each frequency at each moment. Vowels appear as horizontal bands, consonants like "s" as high-frequency hiss, silences as dark gaps. Once audio is in this form, recognising speech looks a lot like recognising patterns in an image, and a transformer can treat it as a sequence of frames.

The analogy is useful and it breaks in one important place: sheet music is exact and symbolic, while a spectrogram is a measurement, blurred by the trade-off between time and frequency resolution (a short window tells you when precisely but not which frequency; a long window the reverse). Speech systems pick a window of about 25 ms, long enough to resolve the harmonics of a voice, short enough to follow a fast consonant.

The two directions

Speech recognition (ASR) goes from audio to text: waveform → log-mel spectrogram → acoustic encoder → text decoder → words. Text-to-speech (TTS) goes the other way: text → (often) phonemes, the units of sound → an acoustic model that predicts a spectrogram or audio tokens → a vocoder that turns those into a waveform. Modern systems blur the boundary: some language models read and write audio tokens directly.

Where the difficulty really is

Beginners often assume the hard part of speech recognition is "hearing" the sounds. In practice, clean, close-microphone speech in a common accent has been easy for years. The difficulty is everything around it: overlapping speakers, background noise, reverberant rooms, narrowband phone lines, rare words and names, languages switching mid-sentence, and the fact that people do not speak in sentences ("so, um, the, the thing is..."). Each of these is a shift away from whatever the training data covered. That is why the most important property of a modern speech model is robustness across conditions, and why the field's big step forward came from training data breadth rather than a cleverer architecture.

The numbers to carry

Speech recognition runs at 16 kHz: 16,000 samples per second. The front end produces one frame every 10 ms, so 100 frames per second, each a vector of 80 numbers. Ten seconds of speech is therefore 160,000 samples or about 1,000 frames: a 1,000 × 80 "image". Speech has roughly 2–3 words per second, so those 1,000 frames become about 25 words. That length mismatch, many frames per word, is the central technical problem of ASR, and the two classic solutions to it (CTC and attention) are the core of this lesson. On the output side, a neural codec turns each second of audio into 75 frames of a few discrete tokens each, which is what lets language models speak.

If you stop here: audio becomes a log-mel spectrogram of 100 frames per second; recognition maps those frames to far fewer text tokens; synthesis predicts acoustic frames or codec tokens and converts them back to sound.

1. Sampling: audio as numbers

A microphone measures air pressure; an analogue-to-digital converter records that measurement at a fixed rate, the sample rate. At 16 kHz there are 16,000 measurements per second, each typically a 16-bit integer converted to a float in [−1, 1]. The sampling theorem says a sampled signal can represent frequencies up to half the sample rate, the Nyquist frequency: 8 kHz at 16 kHz. Most information that distinguishes speech sounds lies below 8 kHz (vowels are defined by resonances below about 3.5 kHz; fricatives like "s" extend higher), which is why 16 kHz is the standard for ASR, and why Whisper resamples all input to 16,000 Hz. Telephone audio, sampled at 8 kHz, cuts off at 4 kHz, which is one reason phone calls are harder to transcribe. Music and high-quality synthesis use 24, 44.1 or 48 kHz.

Ten seconds at 16 kHz is 160,000 numbers. Feeding them directly into a transformer would mean 160,000 positions, and attention over that many is impractical (lm2), so the first job is summarising.

2. Frames and the short-time Fourier transform

Speech changes quickly but not instantly: within about 20–30 ms, a sound is roughly steady. So we cut the signal into short overlapping frames. Whisper uses a 25 ms window (400 samples at 16 kHz) every 10 ms (a hop of 160 samples), so consecutive frames overlap by 15 ms. Each frame is multiplied by a smooth window function (a Hann window, which tapers to zero at both ends) to avoid artificial edges, and then the Fourier transform measures how much of each frequency it contains. Computed with the fast Fourier transform (FFT) on 400 samples, it gives 201 frequency bins from 0 to 8,000 Hz, spaced 16,000 / 400 = 40 Hz apart. Taking the squared magnitude gives the power at each frequency. Doing this for every frame is the short-time Fourier transform (STFT), and the resulting frames × frequencies grid is the spectrogram.

Frame count for 10 seconds, the worked example to know:

samples            = 10 s × 16,000      = 160,000
hop                = 10 ms × 16,000     = 160 samples
frames (no padding) = 1 + (160,000 − 400) / 160 = 998
frames (centred, padded by 200 each side, as Whisper/librosa) = 1 + 160,000 / 160 = 1,001
→ about 100 frames per second, ~1,000 frames for 10 s

The lab computes exactly this: 160,000 samples, 1,001 frames, 201 bins of 40 Hz.

3. The mel scale and the log

201 linear frequency bins are not how we hear. Humans distinguish 100 Hz from 200 Hz easily but barely notice 7,100 Hz versus 7,200 Hz: pitch perception is roughly linear below 1 kHz and logarithmic above. The mel scale models this; a common formula is mel(f) = 2595 × log₁₀(1 + f / 700). On it, 440 Hz is 550 mel, 1,000 Hz is 1,000 mel, 1,760 Hz is 1,416 mel and 8,000 Hz is 2,840 mel: the top 6 kHz of the range is squeezed into less than two-thirds of the scale.

A mel filterbank is a set of overlapping triangular filters with centres evenly spaced on the mel scale. For 80 filters from 0 to 8 kHz: take 82 points evenly spaced between 0 and 2,840 mel (about 35 mel apart), convert back to Hz, and build a triangle from each point to the one two steps along, peaking at the middle one. Low filters are narrow (the first centres are at about 22, 45 and 68 Hz); high filters are wide (the last at about 7,475 and 7,734 Hz). Multiplying the 201-bin power spectrum of each frame by this 80 × 201 matrix gives 80 band energies per frame. Finally take the logarithm: loudness spans many orders of magnitude, perception is roughly logarithmic, and the log turns multiplicative effects (microphone gain) into additive offsets that normalisation can remove. Whisper also clamps and rescales the values to a fixed range.

The result, the log-mel spectrogram, is the standard input: for 10 s of audio, a 1,001 × 80 array. In the lab's test signal (a 440 Hz tone for 5 s, then 1,760 Hz), the loudest band is band 15 in the first half and band 39 in the second, matching the tones' positions on the mel scale.

4. The alignment problem

We now have about 100 frames per second and want about 2–3 words (or, with a subword tokenizer, 3–5 tokens) per second. Training data pairs a clip with its transcript but not with timings: nobody marks that "five" spans frames 212–247. A recognition model must therefore learn both what was said and where, from unaligned pairs. Two families solve this differently.

5. CTC: one output per frame, collapsed

Connectionist temporal classification (CTC) lets an encoder emit a probability distribution over output symbols for every frame, with one extra symbol, the blank (written _), meaning "nothing new here". A frame-level output sequence is turned into text by two rules: merge consecutive repeated symbols, then delete blanks. For example, with characters as symbols:

frames : _ _ h h _ e _ l l _ l l l _ _ o o _ _ _
merge  : _ h _ e _ l _ l _ o _
drop _ : h e l l o

The blank between the two runs of l is what allows a genuine double letter: without it, "lll" would collapse to a single l. Many frame-level paths collapse to the same text (the l's could start one frame earlier, the blanks could be elsewhere). The CTC loss is −log of the total probability of all paths that collapse to the correct transcript, computed efficiently with dynamic programming, the same way the forward algorithm works for hidden Markov models. The model never needs frame-level labels; it learns where the characters go because that maximises the total.

Decoding is simple: greedy CTC takes the most likely symbol at each frame and applies the two rules, which is fast and naturally streaming, since each frame's output is decided as it arrives. CTC's limitation is built into it: given the audio, the outputs at different frames are treated as independent, so the model has no internal sense of which word sequences are likely. "Their" versus "there" must be decided from sound alone. CTC systems are therefore usually combined with an external language model during beam search. The RNN-Transducer (RNN-T) fixes the independence assumption by adding a small prediction network that conditions each output on the previous outputs, while keeping frame-synchronous, streamable decoding; it is the common choice for on-device streaming recognition.

6. Attention encoder–decoders and Whisper's design

The other family treats recognition as translation from audio to text (lm3). An encoder reads all the frames with self-attention; a decoder generates text tokens one at a time, each step cross-attending to the encoder's outputs to decide which part of the audio it is now transcribing. Alignment is learned implicitly by the cross-attention, and the decoder is a language model, so the output is fluent and well-formed. The cost: the decoder needs the encoder's output for the whole window before starting, so it does not stream naturally, and because the decoder can generate freely, it can produce text not supported by the audio.

Whisper is this design, kept deliberately standard. Audio is resampled to 16 kHz, cut or padded to a 30-second window, and turned into an 80-channel log-mel spectrogram with 25 ms windows and a 10 ms stride (3,000 frames for 30 s). A small stem of two 1-D convolutions with GELU activations, the second with stride 2, halves the time axis to 1,500 positions; sinusoidal position embeddings are added; and a transformer encoder processes them. The decoder is a transformer over byte-level BPE text tokens (lm1). Model sizes range from Tiny (4 layers, width 384, 39M parameters) to Large (32 layers, width 1,280, 1,550M parameters).

Whisper's distinctive idea is the multitask token format. The decoder's prompt is a sequence of special tokens that specify the task: a start-of-transcript token; a language token (one of about 99 languages, predicted by the model if not given, which gives language identification); a task token, <|transcribe|> or <|translate|> (translate to English); and <|notimestamps|> or, in timestamp mode, interleaved time tokens marking segment boundaries. If there is no speech, the model is trained to predict <|nospeech|>. One set of weights thus does transcription, translation, language identification, voice-activity detection and timestamping. Text from the previous window can be supplied as a prompt to keep long transcripts consistent.

7. Word error rate, computed by hand

ASR is scored by word error rate (WER): the minimum number of word substitutions (S), deletions (D) and insertions (I) needed to turn the model's output (hypothesis) into the reference, divided by the number of reference words N:

WER = (S + D + I) / N

Worked example. Reference: "please transfer five hundred dollars to savings" (N = 7). Hypothesis: "please transfer nine hundred dollars to my savings". Aligning word by word: please = please, transfer = transfer, five → nine (substitution), hundred = hundred, dollars = dollars, to = to, "my" has no counterpart (insertion), savings = savings. S = 1, D = 0, I = 1. WER = 2/7 = 28.6%. The alignment is found with the same edit-distance dynamic programme used for strings, over words; the lab implements it.

Three cautions make WER honest. Normalise both texts first (case, punctuation, "500" versus "five hundred", "OK" versus "okay"); Whisper's authors released their text normaliser because unnormalised comparisons punish style differences. WER can exceed 100%, since insertions are unbounded. And WER weights all words equally: the substitution of "nine" for "five" above is one error among seven, but it moves $900 instead of $500. For applications, measure what matters, such as entity and number accuracy, alongside WER.

8. Text-to-speech: the classic pipeline

Synthesis runs the other way and has its own stages. Text normalisation expands what is written into what is said ("$500" → "five hundred dollars", "Dr." → "doctor" or "drive" depending on context). A grapheme-to-phoneme step converts words to phonemes, the units of sound, because spelling is a poor guide to pronunciation in English. An acoustic model predicts a mel spectrogram from the phonemes, including their durations, pitch and energy (together called prosody). A vocoder, today a neural network, converts the mel spectrogram into a waveform; this is necessary because the mel spectrogram discards phase and fine frequency detail, which the vocoder must reconstruct plausibly. Systems in this family (the Tacotron and FastSpeech lines plus neural vocoders) produce clear, stable speech for one or a few trained voices.

9. Neural codecs and residual vector quantisation

To let a language model generate audio the way it generates text, audio must become a sequence of discrete tokens. Neural audio codecs such as SoundStream and EnCodec provide them. An encoder (a convolutional network) turns the waveform into a sequence of latent vectors at a fixed frame rate; a quantiser turns each vector into discrete indices; a decoder reconstructs the waveform from the indices. The whole thing is trained to make reconstructions sound right, with reconstruction losses on the waveform and spectrogram plus adversarial losses (a discriminator judging realism), as in the latent-diffusion autoencoder of mm5.

The quantiser uses residual vector quantisation (RVQ). A single codebook of 1,024 vectors (10 bits per index) gives only a coarse approximation of each latent. RVQ applies a second codebook to the error left by the first, a third to the error left by the second, and so on. Each stage refines the approximation; using more stages gives higher fidelity at a higher bitrate.

EnCodec's arithmetic at 24 kHz, worth being able to reproduce:

frame rate   = 24,000 samples/s ÷ 320 samples per frame = 75 frames per second
one codebook = 1,024 entries = 10 bits per frame → 75 × 10 = 750 bits/s
2 codebooks  = 1.5 kbps      8 codebooks = 6 kbps      32 codebooks = 24 kbps

So one second of audio at 6 kbps is 75 frames × 8 codebooks = 600 tokens, each from a vocabulary of 1,024. That is far more tokens per second than speech has words (about 3 per second), which is the cost of representing how something was said (voice, emotion, room acoustics), not just what.

10. Around the recogniser: VAD, segmentation and diarisation

A recogniser alone is not a transcription system. Three supporting components decide much of the real-world quality. Voice activity detection (VAD) marks which stretches of audio contain speech. A small, fast model (often a few hundred thousand parameters, running on 10–30 ms frames) is enough, and it pays for itself twice: it skips silence, which saves compute, and it prevents an attention decoder from hallucinating text on non-speech audio. Segmentation cuts long recordings into pieces the recogniser can handle (at most 30 s for Whisper), ideally at pauses so that words are not split across windows; cutting mid-word causes the edge errors the Whisper paper describes. Speaker diarisation answers "who spoke when": it extracts a speaker embedding (a vector summarising voice characteristics) for each short segment, clusters the embeddings, and labels segments by cluster, so the transcript reads "Speaker A: ... Speaker B: ...". Diarisation errors (two speakers merged, one speaker split) are measured separately from WER, and overlapping speech remains the hardest case for both.

The order matters in a batch pipeline: VAD, then segmentation at pauses, then recognition of each segment (batched across files on GPUs for throughput), then diarisation and alignment of words to speakers using timestamps. In a live voice agent (mm7), the same pieces run incrementally with tight time budgets.

11. Speech as language modelling

With codec tokens, text-to-speech can be posed as next-token prediction. VALL-E (Wang et al., 2023) did exactly this: given phonemes of the target text and the codec tokens of a 3-second recording of a speaker, a transformer predicts the codec tokens of new speech in that voice. Its first stage generates the first codebook autoregressively (one frame at a time), and a second, non-autoregressive stage predicts the remaining codebooks for all frames in parallel, because the later codebooks are refinements that depend mostly on the first. Trained on 60,000 hours of English audiobook speech (the LibriLight set), hundreds of times more than earlier TTS systems, it produced speech in an unseen speaker's voice from a 3-second prompt, keeping the recording's acoustic environment and emotion. The same idea, reading and writing audio tokens, underlies speech-native assistants that take speech in and produce speech out without a text bottleneck (mm7).

Why weak supervision at scale won

Before Whisper, the best English ASR models were trained on curated datasets such as LibriSpeech (about 1,000 hours of read audiobooks) or pretrained without labels (wav2vec 2.0) and then fine-tuned on such sets. They reached very low WER on their own test sets and degraded sharply elsewhere: a model fine-tuned on audiobooks has learned audiobooks. Whisper's bet was the opposite trade: accept noisy transcripts (web captions, with heuristics to remove machine-generated ones, which would teach the model to imitate other ASR systems' errors) in exchange for enormous diversity. The paper's robustness analysis frames this with the effective robustness idea from CLIP (mm2): at equal LibriSpeech performance, how well does a model do elsewhere? Zero-shot Whisper made 55.2% fewer errors on average across other datasets than a supervised LibriSpeech model with nearly the same LibriSpeech score. The lesson generalises: in-distribution benchmark scores reward specialisation; deployment rewards breadth.

Hallucination and loops in attention decoders

An attention decoder is a conditional language model. When the acoustic evidence is strong, cross-attention ties each output token to the audio; when it is weak (silence, music, noise, a language the model handles poorly), the decoder's text prior dominates, and it produces fluent text that was never spoken, often phrases common in its training captions such as "Thank you for watching". It can also fall into repetition loops, where attending to its own recent output makes repeating it the most likely continuation. Whisper's paper describes both and the heuristics used for long-form transcription: 5-beam search (loops are more common in greedy decoding), temperature fallback from 0 up to 1.0 in steps of 0.2 when the average log-probability of the output falls below −1 or its gzip compression ratio exceeds 2.4 (repetitive text compresses unusually well), conditioning on previous text only at low temperature, and combining the no-speech probability with the log-probability threshold to skip silent windows. In production, a separate voice-activity detector in front of the model is the most effective single defence, because it stops silence from ever reaching the decoder.

Streaming versus offline

Offline models see the whole window (30 s for Whisper) before decoding, which gives them context in both directions: a word's identity can depend on what comes after it. Streaming models must emit text while audio arrives, with a bounded look-ahead. CTC and RNN-T are naturally streaming because they are frame-synchronous; their encoders use limited right context (chunked or causal attention). Attention encoder–decoders can be made pseudo-streaming by re-running on growing buffers and committing only text that has stabilised across runs, at extra compute and with a delay. The choice is an accuracy–latency trade-off that dominates voice-agent design (mm7): a few hundred milliseconds of look-ahead buys a noticeable WER improvement.

Self-supervised speech representations

Between hand-designed features and Whisper sits a third approach: learn speech representations without transcripts. wav2vec 2.0 and HuBERT mask parts of the encoded audio and train the model to identify or predict the masked content (contrastively or by predicting cluster labels), analogous to BERT for text or MAE for images (mm1). The learned features capture phonetic content and need very little labelled data to fine-tune into a recogniser, which matters for low-resource languages. Their discretised versions ("semantic tokens", cluster indices of these features) are also used alongside codec ("acoustic") tokens in speech language models: semantic tokens carry what is said, acoustic tokens carry how.

Why RVQ, and how codec LMs handle multiple codebooks

A single vector-quantisation stage needs exponentially many codebook entries to reach high fidelity: eight stages of 1,024 entries give the same number of distinct combinations as one codebook with 1,024⁸ ≈ 10²⁴ entries, which could never be stored or trained. Residual stages keep each codebook small and trainable. Training with "quantiser dropout" (randomly using only the first k stages) makes the codec work at several bitrates and orders information by importance: early codebooks carry the most. For a language model, the multiple tokens per frame are a design problem. Flattening all codebooks into one long sequence multiplies sequence length (8 × 75 = 600 tokens per second); VALL-E's split (autoregressive first codebook, parallel rest) is one answer; others predict all codebooks of a frame at once with small per-codebook heads, or interleave them with a delay pattern so each step predicts one codebook of several frames. The choice trades quality against generation speed.

Text normalisation is half the work

Both directions depend heavily on converting between written and spoken forms. For ASR evaluation, unnormalised comparisons can swing WER by several points without any change in recognition. For TTS, "1/2" might be "one half", "January second" or "one slash two"; "read" has two pronunciations; "St." is "Saint" or "Street". Production systems keep large rule sets and context-aware models for this; errors here are the most common complaints about otherwise good voices, and they are invisible in acoustic quality metrics. Interviewers like this point because it separates people who have shipped speech systems from people who have only trained models.

Evaluating synthesis

TTS quality is measured by listeners. The mean opinion score (MOS) asks raters to score naturalness from 1 to 5; comparative tests ask which of two samples is better. Automatic proxies include WER of an ASR model on the synthesised speech (intelligibility), speaker-similarity scores from a speaker-verification model (for voice cloning) and predicted MOS. Each can be gamed: very slow, over-articulated speech scores well on intelligibility and badly on naturalness. Evaluate on your own text distribution (numbers, names, domain terms), because that is where systems fail.

The public case: Whisper (Radford et al., 2022)

"Robust Speech Recognition via Large-Scale Weak Supervision" introduced Whisper and is worth knowing precisely. Data: 680,000 hours of audio paired with transcripts from the internet, of which 117,000 hours cover 96 languages other than English and 125,000 hours are translation data (non-English speech with English text); transcripts that looked machine-generated were filtered out. Front end: audio resampled to 16,000 Hz, an 80-channel log-magnitude mel spectrogram on 25 ms windows with a 10 ms stride, in 30-second segments. Architecture: an off-the-shelf encoder–decoder transformer with a two-layer convolutional stem (the second layer with stride 2), sinusoidal positions in the encoder, and five sizes from 39M to 1,550M parameters. Multitask format: language, task, timestamp and no-speech tokens in the decoder prompt.

The results that matter are about robustness rather than one benchmark. The best zero-shot model's LibriSpeech test-clean WER of 2.5 was described by the authors as "relatively unremarkable", roughly the 2019 state of the art. But compared with a supervised LibriSpeech model of nearly identical LibriSpeech WER, zero-shot Whisper made 55.2% fewer errors on average across other speech datasets, and its accuracy degraded more gracefully under added noise. The authors also report diminishing returns in English when going from 54,000 hours to the full dataset, and they document the failure modes (repetition loops, skipped words at segment edges, complete hallucinations) together with the decoding heuristics used to suppress them in long-form transcription. A later Large-v2 model was trained for 2.5 times more epochs with added regularisation. Released with open weights and code, Whisper became the default transcription backbone for many products and research projects.

Failure modes in production

Hallucinated text on silence or noise. Typical outputs are generic phrases or repeated lines. Gate with VAD, use no-speech and log-probability thresholds, and monitor the rate of identical outputs across unrelated audio.

Domain vocabulary and names. Drug names, product codes, people's names and jargon are mis-recognised as common words. Mitigations: prompting with expected vocabulary (Whisper accepts previous-text prompts), fine-tuning on in-domain audio, and post-correction against a known entity list.

Channel mismatch. Narrowband phone audio, compressed VoIP and far-field microphones lose information the model relies on. Evaluate on audio recorded the way production audio is recorded, and resample correctly (a wrong sample rate makes everyone sound like a chipmunk or a giant, and destroys accuracy without errors).

Language identification errors. Short clips or code-switched speech get the wrong language token, and the model "translates" or transliterates instead of transcribing. Set the language explicitly when known.

Voice cloning misuse. Codec-LM TTS can imitate a person from seconds of audio. Products need consent flows, watermarking or detection, and policies on whose voices may be synthesised.

Silent cost blow-ups. Transcription cost scales with audio duration, not with how much is said. Meeting recordings with long silences or hold music, processed without VAD, can cost several times what the speech content justifies. Measure the ratio of speech to total audio and gate accordingly.

What to measure

For ASR: WER after normalisation, per language, accent, channel and noise level, plus entity and number accuracy and the hallucination rate on non-speech audio. Real-time factor (processing time divided by audio duration) and cost per audio hour for capacity planning. For TTS: listener MOS or A/B preference on your text, intelligibility via ASR WER, pronunciation error rate on a list of names and numbers, and latency to first audio for interactive use.

Goal: build Whisper's front end in NumPy (framing, Hann window, FFT, an 80-band mel filterbank, log), confirm the frame count for 10 s and for Whisper's 30 s window, decode a CTC path by hand rules, and compute WER by edit distance. NumPy only; the existing gpt_env (Python 3.12). The filterbank uses the HTK mel formula; Whisper's own filters use the Slaney variant, so band edges differ slightly.

import numpy as np

sr, n_fft, hop, n_mels = 16_000, 400, 160, 80        # Whisper's front end: 25 ms window, 10 ms hop

# 1. a 10-second test signal: a 440 Hz tone for 5 s, then a 1,760 Hz tone, plus a little noise
t = np.arange(10 * sr) / sr
x = np.where(t < 5, np.sin(2 * np.pi * 440 * t), np.sin(2 * np.pi * 1760 * t)) * 0.5
x += 0.01 * np.random.default_rng(0).normal(size=x.size)

# 2. short-time Fourier transform: Hann-windowed frames every 10 ms
pad = n_fft // 2
xp = np.pad(x, pad, mode="reflect")                   # centre the frames, as Whisper/librosa do
n_frames = 1 + (xp.size - n_fft) // hop
frames = np.stack([xp[i * hop: i * hop + n_fft] for i in range(n_frames)])
spec = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(frames * np.hanning(n_fft + 1)[:-1], axis=1)) ** 2   # power, 201 bins
print("samples", x.size, "| frames", n_frames, "| FFT bins", spec.shape[1], "| bin width", sr / n_fft, "Hz")

# 3. mel filterbank (HTK formula): 80 triangles evenly spaced on the mel scale
mel = lambda f: 2595 * np.log10(1 + f / 700)
imel = lambda m: 700 * (10 ** (m / 2595) - 1)
edges = imel(np.linspace(mel(0), mel(sr / 2), n_mels + 2))   # 82 edge frequencies
freqs = np.linspace(0, sr / 2, spec.shape[1])
fb = np.zeros((n_mels, freqs.size))
for m in range(n_mels):
    lo, c, hi = edges[m], edges[m + 1], edges[m + 2]
    fb[m] = np.clip(np.minimum((freqs - lo) / (c - lo), (hi - freqs) / (hi - c)), 0, None)
print("filter centres (Hz): first", edges[1:4].round(0), "... last", edges[-3:-1].round(0))
logmel = np.log10(np.maximum(spec @ fb.T, 1e-10))      # (frames, 80)
print("log-mel shape", logmel.shape, "| loudest band at 2 s:", logmel[200].argmax(), "at 8 s:", logmel[800].argmax())

# 4. Whisper pads every input to 30 s: 3,000 frames -> conv stride 2 -> 1,500 encoder positions
print("30 s window:", 30 * sr // hop, "frames ->", 30 * sr // hop // 2, "encoder positions")

# 5. CTC greedy decoding: best label per frame, merge repeats, drop blanks ('_')
path = list("__hh_e_ll_lll__oo___")
out, prev = [], None
for ch in path:
    if ch != prev and ch != "_": out.append(ch)
    prev = ch
print("CTC path", "".join(path), "->", "".join(out))

# 6. word error rate by edit distance
def wer(ref, hyp):
    r, h = ref.split(), hyp.split()
    d = np.arange(len(h) + 1)
    for i in range(1, len(r) + 1):
        prev_diag, d[0] = d[0], i
        for j in range(1, len(h) + 1):
            prev_diag, d[j] = d[j], min(d[j] + 1, d[j - 1] + 1, prev_diag + (r[i - 1] != h[j - 1]))
    return d[-1] / len(r)

Compute it

A call-centre system records 8 kHz audio, upsampled to 16 kHz for Whisper. A 45-second call is transcribed. (a) How many log-mel frames, and how many 30-second windows does Whisper process? (b) What frequency content does the model actually receive? (c) The reference is "my account number is four four seven one" and the output is "my account number is for four seven one two". What is the WER, and is it the right metric?

Answer

(a) At a 10 ms hop, 45 s is about 4,500 frames; Whisper processes it as two 30-second windows (the second padded), so 2 × 3,000 = 6,000 frames and 2 × 1,500 encoder positions, plus whatever overlap the long-form algorithm uses when shifting by timestamps.

(b) Upsampling cannot add information: the 8 kHz recording only contains frequencies up to 4 kHz, so the mel bands above 4 kHz are empty. Fricatives like "s" and "f" are harder to distinguish, one reason phone speech has higher WER.

(c) Alignment: "four" → "for" (substitution), and "two" inserted. S = 1, I = 1, N = 8: WER = 2/8 = 25%. After normalisation that maps "for" before digits to "four" it might fall to 12.5%, but the extra digit "two" still makes the account number wrong. For this use, measure exact-match accuracy of the extracted account number; WER understates a failure that breaks the task.

Estimate it

A speech-native assistant generates replies as EnCodec tokens at 6 kbps (24 kHz, 8 codebooks). A typical spoken reply lasts 12 seconds. How many audio tokens is that, and how does it compare with the text of the same reply?

Answer

75 frames per second × 8 codebooks = 600 tokens per second, so 12 s is 7,200 tokens. At about 2.5 words per second, the same reply is about 30 words, roughly 40 text tokens: about 180× fewer. That gap is why speech-generating models split codebooks into an autoregressive first stage and cheaper parallel stages, use lower frame rates or fewer codebooks, or generate text first and synthesise it with a streaming TTS: generating 7,200 tokens one by one would be far too slow for conversation.
