Call a support line run by a voice agent and ask, "Can I move my appointment to Thursday?" If the reply starts 400 ms after you stop talking, the agent feels like a person. If it starts after two seconds, you assume it did not hear you and say "Hello?", at which moment the agent starts talking too, both of you stop, and the conversation falls apart. Nothing in the agent's answer was wrong. It was simply late, and in spoken conversation, late is wrong.

Text chatbots get away with seconds of delay because reading is asynchronous: the user sees a typing indicator and waits. Speech is different. Humans take turns with remarkable precision: a cross-language study of ten languages (Stivers et al., 2009) found that everywhere, people avoid talking over each other and keep the silence between turns small, with language averages differing by only a couple of hundred milliseconds. We are tuned to read meaning into delays: a long pause signals hesitation, confusion or a dropped call.

A voice agent has to fit an entire pipeline into that window: notice that you have stopped speaking, finish transcribing, run a language model (which may call tools), synthesise speech, and get the audio to your ear over a network. Each stage was covered in isolation earlier (mm6, lm13); this lesson is about how they combine. The central skill is building and defending a latency budget: a stage-by-stage account of where every millisecond goes, which stages can overlap, and which knobs move the total. The second skill is turn-taking: deciding when the user has finished, and handling the user interrupting the agent.

It is also where AI engineering meets classic systems engineering. The models are only part of the answer; the rest is buffering, transport protocols, concurrency (cancelling work that is no longer needed), tail-latency management and careful instrumentation. Interviewers use voice-agent design questions precisely because they test both halves: whether you understand what ASR, LLMs and TTS do, and whether you can reason about a real-time distributed system built from them.

This lesson builds a cascaded voice agent from its parts, computes its latency budget by hand twice (non-streaming and streaming), explains why stage percentiles do not add, works through endpointing as a trade-off with numbers, and covers barge-in, echo, transport and tool calls. The public case is Moshi, a full-duplex speech model whose paper makes the latency argument explicitly. The lab simulates a hundred thousand turns to show the budget, the tail and the endpointing trade-off.

The relay race

Think of a voice agent as a relay race with five runners: the listener (voice activity detection and endpointing), the transcriber (ASR), the thinker (the LLM), the speaker (TTS) and the courier (the network). In a naive race, each runner waits for the previous one to finish the whole lap before starting: the transcriber waits for the full utterance, the thinker for the full transcript, the speaker for the full reply. The total time is the sum of everything. In a well-run race, runners overlap in the exchange zone: the transcriber writes while the user is still talking, the thinker starts as soon as the transcript is final, the speaker starts on the first few words of the reply while the thinker is still generating the rest, and the courier ships audio in 20 ms packets. The total time then depends mostly on the slowest starting latencies, not on the full durations.

The analogy breaks in an instructive place. In a relay, the starting gun is obvious. In conversation, nobody fires a gun when the user finishes: the agent must infer it, from silence and from what was said, and that inference is both a latency cost (you have to wait to be sure) and an accuracy risk (you may be wrong). That is why endpointing is called the hard part.

Thinking in budgets

The habit to build is to treat the target latency as money to be spent. Start from the goal (say 800 ms at the median), list every stage on the critical path, assign each a share, and ask for every proposed change: which line of the budget does this reduce, and by how much? A new LLM that is "20% faster" at generating long answers might save nothing, because only its time to first token is on the critical path. A change of the silence threshold from 600 to 300 ms saves exactly 300 ms on every turn, with no new model. A network region closer to users might save 60 ms each way. Budgets also make trade-offs explicit: spending 100 ms of look-ahead on more accurate streaming ASR is a reasonable purchase if it prevents misheard dates, and a bad one if accuracy was already adequate. Once you have a budget, latency stops being a vague complaint and becomes a list of engineering decisions.

Two architectures

Most production agents are cascades: separate ASR, LLM and TTS components connected by text. They are modular (swap in a better recogniser or voice without retraining anything), let you use the strongest text LLM with tools and retrieval, and expose a transcript you can log, search and filter for safety. The alternative is a speech-to-speech model that reads audio tokens and writes audio tokens directly (mm6's codec tokens), possibly full-duplex, listening while speaking. These keep tone and emotion, handle interruptions and backchannels ("mm-hm") naturally, and cut latency, at the cost of control, debuggability and access to the best text models' reasoning.

If you stop here: voice-to-voice latency is the product's core metric; it is a sum of starting latencies of streaming, overlapping stages; the endpoint decision is the biggest and most subtle term; and cascades trade some latency for control.

1. The metric: voice-to-voice latency, at the tail

Define the turn latency precisely: from the moment the user's speech ends (their last audible word, as heard at their microphone) to the moment the first audio of the agent's reply plays from their speaker. Call it voice-to-voice latency. It includes everything: network in both directions, buffering, every model. It must be measured per turn and summarised by percentiles: the median (p50) describes the typical turn, and the 95th percentile (p95) describes the slow turns that users remember and that break conversations. A product target might be p50 under 800 ms and p95 under 1.2 s.

Why these numbers? Human turn gaps are short: in Stivers et al.'s ten-language study, every language showed avoidance of overlapping talk and minimisation of silence between turns, and Moshi's authors summarise the average gap as 230 ms. People can tolerate an agent being somewhat slower than a person, but by about a second the delay is noticeable, and beyond that users start re-prompting ("Hello?"), which collides with the agent's late reply and derails the dialogue. Latency is not a polish item for voice; it is correctness.

2. The non-streaming pipeline, and why it fails

Start with the simplest design: record until the user has been silent for 800 ms, send the whole utterance to an ASR model, send the full transcript to the LLM, wait for its full reply, synthesise the full reply, send it back. A worked budget with typical median values:

endpoint: wait for 800 ms of silence             800 ms
ASR on the whole utterance (batch)                350 ms
LLM time to first token                           280 ms
LLM generates the rest: 120 tokens at 80 tok/s  1,500 ms
TTS synthesises the whole reply                   600 ms
network and jitter buffer                          90 ms
----------------------------------------------------------
total                                          ≈ 3,620 ms   (lab simulation: p50 3.7 s)

Almost four seconds, dominated by two terms that do not need to be there: waiting for the LLM's whole reply, and a conservative endpoint wait. No single fast model rescues this design.

3. Streaming every stage

The fix is to make each stage start producing output before its input is complete, and to start the next stage on that partial output.

Streaming ASR (mm6: CTC or RNN-T class models, or chunked encoders) emits partial transcripts every 100–200 ms while the user is still speaking. When the turn ends, only a short finalisation remains: processing the last chunk and committing the transcript.

Streaming LLM output (lm13): tokens arrive one at a time after the time to first token (TTFT). The agent does not need the whole reply; it needs the first speakable phrase.

Streaming TTS: synthesis starts on the first clause ("Sure, I can move that.") and produces audio in chunks of tens of milliseconds, while the LLM keeps generating the rest. Splitting at clause or sentence boundaries keeps prosody natural; splitting every few words sounds choppy.

Streaming transport: audio flows in small packets (typically 20 ms) in both directions, so the first chunk of reply audio is on its way as soon as it exists.

The streaming budget, using the lab's median values:

endpoint wait (silence threshold)                 300 ms
ASR finalisation                                   70 ms
LLM time to first token                           280 ms
first phrase: 8 tokens at 80 tokens/s             100 ms
TTS time to first audio                           110 ms
network both ways + jitter buffer                  90 ms
----------------------------------------------------------
sum of medians                                  ≈ 950 ms   (lab: p50 968 ms)

That is a 3.8× improvement with the same models. It is still above an 800 ms target, which is typical: getting below 800 ms requires attacking the two biggest terms, the endpoint wait and TTFT, and squeezing the rest. Notice what is not on the critical path any more: the LLM's total generation time and the TTS time for the whole reply. They happen while the agent is already talking. (They still matter if the agent speaks faster than the LLM generates, in which case audio stalls mid-reply; keep decode speed comfortably above speaking rate, which is about 3 words, or 4 tokens, per second.)

4. Percentiles do not add

A tempting shortcut is to set a p95 target per stage and add them up. The lab shows why that is wrong. Its stage p95s are 300 (endpoint), 114 (ASR), 640 (LLM TTFT), 139 (first phrase), 180 (TTS) and 136 (network) ms, summing to about 1,510 ms. The simulated end-to-end p95 is about 1,330 ms. Slow tails in independent stages rarely happen on the same turn, so the sum of p95s overestimates the end-to-end p95. Equally, the sum of medians (950 ms) is not exactly the end-to-end median (968 ms), because the distributions are skewed. In real systems stages are often correlated (a busy GPU slows ASR and LLM together; a bad network hurts both directions), which can make the tail worse than independence suggests. The engineering rule: record timestamps at every boundary on every turn, and compute percentiles of the end-to-end latency directly, with per-stage breakdowns for the slow turns.

5. Endpointing: the hardest term

The endpoint wait is the time between the user's last word and the agent's decision that the turn is over. The simplest rule is a silence threshold: after VAD reports T milliseconds without speech, declare the turn finished. The trouble is that people pause inside turns: "I'd like to book a table for... um... four people... on Friday." Every hesitation longer than T triggers a premature reply, and the agent interrupts the user.

The lab quantifies the trade-off with a toy model: each turn contains three hesitation pauses with lengths drawn from an exponential distribution with a mean of 250 ms. The chance that at least one pause exceeds the threshold:

ThresholdTurns where the agent cuts inLatency added to every reply
200 ms83%200 ms
400 ms49%400 ms
600 ms25%600 ms
800 ms12%800 ms

(For one pause, P(pause > T) = e^(−T/250): e^(−1.6) = 0.20 at 400 ms; with three independent pauses, 1 − (1 − 0.20)³ = 0.49.) The pause model is invented and real hesitation statistics vary by person and task, but the shape is general: no fixed threshold is both fast and polite. A silence threshold low enough for snappy replies interrupts people constantly; one high enough to be polite adds most of a second to every turn.

Semantic turn detection breaks the trade-off by using more information than silence. A small model reads the partial transcript (and possibly prosodic features: falling pitch and lengthened final syllables tend to mark the end of a turn) and predicts whether the utterance is complete. "Cancel my order" is complete; "My account number is" is not; "for four people on" is not. The system then uses a short wait (say 200–300 ms) when the model is confident the turn is complete and a long one (700 ms or more) when it is not. This cuts both the average delay and the interruption rate.

6. Speculative generation and early starts

Even with good endpointing, the LLM cannot start until the endpoint decision. Speculative generation starts it earlier, on the partial transcript, as soon as the user pauses. If the user does not continue and the final transcript matches, the agent already has its first tokens and part of the TTFT is hidden inside the endpoint wait. If the user continues, the speculative output is discarded. The cost is wasted LLM compute on discarded attempts, and one firm rule: speculative runs must never trigger side effects such as tool calls that book, pay or send anything; those wait for the confirmed turn.

A related trick is the acknowledgement. When the LLM decides to call a slow tool (a database lookup, a booking API taking two seconds), it first emits a short phrase ("Let me check Thursday's availability") that is spoken immediately. The measured time to the final answer is unchanged, but the perceived latency is that of the acknowledgement, and the silence that would make the user think the call dropped is filled.

7. Barge-in and echo cancellation

Users interrupt: "No, not Thursday, Friday." The agent must keep listening while it talks. When VAD detects speech on the user's channel during playback, the agent should stop audio within about 100–200 ms, cancel in-flight LLM generation and TTS, and treat the new speech as the next turn. Two details separate working systems from broken ones.

Echo. On a speakerphone or laptop, the user's microphone picks up the agent's own voice from the speaker. Without acoustic echo cancellation (AEC), which subtracts an estimate of the played audio from the microphone signal, VAD detects the agent's own speech as an interruption and the agent stops itself mid-sentence. WebRTC includes AEC; telephony networks partly handle it; custom pipelines must not forget it. Background TV and other voices cause similar false barge-ins, which is why barge-in detection often requires a minimum duration of speech or a few recognised words.

History truncation. The LLM generated a full reply, but the user heard only the first part. If the full reply is appended to the conversation history, the agent will later refer to things it never said aloud ("as I mentioned, the 7 pm slot..."). The fix: TTS reports how much text corresponds to the audio actually played (word or character timestamps), and the history keeps only that part, optionally marked as interrupted.

8. Transport: WebRTC, WebSockets and telephony

Audio must cross the network twice per turn. WebRTC (net11) is the standard for browsers and apps: it sends audio in small packets over UDP, uses a jitter buffer (a short queue, typically tens of milliseconds) to smooth uneven packet arrival, conceals lost packets instead of waiting for them, adapts bitrate to congestion, and includes echo cancellation and noise suppression. A WebSocket carries audio over TCP, which guarantees in-order delivery: a single lost packet holds up all later audio until it is retransmitted (head-of-line blocking), so on lossy mobile networks it turns packet loss into stalls of hundreds of milliseconds. Phone calls go through a telephony provider and arrive as 8 kHz narrowband audio (mm6: frequencies above 4 kHz are gone), with extra latency from the phone network; ASR must be evaluated on that audio, and many teams find their WER doubles on the phone channel.

9. The LLM inside a voice agent

The LLM stage has voice-specific requirements. Fast TTFT: a smaller model, a nearby region, and prefix caching (lm13) of the stable part of the prompt (system instructions and tool definitions at the start, unchanged across turns), so each turn only prefills the new user text. Speakable output: no markdown, bullet lists, URLs or tables; short sentences; numbers, dates and currency written as they should be said; and a willingness to ask a clarifying question rather than read out a long list of options. Tool use (lm16) with acknowledgements before slow calls, and parallel calls where possible. Robustness to ASR errors: the transcript will contain mistakes, so the prompt should encourage confirming critical values ("That's Friday the 14th at 7 pm, for four. Shall I book it?").

Context management over long calls is the last LLM concern. Each turn adds the user's transcript and the agent's (truncated) reply to the history, so a 15-minute call can accumulate thousands of tokens, and the prefill cost of the uncached tail grows with every turn, slowly pushing up TTFT. Keep the stable prefix cached, summarise or drop old turns that no longer matter, and store structured state (the booking being changed, the verified identity) in a compact form the prompt can reference instead of replaying the whole dialogue. A latency budget that holds on turn 3 and fails on turn 40 is a common surprise in production.

10. One turn, traced end to end

Put the pieces together for one exchange. The user says "Can you move my booking to Friday at seven?" and stops speaking at time 0 (all times relative to the end of speech, median values).

−2,100 ms  user starts speaking; VAD flags speech; ASR partials begin
  −900 ms  partial: "can you move my booking to friday"
  −150 ms  partial: "can you move my booking to friday at seven"
     0 ms  last word ends; VAD sees silence
   +60 ms  turn detector on partial: p(complete) = 0.94 → short wait chosen
   +60 ms  speculative LLM run starts on the partial transcript
  +250 ms  endpoint confirmed (250 ms adaptive wait)
  +310 ms  ASR final transcript = partial → speculative run kept
  +340 ms  LLM first token (TTFT 280 ms from +60, hidden partly in the wait)
  +440 ms  first clause ready: "Sure, let me check Friday at 7 pm."
  +550 ms  TTS first audio chunk
  +640 ms  first audio plays at the user's speaker   ← voice-to-voice 640 ms
  +700 ms  LLM emits tool call check_availability(fri, 19:00) — runs while the clause plays
+1,900 ms  tool returns; LLM continues: "Friday at seven is free; shall I move it?"

Three design choices produced 640 ms instead of the ~950 ms of the plain streaming budget: the adaptive wait (250 ms instead of a fixed 300–600), the speculative start that overlapped most of the LLM's time to first token with the endpoint wait, and the acknowledgement clause spoken while the tool ran. Nothing was booked speculatively: the tool call happened after the turn was confirmed. Notice also what the user experiences after the first clause: 1.2 seconds of tool latency are covered by speech, so the conversation never goes silent. This trace is what an instrumented system should be able to print for any turn.

11. Speech-to-speech and full-duplex models

A cascade passes only text between stages, losing tone, emphasis and hesitation, and it is fundamentally turn-based. Speech-to-speech models remove the text bottleneck: audio tokens in, audio tokens out. Full-duplex models go further and drop turns altogether. Moshi (the public case below) models the user's audio and its own audio as two parallel token streams, advancing both every 80 ms; at each step it predicts its own next audio frame (plus aligned text tokens) while consuming the user's. It can therefore produce backchannels, start speaking slightly before the user finishes, and stop when interrupted, all as learned behaviour rather than hand-written rules. Its latency is bounded by its frame structure rather than by a pipeline of stages.

Where latency hides: queueing and cold paths

Medians in a quiet test environment hide the two biggest sources of production tail latency. The first is queueing at shared model servers: TTFT is prefill time plus time spent waiting for a batch slot, and under load the waiting dominates. A voice product needs capacity headroom (or priority scheduling for voice traffic over batch traffic) because a 2-second queue at peak is a broken conversation, not a slow one. The second is cold paths: the first turn of a session pays for connection set-up, model loading, a cold prefix cache and TLS handshakes. Pre-warming at call start (open connections, send the system prompt to prime the cache, synthesise the greeting in advance) moves these costs out of the first user turn.

Turn detection as a learned model

A semantic turn detector is a small classifier, often a compact transformer over the last few seconds of partial transcript, sometimes with acoustic features. It is trained on conversations labelled at true turn ends versus mid-turn pauses. Its output is a probability that the turn is complete, which the system maps to a wait time: for example 200 ms when p > 0.9, 500 ms when 0.5 < p < 0.9, 900 ms otherwise. Because it runs on every pause, it must be fast (a few milliseconds) and run near the ASR. Errors are asymmetric: cutting the user off is usually worse than waiting a little longer, so thresholds are tuned with that cost in mind. Domain matters too: dictating an account number has long, legitimate pauses between digit groups, so a booking agent's turn detector should treat "and the number is 4 4 7" as incomplete.

Streaming ASR quality versus latency

Streaming recognisers see limited future context. A chunked encoder that waits for 320 ms of look-ahead is more accurate than one with 80 ms, because many sounds are disambiguated by what follows, but the look-ahead adds directly to finalisation latency. Partial transcripts also flicker: early hypotheses change as more audio arrives ("I want to book a" → "I want to book a table"). Downstream stages must treat partials as provisional; speculative LLM runs keyed on partials need a policy for when a changed transcript invalidates them. A common pattern is to commit only words that have been stable across two or three partials.

Why Moshi's latency is 160 ms

Moshi's latency follows from its frame structure, which makes the arithmetic instructive. Its Mimi codec encodes 24 kHz audio into 12.5 frames per second, so each frame covers 80 ms, and is fully causal: given the first 80 ms of audio, it emits a latent frame that can be decoded back to 80 ms. Each frame is quantised with 8 codebooks of 2,048 entries, about 1.1 kbps. The model generates its own frames with an "acoustic delay" of one frame between the first (semantic) codebook and the rest, which improves quality. So the earliest the model can respond to something the user said is one frame to receive it plus one frame of delay: 80 + 80 = 160 ms theoretical, about 200 ms in practice on their hardware. The paper notes that a longer delay pattern (8 timesteps) would have cost 640 ms, incompatible with conversation, which is why the design was chosen. Compare with a cascade's ~950 ms: there is no endpoint wait at all, because the model never has to decide that a turn is over; it simply keeps predicting what to say next, which may be silence.

Inner monologue: keeping text-LLM reasoning in a speech model

Pure audio-token models reason poorly compared with text LLMs: audio tokens are many per word (mm6), and speech data is far smaller than text data. Moshi addresses this with an "inner monologue": alongside its audio tokens, it predicts time-aligned text tokens of its own speech, and it is built on a text LLM backbone (Helium) trained from scratch on text. The text stream gives the model a compact, language-model-friendly representation to reason in, while the audio stream carries voice and timing. In English conversational speech the paper notes that padding tokens make up about 65% of the text stream, because text is much more compact than the 12.5 Hz audio frames it is aligned to. This hybrid, text reasoning plus audio rendering, is the general direction of speech-native assistants.

Evaluating with simulated callers

Voice agents are hard to test by hand, because every test needs a person to speak, and failures depend on timing. The scalable approach is a simulated caller: an LLM plays the customer from a scenario description ("you want to move your booking but only know the old date approximately; you interrupt once"), its text is rendered with TTS in varied voices, speeds and background noise, optionally passed through a phone-channel simulation, and streamed into the agent in real time. The harness records the agent's audio, transcribes it, and scores task success, latency per turn, interruptions and policy violations. Because timing matters, the simulated caller must also pause, hesitate and barge in at realistic moments; a caller that always waits politely tests nothing about turn-taking. Simulations do not replace listening to real calls, but they make regressions visible before users hear them.

Cost per minute

Voice products are priced per minute, so unit economics matter. A cascade's cost is ASR per audio minute (the user's speech, including silence unless VAD gates it), LLM tokens per turn (with prefix caching reducing the cost of the repeated system prompt), TTS per character spoken, and telephony or media-server minutes. A rough budget: a 5-minute call with 20 turns, a 1,500-token cached system prompt, 100 new tokens of context per turn and 60-token replies spends far more on input tokens than output tokens, which is why caching and context trimming matter. Speech-to-speech models process audio tokens in both directions, many per second, and are often several times more expensive per minute than a cascade with a small LLM, which is one reason cascades dominate high-volume use.

The public case: Moshi (Défossez et al., Kyutai, 2024)

"Moshi: a speech-text foundation model for real-time dialogue" frames the problem the way this lesson does: cascaded agents suffer from accumulated latency across components, lose non-textual information such as emotion and non-speech sounds in the text bottleneck, and depend on segmenting dialogue into speaker turns, which cannot represent overlap, interruptions or interjections. Moshi's answer has four parts. Mimi, a streaming neural codec, encodes 24 kHz audio at 12.5 frames per second with 8 residual codebooks of 2,048 entries (about 1.1 kbps), with semantic information distilled into its first codebook from a self-supervised speech model. Helium, a text LLM trained from scratch, is the backbone. An RQ-Transformer (a large temporal transformer over frames and a small depth transformer over codebooks within a frame) models two audio streams in parallel, the user's and Moshi's own, removing the concept of turns. And the inner monologue adds time-aligned text tokens of Moshi's own speech.

The headline number is latency: a theoretical 160 ms (one 80 ms frame plus one frame of acoustic delay) and about 200 ms in practice, which the authors compare with the 230 ms average gap they cite from Stivers et al.'s ten-language study. Training ran in four phases: pre-training on large amounts of unsupervised audio, post-training with simulated two-stream conversations built using diarisation, fine-tuning on the Fisher telephone-conversation corpus for natural overlap, and instruction fine-tuning on synthetic conversations. The model and codec were released with open weights. For an engineer, the paper is the clearest available statement of what cascades give up and what a full-duplex design buys, and its latency arithmetic is a template for reasoning about any frame-based audio model.

Failure modes in production

Talking over the user. Symptom: users complain the agent interrupts them, especially when giving numbers or addresses. Cause: silence threshold too short or turn detector not trained on dictation pauses. Fix: domain-tuned turn detection; longer waits when the transcript ends mid-phrase.

Self-interruption. Symptom: the agent stops mid-sentence for no reason on speakerphone calls. Cause: echo leaking into the microphone triggers barge-in. Fix: echo cancellation, minimum speech duration for barge-in, ignoring speech that matches the agent's own audio.

Latency spikes at peak. Symptom: p95 doubles at busy hours while p50 barely moves. Cause: queueing at the shared LLM or TTS servers. Fix: capacity headroom, priority classes, per-turn timeouts with a fallback phrase.

Ghost context. Symptom: the agent refers to options it never finished saying. Cause: history not truncated on barge-in. Fix: truncate to played audio.

Misheard critical values. Symptom: wrong dates, names or numbers booked. Cause: ASR errors on entities, especially on phone audio. Fix: read back and confirm, spelling flows for names, ASR vocabulary biasing, structured slot validation.

Robotic or rushed delivery. Symptom: users say the agent is hard to follow even though transcripts look fine. Cause: the LLM writes for reading (long sentences, lists, parenthetical remarks) and the TTS reads it faithfully. Fix: a speech-specific system prompt and output post-processing that rewrites lists into short spoken sentences.

What to measure

Per turn: timestamps at end of user speech, endpoint decision, final transcript, LLM first token, TTS first audio, first audio played; derive voice-to-voice latency and per-stage contributions, and report p50, p95 and p99. Per call: task success, number of turns, interruption counts (user barge-ins, agent cut-ins on the user, false barge-ins), escalations to humans, ASR entity error rate, and cost per minute. Review recordings of the slowest and worst-rated calls weekly: timing and tone failures are invisible in transcripts.

total = sum(stages.values())

Try reducing the endpoint to 200 ms and the LLM median to 180 ms and see how far p50 moves. The last block is the endpointing table from the body: no fixed threshold is both fast and polite, which is the case for semantic turn detection.

Budget it

A bank's voice agent runs on phone lines. Measured medians: telephony in and out 150 ms combined, endpoint wait 600 ms, ASR finalisation 120 ms, LLM time to first token 450 ms (70B model, no caching, 2,000-token system prompt), first phrase 12 tokens at 40 tokens/s, TTS first audio 200 ms. Compute the median voice-to-voice latency, and propose changes to reach 900 ms.

Answer

First phrase: 12 / 40 = 300 ms. Total: 150 + 600 + 120 + 450 + 300 + 200 = 1,820 ms. Changes, largest first: semantic turn detection with an adaptive wait (600 → about 300 ms; save 300); prefix caching of the 2,000-token system prompt and a smaller, faster model (TTFT 450 → about 250 ms; save 200) whose decode rate of 80 tokens/s also halves the first phrase (300 → 150 ms; save 150); start TTS on the first clause of 6–8 tokens instead of 12 (another ~50 ms); a lower-latency TTS voice (200 → 120 ms; save 80); streaming ASR tuned for less look-ahead (120 → 80 ms; save 40). New total ≈ 150 + 300 + 80 + 250 + 100 + 120 = 1,000 ms. Reaching 900 ms needs speculative LLM starts on partial transcripts (hiding ~100 ms of TTFT in the endpoint wait) or a co-located telephony edge. Then verify end-to-end at p95, not by adding medians.

Diagnose it

After launch, agent cut-ins (the agent starting to talk while the user is mid-sentence) occur on 18% of turns in the "update address" flow but 3% elsewhere. What is happening and what do you change?

Answer

Address dictation has long, legitimate pauses ("42... Elm Street... apartment 5B") that exceed the silence threshold, and the turn detector sees each fragment as a plausible complete answer. Fixes: make the turn detector flow-aware (when the agent has just asked for an address, raise the completeness bar and the minimum wait); detect incomplete patterns (house number without street); allow the user to continue after a cut-in by merging the next utterance into the same slot instead of starting a new turn; and confirm the full address at the end. Measure cut-in rate per flow and per prompt type, not only globally.
