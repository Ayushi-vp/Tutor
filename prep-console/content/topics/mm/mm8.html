A warehouse has 400 security cameras. Management asks: "Show me every time a forklift drove through the pedestrian zone last week." A sports app wants to answer "How did that goal happen?" moments after it is scored. A marketing team wants a 10-second clip of their product spinning on a beach at sunset, generated from a sentence. Each is a video problem, and video is where multimodal AI runs hardest into arithmetic.

A single second of ordinary 1080p video at 30 frames per second is 30 images of about 6 million numbers each. If one image costs a vision-language model a few hundred tokens, one minute of video at full frame rate costs hundreds of thousands, and an hour costs tens of millions. No context window holds that, and no budget pays for it. Generating video has the mirror problem: a generator must produce all those pixels, consistently, with objects that keep their shape and colour from one frame to the next and motion that looks physically plausible.

The field's answers fall into a small set of ideas that recur on both sides. Do not look at everything: sample frames, and sample them cleverly. Exploit redundancy: neighbouring frames are nearly identical, so compress across time as well as space, with tubelets for understanding and temporal autoencoders for generation. Keep track of time: give tokens temporal positions so the model knows order and duration. And for long content, retrieve, then read: find the relevant minutes before looking closely.

Video is also where costs surprise teams most. A prototype that answers questions about a 20-second demo clip in two seconds can take minutes and dollars per request on real hour-long uploads, because every default (frame rate, resolution, per-frame tokens) multiplies with duration. Engineers who can estimate tokens per video minute before writing code avoid building systems that cannot ship.

This lesson works through both directions with numbers. For understanding: the raw size of video, the token explosion, frame sampling strategies, tubelets and 3-D convolutions with the token arithmetic of a modern video VLM, temporal positions, and long-video pipelines. For generation: why frame-by-frame fails, temporal autoencoders and spacetime patches, the 73,728-token context of a 16-second clip, training stages and the long-context machinery it needs. The public case is Meta's Movie Gen paper, whose numbers make the scale concrete.

The flip book

A video is a flip book: a stack of still pictures shown fast enough that motion appears. Two facts about flip books carry most of this lesson. First, most pages look almost exactly like the page before; only small parts change. If you had to describe the book to a friend, you would not describe every page; you would describe the first page and then what changes. Second, the story is in the changes: a single page cannot tell you whether the ball is rising or falling. Video models are built around these two facts. They skip most pages (sampling), describe groups of pages at once (temporal compression), and make sure the pages they do look at carry timestamps (temporal positions) so change and order can be inferred.

The analogy breaks in a way that matters. A person flipping the book sees every page, and the brain fills in motion seamlessly. A video model sees only the pages it sampled; anything that happens entirely between two sampled pages, such as a brief glance, a dropped object picked up again, or a 300 ms event on a camera, is invisible to it. Sampling is not an approximation that degrades gracefully; it is a hard blind spot.

A first estimate, before any detail

Try the arithmetic once now, because it frames everything else. A user uploads a 3-minute cooking video and asks "at what point does the chef add the garlic?". Suppose your VLM costs 256 tokens per frame. At the source rate of 30 fps, 3 minutes is 5,400 frames, or 1.38 million tokens: impossible. At 1 frame per second it is 180 frames and 46,080 tokens: possible on a long-context model but slow and expensive. At 1 frame per second with frames shrunk to 64 tokens each (224 px), it is 11,520 tokens: comfortable. But adding garlic takes perhaps two seconds, and minced garlic in a pan is a small detail. At one frame per second you will catch the moment; at 224 px you may not recognise what is being added. The transcript ("now the garlic goes in") might answer it outright at almost no cost. Every design decision in video understanding is this kind of trade, between frames, pixels per frame and other signals, against a token budget.

Two directions, same arithmetic

Understanding (video in, text out) extends the vision-language models of mm3: sample frames, encode them, compress tokens, add time information, and let the LLM answer. Generation (text in, video out) extends latent diffusion and flow matching from mm5: compress the video into a latent that is small in both space and time, cut it into spacetime patches, and run a transformer denoiser over all of them at once. On both sides the controlling quantity is the number of tokens, and it grows with duration × frame rate × resolution².

If you stop here: video models see sampled, compressed spacetime blocks with timestamps, never the full video; understanding pays per sampled frame and misses what falls between samples; generation denoises the whole clip jointly in a compressed latent so frames stay consistent.

1. The raw size of video

Start with the numbers that make video different. A 1080p frame is 1,920 × 1,080 pixels × 3 colour channels ≈ 6.2 million values. At 30 frames per second (fps), 10 seconds is 300 frames, about 1.87 billion values, or 1.9 GB as raw 8-bit pixels. Video files are small only because codecs such as H.264 store mostly the differences between frames, exploiting the redundancy described in the primer. A model cannot consume compressed bitstreams directly, so it decodes frames and immediately has to throw most of them away. The question is what to keep.

2. The token explosion

Take a modern VLM front end from mm3: 14-pixel patches merged 2 × 2, so one token per 28 × 28 pixels. A frame resized to 448 × 448 is (448 / 28)² = 16 × 16 = 256 tokens. Now multiply by frames:

VideoFrames at 30 fpsTokens at 256 per frame
10 seconds30076,800
1 minute1,800460,800
1 hour108,00027.6 million

Even a 128K-token context holds less than 17 seconds of full-rate video at this resolution, and attention cost grows with the square of tokens (lm5). Everything that follows is a strategy for reducing frames, tokens per frame, or both.

Tokens are not the only cost. Decoding the compressed video file into frames takes CPU or GPU time proportional to the number of frames decoded, even ones you then discard, unless the decoder can seek to keyframes. Resizing and normalising each kept frame adds more. In pipelines that process thousands of hours a day, frame decoding and preprocessing often cost as much as the model itself, which is why production systems decode at the target sample rate directly (seeking rather than decoding everything), use hardware video decoders, and run preprocessing on the GPU next to the encoder. When you estimate the cost of a video feature, count three things: frames decoded, frames encoded, and tokens sent to the LLM.

3. Frame sampling

The first lever is which frames to keep. Four strategies are common.

Uniform N frames: pick a fixed number (8, 16, 32) evenly spaced across the video. Cost is constant, so it suits classification of short clips; for long videos the gaps become huge (32 frames from an hour is one every 112 seconds).

Fixed frame rate: sample at, say, 1 or 2 fps. Coverage is even in time and cost grows linearly with duration. Qwen2-VL trained with videos sampled at two frames per second.

Content-aware: detect scene changes or keyframes (frames that differ strongly from the previous one, cheap to compute from pixel or embedding differences) and spend frames where something changes. A static camera watching an empty corridor needs few frames until someone walks in.

Query-aware: use the question to choose. A cheap model (a CLIP-style encoder, mm2) scores segments for relevance to "when does the forklift enter the pedestrian zone?", and the expensive VLM only sees the top segments, densely. This is retrieval (lm9) over time.

All four share the blind spot from the primer: events shorter than the sampling interval may be missed entirely. At 2 fps the gap is 500 ms; at 0.2 fps it is 5 seconds.

4. Compressing across time: tubelets and 3-D convolutions

The second lever is merging neighbouring frames. Because consecutive frames are nearly identical, one token can describe a small block of space and time. ViViT (Arnab et al., 2021) called these tubelets: instead of a 16 × 16 × 3 patch from one frame, take a t × 16 × 16 × 3 block spanning t frames, flatten it, and project it to a token, exactly the ViT patch embedding (mm1) extended to three dimensions, equivalent to a 3-D convolution with kernel and stride equal to the tubelet size.

Worked example: a 32-frame clip at 224 × 224. Per-frame patches of 16 × 16 give 14 × 14 = 196 tokens per frame, 32 × 196 = 6,272 tokens. Tubelets of 2 × 16 × 16 give (32 / 2) × 14 × 14 = 16 × 196 = 3,136 tokens, half as many, each a vector of 2 × 16 × 16 × 3 = 1,536 numbers before projection. ViViT names models by tubelet size: ViViT-B/16×2 uses tubelets of 16 × 16 pixels over 2 frames. The lab builds tubelets with a reshape and transpose and checks the first one is frames 0–1 of the top-left 16 × 16 square.

Qwen2-VL uses the same idea inside a VLM: 3-D convolutions with a depth of two, so each token covers two frames, which (in the paper's words) lets it process more frames without increasing sequence length. An image is treated as two identical frames for consistency. The full token arithmetic for a 60-second clip at 2 fps and 448 × 448:

frames          = 60 s × 2 fps            = 120
2-frame tubes   = 120 / 2                 = 60
tokens per tube = (448 / 28)²             = 256    (14 px patches, 2 × 2 merge)
total           = 60 × 256                = 15,360 tokens

That fits under the 16,384-token per-video cap that Qwen2-VL's training used. A 10-minute video at the same settings would be 153,600 tokens and an hour 921,600, so long videos must drop frame rate, resolution or both. Qwen2-VL's evaluation capped extraction at 768 frames per video; 768 frames over an hour is about one frame every 4.7 seconds, and fitting those 384 tubes into ~16K tokens requires about 36 tokens each, which is a 168 × 168 frame. At that point the model is reading thumbnails at intervals of several seconds: good for "what is this video about", useless for "what did the sign at 31:05 say".

5. Where time goes in the model

A transformer is order-blind (lm4), so frames need temporal positions. Options: learned time embeddings added to each frame's tokens; M-RoPE (mm3), whose temporal component gives every token of a frame the same time index and increments it per frame (or per tube); or timestamps as text interleaved before each frame ("<t=12.5s>"), which also let the model answer "when" questions in seconds and cope with irregular sampling. Without temporal information, a model cannot distinguish a door opening from a door closing: both are the same set of frames in different order.

Architecturally, video encoders either apply full spacetime attention over all tokens, or factorise it. ViViT's variants include a factorised encoder (a spatial transformer per frame, then a temporal transformer over frame summaries) and factorised self-attention (within each block, attend spatially within a frame, then temporally across frames at the same location). With T frames of S tokens each, full attention costs on the order of (T·S)², factorised attention T·S² + S·T², a large saving for long clips. TimeSformer's "divided space-time attention" is the same idea. VLMs mostly encode frames (or 2-frame tubes) with an image encoder and leave cross-frame reasoning to the LLM's attention over the concatenated tokens.

6. Temporal redundancy, quantified

How redundant are frames? The lab builds a toy "video" of a random texture panning 2 pixels per frame. The mean absolute difference between consecutive frames is 0.336 (large, because a random texture shifted by 2 pixels is uncorrelated pixel by pixel), but after compensating for the 2-pixel motion it is 0.000: all the change is explained by a single motion vector. Real video is similar: most change between frames is motion of things already visible, which is why video codecs store motion vectors and residuals, and why temporal compression (tubelets, temporal autoencoders) loses little for slow content. The flip side is that fast or sudden content (a flash, a quick hand movement) is exactly what compression and sampling discard first.

7. Long-video understanding: retrieve, then read

For videos longer than a few minutes, the practical pattern mirrors retrieval-augmented generation (lm9). Index first: cut the video into clips (fixed length or at scene changes), and for each clip compute cheap representations: an embedding from a contrastive image–text encoder, a short caption from a small VLM, the speech transcript with timestamps (mm6), and OCR of on-screen text (mm9). Then, per question, retrieve the most relevant clips by embedding similarity and keyword match, and give a strong VLM those clips at higher frame rate and resolution, along with their timestamps and transcript. The answer cites times ("at 31:05, the forklift crosses the yellow line"). Agentic variants let the model request more frames around a moment it found interesting. As with text RAG, failures come from retrieval as often as from reading: a question about something never captioned or transcribed may retrieve nothing relevant.

8. Temporal grounding: answering "when"

Many useful video questions are about time: when did the forklift enter, how long was the shelf empty, what happened just before the fall. Answering them is called temporal grounding, the time analogue of drawing a box around an object (mm10). A model can only ground events in time if it knows the times of the frames it saw, so the input must carry timestamps (as text markers or through temporal positions with a known frame rate). The output is then a time or interval, for example "from 02:14 to 02:31". Two things limit accuracy. The sampling interval sets a floor: with frames every 2 seconds, an answer cannot be more precise than about ±1 second, however good the model. And models are weaker at precise boundaries than at recognising that something happened, so a common pattern is coarse-to-fine: locate the event at low frame rate, then re-sample densely around the candidate time and ask again. For evaluation, measure the overlap between predicted and true intervals (the same intersection-over-union idea used for boxes in mm10, applied to time spans), not just whether the answer mentions the right minute.

9. One question, end to end

Put the understanding pieces together for a concrete request: a 2-minute cooking video and the question "What does the chef add right after the onions?". Decode the video (30 fps, 3,600 frames). The audio track goes to ASR with word timestamps (mm6): at 0:41 the chef says "onions in", at 0:58 "and now the garlic". Sample frames at 2 fps (240 frames), group into 120 two-frame tubes at 448 px (256 tokens each), giving 30,720 tokens, too many for a 16K cap, so drop to 336 px (144 tokens per tube): 17,280 tokens, still over; use 1 fps instead (60 tubes × 144 = 8,640 tokens). Interleave timestamp markers before each tube and the transcript lines at their times. The LLM sees "<0:40> [frames] 'onions in' <0:42> [frames] … <0:58> [frames] 'and now the garlic' …" and answers: "Garlic, at about 0:58, right after the onions at 0:41." Notice what did the work: the transcript gave the answer and its time; the frames confirmed it. Without the audio, the model would need to recognise minced garlic in a 336 px frame, which is much less reliable. In a real system you would also cache the encoded frames and transcript per video, since users ask several questions about the same upload.

10. Video generation: why frame by frame fails

Now reverse the direction. The naive approach to generating a 16-second clip is to generate 256 images, each conditioned on the prompt and maybe the previous frame. Two things go wrong. Independently generated frames resample every detail, so textures, colours and identities flicker. Conditioning on the previous frame helps locally but errors accumulate: after a hundred frames the person has a different face. Consistency has to be a property of the whole clip, which means generating all frames jointly, with attention between every part of every frame. That is only affordable after heavy compression.

11. Temporal autoencoders and spacetime patches

Video generators extend latent diffusion (mm5) to time. A temporal autoencoder (TAE) compresses a video in height, width and time into a latent, and decodes latents back to frames. Movie Gen's TAE compresses 8× along each of time, height and width into 16 latent channels (it is initialised from an image autoencoder and then "inflated" with temporal layers). The generator then cuts the latent into spacetime patches with a 3-D convolution (kernel = stride) and flattens them into a token sequence for a transformer. The arithmetic for Movie Gen's highest-resolution training setting, as stated in its paper:

pixels         : 256 frames × 768 × 768 × 3
TAE (8 × 8 × 8): 256/8 = 32 latent frames,  768/8 = 96 × 96,  16 channels
patchify 2×2×1 : 32 × (96/2) × (96/2) = 32 × 48 × 48
tokens         : 73,728    ("73K tokens")
raw values     : 256 × 768 × 768 × 3 ≈ 453 million → latent 32 × 96 × 96 × 16 ≈ 4.7 million (96× smaller)

The denoiser is a transformer over these 73,728 tokens, with factorised learned position embeddings for time, height and width, trained with flow matching (mm5). Every token can attend to every other, which is what keeps the cat the same cat for 16 seconds. The cost: attention over 73,728 tokens means 73,728² ≈ 5.4 × 10⁹ scores per head per layer. This is why video generation is a long-context training problem as much as a vision one.

12. Training and sampling a video generator

Several practices make this tractable. Joint image–video training: an image is a one-frame video, and images are far more plentiful and diverse than videos, so the same model learns appearance from images and motion from video; Movie Gen begins with a text-to-image warm-up at low resolution (256 px) before video stages. Staged resolution and length: train at 256 px first (short sequences, large batches), then at 768 px with long sequences, where most of the training FLOPs are spent. Variable aspect ratios and durations, so one model generates 16:9, 9:16 and 1:1 clips. A separate upsampler: Movie Gen generates 768 px video and a video-to-video super-resolution model takes it to 1080p, so the expensive generator never runs at full HD. Long-context systems: sequences of 73K tokens with a 30B-parameter model require sharding the sequence itself across GPUs (context parallelism, dl9), not only the weights. Sampling is the same as for images (mm5): integrate the learned velocity from noise to data in tens of steps, with guidance, then decode.

Temporal autoencoders: causality and the first frame

Compressing time 8× means 8 frames share one latent frame, which raises two design questions. First, causality: if the encoder's temporal convolutions look at future frames, the latent for frame block k depends on later frames, which is fine for offline generation but prevents streaming and complicates extending a video. Many video autoencoders use causal temporal convolutions (padding only on the past side) so each latent depends only on the current and previous frames. Second, the first frame: with causal compression, the first frame is often encoded on its own, so that a single image is a valid one-latent-frame video, which is what makes joint image–video training and image-to-video generation natural. Movie Gen notes a related detail: frames are encoded in blocks, and when T is not a multiple of 8, the decoder produces 8·⌈T/8⌉ frames and discards the extras.

Image-to-video and other conditioning

Text-to-video asks the model to invent everything. Image-to-video fixes the first frame (the user's product photo, a generated still) and asks only for motion, which greatly improves identity consistency and gives users control over appearance. In a latent model it is implemented by encoding the given image into the first latent frame and either keeping it fixed during denoising (like inpainting along time, mm5) or feeding it as an extra conditioning input. The same mechanism supports video extension (condition on the last frames of an existing clip), interpolation between two keyframes, and video editing (noise an existing video partially and denoise it with a new prompt, the video version of image-to-image). Movie Gen also trains personalised generation conditioned on a reference image of a person and instruction-based editing. These conditional modes are what most products actually ship, because pure text-to-video is hard to steer precisely.

Why spacetime attention is the bottleneck, and what helps

Attention cost scales with the square of tokens, and video tokens scale with duration × resolution². Doubling clip length doubles tokens and quadruples attention; doubling resolution quadruples tokens and multiplies attention by 16. At Movie Gen's scale the paper reports that most training FLOPs go to the long-context 768 px stage. Tools to manage this: factorised or windowed attention (spatial within frames, temporal across), which loses some long-range interactions; context parallelism, which splits the sequence across GPUs and passes key/value blocks around; fused attention kernels that avoid materialising the score matrix; and higher compression in the autoencoder, which trades reconstruction detail for fewer tokens. The Movie Gen paper observes that its full-attention model, unlike LLMs with grouped-query attention, has no smaller K/V tensors to communicate, which makes context parallelism relatively more expensive, a reminder that tricks from LLM serving do not all transfer.

Extending beyond the training length

A model trained on 16-second clips cannot denoise a 2-minute latent in one pass: positions and attention patterns were never seen at that length, and memory would explode. Longer videos are generated in overlapping chunks: generate the first chunk, then generate the next conditioned on the last few latent frames of the previous one (as in image outpainting, but along time), and blend overlaps. Drift accumulates across chunks, which is why long generated videos tend to change identity or style. Keyframe-first approaches (generate sparse keyframes for the whole duration, then fill in between) trade some motion fidelity for global consistency.

Understanding: the frame-rate versus resolution trade

For a fixed token budget, a video VLM can spend tokens on more frames or on more pixels per frame. The right split depends on the question. Action recognition and event order need temporal density; reading a scoreboard or a licence plate needs spatial resolution on a few frames. Adaptive systems decide per query: first a cheap pass at low resolution and low fps to locate relevant moments, then a second pass that re-encodes only those moments at high resolution. Qwen2-VL's approach of dynamically adjusting per-frame resolution to keep each video under a token cap is a fixed version of the same trade, and it explains why long-video accuracy falls: the cap forces thumbnails.

Motion as a signal: optical flow

Before deep video models, motion was computed explicitly as optical flow: for every pixel, a vector saying where it moved between two frames. Flow is still useful in modern systems. As a cheap trigger, large flow magnitude signals that something is happening, which drives content-aware frame sampling for understanding. As a training signal or filter for generation, flow statistics help remove static clips (slideshows, still images posing as video) and clips with extreme camera shake from training data; Movie Gen's data pipeline, like most, filters for motion. As an evaluation tool, flow-based warping errors between consecutive generated frames give a rough automatic measure of temporal consistency. The lab's panning example is the simplest case: one global flow vector explains every pixel's change, which is exactly what a codec, an autoencoder or a model can exploit.

Audio is half the video

Most real videos have audio, and for many questions it carries more information than the frames: lectures, meetings, tutorials, interviews and news are mostly speech. A video system that ignores the audio track throws away the cheapest, densest signal available. Transcribing with timestamps (mm6) and aligning transcript segments with frames often answers questions that no amount of frame sampling could. On the generation side, Movie Gen includes a separate audio model that generates sound effects and music conditioned on the video, because silent generated video feels incomplete. Multimodal here means at least three modalities.

Evaluation is hard on both sides

For understanding, benchmarks such as Video-MME split questions by video length (short, medium, long) and show accuracy falling with duration, which reflects sampling and token caps as much as reasoning. Watch for questions answerable from a single frame or from the transcript alone: they overstate temporal understanding, and a good evaluation includes order, counting-over-time and duration questions. For generation, automatic metrics (FVD, the video analogue of FID, and text–video similarity scores) correlate weakly with human judgement of motion quality and consistency, so papers rely on large human preference studies. Movie Gen reports head-to-head human evaluations against other systems on overall quality, motion naturalness, consistency and text alignment.

The public case: Movie Gen (Meta, 2024)

"Movie Gen: A Cast of Media Foundation Models" describes a family of models for video, audio and personalised generation; its video model is the clearest public account of what large-scale video generation requires. The largest text-to-video model is a 30-billion-parameter transformer following the Llama 3 design, trained with flow matching, generating HD videos of up to 16 seconds at 16 fps and multiple aspect ratios. A temporal autoencoder (initialised from an image autoencoder, then inflated with temporal layers) compresses videos 8× in time, height and width into 16 channels. A 3-D convolutional patchifier with a 2 × 2 × 1 kernel turns latents into tokens; for 768 × 768 video with 256 frames, the paper states this gives a context of 73K tokens (32 × 48 × 48 = 73,728). Text prompts are encoded by several pretrained text encoders whose outputs are projected into the model's 6,144-dimensional space.

Training proceeds in stages: a text-to-image warm-up at 256 px, joint image–video training at increasing resolution, then 768 px long-context training where most FLOPs are spent; a separate spatial upsampler takes 768 px outputs to 1080p, applied with a sliding window over time. The scale demanded systems work: the paper describes sharding the 30B model and its 73K-token sequences with tensor, sequence/context and data parallelism, and notes that context parallelism is relatively costly for this model because its full multi-head attention produces larger key/value tensors than LLMs with grouped-query attention. For an engineer, the paper converts "video generation is expensive" into specific numbers: compression ratios, token counts and where compute goes. It also shows how much of the work is data: the authors describe filtering large video collections for resolution, motion, duplicates and caption quality, and writing detailed captions with an LLM-based captioner, because a generator can only learn the motion and prompt-following its training clips and captions actually contain.

Failure modes in production: understanding

Missed short events. A camera QA system says nobody entered the zone; the entry happened between two samples. Mitigation: motion- or change-triggered sampling, lower thresholds for high-stakes zones, and evaluation of miss rate by event duration.

Wrong timestamps. The model's answer cites 12:40 for an event at 14:10, because it was given frames without reliable time markers or with irregular sampling. Provide explicit timestamps and verify cited times against retrieved clips.

Transcript dominance. On videos with speech, the model answers from the transcript and ignores contradicting visuals ("the presenter says the light is green; the screen shows red"). Test with cases where audio and video disagree if visual fidelity matters.

Cost blow-ups. A default of 2 fps on hour-long uploads multiplies tokens and latency; set per-video caps and route long videos through the retrieve-then-read pipeline.

Failure modes in production: generation

Temporal inconsistency (flicker, identity drift, objects appearing and vanishing), implausible physics (interpenetration, impossible motion), prompt drift over long durations, and slow generation (minutes per clip for large models). Products constrain duration, offer image-to-video (which anchors appearance), use fast distilled models for previews and large models for finals, and run safety and likeness filters on prompts and outputs, since realistic video of real people is a misuse vector.

Encoding cost dominates repeated questions. Users often ask several questions about the same video; re-decoding and re-encoding frames for each question multiplies cost. Cache encoded frames and transcripts per video, and use prefix caching for the video tokens when the serving stack supports it.

What to measure

Understanding: accuracy by question type (single-frame, order, count, duration, grounding), by video length and by sampling setting; miss rate by event duration; tokens and latency per video minute. Generation: human preference on your prompts, temporal-consistency checks (identity similarity across frames), prompt adherence, generation time and GPU-seconds per clip.

Goal: build ViViT-style tubelets with a reshape and transpose, compute a Qwen2-VL-style token budget for videos from 1 minute to 1 hour, reproduce Movie Gen's 73,728-token count, and measure temporal redundancy in a toy panning video. NumPy only; the existing gpt_env (Python 3.12).

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 1. tubelets: a (T, H, W, C) video -> (N, t*h*w*C) tokens, ViViT-style 2 x 16 x 16
def tubeletify(video, t, p):
    T, H, W, C = video.shape
    x = video.reshape(T // t, t, H // p, p, W // p, p, C)
    x = x.transpose(0, 2, 4, 1, 3, 5, 6)                 # (T/t, H/p, W/p, t, p, p, C)
    return x.reshape(-1, t * p * p * C)

video = rng.random((32, 224, 224, 3)).astype(np.float32)  # 32 frames at 224 px
tok = tubeletify(video, 2, 16)
print("32 frames, 2x16x16 tubelets:", tok.shape, "| per-frame patches would be", 32 * 14 * 14)
assert np.array_equal(tok[0], video[0:2, 0:16, 0:16].reshape(-1))   # first tubelet = frames 0-1, top-left 16x16

# 2. token budget of a video VLM: sample fps, 2-frame tubes, 14 px patches merged 2x2 (28 px per token)
def video_tokens(seconds, fps, side):
    frames = int(seconds * fps)
    return frames, (frames // 2) * (side // 28) ** 2
for secs, fps, side in [(60, 2, 448), (600, 2, 448), (3600, 2, 448), (3600, 768 / 3600, 448), (3600, 768 / 3600, 168)]:
    f, n = video_tokens(secs, fps, side)
    print(f"{secs:5d} s @ {fps:4.2f} fps, {side} px: {f:5d} frames -> {n:9,d} tokens"
          f"{'  (over 16,384 cap)' if n > 16_384 else ''}")

# 3. generation side: Movie Gen's latent token count (TAE 8x8x8, then 2x2x1 patches)
T, H, W = 256, 768, 768
lat = (T // 8, H // 8, W // 8)
print("Movie Gen 768 px, 256 frames: latent", lat, "-> tokens", lat[0] * (lat[1] // 2) * (lat[2] // 2))

# 4. temporal redundancy: a slowly panning synthetic 'video' and its frame-to-frame change
base = rng.random((96, 400))
frames = np.stack([base[:, s:s + 256] for s in range(0, 64, 2)])   # pan 2 px per frame
diff = np.abs(np.diff(frames, axis=0)).mean()
shifted = np.abs(frames[1][:, :-2] - frames[0][:, 2:]).mean()
print(f"mean |frame t+1 - frame t| = {diff:.3f}; after compensating the 2 px pan = {shifted:.3f}")

What to look for. Estimate it

A retail chain wants a VLM to review 8 hours of footage per store per day from 1 camera, across 500 stores, answering "were any shelves left empty for more than 10 minutes?". Each frame at 448 px is 256 tokens (with 2-frame tubes, 128 tokens per frame). Estimate tokens per day at 2 fps, propose a cheaper design, and say what you would lose.

Answer

8 h × 3,600 s × 2 fps = 57,600 frames per store, × 128 tokens = 7.4 million tokens per store per day, × 500 stores = 3.7 billion tokens per day, far too expensive and slow for the question. The question is about states lasting 10 minutes, so sample at one frame per minute (480 frames per store, 61,440 tokens, 30.7M per day across stores, 120× cheaper), or better, run a cheap shelf-occupancy detector or embedding-change detector on every frame at low resolution and call the VLM only when a shelf region looks empty for several consecutive samples. What you lose: events shorter than the sampling interval (irrelevant for a 10-minute criterion), and any question you did not design for (theft, falls), which would need different sampling.

Compute it

A video generator uses an autoencoder compressing 4× in time and 8× in height and width, 2 × 2 × 1 patches, and generates 5-second clips at 24 fps and 512 × 512. How many tokens, and how does attention cost change if you double the duration and the resolution to 1,024 px?

Answer

Frames: 5 × 24 = 120 → 120 / 4 = 30 latent frames; spatial 512 / 8 = 64 → 64 / 2 = 32 per side after patches. Tokens = 30 × 32 × 32 = 30,720. Doubling duration doubles latent frames (60); doubling resolution doubles each side (64 × 64 after patches): tokens = 60 × 64 × 64 = 245,760, 8× more. Attention scales with tokens squared: 64× the attention cost (and 8× the linear-layer cost). That is why longer, higher-resolution generation relies on more temporal compression, windowed attention, lower base resolution plus upsampling, or chunked generation.
