In net2 you saw that page load time is dominated by round trips, and that a user far from the server pays for every one of them: a visitor in Sydney loading a site hosted in Virginia spends close to a second on handshakes and the first request before the server does any work. You cannot make light travel faster, and you cannot run your whole application in every city on earth. What you can do is put something of yours close to every user.

That is what a content delivery network does. A CDN runs servers in hundreds of cities, called points of presence, and arranges for each user's traffic to reach a nearby one. That edge server completes the connection handshakes over a short round trip, answers directly from its cache whenever it can, and otherwise forwards the request to your origin over a fast, already-open connection. Most of the bytes on the modern internet, including video, software downloads, images and web pages, are delivered this way.

CDNs have also become a security and compute layer. Because they sit in front of your origin and have enormous aggregate capacity, they absorb denial-of-service attacks, filter malicious requests, rate-limit bots and terminate TLS; increasingly they also run your code at the edge. And because they sit in front of everything, their mistakes are enormous too: a single configuration change at a major CDN has more than once taken a large fraction of the web offline for an hour.

For an engineer, the practical skills are: understanding how users are routed to an edge (DNS steering and anycast), designing cache keys and caching headers so the CDN actually caches, invalidating content correctly, protecting the origin, and knowing how CDNs fail. This lesson covers each, and connects them to system design questions such as "how would you serve a breaking-news spike?" or "how do you make an API faster for users in Asia?".

The idea: bring the server to the user

A CDN is a network of edge servers grouped into points of presence (PoPs), which are data centres in cities around the world, each connected to many local internet providers. The site's own servers are called the origin. Users connect to a nearby PoP instead of the origin, and the PoP does three things: it finishes handshakes quickly, because the round trip is short; it serves cached copies of content, so most requests never reach the origin; and it forwards what it cannot serve to the origin over efficient, long-lived connections.

The analogy: local warehouses

An online shop with one central warehouse ships every order across the country, slowly. Opening local warehouses stocked with popular items means most orders are delivered from nearby the same day; unusual items are fetched from the central warehouse, but even then the local depot has a regular truck already running to it. A CDN is that network of local warehouses for data. Where the analogy breaks: copying data is almost free, so every warehouse can hold popular items at once, and "stocking" happens automatically the first time someone nearby asks.

One request, three possible paths

What a CDN changes in your architecture

Adding a CDN inserts a layer that every request passes through, which has consequences beyond speed. Your TLS certificates live at the CDN as well as, or instead of, your load balancer. Client IP addresses reach your servers in headers (net10), not as the connection's source. Caching behaviour becomes something you design explicitly with headers and rules, because the CDN will cache whatever you allow. Your origin should stop accepting traffic from anywhere else. And your status page needs to account for the CDN as a dependency that can fail independently of you. None of this is hard, but each is a classic interview follow-up to "we'll put a CDN in front".

How big is the effect?

For a static asset with a 95% hit ratio, 95 of every 100 requests never touch your servers, and those 95 are answered from a few milliseconds away. For a busy site that is the difference between needing a handful of origin servers and needing a hundred. For dynamic pages that cannot be cached, the gain is the handshakes: a few hundred milliseconds per new connection for distant users, which in e-commerce studies translates directly into conversion rates.

Four questions the rest of the lesson answers

  • Routing: how does each user reach a nearby PoP? (DNS steering or anycast.)
  • Caching: which responses may the edge store, for how long, and under what key?
  • Invalidation: how do you change content that is cached in hundreds of places?
  • Everything else: speeding up uncacheable requests, protecting the origin, security and compute at the edge, and what happens when the CDN itself fails.

Keep the warehouse picture in mind: routing is choosing the nearest warehouse, caching is deciding what to stock, and invalidation is recalling a product that changed.

1. Getting users to the right edge: DNS steering

The first method uses DNS (net6). The site's hostname is a CNAME to a name on the CDN's DNS, such as www.example.com → example.cdn-provider.net. When a resolver asks the CDN's authoritative servers for that name, they choose a PoP for this particular query, taking into account where the query came from, which PoPs are healthy, and how loaded they are, and answer with that PoP's address and a short TTL (often 20 to 60 seconds).

The weakness is in "where the query came from". The authoritative server sees the resolver's address, not the user's. A user in Chennai using their ISP's resolver in Chennai is mapped well; a user in Chennai using a public resolver instance in Singapore may be sent to Singapore. The EDNS Client Subnet extension lets resolvers pass a truncated form of the client's address to fix this, at some cost to privacy and cache efficiency. DNS steering also reacts to failures only as fast as TTLs expire.

2. Getting users to the right edge: anycast

The second method uses routing itself. In anycast, every PoP announces the same IP prefix, say 203.0.113.0/24, to the networks it connects to, using BGP (net3). Each network's routers then see several paths to that prefix and pick one by their normal rules, which usually means the nearest PoP in network terms. A user in Chennai and a user in Frankfurt both connect to 203.0.113.10, and each reaches a different machine.

Anycast has three big advantages: it needs no DNS tricks (the same address works everywhere, which is why public DNS resolvers like 1.1.1.1 and 8.8.8.8 use it), failover is as fast as a route withdrawal, and denial-of-service traffic is naturally divided across the whole network. Its main caveat is that routing decisions belong to other networks, whose policies sometimes send users to a surprisingly distant PoP, and a route change in the middle of a TCP connection could, in principle, deliver its packets to a different PoP that has no record of it. In practice routes are stable over connection lifetimes, and CDNs use connection-forwarding techniques to cope when they are not. QUIC's connection IDs (net5) make it more robust still.

3. What gets cached: freshness and headers

An edge caches a response according to HTTP's caching rules (net7), usually with some CDN-specific overrides. The key directives:

  • Cache-Control: public, max-age=60, s-maxage=600: browsers may reuse it for 60 seconds, shared caches such as the CDN for 600.
  • CDN-Cache-Control (RFC 9213), or a vendor-specific header like Surrogate-Control: rules for the CDN only, which the CDN strips before responding, letting you cache for hours at the edge but only seconds in browsers, so purges take effect for users quickly.
  • private or no-store: never stored by the CDN. Every personalised or authenticated response should say so explicitly.
  • stale-while-revalidate=30: after expiry, serve the stale copy for up to 30 seconds while fetching a new one in the background, so users never wait for revalidation.
  • stale-if-error=86400: if the origin returns errors or is unreachable, keep serving the last good copy for up to a day. This turns an origin outage into slightly old content instead of an error page.

Most CDNs also let you set rules in their configuration, such as "cache everything under /static/ for a day regardless of headers". Such rules are powerful and dangerous: they are how private pages end up cached (the Steam incident in net7).

4. The cache key

The cache key decides which requests share a stored response. By default it is typically the scheme, host, path and full query string, plus any request headers named in the response's Vary header. Everything that varies the key without varying the content lowers the hit ratio, the fraction of requests served from cache.

A worked example. A product page is requested 100,000 times an hour. Marketing emails add ?utm_source=newsletter&utm_campaign=sale_17&utm_medium=email with per-campaign values; ads add ?gclid=... with a unique value per click. If the cache key includes the full query string, nearly every ad click is a new key and a guaranteed miss, and the hit ratio for that page might fall below 50%. Stripping tracking parameters from the key (the origin still receives them if needed) can bring it above 95%. The same logic applies to headers: Vary: User-Agent creates a separate copy for each of thousands of browser versions; Vary: Cookie creates one per user, which is effectively no caching. If the page genuinely differs between mobile and desktop, have the edge compute a normalised device class and vary on that instead.

Personalisation is the other enemy of caching. The standard pattern is to cache the page shell identically for everyone and fetch personal parts, such as the cart count or a greeting, from a small uncached API call in the browser, or to assemble them at the edge.

5. Invalidation

"There are only two hard things in computer science: cache invalidation and naming things," goes the old joke, and CDNs are where it bites hardest. The options, from bluntest to finest:

  • Wait for TTLs to expire. Fine for content where minutes of staleness are acceptable.
  • Purge by URL or prefix. Tell the CDN to drop specific URLs, or everything under /products/. Propagates to all PoPs, typically within seconds.
  • Purge by surrogate key (cache tag). The origin labels each response with the entities it contains, for example Surrogate-Key: product-42 category-kettles. When product 42's price changes, one API call purges every cached page carrying product-42, whatever its URL. This lets pages be cached for hours yet updated within seconds of an edit.
  • Versioned URLs. For static assets, put a content hash in the file name (app.3f9a1.js) and cache it for a year. A change produces a new URL, so nothing needs purging, and browsers and every other cache update automatically.

Remember what a purge cannot do: it clears the CDN, not browsers or other caches downstream that obeyed max-age. That is why browser TTLs on mutable content are kept short even when CDN TTLs are long.

A common production workflow combines these. When an editor updates product 42, the application writes to the database, then publishes an event; a small worker consumes it and calls the CDN's purge-by-tag API for product-42. Pages keep long CDN TTLs, browsers get short ones, and the next request for any affected page fetches fresh content from the origin through the shield. If the purge call fails, the worker retries, and the TTL is the upper bound on how stale anything can get.

6. Tiered caching, origin shields and coalescing

A CDN with 300 PoPs has 300 separate caches. When a new article is published, each PoP misses the first time one of its users asks, and without further help that is 300 requests to the origin for one object, and for rarely requested objects, each PoP's cache may never warm up. Tiered caching fixes this by sending edge misses to a mid-tier cache, often a single designated origin shield near the origin, instead of the origin itself:

The cost is an extra hop on misses, and the benefit is dramatic origin protection. For hot dynamic endpoints, a related trick is micro-caching: cache an API response for just one to five seconds. Data is at most a few seconds old, but ten thousand requests per second become one origin request per second.

7. A request through the edge, step by step

Putting the pieces together for GET https://www.shop.example/products/42?utm_source=mail from a user in Chennai. DNS resolves www.shop.example through a CNAME to the CDN, which answers with its anycast address. The user's packets reach the Chennai PoP; an L4 balancer inside picks a server. TCP and TLS complete in about 10 ms, using the certificate the CDN holds for the site. The edge normalises the cache key, dropping utm_source, and looks up www.shop.example/products/42. On a hit it responds immediately, adding age and a cache status header. On a miss it asks the shield; the shield, on its own miss, fetches from the origin over a warm connection, stores the response according to s-maxage, and returns it; the edge stores it too and responds. Along the way, WAF rules and rate limits ran before any of this, and an edge function may have added a header or rewritten the URL. Every step is a place where configuration decides the outcome, which is why CDN configuration deserves the same review as code.

8. Beyond caching: dynamic acceleration, security and compute

Dynamic acceleration. For uncacheable requests, the edge still helps: TCP and TLS handshakes complete over a short round trip to the edge, and the edge forwards the request to the origin over persistent, already warmed-up connections (no handshakes, no slow start), often across the CDN's own well-provisioned backbone rather than the public internet's default routes. For a distant user this saves several hundred milliseconds per new connection.

Security. Sitting in front of the origin with huge aggregate capacity, CDNs absorb volumetric DDoS attacks, run web application firewalls, detect bots and enforce rate limits (net14). None of that helps if attackers can reach the origin directly, so the origin must accept only CDN traffic: an allow-list of the CDN's address ranges, authenticated origin pulls where the CDN presents a client certificate (net9), or a private tunnel from the origin to the CDN so the origin has no public inbound ports at all.

Edge compute. Platforms such as Cloudflare Workers, Fastly Compute and CloudFront Functions or Lambda@Edge run your code at the PoPs: redirects and rewrites, authentication token checks, A/B test assignment, header manipulation, image resizing, personalisation of cached pages. The limits are tight CPU and memory budgets, and the fact that your primary data usually lives in one region; logic that needs the database on every request still pays the long round trip. Edge key-value stores with eventual consistency fill part of that gap.

Video. Streaming video is the largest single use of CDNs. Content is encoded at several qualities and split into segments of a few seconds (HLS or DASH), fetched over HTTP by players that switch quality as bandwidth changes. Segments are ideal CDN objects, and the biggest video services go further and place cache appliances inside ISPs' own networks (Netflix's Open Connect), so popular titles never cross the wider internet.

Cache key design as an architecture decision

Every dimension you add to the cache key multiplies the number of cached variants: languages times device classes times A/B test buckets times currencies can turn one page into hundreds of copies, each with its own hit ratio. Senior engineers treat the key as a design artefact. They normalise inputs at the edge (collapse device types into two or three classes, strip or sort query parameters, lowercase), move variation into the URL where it is meaningful (/fr/ rather than Accept-Language), push personalisation to client-side calls or edge assembly, and measure hit ratio per route to catch regressions. A useful habit is to ask, for every response header and query parameter, "does this change the bytes of the response?" If not, it does not belong in the key.

Protecting the origin from the CDN itself

A CDN normally shields the origin, but several events can turn it into a firehose pointed at it: a full purge after a deploy, a cache key change that invalidates everything implicitly, a new PoP coming online cold, or a flood of requests for objects that do not exist (which are never cached unless you cache 404s briefly). Defences are layered: origin shields and request coalescing, stale-while-revalidate so expiry never blocks users, stale-if-error so origin errors are hidden, short negative caching of 404s, purging by tag rather than everything, and rate limits or concurrency limits on origin fetches so that a surge degrades to slightly stale content rather than an origin collapse.

How anycast CDNs handle load and failure inside a PoP

Anycast gets a packet to a PoP, but a PoP contains many servers. Inside, L4 load balancers spread connections across machines using consistent hashing and ECMP (net10), and machines that fail or are overloaded stop receiving new connections. When a whole PoP is overloaded, the CDN can withdraw or de-prioritise some of its announcements (for example with BGP path prepending, which makes the route look longer) so neighbouring networks shift some users to other PoPs. The same machinery is used during DDoS attacks: attack traffic lands on the PoPs nearest its sources, where it is filtered, while other PoPs continue serving normally. Cloudflare's June 2022 outage shows the flip side: a routing policy change on the spine routers of 19 large data centres, just 4% of its locations, stopped them advertising correctly and affected about half of all requests, because those were its busiest PoPs.

Edge compute consistency and state

Edge functions are stateless per request, so any state lives in platform services: key-value stores replicated to every PoP with eventual consistency (writes can take seconds or more to reach everywhere), durable per-object coordination services pinned to one location, or ordinary origin databases. Designs that work well at the edge read mostly-static data (feature flags, routing tables, public keys for token verification) and write rarely. Designs that read and write user data on every request usually belong in a regional backend, possibly with regional replicas, not at the edge. The honest interview answer is that edge compute moves computation close to users, but data gravity still decides where the work happens.

Multi-CDN and steering

Very large properties use two or more CDNs for resilience and performance, steering users between them with DNS based on real-user measurements and health checks. The operational burden is significant: cache rules, WAF policies, certificates, logging formats, purge APIs and edge logic must be kept consistent across vendors, often through an abstraction layer or configuration generated from one source. Traffic splitting also lowers each CDN's hit ratio. The payoff is that the June 2021 Fastly or June 2022 Cloudflare kind of outage becomes a traffic shift rather than a site outage.

Costs

CDN pricing typically combines bytes delivered (with regional differences), requests, and add-ons such as WAF, bot management and edge compute. Cloud egress from the origin to the CDN is often a large hidden cost, which origin shields and high hit ratios reduce. Very high request rates of tiny objects can make request fees dominate. For AI applications that stream large model outputs or serve model weights and datasets, egress becomes the main line item, and caching or placing artefacts close to consumers is as much a cost decision as a performance one.

Characteristic failures

  • Private data cached publicly: an emergency or misapplied cache rule stores personalised responses (net7's Steam example). Every personalised response must carry private or no-store, and the CDN must respect origin headers.
  • Low hit ratio: origin overload on busy days because tracking parameters, Vary headers or cookies fragment the cache. Watch hit ratio per route.
  • Cached errors: a brief origin failure produces 500 or 404 responses that the CDN caches, prolonging the outage. Configure short or zero TTLs for error responses, and use stale-if-error.
  • Stale after deploy: HTML cached too long references removed assets, or purges miss some URLs. Use versioned assets, short HTML TTLs and tag-based purges.
  • Origin bypass: attackers find the origin's IP and send traffic around the CDN's protections. Lock the origin down.
  • CDN-wide incidents: configuration pushes and software bugs at the provider take down many sites at once, as in the Fastly outage of 8 June 2021, where a customer's valid configuration change triggered a latent bug and about 85% of Fastly's network returned errors for up to 49 minutes, and Cloudflare's outage of 21 June 2022, where a network configuration change in 19 data centres affected half of its requests for about 75 minutes.

Observability

CDNs report cache status per request in response headers, such as cf-cache-status: HIT, x-cache: MISS, HIT (one value per tier) and age, and identify the serving PoP (cf-ray ends with a location code; Fastly's x-served-by lists the shield and edge nodes). Their analytics give hit ratio by path, status codes at edge versus origin, bandwidth and top URLs. Real-user monitoring by country shows whether users actually get nearby, fast responses; synthetic checks from several regions catch regional routing problems. On the origin side, watch request rate from the CDN: a sudden rise means something has stopped caching.

A debugging order for "the site is slow in one country"

Check which PoP those users reach (a trace endpoint or the PoP code in response headers, from a probe in that country); whether responses there are hits or misses; the edge's time to first byte versus the origin's; and whether the users' DNS resolvers or networks route them unexpectedly far (a resolver without Client Subnet support, or an ISP with poor peering to the CDN). The fix may be a cache configuration change, an origin shield closer to that region, or a support ticket with the CDN about routing.

Planning for the CDN itself failing

Decide in advance what happens when the CDN has a bad hour. Options range from accepting the risk (common, and reasonable for many sites), to a documented bypass that points DNS at origin load balancers sized to take the load directly, to a second CDN kept warm with a share of traffic. Whatever the choice, keep DNS TTLs on the switchable records short, practise the switch, and make sure the origin can actually serve without the CDN's caching and protection before you need it.

Knobs

Cache rules per path; cache key normalisation (query parameters, headers, cookies); TTLs for edge and browser separately; stale-while-revalidate and stale-if-error; error caching TTLs; tiered caching and shield location; request coalescing; purge strategy and surrogate keys; origin protection (allow-lists, authenticated pulls, tunnels); WAF and rate-limit rules; and edge function deployment and rollback.

Fifteen minutes with curl and dig. You will find the PoP you reach, read cache headers from several tiers, watch a miss become a hit, and see anycast and DNS steering from your own machine. On Windows PowerShell use curl.exe.

  1. Which PoP am I using? curl -s https://www.cloudflare.com/cdn-cgi/trace. Look for: colo= followed by an airport code such as BOM (Mumbai) or MAA (Chennai): the anycast PoP your packets reached. Also note http= and tls=, the protocol versions negotiated with that edge.
  2. Anycast from your machine. ping -c 3 1.1.1.1 and traceroute -n 1.1.1.1 (tracert -d on Windows). Look for: a round-trip time of a few milliseconds and a short path: the same address that users on other continents reach just as quickly, served from a location near each of them.
  3. Read the tiers. curl -sI https://www.fastly.com | grep -iE 'x-served-by|x-cache|age'. Look for: x-served-by listing more than one cache node (the shield and the edge nearest you, identified by airport codes), and x-cache with one HIT or MISS per tier, such as MISS, HIT: the edge missed and the shield had it.
  4. Watch a miss become a hit. Pick a static asset URL from a CDN-hosted site (view page source and copy a .js or .css link). Request it with a random query string so it is surely uncached: curl -sI "URL?lab=$RANDOM" | grep -iE 'cache|age', then run the same command again with the same query string. Look for: MISS then HIT, and an age header appearing: the second request was served from the edge. (Some CDNs ignore unknown query parameters in the key, in which case you get a HIT immediately, which itself shows cache key normalisation.)
  5. Time it. For the same URL, run curl -s -o /dev/null -w 'ttfb %{time_starttransfer}\n' URL a few times. Look for: a low, stable TTFB on hits; compare with a fresh random query string, whose TTFB includes the trip to the shield or origin.
  6. DNS steering. dig +short www.netflix.com @1.1.1.1 and dig +short www.netflix.com @8.8.8.8, or compare with a friend in another city. Look for: CNAME chains ending in CDN hostnames, and different addresses for different resolvers or locations: DNS-based steering at work.
Improve the hit ratio

An e-commerce site's CDN hit ratio is 35%, and the origin is struggling on sale days. Findings: product pages include ?utm_source=…&utm_campaign=… from marketing emails; responses carry Vary: User-Agent, Cookie; product pages have Cache-Control: no-cache because "prices change"; each of 250 PoPs fetches directly from the origin. Propose changes.

Answer
  • Normalise the cache key: strip utm_* and other tracking parameters (and sort the rest) at the edge; every email variant currently creates a separate cache entry.
  • Fix Vary: Vary: User-Agent creates thousands of variants; if mobile and desktop differ, vary on a normalised device-class header the edge computes. Remove Vary: Cookie for public pages; serve the page shell identically to everyone and fetch personalised parts (cart, greeting) client-side from an uncached API.
  • Cache product pages briefly instead of no-cache: s-maxage=60, stale-while-revalidate=30, plus surrogate-key purges (product-42) when a price changes, so updates are immediate while traffic is absorbed. Checkout verifies the price server-side anyway.
  • Enable tiered caching / an origin shield so 250 PoPs share a mid-tier cache and misses collapse into one origin request per object; turn on request coalescing.
  • Expected result: hit ratio into the 90s for product traffic; origin load drops by an order of magnitude on sale days. Verify with the CDN's cache status headers and analytics.
Estimate it

A breaking-news article gets 200,000 requests per second worldwide for ten minutes. The CDN has 300 PoPs. Compare origin load with (a) no caching of the article, (b) edge caching with s-maxage=30 and no shield, (c) the same plus an origin shield and request coalescing.

Answer

(a) No caching: all 200,000 requests per second reach the origin, which almost certainly falls over.

(b) Edge caching, 30-second TTL, no shield: each PoP fetches the article at most once per 30 seconds, but without coalescing, simultaneous misses at a PoP when its copy expires may send several requests each. Roughly 300 PoPs / 30 s = 10 origin requests per second, with bursts of perhaps a few hundred at each expiry across PoPs. Manageable, a reduction of about 20,000 times.

(c) With a shield and coalescing: edges ask the shield, and the shield fetches from the origin once per TTL: about one origin request every 30 seconds for the article. Add stale-while-revalidate and even that refresh happens in the background without users waiting. The origin barely notices the spike; the remaining cost is edits, which a surrogate-key purge distributes in seconds.
