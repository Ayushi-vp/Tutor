Every earlier lesson in this track described how to make traffic flow. This one is about stopping the traffic that should not. The two goals pull against each other: every open port, route, peering link and API is a convenience for your systems and an opportunity for an attacker. Network security is the discipline of keeping the first while shrinking the second.

The stakes are concrete. In 2019 an attacker tricked a misconfigured firewall at Capital One into fetching credentials from the cloud's internal metadata service and used them to copy data on more than 100 million people. Databases left reachable from the internet are found by automated scanners within minutes of being exposed. Denial-of-service attacks now peak in the tens of terabits per second, far more than any single company's links could carry. And security controls themselves can cause outages: a single bad firewall rule at Cloudflare in 2019 consumed nearly all the CPU serving HTTP traffic across its network and dropped about 80% of its traffic for 27 minutes.

For backend, platform and AI engineers, network security shows up in design reviews and interviews as a set of recurring questions. Which subnets should this service live in? Who can reach the database, and how is that expressed? How do engineers get into production? What stops a compromised service from sending data anywhere? What happens when someone floods the login endpoint? What does "zero trust" actually mean, beyond the marketing? Each has a principled answer.

This lesson starts from threat modelling and the idea of defence in depth, then works through the controls layer by layer: firewalls and cloud security groups, segmentation and least privilege, administrative access, egress control and server-side request forgery, web application firewalls, denial-of-service attacks and rate limiting, and finally the shift from perimeter security to zero trust. The production section covers how these fail and how teams keep secure designs secure as systems change.

Start with a threat model

Security decisions only make sense against a threat model: what are you protecting (customer data, credentials, availability), from whom (opportunistic internet scanners, targeted criminals, a malicious insider, a compromised dependency), and by which paths could they get in? You will never block everything, so the goal is to make the likely attacks hard, make the damage of a successful one small, and make attacks visible.

A useful default assumption is assume breach: design as if some component will be compromised, and ask how far an attacker could go from there. That question, "what is the blast radius?", drives most of the controls in this lesson.

The analogy: a well-run office building, and where it fails

A traditional office protects itself with a perimeter: a front door with a guard, and once you are inside with a badge, you can walk into most rooms. That is perimeter security, and it has a known failure: anyone who gets past the door, by tailgating or with a stolen badge, can wander everywhere. A better-run building also locks the server room, the finance office and the records room separately, checks badges at each, logs who entered, and gives each person access only to the rooms they need. That is defence in depth and least privilege. The most modern version, zero trust, stops treating "inside the building" as meaningful at all: every door checks who you are, whether your device is healthy and whether you should be there right now.

Where the analogy breaks: in networks, attackers can try millions of doors per second from anywhere in the world, automatically, so defaults matter far more than in a building: anything left open will be found.

Three kinds of attacker you plan for

It helps to name the adversaries, because they behave differently. Opportunistic scanners sweep the entire IPv4 internet continuously looking for open ports, default passwords and known vulnerabilities; they find anything exposed within minutes and are defeated mostly by not exposing things. Targeted attackers study your organisation, phish your staff and chain small weaknesses together; they are slowed by defence in depth and caught by detection. Denial-of-service attackers do not want in at all; they want you offline, and are handled by capacity, filtering and design. A fourth, increasingly important category is compromised dependencies: a library or vendor you trust that turns malicious, which is why egress control and least privilege matter even for code you did not write.

The layers

The rest of the lesson works from the outside in, with one theme throughout: express access by identity (which service, which person) wherever possible, rather than by IP address, because identities are stable and meaningful while addresses change constantly in cloud and container environments.

1. Firewalls: stateless and stateful

A firewall allows or blocks traffic according to rules about source and destination addresses, ports and protocol. The important distinction is state. A stateless firewall judges every packet on its own. To let a server make an outbound HTTPS call, it needs a rule allowing packets out to port 443 and a rule allowing the replies back in, which arrive at the client's ephemeral port (1024 to 65535), so stateless rules end up broader than intended. A stateful firewall tracks connections: once it has allowed an outbound connection, it automatically allows that connection's replies and nothing else. Almost all modern firewalls, including cloud security groups, are stateful; network ACLs are the main stateless exception.

Two principles govern rule design. Default deny: block everything, then allow what is needed, rather than allowing everything and blocking known-bad. And least privilege: each allowed path should be as narrow as possible in source, destination and port.

2. Cloud building blocks

In a cloud VPC (net3), the controls map onto these ideas:

  • Public subnets have a route to the internet gateway. Only components that must be reached from the internet belong there: load balancers, NAT gateways, perhaps a CDN-facing proxy.
  • Private subnets hold application servers and databases. They have no inbound route from the internet at all; outbound traffic goes through a NAT gateway (or nowhere).
  • Security groups are stateful, allow-only firewalls attached to each instance or network interface. Their most useful feature is that a rule can reference another security group instead of an IP range: "the database security group allows port 5432 from the app security group". Any instance in the app group can connect, however many there are and whatever their addresses, and nothing else can.
  • Network ACLs are stateless, ordered allow and deny rules on whole subnets. Because they are stateless, allowing outbound calls requires allowing inbound replies on ephemeral ports. Teams use them as a coarse second layer, for example to deny a known-bad address range across a subnet.
  • Private connectivity: VPC endpoints (or PrivateLink) let workloads reach cloud services such as object storage, or a partner's service, without crossing the internet; peering and transit gateways connect VPCs privately.

3. Segmentation and least privilege

Segmentation means dividing systems into zones with only the necessary paths between them, so that one compromise does not grant access to everything (limiting lateral movement). Common boundaries: production separate from staging and development (ideally separate accounts, not just subnets), tiers separated (edge, application, data), and sensitive systems such as payments or personal data isolated further. Within Kubernetes, pods can talk to any other pod by default; a NetworkPolicy in each namespace that denies everything by default, followed by specific allows selected by pod labels ("pods labelled app=api may receive from pods labelled app=frontend on port 8080"), brings the same discipline inside the cluster. Policies only work if the cluster's network plugin enforces them, such as Calico or Cilium.

The trend is toward microsegmentation by identity rather than by subnet: rules that say "the checkout service may call the payments service" enforced by security-group references, NetworkPolicy labels, or a service mesh's mTLS identities (net9). Identity-based rules keep working as workloads move and scale, where IP-based rules rot.

4. Administrative access

How engineers reach production is one of the most attacked paths. The anti-patterns are SSH or database ports open to 0.0.0.0/0 (scanned and attacked within minutes), IP allow-lists for offices or home connections (brittle, and they authenticate a location, not a person), and shared keys or passwords (no accountability, impossible to revoke for one person). Modern practice: no inbound administrative ports at all. Engineers authenticate with single sign-on and multi-factor authentication to a session manager (such as AWS Systems Manager Session Manager or Google's Identity-Aware Proxy for TCP) that opens an audited session to a specific instance, or port-forwards to a database, using the engineer's own identity and short-lived credentials. Access to production data can require just-in-time approval, and every session is logged.

A useful test for any access path is to ask three questions: who can use it (a named person, a service identity, or anyone who can reach an address), how that is verified (strong authentication or network location), and whether its use is recorded. Paths that fail all three, such as a shared key to an open port, are the ones attackers find first.

5. Egress control and SSRF

Teams guard inbound traffic carefully and often leave outbound wide open. Yet most attacks need to reach out: malware downloading tools or contacting its command server, stolen data being sent away (exfiltration), or a server being tricked into making requests. Egress control restricts which destinations workloads may reach: an egress proxy or firewall with an allow-list of required external domains (net10), DNS filtering, and private endpoints for cloud services so that most traffic never needs the internet.

SSRF (server-side request forgery) is the network attack most specific to cloud applications. Any feature that fetches a URL supplied by a user, such as a webhook, an image importer, a "fetch this link preview" or an LLM tool that browses, can be pointed at internal addresses. The most valuable target is the cloud instance metadata service at 169.254.169.254, which hands out temporary credentials for the instance's role to anything on the instance that asks. That is how the Capital One breach worked in 2019: an attacker abused a misconfigured web application firewall to make requests to the metadata service, obtained credentials for its role, which could read far more storage buckets than it needed, and copied the data. Defences, in layers: validate and restrict outbound URLs in code (resolve the hostname and reject private, loopback and link-local addresses, and re-check after redirects); route user-directed fetches through an egress proxy that enforces the same rules; enforce IMDSv2, which requires a session token obtained with a PUT request carrying a special header, something a simple SSRF cannot do; and give every role the least privilege it needs, so stolen credentials are worth little.

6. Web application firewalls

A WAF is a layer-7 filter, usually at the CDN or load balancer, that inspects HTTP requests. It applies managed rule sets that detect common attack patterns, such as SQL injection strings and script injection, often organised around the OWASP Top 10 list of common web vulnerabilities; custom rules (block this path, require this header); rate limits; and bot management. Its strengths are speed and central control: when a new vulnerability is announced, a WAF rule can block exploitation across all applications within minutes, a virtual patch while code is fixed. Its limits are equally important: attackers find encodings and variants that rules miss, rules produce false positives that block real users, business-logic abuse (such as scraping prices or trying stolen passwords slowly) looks like normal traffic, and a WAF is software on the request path that can fail or be misconfigured, as both the Capital One breach and Cloudflare's 2019 outage show. New rules should run in count-only mode first and be rolled out in stages.

7. Denial of service, by layer

A DDoS attack tries to make a service unavailable by overwhelming some resource. Attacks fall into three families, each needing a different defence:

Volumetric attacks have grown enormously: Cloudflare reported successive records through 2025, ending with a 31.4 Tbps attack lasting just 35 seconds. No company's own internet links can absorb that; the only defence is a provider whose anycast network spreads the attack across hundreds of locations and filters it there (net12), with an origin that cannot be reached any other way. Application-layer attacks are the opposite: modest bandwidth, requests that look legitimate, aimed at your most expensive operations. They are defended in your own design: rate limiting, commonly with a token bucket per client or key (a bucket holds up to B tokens, refills at R per second, and each request spends one, so short bursts are allowed but the average is capped); caching so repeated requests are cheap; requiring authentication or a challenge for expensive endpoints; and autoscaling with limits, so an attack costs money but does not melt shared dependencies such as the database.

8. Encryption in transit, inside the network too

Older designs encrypted traffic from users to the edge and left internal traffic in plaintext, on the theory that the internal network was trusted. That assumption fails in shared cloud networks, where you do not control the physical path, and in any breach, where an attacker inside can read internal traffic, including credentials and tokens. The modern default is to encrypt everything: TLS to load balancers and re-encryption to backends (net9), TLS to databases and caches, and mTLS between services, usually automated by a service mesh so application teams do not manage certificates by hand. Encryption also carries identity, which is what makes the next idea possible.

9. From perimeter to zero trust

The traditional model trusts the internal network: anything on the corporate LAN or VPN is inside, and internal apps often accept any request from an internal address. That fails badly when an attacker gets one foothold, a phished laptop or a vulnerable server, because the inside is flat and trusting. Google's BeyondCorp work, published from 2014, described the alternative that became known as zero trust, later formalised by NIST in SP 800-207.

In practice zero trust means: staff reach internal applications through identity-aware proxies with single sign-on, multi-factor authentication and device posture checks, rather than a VPN that grants broad network access; services authenticate each other with mTLS and short-lived certificates and enforce authorisation policies per call (net9); credentials are short-lived and scoped; and every access is logged and evaluated continuously. Firewalls and segmentation remain, but as noise reduction and blast-radius limits, not as the thing that grants trust. VPNs still have roles, such as site-to-site links between networks.

Identity beats addresses

IP-based rules were natural when servers had fixed addresses for years. In clouds and Kubernetes, addresses are assigned and reassigned every few minutes, the same address may belong to a different workload tomorrow, and NAT hides the true source (net3). Rules written as IP ranges therefore drift towards being either too broad (a whole /16 allowed "to be safe") or broken. The durable alternatives all attach policy to identity: security-group references and tags in the cloud, pod labels and service accounts in Kubernetes NetworkPolicies, SPIFFE identities in mTLS certificates for a service mesh, and IAM roles for access to managed services. A senior design review question is always "what identity is this rule really about?"

Egress as a control point

Egress control is one of the highest-value, least-implemented controls. A default-deny egress posture, where each service declares the external domains it needs and everything else is blocked and logged, defeats a large class of attacks at once: malware cannot fetch payloads or reach its command server, exfiltration must go through a monitored path, and SSRF cannot reach arbitrary destinations. The implementation challenges are operational: domains resolve to changing IPs (so rules need an egress proxy or DNS-aware firewall, not IP lists), dependencies change, and developers need a fast, self-service way to request new destinations. DNS query logs are a cheap first step even before enforcement, because unusual lookups are an early indicator of compromise and of DNS-based exfiltration (net6).

Detection: assume the controls will fail

Preventive controls reduce risk; they never remove it. Mature teams therefore invest in detection: VPC flow logs to see which connections were accepted and rejected (a burst of rejected connections from inside is often an attacker scanning), DNS logs, load balancer and WAF logs, cloud control-plane audit logs (who changed a security group, who created an access key), and managed threat detection services that correlate these. What matters is triage: alerts routed to someone who can act, with runbooks, and regular exercises. The combination of "reduce blast radius" and "see it quickly" is what keeps a compromise small.

Policy as code

Most network exposures are self-inflicted: a temporary rule allowing 0.0.0.0/0 on port 22 that nobody removed, a storage bucket made public for a demo, a peering link added without review. Managing network configuration as infrastructure as code (Terraform, CloudFormation, Kubernetes manifests) makes every change reviewable, and policy as code tools (such as Open Policy Agent rules, cloud configuration rules or scanners in CI) automatically reject changes that violate standards, such as admin ports open to the world, databases in public subnets, or security groups without owners. Continuous scanning of the running environment catches drift from changes made outside the pipeline.

DDoS readiness as an engineering practice

DDoS resilience is decided before the attack. Know what your provider absorbs automatically and what needs configuration; make sure the origin cannot be reached except through the protected edge (net12); identify your most expensive endpoints and give them tighter rate limits, caching or authentication; prepare switches you can flip under attack, such as a stricter challenge mode, turning off costly features, or serving a static page; and practise them. Application-layer attacks often cause collateral damage through shared dependencies, so isolating the database and critical paths from expensive, attackable features matters as much as edge filtering.

Shared responsibility in the cloud

Cloud providers secure the physical network, the hypervisors and the managed services' internals; customers secure what they configure: which ports are open, which subnets are public, who holds which role, whether data is encrypted and which destinations workloads can reach. Nearly every well-known cloud breach has been on the customer side of that line, through misconfiguration rather than a provider flaw. That is why configuration review, policy as code and least-privilege roles carry so much weight in cloud security, and why "the cloud provider handles security" is a dangerous misunderstanding.

Security controls as availability risks

Firewalls, WAFs and proxies are inline on every request, so they are also availability risks. Cloudflare's July 2019 incident is the textbook case: a WAF rule with a regular expression prone to catastrophic backtracking was deployed globally at once and drove CPU on HTTP-serving machines to nearly 100%, causing about 80% traffic loss for 27 minutes; recovery was slowed because the team's internal tools also depended on the affected systems. The fixes Cloudflare described apply broadly: regular-expression engines with linear-time guarantees, per-rule CPU budgets, performance tests in CI, staged rollouts even for urgent security changes with the ability to accelerate them deliberately, and kill switches that work when everything else is broken.

Characteristic failures

  • Exposed management ports and databases: a security group with 0.0.0.0/0 on 22, 3389, 5432 or 6379, found by scanners within minutes. Detect with configuration scanning; prevent with policy as code.
  • Flat internal networks: a single compromised host can reach everything, turning a small incident into a breach. Segment by tier and sensitivity, default-deny inside clusters.
  • SSRF to metadata and internal services: URL-fetching features reaching 169.254.169.254 or internal admin endpoints. Enforce IMDSv2, validate destinations, route through an egress proxy.
  • Over-privileged roles: network controls hold, but a stolen credential can read everything. Least privilege for every role limits the value of any credential.
  • Origin bypass: attackers find the origin's address and go around the CDN's DDoS and WAF protections. Allow only CDN traffic at the origin.
  • Controls that break availability: a WAF rule blocking legitimate traffic or consuming CPU, a NetworkPolicy change that cuts a critical path, a firewall change that drops health checks. Stage and monitor security changes like any deploy.
  • Security groups nobody understands: hundreds of rules with no owners or descriptions; nobody dares remove any. Tag rules with owners and reasons, and review periodically.

War story: Capital One, 2019

An attacker exploited a server-side request forgery weakness in a misconfigured ModSecurity web application firewall running on an EC2 instance in Capital One's AWS environment. The firewall could be made to send requests to the instance metadata service, which returned temporary credentials for the IAM role attached to the instance. That role was allowed to list and read the contents of a large number of S3 buckets. The attacker used the credentials to download data from credit card applications affecting more than 100 million people in the US and around 6 million in Canada. Every layer contributed: an SSRF-able component, a metadata service that answered simple GET requests (AWS introduced IMDSv2 later that year), a role with far more privilege than a firewall needed, and no egress or anomaly control that stopped bulk downloads. It is the canonical example of why defence in depth, least privilege and egress control all matter.

A response checklist when you suspect a compromise

Contain first: isolate the affected instances by swapping their security groups for a quarantine group rather than deleting them, so evidence survives; revoke and rotate the credentials they could access; and block known attacker destinations at egress. Then investigate with flow logs, DNS logs and audit logs to find where the attacker went and what they touched, and only then rebuild from known-good images. Rehearsing this before an incident, including who is allowed to make which changes, is what makes the first hour calm instead of chaotic.

Observability

Flow logs (accepted and rejected), DNS query logs, WAF logs with rule hits, rate-limit counters, load balancer logs, and cloud audit logs of configuration changes. Dashboards of top rejected sources, unusual egress destinations and spikes in 4xx or 429 responses surface attacks and misconfigurations. Regular external scans of your own public address ranges show what the internet can see.

Knobs

Security group and NetworkPolicy rules; network ACLs; egress allow-lists and proxy rules; IMDSv2 enforcement and hop limit; WAF managed rule sets, custom rules and their modes (count or block); rate limits per endpoint and key; DDoS protection levels and challenge modes; session manager and identity-aware proxy policies; and the policy-as-code rules that gate every infrastructure change.

Twenty minutes. You will audit what your own machine exposes, implement and flood a token-bucket rate limiter, and read a default-deny Kubernetes policy.

  1. What am I exposing? List listening sockets: ss -tlnp (Linux/WSL), lsof -iTCP -sTCP:LISTEN -n -P (macOS) or netstat -ano | findstr LISTENING (Windows). Look for: which services listen on 0.0.0.0 or [::] (all interfaces, reachable from your network) versus 127.0.0.1 (this machine only). Anything on all interfaces that you do not need is attack surface. If you have nmap, nmap -sT -p 1-10000 127.0.0.1 shows the same from the outside view.
  2. A token bucket. Save as bucket.py and run it: 
    import time
class TokenBucket:
    def __init__(self, rate, burst):
        self.rate, self.burst = rate, burst          # tokens/second, bucket size
        self.tokens, self.last = burst, time.monotonic()
    def allow(self):
        now = time.monotonic()
        self.tokens = min(self.burst, self.tokens + (now - self.last) * self.rate)
        self.last = now
        if self.tokens >= 1:
            self.tokens -= 1
            return True
        return False

b = TokenBucket(rate=5, burst=10)          # 5 requests/s on average, bursts of 10
t0 = time.monotonic(); allowed = denied = 0
while time.monotonic() - t0 < 3:           # an attacker firing as fast as possible
    if b.allow(): allowed += 1
    else: denied += 1
print(f"allowed {allowed}, denied (would be 429) {denied}")
    Look for: about 25 allowed in 3 seconds (the 10-token burst plus 5 per second) and a huge number denied. Change burst to 1 and see bursts disappear; this is the trade-off between tolerating legitimate spikes and stopping floods. Real gateways keep one bucket per key or client, often in Redis so all instances share it.
  3. Read a real policy. This Kubernetes NetworkPolicy set denies all traffic in a namespace, then allows the frontend to call the API on one port: 
    apiVersion: networking.k8s.io/v1
kind: NetworkPolicy
metadata: { name: default-deny, namespace: shop }
spec:
  podSelector: {}                 # every pod in the namespace
  policyTypes: [Ingress, Egress]  # no rules listed = nothing allowed
---
apiVersion: networking.k8s.io/v1
kind: NetworkPolicy
metadata: { name: frontend-to-api, namespace: shop }
spec:
  podSelector: { matchLabels: { app: api } }
  ingress:
  - from: [ { podSelector: { matchLabels: { app: frontend } } } ]
    ports: [ { protocol: TCP, port: 8080 } ]
    Look for: rules expressed with labels, not IP addresses, and the consequence of denying egress too: the API pods now cannot even reach DNS or the database until you add egress rules for them. If you have a local cluster with an enforcing network plugin (for example kind with Calico), apply them and test with kubectl exec and curl.
  4. Check security headers. curl -sI https://github.com | grep -iE 'strict-transport|content-security|x-frame|x-content-type'. Look for: the HSTS, CSP and related headers from net7, which are the browser-side layer of defence in depth.
  5. If you use AWS: on an EC2 instance, curl -s http://169.254.169.254/latest/meta-data/ works only if IMDSv1 is allowed. With IMDSv2 enforced, you must first get a token: TOKEN=$(curl -s -X PUT http://169.254.169.254/latest/api/token -H 'X-aws-ec2-metadata-token-ttl-seconds: 60') then pass -H "X-aws-ec2-metadata-token: $TOKEN". What it proves: a simple SSRF that can only make GET requests cannot perform the PUT step.
Review the design

A startup's AWS setup: all EC2 instances, including PostgreSQL, sit in public subnets with public IPs; the database security group allows 5432 from 0.0.0.0/0 "because the office IP changes"; SSH (22) is open to the world; developers connect with a shared SSH key; the app calls external APIs directly. Redesign it and prioritise the fixes.

Answer
  1. Immediately: remove 5432 and 22 from 0.0.0.0/0; an internet-exposed database is scanned within minutes. Rotate the database credentials and the shared SSH key; check logs for unauthorised access.
  2. Network layout: a VPC with public subnets containing only the load balancer and NAT gateways; application instances and the database in private subnets without public IPs (ideally a managed database). The database security group allows 5432 only from the application's security group.
  3. Admin access: no SSH from the internet; use SSM Session Manager (no open ports, IAM-authenticated, audited) with individual identities; database access for developers through a session manager port-forward or an identity-aware proxy, not IP allow-lists.
  4. Egress: outbound traffic via the NAT gateway, restricted where practical (egress proxy or firewall allow-list of partner APIs); VPC endpoints for S3 and other AWS services; IMDSv2 enforced.
  5. Edge: CDN and WAF in front of the load balancer with rate limiting on login and expensive endpoints, and the load balancer accepting traffic only from the CDN.
  6. Visibility and governance: flow logs, CloudTrail and threat-detection alerting, and everything in infrastructure as code with policy checks so review catches the next "temporary" open port.
Design it

Your product adds an AI agent that can fetch arbitrary web pages the user mentions and summarise them. What network risks does this create, and how do you contain them?

Answer
  • Risk: a built-in SSRF engine. A user (or a prompt injected into a fetched page) can ask the agent to fetch http://169.254.169.254/, internal admin panels, databases' HTTP interfaces or other tenants' services, and the summary leaks the response.
  • Isolation: run the fetcher as a separate service in its own subnet or sandbox with no route to internal networks, no instance role credentials (or IMDS disabled or IMDSv2 with hop limit 1), and no access to other services.
  • Egress path: all fetches go through an egress proxy that resolves hostnames itself and blocks private, loopback, link-local and metadata ranges, re-checks after every redirect, and limits ports, response sizes and timeouts.
  • Abuse limits: rate limits per user, caching of fetched pages, and blocking of known-bad destinations, so the feature cannot be used to attack third parties at scale.
  • Output handling: treat fetched content as untrusted data, not instructions, and log what was fetched for audit.
