When your laptop sends a request across a café's Wi-Fi, the packets pass through the café's router, the internet provider's equipment, and a dozen other networks before reaching the server. Any of those could be compromised, misconfigured or run by someone curious. Without protection, whoever controls one of those points can do three things: read everything, including passwords, cookies and API keys; change what you send or receive, injecting scripts into pages or altering an account number; and impersonate the server entirely, so you log in to the attacker instead.

TLS, Transport Layer Security, is the protocol that stops all three. It is the S in HTTPS, and it also secures connections to databases, message brokers, email servers, and the calls between your own microservices. It achieves this with a clever combination of cryptography and a global system of trust called PKI, the public key infrastructure, in which certificate authorities vouch for which public key belongs to which domain name.

For backend and AI engineers, TLS is more than a checkbox. Certificates expire and take systems down with them: an expired certificate inside Ericsson's software knocked out mobile data for over 30 million people in the UK and customers in ten other countries on a single day in 2018. Certificate lifetimes are being cut from over a year to 47 days by 2029, so renewal must be fully automated. Choosing where to decrypt traffic decides where plaintext exists in your system. Service meshes use mutual TLS to give every service a cryptographic identity, which is the foundation of zero-trust networking. And interviewers regularly ask you to walk through a handshake or debug a certificate error.

This lesson starts from the threat, builds the cryptographic tools one at a time in plain terms, walks through the TLS 1.3 handshake message by message, explains certificates, chains of trust and how clients validate them, covers how certificates are issued, revoked and monitored, compares places to terminate TLS, and finishes with mutual TLS. The production section is a tour of the certificate failures that cause real outages.

Three promises

TLS gives a connection three properties against anyone on the network path:

  • Confidentiality: they cannot read the data.
  • Integrity: they cannot change it without being detected.
  • Authentication: you know you are talking to the real server for that name (and, with mutual TLS, the server knows who you are).

Encryption alone is not enough. If you encrypt perfectly but to the wrong party, the attacker simply decrypts, reads and re-encrypts to the real server: a man-in-the-middle. Authentication is what makes the encryption meaningful.

The analogy: a sealed conversation with an ID check

Imagine meeting a stranger who claims to be your bank's manager. First you check their ID card, issued by a government office you trust; the card has their photo and name, and the office's seal is hard to forge. That is the certificate, issued by a certificate authority (CA) your device already trusts. But anyone could steal and show a card, so you also ask them to prove they are the person in the photo; in TLS the server proves it holds the private key matching the certificate by signing something unique to this conversation. Then the two of you agree on a secret code in a way that eavesdroppers cannot work out, even though they hear every word; that is the key exchange. From then on you talk in that code, and every sentence carries a seal that breaks if anyone alters it.

Where the analogy breaks: the "ID card" check is done by software in milliseconds, against a list of about 150 trusted root CAs built into your operating system or browser, and the "secret code" is fresh for every connection and thrown away afterwards.

Where you meet TLS as an engineer

Most of the time TLS is invisible: your HTTP client library performs the handshake and validation for you. You meet it directly in four situations. Configuring a load balancer, CDN or ingress with certificates and choosing TLS versions. Automating certificate issuance and renewal, and being paged when it fails. Debugging a client that refuses a connection with a certificate error, which is almost always one of five causes: expired, wrong name, untrusted issuer, incomplete chain, or wrong clock. And designing how services authenticate each other, where mutual TLS is the standard answer. The body covers each.

What it costs

One extra round trip for a new connection (TLS 1.3), zero with 0-RTT resumption, a small amount of CPU for the key exchange and signature, and a few kilobytes for the certificate chain. After the handshake, encryption on modern hardware is nearly free. The old argument that HTTPS is too slow for internal traffic no longer holds; the real costs are operational: managing keys and certificates.

If you stop here, keep this model: the certificate proves the server's identity, a fresh key exchange creates the secret, and fast symmetric encryption with tamper detection protects everything after.

1. The cryptographic tools, one at a time

Symmetric encryption uses one secret key for both encrypting and decrypting. It is extremely fast (modern CPUs have AES instructions and encrypt gigabytes per second per core), so it protects all the actual data. TLS 1.3 only allows authenticated encryption (AEAD) ciphers, AES-GCM and ChaCha20-Poly1305, which produce ciphertext plus a tag; if a single bit is altered, decryption fails. That gives integrity as well as confidentiality. The problem symmetric encryption cannot solve is how two strangers get the same secret key without anyone else learning it.

Asymmetric cryptography uses a key pair: a public key that can be shared with the world and a private key kept secret. It does two jobs in TLS. Signatures: the private key signs data, and anyone with the public key can verify the signature, proving the signer holds the private key. Key exchange: two parties can arrive at a shared secret using each other's public values.

Diffie-Hellman key exchange is the key exchange TLS uses. Each side generates a fresh key pair for this connection only, sends its public part (the key share), and combines its own private part with the other's public part. Both get the same result, but an eavesdropper who sees both public parts cannot compute it. The modern form uses elliptic curves (ECDHE; the most common curve is X25519). Because these key pairs are ephemeral, created per connection and discarded, TLS gets forward secrecy: even if the server's long-term private key is stolen next year, recordings of today's traffic cannot be decrypted, because the long-term key was only ever used to sign, never to protect the session secret.

From the Diffie-Hellman result, both sides derive a set of symmetric keys (separate ones for each direction and for the handshake versus the data) with a key derivation function. That is the whole trick: asymmetric crypto to agree and authenticate, symmetric crypto to protect the traffic.

2. The TLS 1.3 handshake, message by message

  1. ClientHello (plaintext): the TLS versions the client supports, its cipher suites, one or more Diffie-Hellman key shares (it guesses which group the server will choose, usually X25519, so it can send a share straight away), SNI with the hostname it wants, ALPN listing application protocols such as h2 and http/1.1 (net8), and a random value.
  2. ServerHello (plaintext): the chosen version and cipher suite and the server's own key share. Both sides can now compute the shared secret and derive handshake keys. Everything from here on is encrypted.
  3. EncryptedExtensions: remaining negotiated settings, such as the chosen ALPN protocol.
  4. Certificate: the server's certificate chain (section 3).
  5. CertificateVerify: a signature, made with the certificate's private key, over a hash of all the handshake messages so far. This proves the server holds the private key and that nobody tampered with the negotiation, because the signature covers everything both sides sent.
  6. Finished: a message authentication code over the whole transcript, confirming both sides saw the same handshake.
  7. The client validates the certificate chain and the signature, sends its own Finished, and can immediately send application data, such as the HTTP request, in the same flight.

Total cost: one round trip. If the client guessed a key-share group the server does not support, the server answers with a HelloRetryRequest and it costs a second round trip. On later connections, the server can give the client a session ticket, which lets it resume with a pre-shared key and skip the certificate exchange; with 0-RTT, the client even sends its first request in its first flight, which can be replayed, so it is only for idempotent requests (net2).

TLS 1.2, still widely supported, needed two round trips, sent the certificate in plaintext, and allowed key exchange by encrypting the secret with the server's RSA key, which has no forward secrecy. TLS 1.0 and 1.1 are formally deprecated (RFC 8996) and should be disabled.

3. Certificates and chains of trust

An X.509 certificate is a signed data structure containing, among other things:

  • the subject public key;
  • the names it is valid for, in the Subject Alternative Name (SAN) extension, such as api.example.com and *.example.com (a wildcard covers exactly one label: a.example.com yes, example.com and a.b.example.com no);
  • the issuer, the CA that signed it;
  • a validity period (not before and not after dates);
  • usage constraints, such as "may be used for TLS server authentication" or "is a CA";
  • the CA's signature over all of the above.

Root CAs do not sign server certificates directly: their private keys are kept offline in hardware, and they sign intermediate CA certificates, which sign the leaf certificates for servers. So the server sends a chain, and the client builds a path to a root it already trusts:

The trust store is the list of root certificates a client trusts, and there is more than one on a typical machine: the operating system's, the browser's own (Firefox ships one), Java's cacerts, Python's certifi package used by requests, Node.js's bundled list, and whatever was baked into a container image. When a certificate works in one client and not another, different trust stores are a prime suspect.

Hostname matching uses only the SAN list; the old Common Name field is ignored by modern clients. When you connect by IP address, the certificate needs an IP SAN, which public CAs rarely issue, one reason internal services should be addressed by name.

4. How certificates are issued: ACME and domain validation

A publicly trusted CA must verify that you control the domain before issuing. Almost all issuance is now automated with ACME (Automatic Certificate Management Environment), the protocol created for Let's Encrypt and now supported by many CAs. Your ACME client generates a key pair and asks for a certificate; the CA responds with a token and a choice of challenges:

  • HTTP-01: serve the token at http://your-domain/.well-known/acme-challenge/<token>.
  • DNS-01: publish it in a TXT record at _acme-challenge.your-domain. Required for wildcard certificates, and works for servers not reachable from the internet.
  • TLS-ALPN-01: present it during a special TLS handshake on port 443.

The CA checks the challenge from several network locations, then issues the certificate. Tools such as certbot, cert-manager in Kubernetes, and cloud certificate managers run this loop and renew automatically.

Automation is no longer optional. Public certificate lifetimes, which were 398 days, are being cut under a 2025 CA/Browser Forum decision: to 200 days for certificates issued from 15 March 2026, 100 days from 15 March 2027, and 47 days from 15 March 2029. Let's Encrypt certificates have long been 90 days. Every endpoint therefore needs automated renewal and automated deployment of the renewed certificate, plus monitoring that alerts well before expiry if either fails.

5. Revocation, Certificate Transparency and CAA

If a private key leaks, the certificate should stop being trusted before it expires. Two mechanisms exist. A CRL (certificate revocation list) is a CA-published list of revoked certificates. OCSP (Online Certificate Status Protocol) lets a client ask the CA about one certificate, and OCSP stapling lets the server attach a recent signed answer to the handshake. In practice revocation is weak: browsers usually "soft-fail" (if the check does not answer, they proceed), many API clients never check, and OCSP leaked every site a user visited to the CA. Let's Encrypt shut down its OCSP service in August 2025 and publishes CRLs only; browsers ship their own compressed revocation sets for important cases. That weakness is exactly why lifetimes are shrinking: a certificate that expires in weeks limits the damage.

Certificate Transparency (CT) attacks the problem from another angle. Every publicly trusted certificate must be recorded in public, append-only, cryptographically verifiable logs, and browsers reject certificates that lack proof of logging. So if any CA issues a certificate for your domain, legitimately or not, it becomes visible, and you can monitor the logs (for example with crt.sh) and react. A CAA DNS record (net6) lists which CAs may issue for your domain; compliant CAs must check it before issuing.

8. Mutual TLS

In ordinary TLS only the server is authenticated; clients prove who they are later, with a password or token inside the encrypted connection. In mutual TLS (mTLS), the server also sends a CertificateRequest in its handshake flight, and the client answers with its own Certificate and CertificateVerify, proving it holds the private key for a certificate the server trusts (usually one issued by a private CA). Both ends are authenticated before any application data flows.

mTLS is used where the caller is a machine rather than a person: service-to-service calls inside a platform, partner APIs between companies, and IoT device fleets. Inside Kubernetes, service meshes (such as Istio or Linkerd) automate it completely: each workload gets an identity, often a SPIFFE ID like spiffe://prod.example.com/ns/payments/sa/api derived from its namespace and service account, carried in a short-lived certificate (hours to a day) issued and rotated by the mesh's CA. The proxies beside each service perform mTLS for it, and policies then say which identities may call which services. Note the split: mTLS authenticates the caller; a separate authorisation policy decides what that caller may do.

Key management: where the private key lives

Everything in TLS rests on private keys staying private. Public CAs keep root keys in offline hardware security modules (HSMs) and bring them out only in audited ceremonies to sign intermediates. For your own services, keys live on load balancers, in cloud certificate managers (where you often cannot even export them), in Kubernetes secrets, or in secret stores such as Vault. The more places a key is copied, the larger the blast radius of one leak; wildcard certificates copied to dozens of servers are a common anti-pattern. Session ticket keys deserve the same care: TLS 1.3 servers encrypt resumption state with a ticket key, and if that key is long-lived and stolen, resumed sessions lose forward secrecy, so servers rotate ticket keys every few hours.

Post-quantum key exchange

A large enough quantum computer could break elliptic-curve Diffie-Hellman, and an adversary can record encrypted traffic today and decrypt it once such a machine exists: "harvest now, decrypt later". Since late 2024, major browsers and CDNs have enabled a hybrid key exchange, X25519 combined with ML-KEM-768, where ML-KEM is the lattice-based algorithm NIST standardised as FIPS 203 in August 2024. The shared secret combines both, so the connection is safe if either algorithm holds. The cost is size: the ML-KEM key share adds about 1.2 KB, pushing the ClientHello past a single packet, and some servers and middleboxes that assumed a one-packet ClientHello failed, the same ossification problem seen with TCP and QUIC. Post-quantum signatures for certificates are further off because they are much larger, and forging a signature matters only at connection time, not years later.

Encrypted Client Hello

Even with TLS 1.3, the ClientHello is plaintext, and its SNI field reveals which site you are visiting. Encrypted Client Hello (ECH), published as RFC 9849 in 2026, lets a client encrypt an "inner" ClientHello, including the real SNI, under a public key the server's provider publishes in DNS (in the HTTPS record), while the visible "outer" ClientHello names only the provider's shared front-end. It works best on large CDNs, where one front end serves many sites, and it removes the visibility that some enterprise filters and national firewalls rely on, which is why its deployment is politically contested.

TLS inspection and why it breaks things

Many enterprises run "TLS inspection" proxies: the company installs its own root CA on employee devices, and the proxy terminates every TLS connection, inspects the plaintext, and re-encrypts to the destination with a certificate it mints on the fly, signed by that company CA. It works for browsers that trust the company root, but it breaks applications that pin certificates, tools with their own trust stores (a Python script using certifi will reject the proxy's certificates), and mTLS, since the proxy cannot present the client's private key. When a developer tool fails with certificate errors only on the office network, inspection is the first suspect, and the usual fix is pointing the tool at the corporate CA bundle.

Performance: what a handshake costs

A full handshake costs one round trip plus CPU: one ECDHE computation on each side and one signature by the server. ECDSA P-256 signatures are much cheaper for the server than RSA-2048 and produce smaller certificates, so modern deployments prefer ECDSA leaf certificates, often served alongside RSA for old clients. Short chains mean fewer bytes in the first flight, which matters because a large certificate chain can exceed TCP's initial window and cost an extra round trip. Resumption skips the certificate and signature entirely. The bulk encryption itself is rarely a bottleneck: AES-GCM with hardware support runs at many gigabytes per second per core, and ChaCha20-Poly1305 is preferred on devices without AES instructions.

Internal PKI done well

For internal services, a private CA (a cloud private CA service, Vault, cert-manager with an internal issuer, or a mesh CA built on SPIFFE/SPIRE) issues certificates that only your own clients trust. Good practice mirrors the public web: an offline or HSM-protected root, intermediates per environment or cluster, short-lived leaf certificates issued automatically, workload identities rather than IP addresses in certificates, and a tested plan for rotating the root before it expires, which means distributing the new root to every trust store long before the old one lapses.

How TLS takes systems down

  • Expired certificates. The classic self-inflicted outage: sudden, total, at a predictable minute. On 6 December 2018, an expired certificate embedded in versions of Ericsson's mobile core software shut down equipment at operators in 11 countries; in the UK alone, over 32 million O2 customers lost 4G data and texting for most of a day. Internal certificates, client certificates for mTLS and partner APIs, and certificates on appliances are the ones most often forgotten.
  • Incomplete chains. A renewed certificate deployed without its intermediate works in browsers and fails in curl, Java, Python and mobile SDKs with "unable to get local issuer certificate" or "PKIX path building failed".
  • Root and trust store changes. When Let's Encrypt's old DST Root CA X3 expired on 30 September 2021, clients with outdated trust stores or old TLS libraries (OpenSSL 1.0.2, old macOS and Android versions, embedded devices) could no longer validate sites using Let's Encrypt, even though the sites' certificates were fine. Outdated container base images and language bundles cause the same class of failure.
  • Clock skew. A device or VM with the wrong time sees valid certificates as "not yet valid" or "expired". Common after a machine sleeps for long periods or boots without network time.
  • Name mismatch. Moving a service behind a new hostname, or calling it by IP, without the name in the certificate's SAN list.
  • Hardening side effects. Disabling TLS 1.2 or old cipher suites breaks old clients and partners; test against real client traffic (your logs record negotiated versions and ciphers) before tightening.
  • mTLS rotation failures. The CA rotates its root or intermediate, but some services still trust only the old one, and calls between them fail with handshake errors.

A debugging order

When a TLS connection fails: reproduce with openssl s_client -connect host:443 -servername host from the failing client's network to see the chain actually served and the verify result; compare with the same command from a working network (an inspection proxy or a different load balancer may be answering); check the client's clock; identify which trust store the failing client uses; and only then look at versions and ciphers. Most incidents are resolved at the first or second step.

Observability

Inventory every certificate you depend on, public and private, including client certificates, and monitor expiry from the outside (a synthetic check that connects and reads the chain) as well as from the issuing system, because "renewed" is not the same as "deployed". Alert at 30, 14 and 7 days. Log negotiated TLS version, cipher and handshake errors at load balancers; a rise in handshake failures after a change is the fastest signal. Subscribe to Certificate Transparency monitoring for your domains to catch unexpected issuance. For debugging, openssl s_client shows exactly what a server sends; curl -v shows how a client validated it; online checkers such as SSL Labs grade public endpoints.

Knobs

Minimum TLS version (1.2 or 1.3); cipher suite and key-exchange group preferences (including hybrid post-quantum groups); certificate key type (ECDSA, optionally with RSA fallback); full chain configuration; OCSP stapling where still used; session ticket key rotation; 0-RTT acceptance; HSTS (net2); CAA records; ACME renewal timing (renew at about two-thirds of the lifetime); and, for mTLS, certificate lifetimes, trusted CA bundles and authorisation policies.

Twenty-five minutes with openssl and curl (Linux, macOS, WSL or Git Bash on Windows). You will read a real chain, see each class of certificate error, and build a tiny private CA with working mutual TLS.

  1. Read a real chain. openssl s_client -connect www.wikipedia.org:443 -servername www.wikipedia.org -showcerts </dev/null. Look for: the "Certificate chain" list (0 s: the leaf, 1 s: the intermediate, each with i: its issuer), "Verify return code: 0 (ok)", and in newer OpenSSL versions the negotiated protocol and group. Pipe the output into openssl x509 -noout -subject -issuer -dates -ext subjectAltName to see the leaf's names and dates.
  2. See each failure. Run curl -sv -o /dev/null against https://expired.badssl.com, https://wrong.host.badssl.com, https://untrusted-root.badssl.com and https://incomplete-chain.badssl.com. Look for: "certificate has expired", "no alternative certificate subject name matches", "self-signed certificate in certificate chain" and "unable to get local issuer certificate". Open the incomplete-chain one in a browser too; it may well load, which is exactly the production trap.
  3. Check TLS versions. curl -sv --tlsv1.3 --tls-max 1.3 https://www.wikipedia.org -o /dev/null 2>&1 | grep -i 'SSL connection'. Look for: "TLSv1.3" and the cipher, such as TLS_AES_128_GCM_SHA256.
  4. Search Certificate Transparency. Open https://crt.sh/?q=wikipedia.org. Look for: every certificate issued for the domain, with issuers and dates. This is what monitoring for mis-issuance looks at.
  5. Build a private CA and mTLS (in an empty directory; on Git Bash run export MSYS_NO_PATHCONV=1 first): 
    openssl req -x509 -newkey ec -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:P-256 -nodes \
  -keyout ca.key -out ca.crt -days 30 -subj "/CN=Lab CA"
openssl req -newkey ec -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:P-256 -nodes \
  -keyout srv.key -out srv.csr -subj "/CN=localhost"
printf "subjectAltName=DNS:localhost\n" > srv.ext
openssl x509 -req -in srv.csr -CA ca.crt -CAkey ca.key -CAcreateserial \
  -out srv.crt -days 7 -extfile srv.ext
openssl req -newkey ec -pkeyopt ec_paramgen_curve:P-256 -nodes \
  -keyout cli.key -out cli.csr -subj "/CN=client-a"
openssl x509 -req -in cli.csr -CA ca.crt -CAkey ca.key -CAcreateserial \
  -out cli.crt -days 7
openssl s_server -accept 8443 -cert srv.crt -key srv.key \
  -CAfile ca.crt -Verify 1 -www

(b) curl --cacert ca.crt https://localhost:8443/ fails differently: the server requires a client certificate. (c) curl --cacert ca.crt --cert cli.crt --key cli.key https://localhost:8443/ succeeds and prints the server's status page. Debug it

After renewing the certificate for api.example.com, browsers work fine, but a partner's Java service and curl on a Linux server fail with "unable to get local issuer certificate". The mobile app, which pins the old certificate's public key, also breaks. What happened, how do you confirm it, and how should pinning be done?

Answer
  • Missing intermediate: the server was configured with only the leaf certificate, not the full chain. Browsers often cope, because they cache intermediates or fetch them using the certificate's Authority Information Access URL; curl, Java and most API clients do not, so they cannot build a path to a trusted root.
  • Confirm: openssl s_client -connect api.example.com:443 -servername api.example.com -showcerts shows only one certificate in the chain and "verify error:num=20: unable to get local issuer certificate". Online checkers such as SSL Labs report "chain incomplete".
  • Fix: install the full chain (leaf followed by intermediates, often the fullchain.pem file from the ACME client), reload, and add a synthetic check that validates the chain with a strict client, plus expiry alerts.
  • Pinning: the app pinned the leaf key, and renewal generated a new key, so the pin no longer matches. If pinning is required, pin the public keys of the intermediate or root CA, or several keys including a pre-generated backup key that renewals will use, and ship a remotely updatable pin set with a kill switch. Many teams now avoid pinning and rely on Certificate Transparency monitoring instead.
Design it

You run 40 microservices on Kubernetes in two clusters. Security asks that every service-to-service call be encrypted and that each service can only be called by the services that need it. Outline the design.

Answer
  • Identity: give each workload an identity from its namespace and service account, expressed as a SPIFFE ID, rather than relying on IP addresses, which change constantly.
  • Certificates: a mesh or SPIRE CA per cluster, with intermediates chaining to a shared root kept in an HSM or cloud KMS, so the two clusters trust each other. Issue short-lived workload certificates (for example 24 hours) and rotate them automatically.
  • Transport: sidecar or node proxies perform mTLS on every call, in strict mode (plaintext rejected), so application code needs no changes.
  • Authorisation: policies keyed on caller identity, such as "checkout may call payments on POST /charges", default deny, managed as code with review.
  • Operations: monitor certificate issuance and expiry, handshake failures and policy denials; plan and rehearse root and intermediate rotation (trust both old and new during overlap); decide how ingress traffic is authenticated at the edge and propagated inward.
