Your laptop has perhaps 8 or 16 CPU cores and, at any moment, several hundred threads: the browser's rendering threads, a video call encoding audio, an editor, a compiler, background indexers, the music player. Most of them are asleep, waiting for something, but dozens may want a CPU at the same instant. Something has to decide, thousands of times a second, which thread runs on which core, for how long, and who goes next. That something is the scheduler.

Get it wrong and the failures are immediate and human. If the compiler hogs a core for a full second, your typing lags and the video call stutters, even though the machine has plenty of total CPU. If the scheduler switches between threads too eagerly, the machine spends its time saving and restoring state instead of working, and the compiler takes twice as long. If one kind of thread is always preferred, another may never run at all. On servers the same trade-offs show up as p99 latency, throughput and noisy neighbours, and in containers as the baffling "throttling" that makes a service slow while its CPU graph looks half empty.

Scheduling is also one of the most reliably examined topics in OS interviews. Campus and SDE rounds ask you to compute waiting and turnaround times for FCFS, SJF and round robin on a small example; senior rounds ask how Linux actually schedules, why CPU limits cause latency spikes, and what priority inversion is.

This lesson starts with the metrics that define "good", works through the classic algorithms on paper with real numbers so you can do the interview arithmetic, builds up the multi-level feedback queue, then explains how Linux really does it: proportional sharing with CFS, its 2023 replacement EEVDF, scheduling classes, multicore balancing and the cgroup quotas behind container CPU limits.

The checkout analogy

Picture a supermarket with one checkout (one CPU) and a line of customers (runnable threads). Each customer has some number of items (the CPU time their job needs). The cashier (scheduler) chooses the order. Serve in arrival order, and someone with one item may wait twenty minutes behind a family's weekly shop. Serve smallest baskets first, and short shoppers are delighted, but the family may never get served if small baskets keep arriving. Scan a few items for each customer in turn, and everybody gets started quickly, but everybody finishes late, and the constant switching (bagging, payment, greeting) wastes the cashier's time.

Where the analogy breaks: real threads keep leaving the line to wait for something (disk, network, a keypress) and come back later, often after only a sliver of CPU. That pattern of short CPU bursts separated by waits is typical of interactive programs, and recognising it is the key to good scheduling. Also, unlike shoppers, threads do not tell you how many items they have; the scheduler must guess.

What "good" means

Different goals pull in different directions, so it helps to name them precisely:

  • Turnaround time: completion time minus arrival time. Matters for batch jobs: "how long until my build finishes?"
  • Response time: time from arrival until the job first runs. Matters for interactive work: "how long until the key I pressed shows up?"
  • Throughput: jobs completed per unit time; hurt by overhead such as excessive context switching.
  • Fairness: each thread (or user, or container) gets its due share, and nobody starves.

Two other words recur. A non-preemptive scheduler lets the running thread keep the CPU until it blocks or finishes; a preemptive one can take the CPU away, using the timer interrupt from os1. A time slice (or quantum) is how long a thread runs before the scheduler reconsiders.

Mechanism versus policy

It helps to separate two things. The mechanism is how a switch happens: the timer interrupt and the context switch from os1, which every scheduler uses. The policy is the decision of who runs next, and that is what this lesson is about. Policies are shaped by the kind of threads they serve. A CPU-bound thread (a compiler, a model training loop) wants long uninterrupted stretches, because every switch costs it warm caches. An I/O-bound thread (an editor, a web handler waiting on a database) runs briefly and then blocks, and what it wants is to be run promptly when its data arrives. A good scheduler gives the I/O-bound thread quick access and the CPU-bound thread long slices, which also keeps disks and network busy at the same time as the CPU: the interactive thread issues its next I/O request quickly, then the CPU-bound thread soaks up the waiting time.

The big picture of the lesson in one sentence: classic algorithms show the pure trade-offs, MLFQ shows how to get good behaviour without knowing job lengths, and Linux's fair schedulers show how to share a CPU proportionally among hundreds of threads with predictable latency.

1. The metrics, as formulas

For each job: turnaround = completion − arrival; waiting time = turnaround − CPU time used (time spent ready but not running); response time = first run − arrival. Interview problems give a table of jobs with arrival times and burst lengths and ask for the averages under each algorithm. Draw a Gantt chart (a timeline of which job runs when) first, then read the numbers off it. Every example below can be checked that way.

2. First-come-first-served (FCFS)

Run jobs in arrival order, each to completion. It is the simplest possible scheduler, it is fair in the queueing sense, and nothing starves. Its weakness is the convoy effect. Suppose A needs 100 ms, B and C need 10 ms each, and all arrive at time 0 in that order:

B (10)100-110110100

Two short jobs spent most of their lives waiting behind one long one. In an OS the convoy is often a CPU-bound thread holding the CPU while I/O-bound threads, which would only need a moment before going back to waiting on disk, sit idle, leaving the disk idle too.

3. Shortest job first (SJF) and shortest remaining time first (SRTF)

Run the shortest job first. Same three jobs: B 0-10, C 10-20, A 20-120. Turnarounds 10, 20, 120: average 50, less than half of FCFS. In fact, if all jobs arrive together and lengths are known, SJF is provably optimal for average turnaround: if any schedule ran a longer job right before a shorter one, swapping them would reduce the short job's wait by more than it increases the long one's.

Now let jobs arrive at different times. A (100) arrives at 0; B and C (10 each) arrive at 10. Non-preemptive SJF has already started A and must let it finish: A 0-100, B 100-110, C 110-120, turnarounds 100, 100, 110, average 103.3. The preemptive version, shortest remaining time first (SRTF, also called STCF), stops A when shorter jobs arrive: A 0-10, B 10-20, C 20-30, A 30-120. Turnarounds 120, 10, 20: average 50. Preemption is what lets the scheduler correct for arrivals it could not foresee.

Two problems keep SJF out of real kernels. The OS does not know how long a thread will run before it next blocks; it can only estimate from history (a classic textbook trick is an exponential average of previous bursts: estimate = α × last_burst + (1 − α) × previous_estimate). And a steady stream of short jobs can postpone a long one forever, which is starvation.

4. Round robin (RR) and the size of the slice

Round robin is FCFS with preemption: each ready thread runs for at most one time slice, then goes to the back of the queue. The primer's figure showed the trade-off on three 5 ms jobs: RR improves average response time from 5 ms to 1 ms but worsens average turnaround from 10 ms to 14 ms, because every job is stretched out until near the end.

The slice length is the main design knob. Too long, and RR degenerates to FCFS with poor response. Too short, and context-switch overhead dominates: if a switch costs about 10 µs including the cache disruption, a 10 ms slice wastes 0.1% of the CPU but a 100 µs slice wastes about 10%. A common rule of thumb in textbooks is to make the slice longer than most CPU bursts, so interactive threads usually finish their burst and block voluntarily before the slice ends. Real kernels use slices of roughly a millisecond to tens of milliseconds.

5. Priority scheduling

Give each thread a priority and always run the highest-priority ready thread, preempting lower ones. SJF is a special case where priority is the inverse of job length. Priorities express things the scheduler cannot infer: the audio thread matters more than the indexer. The danger is starvation of low priorities, solved by aging (raising a thread's priority the longer it waits). A subtler danger is priority inversion: a high-priority thread waits for a lock held by a low-priority one, which is itself kept off the CPU by medium-priority threads. The deep section tells the Mars Pathfinder story and the fix, priority inheritance.

6. Multi-level feedback queue (MLFQ)

Can we get SJF-like turnaround and RR-like response without knowing job lengths? MLFQ, first described by Corbató in 1962 for the CTSS time-sharing system, learns from behaviour. OSTEP states it as five rules:

  1. If priority(A) > priority(B), A runs.
  2. If priorities are equal, they run round robin.
  3. A new job enters at the highest priority.
  4. Once a job uses up its time allotment at a level (however many times it gave up the CPU along the way), its priority drops one level.
  5. After some period S, move all jobs back to the top queue.

Walk through it. An editor wakes for a keypress, runs 1 ms and blocks again; it never exhausts its allotment, so it stays in the top queue and gets near-instant response. A compiler starts in the top queue too (rule 3: every job is assumed short until proven otherwise), uses its full 10 ms allotment, drops to Q1, uses 20 ms there, and settles in Q0 where it runs in long slices whenever nothing more urgent is ready. Short jobs finish before sinking far, approximating SJF.

Rules 4 and 5 each fix a flaw in earlier versions. Without rule 5, a flood of interactive jobs would starve Q0 forever, and a job that changes from CPU-bound to interactive could never climb back. Without rule 4's accounting across yields, a program could game the scheduler by sleeping for a microsecond just before its slice ends, staying in the top queue while using 99% of the CPU. Windows, the classic BSDs and Solaris use variants of MLFQ; their tuning (number of queues, slices per level, boost period) is the hard part.

7. Proportional share and Linux CFS

A different philosophy: instead of guessing which jobs are short, guarantee each thread a share of the CPU in proportion to a weight. Linux's Completely Fair Scheduler (CFS), default from kernel 2.6.23 in 2007 until 6.6 in 2023, did this with one quantity per task: virtual runtime (vruntime).

Each task's weight comes from its nice value, from −20 (most favoured) to 19 (least). Nice 0 has weight 1024, and each step multiplies or divides by about 1.25: nice 1 is 820, nice 5 is 335, nice −5 is 3121, nice 19 is 15. When a task runs for Δt of real time, its vruntime increases by Δt × 1024 / weight. A nice 0 task's vruntime advances at real speed; a heavier task's advances more slowly. CFS kept runnable tasks in a red-black tree sorted by vruntime and always ran the leftmost, the task with the least weighted CPU so far.

Trace two CPU-bound tasks sharing one core: T0 at nice 0 (weight 1024) and T5 at nice 5 (weight 335). Whenever T0 runs 3 ms, its vruntime rises by 3 ms; for T5 to keep up, its vruntime must rise by 3 ms too, which takes only 3 × 335/1024 ≈ 0.98 ms of real time. So for every 3 ms T0 runs, T5 runs about 1 ms: shares of 1024/(1024+335) ≈ 75% and 25%. Weights are relative: two nice 5 tasks alone would split 50/50.

A few details made CFS work in practice. The slice was not fixed: CFS aimed to run every runnable task once within a "target latency" (6 ms by default, scaled up with core count), dividing it by weight, with a minimum granularity so slices did not become absurdly short with many tasks. A task waking from a long sleep had its vruntime raised to near the current minimum, so it could not bank credit by sleeping and then hog the CPU, though it got a small bonus so interactive tasks felt responsive. And with group scheduling, each cgroup was itself an entity with a weight, so a container with 50 threads competed as one entity against a container with 1, rather than getting 50 times the CPU.

8. EEVDF: Linux's scheduler since 6.6

CFS was good at fairness but had no direct way to express latency. A task that needed the CPU quickly but only briefly (an audio thread, a UI thread) could only get priority by getting a bigger weight, which also gave it more CPU than it needed, so CFS grew heuristics and tunables. In Linux 6.6 (2023) the pick logic was replaced by EEVDF, Earliest Eligible Virtual Deadline First, an algorithm published by Stoica and Abdel-Wahab in 1995.

EEVDF keeps weighted fair sharing and adds two ideas. First, lag: for each task, the difference between the CPU time it should have received under ideal weighted sharing and what it actually received. A task with lag ≥ 0 is owed service and is eligible; a task that has run ahead of its share (negative lag) must wait. Second, a virtual deadline for each eligible task: its eligible time plus its requested slice divided by its weight. Among eligible tasks, the scheduler runs the one with the earliest virtual deadline.

The effect: two tasks with the same weight get the same long-run share, but if one asks for a 1 ms slice and the other for a 10 ms slice, the short-slice task's deadline comes sooner, so it runs sooner and more often, in smaller pieces. Latency and share become separate dials. Lag also makes sleep handling principled: a task's lag is remembered (and decays) across sleeps, so a task cannot game the scheduler by sleeping, without needing CFS's special placement rules. Later kernels let a task request its slice through sched_setattr(). For most workloads, the visible difference from CFS is small; the code is simpler and has fewer knobs.

9. Scheduling classes and real-time policies

Linux does not run one algorithm but several scheduling classes in strict priority order. A runnable task in a higher class always runs before any task in a lower class:

Class / policyUseHow it picks
SCHED_DEADLINEPeriodic real-time work with guaranteesEarliest deadline first, with admission control
SCHED_FIFO, SCHED_RRReal-time: audio, control loopsFixed priority 1-99; FIFO runs until it blocks, RR time-slices equal priorities
SCHED_OTHER (NORMAL), SCHED_BATCHEverything normal; BATCH for non-interactive CPU workFair sharing by weight (CFS, now EEVDF)
SCHED_IDLEWork that should only use otherwise idle CPUVery low weight

A runaway SCHED_FIFO thread could lock up a core forever, so by default real-time tasks are limited to 950 ms of every 1,000 ms (/proc/sys/kernel/sched_rt_runtime_us), leaving a sliver for normal tasks to recover the system. chrt sets these policies from the shell, and ps -eo pid,cls,rtprio,ni,cmd shows them.

10. Many CPUs: run queues, balancing and affinity

With many cores, a single global queue would need a lock that every CPU fights over on every scheduling decision. Linux instead keeps a run queue per CPU. Each CPU schedules its own tasks independently, and tasks tend to stay on the CPU whose caches they have warmed. The cost is imbalance: one CPU might have five runnable tasks while another idles. The kernel's load balancer fixes this periodically and whenever a CPU goes idle, pulling tasks from busier queues. It follows the hardware's shape, organised into scheduling domains: moving a task between two hyperthreads of one core is nearly free, between cores sharing a last-level cache is cheap, and across NUMA nodes (separate sockets with their own memory, where remote memory access is slower) is expensive, so the balancer is increasingly reluctant at each level.

You can override placement. CPU affinity (taskset -c 2,3 cmd, sched_setaffinity) restricts a task to certain CPUs; cpusets do the same for whole cgroups; the isolcpus boot option keeps the scheduler from placing ordinary tasks on chosen cores, which low-latency trading and packet-processing systems use to give a thread a core to itself.

11. Sharing among groups: cgroup weights and quotas

Fairness among threads is not what you want on a shared server; you want fairness among services. cgroups (os13) give two CPU controls. Weights (cpu.weight, default 100) set relative shares between groups when the CPU is contended and impose nothing when it is idle; Kubernetes CPU requests become weights. Quotas (cpu.max, written as "quota period", e.g. 200000 100000 for 2 CPUs) cap a group to a fixed amount of CPU time per period even when the machine is idle; Kubernetes CPU limits become quotas. When a group exhausts its quota, all its threads are throttled until the next period begins. The production section shows why that is a frequent cause of latency spikes.

Priority inversion and the Mars Pathfinder

In 1997, the Mars Pathfinder lander began resetting itself repeatedly. Its VxWorks system had a high-priority bus-management task that needed a mutex also used by a low-priority meteorological task. While the low-priority task held the mutex, medium-priority communication tasks preempted it and ran for long stretches. The high-priority task waited on the mutex, missed its deadline, and a watchdog reset the system. This is priority inversion: the effective priority of the high task drops to that of the low task holding its lock, and any medium task can then delay it without bound. The engineers reproduced it on a ground replica and fixed it by remotely enabling priority inheritance on that mutex: while a low-priority task holds a lock that a higher-priority task wants, it temporarily runs at the waiter's priority, so medium tasks cannot preempt it, and it releases the lock quickly. Linux supports inheritance through PI futexes (PTHREAD_PRIO_INHERIT) and uses it pervasively in the PREEMPT_RT kernel.

Why EEVDF's eligibility rule matters

Pure "earliest virtual deadline first" without eligibility would let a task that has already received more than its share keep winning if it has short slices. Eligibility (lag ≥ 0) caps that: a task that runs ahead must wait until the others catch up. Combined with deadlines, this gives a provable bound: each task's received service never drifts from its ideal share by more than about one slice. That bound is why EEVDF can let tasks choose slice length freely without breaking fairness. When a task sleeps and wakes, its preserved lag decides whether it is owed time; positive lag lets it run soon, negative lag (it ran ahead before sleeping) makes it wait, closing the loophole CFS patched with heuristics.

sched_ext: schedulers as BPF programs

Linux 6.12 merged sched_ext, a scheduling class whose policy is supplied at runtime by an eBPF program (os14). If the BPF scheduler misbehaves (stalls a task too long, for example), the kernel kicks it out and falls back to the fair scheduler, so experimenting is safe. It exists because different workloads want genuinely different policies: Meta and Google have reported gains from workload-specific schedulers for web serving and caching, and game-oriented schedulers on Linux desktops prioritise the frame-rendering threads. It makes the point that "the Linux scheduler" is increasingly a framework with a default policy.

Hyperthreads and core scheduling

Simultaneous multithreading (Intel Hyper-Threading) presents one physical core as two logical CPUs that share execution units and caches. Two busy threads on sibling hyperthreads each run slower than alone, often achieving 1.1-1.3x the throughput of one thread, not 2x, so the load balancer prefers spreading busy tasks across physical cores first. After side-channel attacks such as L1TF and MDS showed that siblings could leak data to each other, Linux added core scheduling: tasks are tagged with a cookie, and the scheduler only lets tasks with the same cookie (the same trust domain, for example one VM or one container) run on sibling hyperthreads simultaneously. Cloud providers use it to keep tenants apart without disabling SMT entirely.

Tickless kernels and timer slack

Scheduling decisions used to happen on a periodic tick at HZ (100 to 1000 times per second). Idle CPUs taking useless ticks waste power, so NO_HZ_IDLE stops the tick on idle CPUs, and NO_HZ_FULL can stop it even on a CPU running a single task, which latency-critical and HPC systems use with isolated cores so the application is never interrupted. Relatedly, Linux applies timer slack (50 µs by default for normal tasks) so that wake-ups from many sleeping threads can be coalesced, saving power at the cost of slightly late timers.

Why "load" is not "CPU usage"

The Linux load average counts tasks that are runnable or in uninterruptible sleep (os2), averaged exponentially over 1, 5 and 15 minutes. A load of 8 on an 8-core machine might mean full CPU use, or 8 tasks stuck on a dead NFS mount with idle CPUs. Better signals of CPU contention are run-queue latency (how long runnable tasks wait), the r column of vmstat compared with the core count, and Pressure Stall Information (/proc/pressure/cpu), which reports the share of time some tasks were runnable but waiting for a CPU.

CPU throttling in containers

The most common scheduling problem in modern production is a container that is throttled. With a CPU limit of 2, the cgroup gets 200 ms of CPU time per 100 ms period. A JVM or Go service with 16 threads (because its runtime sized thread pools by the host's 16 cores) can burn that budget in about 12.5 ms of wall time during a burst, and then every thread is frozen for the remaining 87.5 ms. A request that arrived during the burst sees an extra 50-90 ms of latency, while the dashboard shows average CPU at 40% of the limit. Look at cpu.stat in the cgroup (nr_periods, nr_throttled, throttled_usec) or the container_cpu_cfs_throttled_periods_total metric; a throttled-period ratio of more than a few percent on a latency-sensitive service is a red flag. Remedies: raise or remove the limit while keeping requests (which set weights), make runtimes container-aware (modern JVMs and Go read cgroup limits; check GOMAXPROCS and GC thread settings), shrink thread pools, or allow bursting with cpu.max.burst. An old kernel bug also caused throttling of services that were nowhere near their quota on multicore machines because of how per-CPU quota slices expired; it was fixed in Linux 5.4, and is a reason to distrust old blog advice.

Measuring run-queue latency

CPU utilisation tells you how busy CPUs are, not how long work waits for them. Tools that measure waiting directly: runqlat (bcc/bpftrace) prints a histogram of how long tasks waited on a run queue; perf sched latency reports per-task scheduling delays; /proc/<pid>/schedstat gives cumulative time running and time waiting for each task; and PSI (/proc/pressure/cpu or cpu.pressure per cgroup) gives a percentage of time stalled. A service at 60% CPU can have painful queueing if its work arrives in bursts; a service at 95% with steady load may be fine. Little's law (net13) links the two: queueing grows sharply as utilisation approaches 100%.

Noisy neighbours and isolation

On shared hosts, a batch job can hurt a latency-sensitive service even when CPU shares are fair, because they share last-level cache, memory bandwidth and hyperthread execution units. Mitigations escalate: give batch work low weight or SCHED_IDLE; separate them onto different cores with cpusets (Kubernetes' static CPU manager policy gives Guaranteed pods with integer CPU requests exclusive cores); keep latency-critical threads off hyperthread siblings of batch threads; and, for extreme cases, use cache partitioning (Intel RDT/CAT). Measure with per-cgroup PSI and tail latency rather than averages.

Priorities that backfire

Raising a service to a real-time class "to make it faster" is a classic mistake: a SCHED_FIFO thread in a busy loop starves everything on its core, including kernel threads that flush disks and handle network softirqs, and only the 95% real-time throttle keeps the box responsive. Use real-time classes only for threads with short, bounded work per wake-up. Similarly, nice -n 19 on a backup job does not stop it from hurting latency through disk and cache; pair it with ionice -c3 and cgroup I/O limits.

Thread counts and the scheduler

Very high runnable-thread counts make every scheduling decision and every cache warm-up more expensive. A service with 2,000 worker threads on 16 cores spends a visible share of CPU in the scheduler (perf top shows functions such as __schedule and pick_next_task_fair) and has high involuntary context-switch rates (pidstat -w). The fix is architectural: thread pools sized to cores for CPU work, async I/O or virtual threads for waiting work (net13, py9).

  1. Save as sched.py: 
    def simulate(jobs, policy, q=1):
    # jobs: {name: (arrival, burst)}; time advances in 1 ms steps
    left = {n: b for n, (a, b) in jobs.items()}
    first, done, t, queue, last = {}, {}, 0, [], None
    while len(done) < len(jobs):
        for n, (a, b) in jobs.items():          # admit arrivals
            if a == t: queue.append(n)
        ready = [n for n in queue if n not in done]
        if not ready: t += 1; continue
        if policy == "FCFS":   cur = ready[0]
        elif policy == "SRTF": cur = min(ready, key=lambda n: left[n])
        elif policy == "RR":
            cur = ready[0]
            if cur == last and used == q:     # slice over: rotate
                queue.remove(cur); queue.append(cur); used = 0
                cur = [n for n in queue if n not in done][0]
        used = used + 1 if cur == last else 1
        first.setdefault(cur, t); left[cur] -= 1; t += 1; last = cur
        if left[cur] == 0: done[cur] = t; queue.remove(cur)
    ta = sum(done[n] - jobs[n][0] for n in jobs) / len(jobs)
    rs = sum(first[n] - jobs[n][0] for n in jobs) / len(jobs)
    print(f"{policy:5} turnaround {ta:6.1f}  response {rs:5.1f}")

Compute, then explain: the exam table

Four processes arrive as follows (times in ms): P1 arrives at 0 with burst 8, P2 at 1 with burst 4, P3 at 2 with burst 9, P4 at 3 with burst 5. Compute the average waiting time under (a) FCFS, (b) non-preemptive SJF, (c) SRTF, (d) round robin with a 4 ms quantum. Then say which you would choose for an interactive desktop and why none of them is what Linux does.

Answer

Waiting time = completion − arrival − burst. Draw the Gantt chart for each.

(a) FCFS: P1 0-8, P2 8-12, P3 12-21, P4 21-26. Waits: P1 0, P2 12 − 1 − 4 = 7, P3 21 − 2 − 9 = 10, P4 26 − 3 − 5 = 18. Average 35/4 = 8.75.

(b) Non-preemptive SJF: only P1 is present at 0, so it runs 0-8. At 8 the shortest waiting is P2 (4): 8-12, then P4 (5): 12-17, then P3 (9): 17-26. Waits: P1 0, P2 12−1−4 = 7, P4 17−3−5 = 9, P3 26−2−9 = 15. Average 31/4 = 7.75.

(c) SRTF: P1 runs 0-1 (7 left). At 1, P2 (4) is shorter: P2 runs 1-5 (P3 arriving at 2 with 9, P4 at 3 with 5, neither shorter than P2's remaining). At 5 remaining: P1 7, P3 9, P4 5 → P4 5-10. Then P1 10-17, then P3 17-26. Waits: P1 17−0−8 = 9, P2 5−1−4 = 0, P3 26−2−9 = 15, P4 10−3−5 = 2. Average 26/4 = 6.5, the lowest, as theory predicts.

(d) RR, q = 4: queue order by arrival. P1 0-4 (4 left; P2, P3, P4 arrived meanwhile, P1 rejoins after them), P2 4-8 (done), P3 8-12 (5 left), P4 12-16 (1 left), P1 16-20 (done), P3 20-24 (1 left), P4 24-25 (done), P3 25-26 (done). Choice. For an interactive desktop, response time matters more than average waiting, which favours preemptive time slicing, but SRTF needs burst lengths nobody knows. A real system wants the effect of SRTF (short interactive bursts first) without the knowledge, which is what MLFQ approximates by watching behaviour. Linux does neither: it shares the CPU in proportion to weights with EEVDF, and gets responsiveness because interactive tasks run briefly, stay owed service (non-negative lag) and so are eligible with early deadlines when they wake.
