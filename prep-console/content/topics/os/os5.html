The tools in os4 (mutexes, semaphores, condition variables) are like a box of woodworking tools: knowing what each one does is not the same as knowing how to build a chair. Synchronization bugs rarely come from not knowing what a mutex is. They come from combining correct primitives in the wrong order, waking the wrong thread, or letting one kind of thread starve another. To learn how to combine them, computer scientists distilled the recurring shapes of concurrent programs into a handful of classic problems.

Three of them cover most of what you will meet. Producer-consumer is the shape of every pipeline: one part of a system makes work, another part does it, and a buffer sits between them. Your web server's request queue, your logger, the pipe between grep and wc, and a Kafka topic are all producer-consumer. Readers-writers is the shape of shared data that is read constantly and changed occasionally: configuration, caches, routing tables. Dining philosophers is the shape of tasks that each need several shared resources at once: a bank transfer locking two accounts, a transaction locking two rows. It is the smallest example of deadlock.

These problems are interview staples for a reason. At campus and SDE level you will be asked to write the semaphore solution to the bounded buffer or explain why the naive philosophers deadlock. LeetCode's concurrency set (print in order, FooBar, H2O, dining philosophers) turns them into coding questions. At senior level the same ideas appear disguised: "why did this queue hang", "why do config updates cause latency spikes", "how would you prevent deadlock between these two services".

This lesson solves each problem properly, shows the tempting wrong versions and exactly how they fail, explains the trade-offs between solutions, and connects each one to the production systems where it lives.

Three scenes

A bakery counter. Bakers put trays of bread on a rack with ten shelves; shop staff take trays off to sell. If the rack is full, bakers must wait; if it is empty, staff must wait; and two people should not shove a tray onto the same shelf at once. That is producer-consumer with a bounded buffer: two kinds of waiting (full, empty) plus mutual exclusion on the rack itself.

A museum noticeboard. Any number of visitors can read the board at the same time without bothering each other. But when the curator changes a notice, nobody should be reading half of an old notice and half of a new one, and two curators should not edit at once. That is readers-writers: many readers or one writer. The hard part is fairness: if visitors keep arriving, does the curator ever get a turn? If the curator is always first, do visitors wait too long?

A dinner with too few forks. Five philosophers sit around a round table. Between each pair lies one fork, five in all. A philosopher alternates between thinking and eating, and needs both the fork on the left and the fork on the right to eat. If every philosopher picks up the left fork at the same moment, each then waits forever for the right one, which their neighbour holds. That is dining philosophers, and the frozen table is a deadlock.

Which tool fits which need

Before solving anything, it helps to map each need onto the os4 toolbox. "Only one at a time may touch this" is mutual exclusion, so a mutex. "Wait until there are some of these" (free slots, filled slots, seats at the table) is counting, so a counting semaphore, or a mutex plus a counter plus a condition variable. "Wait until this particular thing becomes true" (the queue is not empty, no writer is active, both my forks are free) is a condition, so a condition variable with a while loop. Most solutions in this lesson are just careful combinations of these three answers, and most bugs come from mixing them up: blocking on a condition while holding the lock that someone else needs to make the condition true, or using one waiting room for two different conditions. Keep asking two questions as you read: what exactly is each thread waiting for, and who is responsible for waking it?

What makes these problems hard

Each scene combines two needs. One is safety: nothing bad happens (no two writes to the same shelf, no reading a half-written notice, no fork used by two philosophers). The other is liveness: something good eventually happens (every tray eventually gets sold, the curator eventually updates the board, every philosopher eventually eats). It is easy to get safety by locking everything, and easy to get speed by locking nothing. Getting both, plus fairness, is the skill. The rest of this lesson builds each solution in steps, showing the wrong turns because interviewers ask about them.

1. Producer-consumer: the setup

The buffer is usually a ring buffer: an array of N slots with an index where the next item goes (tail) and an index where the next item comes from (head), both wrapping around modulo N. Three things must be synchronised:

  1. A producer must not insert when all N slots are full.
  2. A consumer must not remove when no slot is full.
  3. Two producers (or two consumers) must not update tail (or head) at the same time, or they would write the same slot.

Requirements 1 and 2 are waiting for a condition; requirement 3 is mutual exclusion. The classic solutions use semaphores for the first two and a mutex for the third, or one mutex plus condition variables for all three.

2. The semaphore solution

Use three semaphores. empty counts free slots and starts at N. full counts filled slots and starts at 0. mutex starts at 1 and protects the indexes.

producer:                         consumer:
  item = make()                     wait(full)      # wait for a filled slot

The counting semaphores carry the "how many" information, so nobody needs to look at the buffer to decide whether to wait. The mutex is only needed when there are several producers or several consumers; with exactly one of each, they always touch different indexes.

The famous bug: order of waits. Suppose the producer does wait(mutex) first and then wait(empty). When the buffer is full, the producer takes the mutex and then sleeps on empty while still holding it. The consumer, to make a slot free, must wait(mutex), which never succeeds. Deadlock. The rule: never block on a condition while holding a lock that the thread who would satisfy the condition needs. Waits on counting semaphores go outside the mutex. (The order of the posts at the end does not matter for correctness.)

3. The condition-variable solution, built in two attempts

Most production code uses a mutex and condition variables instead. First attempt, with a single condition variable:

def put(x):                          def get():
    with cv:                             with cv:
        while len(q) == N: cv.wait()         while not q: cv.wait()
        q.append(x)                          x = q.popleft()
        cv.notify()                          cv.notify()
                                             return x

It has correct while loops, the check and the wait are under the mutex, and it still hangs. Take N = 1, one producer P and two consumers C1 and C2:

  1. C1 and C2 call get, find the queue empty and wait.
  2. P puts an item and notifies; C1 is woken (made runnable). P loops to put again, finds the queue full and waits.
  3. C1 runs, takes the item, and calls notify(), meaning to wake P. But both C2 and P are waiting on the same condition variable, and the notify happens to wake C2.
  4. C2 rechecks (good), finds the queue empty, waits again. C1 comes back, finds it empty, waits.
  5. Now P, C1 and C2 are all asleep. Nobody will ever notify. Hang.

The bug is that one condition variable serves two different conditions ("not full" for producers and "not empty" for consumers), so a signal meant for one kind of waiter can be absorbed by the other kind. Two fixes: notify_all() (correct but wakes everyone, the thundering herd), or the proper design with two condition variables sharing one mutex:

import threading, collections
class BoundedQueue:
    def __init__(self, n):
        self.q, self.n = collections.deque(), n
        self.lock = threading.Lock()
        self.not_full = threading.Condition(self.lock)
        self.not_empty = threading.Condition(self.lock)
    def put(self, x):
        with self.lock:
            while len(self.q) == self.n:
                self.not_full.wait()
            self.q.append(x)
            self.not_empty.notify()        # wakes a consumer, never a producer
    def get(self):
        with self.lock:
            while not self.q:
                self.not_empty.wait()
            x = self.q.popleft()
            self.not_full.notify()         # wakes a producer, never a consumer
            return x

This is almost exactly how Python's queue.Queue is written (it has a third condition for join()), and how Java's ArrayBlockingQueue works with its notEmpty and notFull conditions. Go's buffered channels give the same semantics as a language feature.

4. Readers-writers: the first solution (reader preference)

The goal is to let any number of readers in together but give a writer the resource alone. The classic 1971 solution by Courtois, Heymans and Parnas counts active readers:

readers = 0
mutex = Semaphore(1)     # protects the readers counter
room  = Semaphore(1)     # held by a writer, or by the readers as a group

reader:                               writer:
    wait(mutex)                           wait(room)
    readers += 1                          ... write ...
    if readers == 1: wait(room)  # first reader locks writers out
    post(mutex)                           post(room)
    ... read ...
    wait(mutex)
    readers -= 1
    if readers == 0: post(room)  # last reader lets writers in
    post(mutex)

It is correct: a writer holds room alone, and readers hold it collectively from the first in to the last out. Its flaw is fairness. While at least one reader is inside, new readers walk straight in without touching room. If reads overlap continuously (a popular cache under load), readers never drops to zero and a waiting writer starves. This is reader preference.

5. Writer preference and fair versions

The second classic solution reverses the preference: once a writer is waiting, newly arriving readers must wait too, so the writer gets in as soon as the current readers finish. Now continuous writes can starve readers. The usual practical answer is a fair lock that serves arrivals roughly in order. A simple way, from Downey's Little Book of Semaphores, adds a turnstile: every reader passes through the turnstile (wait then immediately post) on arrival, and a writer holds the turnstile while it waits for the room. A writer that has arrived therefore blocks readers who come after it, while readers who arrived before it finish normally.

Real libraries choose differently, and it is worth knowing which you are using. glibc's pthread_rwlock_t prefers readers by default (a non-portable attribute selects writer preference). Go's sync.RWMutex blocks new readers once a writer is waiting, which also means a reader that recursively takes the read lock can deadlock if a writer arrives in between. Java's ReentrantReadWriteLock has a non-fair default and an optional fair mode. Linux kernel rwlock_t favours readers; its sleeping rw_semaphore is fairer.

6. Dining philosophers: four ways out

Edsger Dijkstra posed this as an exam problem in 1965 (with computers and tape drives), and Tony Hoare gave it the philosophers. The naive code, each philosopher i doing lock(fork[i]); lock(fork[(i+1) % 5]); eat(); unlock both, deadlocks as the primer's figure shows. Deadlock requires four conditions at once (os6 covers them formally): resources held exclusively, held while waiting for more, not taken away, and a circular chain of waits. Each solution removes one of them.

Solution A: order the resources. Number the forks 0-4 and make every philosopher pick up the lower-numbered of their two forks first. Philosophers 0-3 still pick left then right, but philosopher 4, whose forks are 4 and 0, picks fork 0 first. Now suppose everyone reaches for their first fork at once: philosopher 0 and philosopher 4 both reach for fork 0, and only one gets it. Whoever loses holds nothing, so the chain is broken and at least one philosopher (philosopher 3, whose second fork 4 is free) can eat. This breaks circular wait. It is cheap, needs no coordinator, and is the technique databases, kernels and careful application code use: a global lock order.

Solution B: limit who may try. A semaphore seats initialised to 4 (one fewer than the philosophers): wait(seats) before reaching for forks, post(seats) after eating. With at most four reaching for five forks, by the pigeonhole principle at least one of them can get both. This is the arbitrator or waiter solution. It is simple but caps concurrency and puts a central gate in front of every meal.

Solution C: take both or neither. Pick up both forks atomically, under one mutex, only when both are free; otherwise wait. Tanenbaum's textbook version gives each philosopher a state (THINKING, HUNGRY, EATING) and a condition variable; a hungry philosopher eats when neither neighbour is eating, and a philosopher who finishes checks both neighbours and signals any who can now eat. This breaks hold and wait. Its weakness is starvation: two neighbours who alternate eating can keep the philosopher between them hungry forever.

Solution D: back off. Take the left fork, try to take the right one (trylock or a timeout), and if that fails, put the left one down and retry later. This breaks no preemption by having threads release voluntarily. It avoids deadlock but risks livelock: all five pick up, fail, put down and retry in lockstep forever. Random back-off makes livelock vanishingly unlikely, which is exactly what Ethernet and many distributed protocols do.

7. Two more patterns you will meet: barriers and rendezvous

A barrier makes N threads wait until all have reached a point, then releases them together, as in parallel simulations where every thread must finish step k before any starts step k+1, or data-parallel training where workers synchronise gradients each step. With a mutex, a counter and a condition variable: each arrival increments the count; if it is the last, it resets the count, increments a "generation" number and broadcasts; otherwise it waits until the generation changes (checking the generation, not the count, is what makes the barrier reusable). Python has threading.Barrier, Java CyclicBarrier. A rendezvous is the two-thread version: two semaphores at 0, each thread posts its own and waits on the other's, and neither proceeds until both have arrived.

8. Interview coding versions

LeetCode's concurrency problems are these patterns in disguise. "Print in order" (three threads must run first, second, third) is a chain of signals: two semaphores or events initialised to 0. "Print FooBar alternately" is two semaphores passing a turn back and forth, foo starting at 1 and bar at 0. "Building H2O" is admission control plus a barrier (see the questions). "The dining philosophers" is solution A or B. In all of them, state the invariant first ("at most two H in the current molecule"), choose the primitive that encodes it, and check the two failure modes: can a thread wait forever (liveness), and can two threads be inside something together that should be exclusive (safety)?

Why the semaphore bounded buffer needs no buffer inspection

A subtle strength of the semaphore solution is that the counting semaphores encode the invariant empty + full + (items in transit) = N. A producer that returns from wait(empty) has reserved a slot even before taking the mutex, so the mutex only orders index updates, and producers and consumers never have to look at the buffer to decide whether to wait. This is also why a lock-free single-producer single-consumer ring buffer is possible: with one producer and one consumer, the producer only writes tail and the consumer only writes head, each reads the other's with acquire semantics, and no mutex is needed at all. That design powers kernel network-card rings, audio buffers and io_uring's submission and completion queues (os11).

Choosing a condition-variable signalling strategy

Three refinements matter in high-throughput queues. Signal only on transitions that can unblock someone (put only needs to signal when the queue was empty, get only when it was full), which avoids futile wake-ups at the cost of more delicate code. Signal after updating state but before or after releasing the mutex? Signalling while holding the mutex is always correct; signalling after releasing it can save a wake-then-block bounce, and with modern futex "requeue" optimisations the difference is small. And for many consumers, batch: a consumer that drains up to k items per lock acquisition cuts lock traffic by k. Java's LinkedBlockingQueue goes further by using two locks, one for the head and one for the tail, so a producer and a consumer never contend unless the queue is nearly empty.

The cost model of reader-writer locks

A reader-writer lock looks like free concurrency for readers, but each reader acquisition atomically increments a shared counter and each release decrements it. On a 64-core machine with short read sections, that counter's cache line moves between cores on every operation, so readers serialise on cache traffic even though they never block each other logically. Benchmarks often show a plain mutex beating an rwlock for sections under a microsecond. Scalable alternatives distribute the reader count: per-CPU reader counters that writers must sum (Linux's percpu-rwsem; "big-reader" locks), seqlocks where readers write nothing, and RCU or copy-on-write publication where readers just load a pointer. The rule of thumb: an rwlock helps when read sections are long (microseconds or more) and writers are rare; for tiny reads of rarely-changed data, publish immutable snapshots.

Starvation-free dining: Chandy-Misra

For a distributed version (philosophers are processes that communicate only by messages), Chandy and Misra's 1984 solution makes forks "clean" or "dirty". Initially each fork is dirty and held by the lower-numbered neighbour, which makes the precedence graph acyclic. A philosopher who wants a fork asks its holder; the holder keeps a clean fork but must give up a dirty one, cleaning it before sending. After eating, both forks become dirty. Because a philosopher who has just eaten always yields to a hungry neighbour, nobody starves, and the graph stays acyclic so nobody deadlocks. The idea of ordering by recent use, so the one who waited longest wins, reappears in fair lock designs and in wound-wait and wait-die schemes in databases (os6).

The kernel's own bounded buffer: the pipe

Every shell pipeline is a producer-consumer system built by the kernel. A Linux pipe is a ring of page-sized buffers, 64 KiB in total by default (adjustable with fcntl(F_SETPIPE_SZ)). A writer that fills it blocks in write() until the reader drains some; a reader of an empty pipe blocks in read() until data arrives or every writer has closed its end, at which point read() returns 0 (end of file). Inside, the kernel uses exactly this lesson's design: a mutex on the pipe and two wait queues, one for readers and one for writers. That is why producer | slow_consumer automatically slows the producer down: backpressure for free (os12).

The sleeping barber and admission control

Another classic, Dijkstra's sleeping barber, models a server with a waiting room of fixed size: customers who find the waiting room full leave immediately rather than block. Its solution is a bounded buffer whose producers use a non-blocking try on the free-slot count instead of waiting. That is exactly load shedding: a web server that returns 503 when its request queue is full, rather than queueing indefinitely. Recognising when a producer should block (backpressure within a process or pipeline) versus give up immediately (shedding load from external clients who will retry) is a real design decision in every queue you build.

Bounded queues are your backpressure

Every production pipeline has queues: thread-pool task queues, in-process buffers before a database writer, socket buffers, broker topics. The most common failure is an unbounded queue between a fast producer and a slow consumer. Java's Executors.newFixedThreadPool uses an unbounded LinkedBlockingQueue, and Python's ThreadPoolExecutor also queues without limit, so a traffic spike or a slow downstream silently turns into gigabytes of queued tasks, then minutes of latency, then an out-of-memory crash. Bound the queue, decide what happens when it is full (block the producer, reject with an error, drop the oldest), and expose its depth as a metric. Queue depth that trends upward at constant input is the earliest warning that consumers are falling behind.

Thundering herds

Broadcast-style wake-ups appear in real systems beyond condition variables. Historically, many processes blocked in accept() or epoll_wait() on the same listening socket would all be woken by one incoming connection, and all but one would find nothing to do; Linux added EPOLLEXCLUSIVE and SO_REUSEPORT (net13) to wake only one. Cache stampedes are the distributed version: a popular cache entry expires and a thousand requests all recompute it at once (sc2's request coalescing and early refresh are the fixes). The common cure is to make one waiter do the work while the rest wait for its result.

Reader-writer locks in services

Read-mostly shared state (feature flags, routing tables, model registries, in-memory caches) is often protected with a reader-writer lock. Watch for three production problems: writer starvation under reader preference (config updates that never apply under load), reader stalls under writer preference (a slow writer stops all requests), and upgrade deadlocks (two readers each try to upgrade to a writer lock and wait for each other). The robust pattern is copy-on-write publication: build the new version privately, swap an atomic reference, and let readers use whichever version they loaded. Go's atomic.Value/atomic.Pointer, Java's volatile references to immutable maps, and Python's plain attribute rebinding (atomic under the GIL) all support it.

Deadlock in real systems looks like the philosophers

A bank transfer service that locks the source account then the destination deadlocks when transfers A→B and B→A run together; the fix is solution A, locking accounts in ID order. Databases see the same thing between transactions that update rows in different orders, detect the cycle, and abort one (os6, sc5); applications that order their updates avoid most of those aborts. In microservices, two services that each hold a connection or a lock while calling the other recreate the table at a larger scale. In each case, look for the cycle and break it with a global order, a single coordinator, or timeouts with back-off.

Diagnosing a hung pipeline

When a producer-consumer system stops, take a thread dump (jstack, py-spy dump, Go's SIGQUIT stack dump) and classify each thread: waiting on not-full, waiting on not-empty, waiting on a lock, or running. "Everyone waiting on conditions and nobody running" is a lost or misdirected signal (the single-condition-variable bug, or a notify outside the lock); "consumers all blocked on something downstream" is a dependency stall; "producers blocked on a full queue while consumers are blocked on a lock the producer holds" is the wrong-order deadlock from section 2.

Build the bounded buffer, prove it conserves items, then deadlock the philosophers and fix them. Python 3 on any OS.

Design: a log shipper that must never block the request path

Your web service handles 20,000 requests per second across 64 worker threads. Each request produces one log record (about 500 bytes), which must be shipped to a remote collector over the network in batches. The collector occasionally stalls for 30 seconds. Requirements: request latency must never be affected by the collector, memory used for logs must stay under 200 MB, and the team wants to know when logs are dropped. Design the synchronization between request threads and the shipping thread, and say what happens during a 30-second stall.

Answer

Shape. This is producer-consumer: 64 producers (request threads) and one consumer (the shipper). Use a bounded buffer between them. The bound comes from the memory budget: 200 MB / 500 bytes ≈ 400,000 records; choose, say, 300,000 to leave headroom. At 20,000 records/s that holds 15 seconds of logs.

Producer behaviour. Request threads must never block, so they cannot use a blocking put. They use a non-blocking try-put (the sleeping-barber pattern): if the buffer is full, drop the record and increment a logs_dropped counter (an atomic or per-thread counter to avoid contention). Alternatively drop the oldest record (a ring that overwrites), which keeps the most recent context; either way, count drops and export them as a metric with an alert.

Contention. 64 threads × 20,000/s through one mutex is plausible but the lock will be hot. Keep the critical section to a pointer append, and serialise records before taking the lock. If profiling shows contention, shard: one small buffer per worker thread (or per CPU), drained by the shipper in turn, so producers never contend with each other.

Consumer behaviour. The shipper waits on the not-empty condition (or wakes every 100 ms, whichever comes first), takes up to, say, 5,000 records per lock acquisition (batching cuts lock traffic), releases the lock, and sends the batch over the network outside the lock. It retries a failed send with back-off, holding at most one batch in hand.

During a 30-second stall. The shipper is stuck sending one batch. Producers keep adding: the buffer fills after about 15 seconds; for the remaining 15 seconds, roughly 300,000 records are dropped, the drop counter climbs, and an alert fires. Request latency is unaffected because no request thread ever waits, and memory stays at the bound. When the collector recovers, the shipper drains the backlog in large batches. If losing logs is unacceptable, the answer changes: spill to local disk when the buffer is full (a second, much larger bounded buffer) rather than blocking requests.
