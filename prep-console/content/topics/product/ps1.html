Picture a strong engineer in the last round of a senior ML loop. The prompt is "design the recommendations for our short-video app". They draw a clean two-stage system: candidate generation with embeddings, a ranking model with a few hundred features, a feature store, a serving path that hits a 100-millisecond budget. Twenty-five minutes in, the interviewer asks a quiet question: "What is the ranking model optimising, and how would you know the launch was a success?" The candidate says "click-through rate, and offline AUC". The interviewer follows up: "Clicks are up 4% in the experiment, but people who got the new model are 1% less likely to come back next week. Do you ship?" The candidate hesitates, then says "probably yes, clicks are the main metric".

That answer costs the level. Not because the architecture was weak, but because the candidate showed they could build a system without being able to say what it is for or whether it worked. A ranking model that drives clicks while users quietly leave is a well-engineered way of damaging the product. At senior levels, interviewers expect you to catch that before anyone asks.

The same gap shows up in other places. In "how would you add an LLM to this product?" questions, the weak answer jumps to the prompt and the vector database; the strong one asks who the feature is for, what success looks like, what it costs per user and what happens when the model is wrong. In metric questions ("daily active users dropped 10%, what happened?"), the weak answer lists guesses; the strong one has a method. In behavioural rounds, the weak impact story says "we launched X"; the strong one says what moved, by how much, and how they knew it was the launch that moved it.

This lesson defines product sense in terms an engineer can practise. It covers the four judgements it is made of, where senior loops test it, what interviewers actually write on their scorecards, and a six-step structure (users, goal, metrics, solution, risks, launch) that works for most product and metric questions. It ends with a full worked example with numbers, the habits that separate senior answers from mid-level ones, and how the same thinking shows up in real engineering work. The rest of the track goes deep on each step.

Product sense is the judgement to decide what to build and for whom, define what success means before you build it, choose a solution that fits the problem, anticipate what could go wrong, and decide from evidence whether the thing you built should stay. For an ML or AI engineer it has a sharper edge: a model optimises exactly the objective you give it, so choosing that objective is a product decision that engineers make every day, whether or not they think of it that way.

A useful analogy is a good doctor's consultation. The doctor does not start by prescribing. They first find out who the patient is and what is bothering them (the user and the problem). They decide what "better" would look like for this patient: no pain when walking, not a perfect blood test (the goal). They pick a few measurements that track it, plus a few that must not get worse, such as kidney function while on a drug (metrics and guardrails). Then they choose the least invasive treatment that could work, rest and physiotherapy before surgery (the solution, simplest first). They warn about side effects and what to watch for (risks), and they book a follow-up to check whether it worked (launch and measure).

The analogy breaks in two useful places. First, a doctor has one patient; a product has millions of users whose interests can conflict. A change that delights power users can confuse new ones, and a feed that helps viewers watch more may starve small creators. Much of product judgement is weighing groups against each other. Second, a doctor cannot easily give half of the patients the treatment and half a placebo for every decision, but a software product can. Online experiments let you measure what a change did rather than guess, which is why "how would you measure it" is a central question in every round.

The order matters more than the names. Metrics come before the solution because if you pick the solution first, you will unconsciously pick metrics it is good at. The solution step says "simplest first" because the cheapest thing that could work, often a hand-written rule or a popularity list, is both your baseline and sometimes your answer. Risks come before launch because the launch plan must include ways to detect each risk. And the dashed arrow back to the goal is the honest part: most launches teach you something that changes what you aim for next.

A reader who stops here has a correct, simplified picture: product sense means starting from users and a clearly stated goal, measuring the right thing, preferring simple solutions, naming risks, and letting evidence decide. The body explains each piece, how interviewers score it, and how to run the whole structure in a timed round.

One more framing helps. Product questions in interviews come in three shapes, and the same structure handles all of them with a different emphasis. A design question ("build X", "add an LLM to Y") spends most of its time on users, goal and solution. A measurement question ("how would you measure the success of X?") spends most of its time on metrics and launch. A diagnosis question ("metric Z dropped, why?") runs the structure backwards: it starts from a moved metric and works back to which users, which goal and which change explain it. Recognising which shape you have been given in the first minute tells you where to spend your time.

Finally, notice what the structure does not contain: there is no step called "ideas". Ideas appear inside the solution step, after the user, goal and metrics are fixed, and they are judged against those metrics. That ordering is what separates product sense from brainstorming. A candidate who produces ten clever features in the first five minutes has shown creativity; a candidate who produces one feature, explains whom it helps, how its success would be measured and what it would cost, has shown judgement. Interviews for senior engineers reward the second, and so do real launch reviews.

1. The four judgements inside product sense

"Product sense" sounds like a talent. It is more useful to treat it as four separate judgements you can practise and be scored on.

The first is understanding users and their problem. A product serves people with different needs, and the same feature can help one group and hurt another. The judgement is to name the groups that matter (a segment is a subset of users sharing a property such as being new, being on mobile or being a creator rather than a viewer), say which one you are designing for and why, and describe the problem in their words rather than the company's. "Busy parents waste twenty minutes every evening deciding what to cook" is a user problem. "We need more engagement" is a company wish.

The second is defining success. Before building anything you decide what would count as it working, and you express that as metrics. A metric is a number computed from logged behaviour or survey answers, defined precisely enough that two people would compute the same value. The judgement is choosing metrics that move when users genuinely get more value and do not move when they do not. ps2 is entirely about this.

The third is choosing the solution, including whether it should involve machine learning at all. The judgement is to propose the simplest approach that could reach the goal, compare it with more complex ones on expected value and cost, and say what evidence would justify the step up. For ML and AI engineers this includes translating the product goal into a training objective (ps3) and deciding whether an LLM is worth its cost per query (ps8).

The fourth is judging outcomes: reading an experiment, weighing a win on one metric against a loss on another, diagnosing why a number moved, and making a launch call. This is where most real decisions happen, and where engineers who built the system have the most to add, because they know what the logs mean. ps4, ps6 and ps7 cover it.

Interviews rarely announce which judgement they are testing. A system design prompt tests the first three in its opening minutes; a metric-drop question tests the fourth; a "should we build X" question tests all four. Knowing the four lets you notice which one the interviewer is probing when they interrupt.

2. Where it shows up in senior loops

For ML and AI engineers, product judgement is tested in five places, usually without a round being labelled "product sense".

The ML system design round. Most formats for this round, including the widely described format used for ML engineers at large social and ads companies, start with problem framing: what is the business goal, what does the model predict, what label do you train on, which offline metric, which online metric. Candidates who rush through this to reach the architecture are often scored down even when the architecture is good, because the framing determines whether the system is useful. The worked recommendation, search, ads and feed designs in this app (hd3, hd9, hd10, hd11) each have a metrics section for this reason.

"Add AI to this product" questions. A common senior AI-engineer prompt: "We run an email client. How would you add AI summaries?" or "Our support team is overwhelmed; would you build an LLM bot?" These test whether you can decide if the feature is worth it, define success and a launch bar, design for the model being wrong, and estimate cost per user. ps8 to ps10 cover them.

The metric part of every design round. Even a classic infrastructure design, such as a URL shortener or a notification service, ends with "how would you know it is working?" Availability and latency are the engineering answer; a senior answer also says how you would know users are getting value (are shortened links clicked? do notifications get opened and do users not turn them off?).

Product analytics and metric rounds. Some loops, especially for applied scientists and ML engineers working close to products, include a dedicated case: define metrics for a feature, diagnose a drop, or design an experiment. ps4 to ps7 and the cases in ps12 practise these directly.

Behavioural rounds. "Tell me about a project with significant impact" is a product-sense question in disguise. Interviewers listen for whether you chose what to work on for a reason, how you measured impact, and whether you can separate your change's effect from everything else that moved at the same time (beh6 covers story structure).

4. The six-step structure, step by step

Step 1, users. Name two to four segments, say who you are designing for, and state their problem in one or two sentences. Useful axes: new versus established users, producers versus consumers (creators and viewers, sellers and buyers, drivers and riders), free versus paying, and heavy versus light users. If the product is a marketplace, say upfront that a change for one side affects the other.

Step 2, goal. One sentence, in user terms, that a non-engineer would agree with: "help busy parents decide what to cook in under two minutes and cook at home more often". The goal is not a metric yet; it is what the metrics are trying to capture. If the company's goal (revenue, growth) differs from the user goal, say how they connect: "if they cook more, they stay subscribed".

Step 3, metrics. One primary metric that best captures the goal and that an experiment can move within a few weeks; two or three guardrail metrics that must not get significantly worse; and, when the primary metric can be gamed, a counter-metric that catches the most likely gaming. Mention a diagnostic metric or two (funnel steps) only if they help explain movements. ps2 covers how to choose these well.

Step 4, solution. Start with the simplest approach that could move the primary metric, often a heuristic or a rule. Then describe the stronger approach (a model, an LLM) and what it would add. Make the comparison concrete: expected improvement, build effort, running cost, risk. For ML features, this is also where you state the training label and objective and how they relate to the primary metric.

Step 5, risks. List the two to four most important ways this could go wrong: for users (wrong or harmful outputs, confusion, privacy), for the business (cost, cannibalising another feature, brand damage) and for measurement (the metric moves for the wrong reason). For each, say how you would detect it: a guardrail, a review sample, an alert.

Step 6, launch. How you would test (usually a randomised experiment, sometimes a staged rollout), how long and on how many users, what result would make you ship, and what you would watch afterwards. Writing the launch criteria before the test starts is the single habit that most protects a team from fooling itself; ps7 covers it.

5. Clarifying questions that earn their time

Open prompts are deliberately vague. Some candidates respond with ten questions; others with none. Both lose time or miss the point. A clarifying question is worth asking only if its answer would change what you propose. Three tests help: does it change who the user is, does it change what success means, or does it rule a solution in or out?

For "add AI meal plans to our recipe app", good questions are "is this for all users or a subscriber feature?" (changes the goal: engagement versus conversion), "are there hard constraints such as allergies or medical diets?" (rules out loose generation without a deterministic filter), and "is the aim to grow, retain or monetise?" (changes the primary metric). Weak questions are ones whose answer you would not use: "what tech stack do you use?" or "how many engineers do we have?" in the first minute.

When the interviewer answers "you decide", that is an invitation to show judgement. Pick, say why in one sentence, and note what would change for the other choice. Then convert everything else into stated assumptions with numbers ("I'll assume four million monthly users, a fifth of them subscribers"). Assumptions are not a weakness; they make your reasoning checkable, and they give the interviewer an easy way to steer you.

6. A worked example, end to end

Prompt: "Panfry is a recipe app (a hypothetical company). Leadership wants AI-generated weekly meal plans. How would you approach it?" Here is a strong answer, compressed, with the numbers made explicit.

Clarify. "Is this a subscriber feature? Any hard dietary constraints?" Interviewer: "Subscriber feature; allergies matter." Assumptions stated aloud: 4 million monthly active users, 900,000 weekly active cooks, 200,000 paying subscribers at $5 a month, and cooking from the app is logged when someone opens step-by-step "cook mode" and reaches the final step.

Users. Three segments: busy parents planning for a family, fitness-focused users tracking protein and calories, and occasional cooks browsing for inspiration. The plan feature targets the first: their problem is decision fatigue and the weekly shop, not a lack of recipes. Fitness users are a good second segment later; occasional cooks are unlikely to plan a week.

Goal. "Help subscribers who cook for a household decide the week's meals in a couple of minutes, so they cook from Panfry more often and stay subscribed."

Metrics. The primary metric is completed cooking sessions per weekly active subscriber, because it measures the value (cooking happened), it is per user (not inflated by growth), and it should respond within weeks. Today's assumed value is 1.6 sessions per week with a standard deviation of 1.9. Guardrails: subscription cancellation rate, allergy and complaint reports per thousand plans, plan-generation latency at the 95th percentile, and cost per plan. Counter-metric: plans generated but never cooked from, which would rise if a pushy prompt inflated plan creation without value. Diagnostic: the funnel from "open planner" to "plan accepted" to "at least one planned recipe cooked".

Solution. Baseline first: a rule-based planner that picks seven highly rated recipes matching the user's saved diet tags, avoids repeats from the last two weeks and balances cooking time across weekdays. It costs nothing per plan and can ship in two weeks. The AI version uses an LLM to take free-text preferences ("kids hate mushrooms, Tuesday is football night, use up the spinach") and assemble a plan from the existing recipe catalogue, never inventing recipes. Allergens are filtered by a deterministic check against recipe ingredient data after the model proposes a plan, because a model's "I avoided peanuts" is not a guarantee. The question the experiment must answer is whether the LLM version beats the rule-based one by enough to pay for itself.

Cost. Assume a plan request uses about 2,500 input tokens (preferences plus a shortlist of candidate recipes) and 1,200 output tokens. At October 2026 list prices for a small model (Claude Haiku 4.5 at $1 per million input tokens and $5 per million output tokens), that is 2,500 × $1/1M + 1,200 × $5/1M = $0.0025 + $0.006 = $0.0085 per plan. If 300,000 subscribers each generate one plan a week, that is $2,550 a week, about $133,000 a year. At $60 a year per subscriber, the feature pays for its model costs if it keeps about 133,000 / 60 ≈ 2,200 extra subscribers a year, about 1.1% of the 200,000 base. That is a plausible bar, which makes the idea worth testing; if the arithmetic had needed 20% of subscribers, the answer would be to start with the rule-based planner. (Prices move fast: the newer Claude Haiku 5.5 lists a tenth of Haiku 4.5's price for prompts up to 100,000 tokens, which would cut the bill to about $13,000 a year and make the cost bar almost irrelevant; the question would then be whether the cheaper model's plans are good enough, which is ps8's subject.)

Risks. Allergen mistakes (detected by the deterministic filter and by allergy reports, which are a guardrail with a hard threshold of zero confirmed incidents); plans users do not cook from (the counter-metric); cost growing if users regenerate plans repeatedly (cap regenerations, watch cost per subscriber); and the novelty of a new feature inflating early usage (watch the effect week by week, ps6).

Launch. A randomised experiment among subscribers with three arms: control (no planner), rule-based planner and LLM planner. To detect a 3% relative lift on a mean of 1.6 (δ = 0.048) with standard deviation 1.9, the usual approximation for 80% power at 5% significance (math7) gives about n = 16σ²/δ² = 16 × 3.61 / 0.0023 ≈ 25,000 subscribers per arm. With 200,000 subscribers, 25,000 per arm is affordable, and the test runs three weeks so it covers several weekly cycles. Launch criteria, written before the test: ship the LLM planner if it beats the rule-based planner on the primary metric by at least 3% with no guardrail regression and zero confirmed allergen incidents; otherwise ship the rule-based planner if it beats control.

Summary. "Target households, measure cooking not clicking, test a cheap rule-based planner against the LLM one, and let the LLM earn its $133,000 a year."

7. Communicating while you think

Structure is only visible if you say it. Start by announcing the plan: "I'll spend a couple of minutes on who this is for and what success means, then the solution, then risks and how we'd launch." Signpost transitions ("that's my metric set; now the solution"). At the end of each step, summarise the decision in one sentence. When the interviewer interrupts, treat it as information: an interruption usually means "go deeper here" or "you are off track", and either way the right response is to follow it.

Think out loud, but in finished thoughts. "Hmm, maybe clicks, or maybe time spent, or maybe…" is hard to score. "Two candidates for the primary metric: clicks, which respond fast but can be gamed by thumbnails, and cooked sessions, which are closer to value; I'll take cooked sessions" shows the same thinking and a decision.

Metrics are contracts between teams

At a company of any size, a metric is not just a measurement; it is an agreement about what counts as success, and teams organise their work around it. If the recommendations team is measured on watch time and the creator team on uploads, each will make choices that look good on its own metric and may hurt the other's. A senior engineer notices when the metric they are given conflicts with a neighbouring team's, and raises it before the conflict becomes a launch-review argument. In interviews, saying "I'd check this doesn't conflict with what the creator team is measured on" signals that you have worked in real organisations.

Dashboard metrics versus decision metrics

A company tracks hundreds of metrics on dashboards to monitor health. Only a few are used to make launch decisions. The difference matters: a dashboard metric can be noisy, lagging or hard to attribute, because it is for spotting trends; a decision metric must be sensitive enough to move in an experiment's duration, attributable to the change being tested, and agreed in advance. Many weak answers name a company-level dashboard metric (monthly active users, revenue) as the launch criterion for a small feature. The feature cannot plausibly move it enough to detect within weeks, so the experiment would come back flat whatever the feature did. The senior move is to pick a decision metric that the feature can move and to explain how it connects to the company-level metric.

Reframing the prompt, politely

Sometimes the prompt contains a bad assumption: "how do we increase time spent in our meditation app?" (most meditation users want a short, effective session, not more time). Strong candidates name the tension and propose a better goal ("sessions completed per week and a self-reported calm score"), while still answering the question asked. The skill is doing this without lecturing: acknowledge the business reason behind the original goal, show how the reframed goal serves it better in the long run, and let the interviewer choose. Interviewers often plant such a flaw deliberately to see whether you notice it.

Second-order effects and ecosystems

Many products are ecosystems: viewers and creators, riders and drivers, buyers and sellers, readers and advertisers. A change that helps one side shifts behaviour on the other, which shifts the first side's experience again weeks later. A feed change that favours already-popular videos may raise viewer watch time this month and reduce uploads from small creators over the next quarter, which narrows what viewers can watch next year. Senior answers name the other side of the ecosystem, pick a guardrail for it, and acknowledge that a standard short experiment measures only the first-order effect (ps6 covers designs for the rest).

Short-term versus long-term

Experiments typically run for a few weeks, but the business cares about months and years. Some changes win short-term and lose long-term: more notifications raise opens this week and raise disabling of notifications over months; more ads raise revenue today and reduce usage later. Google's Rules of Machine Learning puts it directly in Rule 39: launch decisions are a proxy for long-term product goals. Senior answers say which direction the long-term effect is likely to go for this specific change, and propose a holdout (a small group kept on the old experience for months) when the risk is real.

When data cannot decide

Some decisions cannot be tested well: a change with network effects across the whole product, a brand-level change, or something with small samples (an enterprise product with 300 customers). Other decisions are cheap to reverse and should simply be made quickly. Strong candidates distinguish these: for reversible, low-cost changes they decide fast and monitor; for irreversible or high-stakes ones they invest in better evidence (a longer test, a staged rollout, user research). Saying "this is a two-way door, so I'd ship behind a flag and watch the guardrails" is product judgement too.

Twyman's law and scepticism about your own wins

Twyman's law is the experimenter's rule of thumb that any figure that looks interesting or different is usually wrong. In practice, a result far better than expected is more likely to come from a bug (logging double-counting, a broken control, bots in one arm) than from a breakthrough. The Bing ad-headline experiment triggered exactly such an alert: the treatment produced revenue so high that an automated "too good to be true" check fired, and the team investigated before believing it. Here the result was real. The habit is what matters: a senior engineer's first reaction to a huge win is to check the data, and to say so in the interview.

What changes for AI and LLM features

Product judgement for features built on a large language model (LLM, a model trained on large amounts of text that generates text in response to a prompt) has three extra dimensions that classic features lack. First, the output is variable and sometimes wrong, so "how good is it?" is a distribution rather than a yes or no: the feature might be excellent 90% of the time and embarrassing 2% of the time, and the product design has to decide what the user sees in that 2% (ps9). Second, every use has a marginal cost. A classic feature costs roughly the same whether 1,000 or 1,000,000 people use it; an LLM feature's bill scales with usage, so success can be expensive, and cost per active user becomes a guardrail (ps8). Third, the offline evaluation of quality and the online measure of value are further apart than usual: a summary can score well on a quality rubric and still not save anyone time (ps10).

In interviews, a candidate who raises these three points unprompted, with a rough number for each (an error rate the product can tolerate, a cost per user, and a plan to measure value online rather than only quality offline), is showing exactly the senior AI-engineer judgement the question was written to find.

Depth on demand

Interviewers test whether your product reasoning rests on real understanding by drilling down: "how exactly is 'cooked session' logged?", "what's the denominator?", "how many users do you need?", "what does a plan cost?". A candidate who can go from "help families cook more" down to an event definition, a power calculation and a cost per request in two minutes is showing the full range a senior role needs. Practise this deliberately: for every metric you propose, be ready to define its numerator, denominator, time window and logging source.

Where product sense lives in real engineering work

Outside interviews, product judgement shows up as artefacts and rituals. A design document for an ML feature has a "goals and non-goals" section and a "success metrics" section; reviewers push back when the success metric is missing or is a model metric only. A launch review examines the experiment results against criteria agreed before the experiment ran. Metric owners, a named team or person for each important metric, maintain its definition, so that "weekly active user" means the same thing in every dashboard. Post-launch reviews a few months later check whether the effect lasted. Engineers who want senior scope take part in all of these, not just the build.

Characteristic failure modes

Shipping without instrumentation. The feature launches, and only then does someone ask how to measure it, discovering that the key event was never logged. Rule 2 of Google's Rules of Machine Learning, "first, design and implement metrics", exists because this is common. The symptom is a launch review where every chart starts on launch day, so there is no before-and-after comparison.

Choosing the metric after seeing the data. A team runs an experiment, looks at forty metrics, finds three that improved, and declares success on those. With forty metrics and a 5% false-positive rate, about two would look significant by chance alone. The fix is pre-registered launch criteria (ps7).

Local optimisation. A team improves its own metric at another team's expense: a notifications team raises opens by sending more, the app-store rating falls, and the growth team pays for it. The symptom is a company-level metric drifting down while every team reports wins. Guardrails owned by other teams are the remedy.

Average hides the story. An effect that is positive overall but strongly negative for new users can quietly damage growth. Segment checks for the segments that matter (new users, the main platforms, the largest countries) belong in every launch review, interpreted with care (ps4 and ps7).

Mistaking model metrics for product metrics. An ML team reports a 3-point AUC gain as the outcome. Nobody checks that users benefited. Offline metrics are necessary for iterating on models, but the launch decision belongs to online product metrics (ml16).

A real case: the Bing ad headline

Ron Kohavi and Stefan Thomke describe in Harvard Business Review (2017) how, in 2012, a Microsoft employee working on Bing proposed changing the way ad headlines were displayed. It would take an engineer only a few days, but it was one of hundreds of ideas, the program managers judged it low priority, and it sat for more than six months. An engineer who saw how cheap it was finally ran an A/B test. Within hours, revenue in the treatment was so high that it triggered a "too good to be true" alert, which usually signals a bug. Analysis showed the change had increased revenue by 12%, which on an annual basis came to more than $100 million in the United States alone, without hurting key user-experience metrics. The article calls it the best revenue-generating idea in Bing's history.

Three lessons from this case carry through the whole track. First, experienced people are poor at predicting which ideas will matter, so a cheap way to test ideas is worth more than confident prioritisation. Second, the result was trusted because the team had both a guardrail set ("without hurting key user-experience metrics") and a habit of distrusting surprising wins. Third, the success criterion was clear in advance: revenue per search was the goal, and user-experience metrics were the guardrails. Without those, a 12% revenue jump could just as easily have been a change that annoyed users into clicking ads, which is exactly the trap ps2 examines.

Signals that a team has weak product judgement

You can often spot it from the outside: dashboards dominated by raw counts that rise with growth; launches justified by "users loved it in the demo"; experiment write-ups with no pre-stated criteria; arguments in launch reviews about what a metric means; and no one able to say what the model's label is a proxy for. In an interview, describing one of these from your own experience, and what you changed, is a strong behavioural answer.

Framing drill (8 minutes)

Tunely, a hypothetical music-streaming app with 30 million monthly users, wants to add an "AI DJ" that talks between songs, introducing tracks and explaining why they were picked. The interviewer asks: "How would you decide whether to build this, and how would you know it worked?" Answer using the six steps, with at least three numbers.

Answer

Users. Lean-back listeners who use radio-style stations and do not want to choose songs (assume 30% of monthly users); active playlist curators (who will mostly ignore a DJ); and new users who have not yet shown taste, for whom explanations might speed up discovery. Target lean-back listeners first.

Goal. "Make lean-back listening feel personal enough that users listen longer per session and discover artists they go on to save."

Metrics. Primary: listening minutes per weekly active lean-back user, because the DJ's value shows up as longer, more satisfying sessions. Counter-metric: skip rate in the first 10 seconds after a DJ segment, and "turn off DJ" rate, because talking can annoy. Guardrails: next-week return rate, premium cancellations, and cost per listening hour. Diagnostic: new artists saved per user, which tests the discovery claim.

Solution. Baseline: templated, pre-recorded intros using metadata ("next up, a 2019 track from an artist you saved"). AI version: an LLM writes short scripts from listening history and track metadata, converted to speech. Cost estimate: one 60-word segment every 4 songs is about 4 segments an hour; if each script costs about $0.001 in model tokens and speech synthesis costs about $0.004, that is about $0.02 per listening hour. With 9 million lean-back users listening 5 hours a week, that is about $900,000 a week, a serious cost to justify, so the experiment must show a meaningful lift or the segments must be cached and reused across users.

Risks. Annoyance (counter-metrics), factual errors about artists (sample review, a source of truth for facts), cost (cap segments per hour), and novelty (track the effect week by week).

Launch. A three-arm test (control, templated, AI) for four weeks among lean-back users. Ship the AI DJ only if it beats templated intros on listening minutes by a pre-agreed margin large enough to pay for its cost, with no increase in DJ-off rate beyond a threshold and no drop in next-week return rate.

The strong signal here is the cost estimate changing the plan: once you see roughly $900,000 a week, you either need a large lift or a cheaper design, and you say so.
