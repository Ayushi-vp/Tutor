Six months after launching an AI coding assistant inside its developer platform, a company reviews the results. The team's slides are confident: 38% of new code in enabled repositories was "written by AI", the thumbs-up rate is 84%, and weekly active users of the assistant doubled. The finance lead asks a simple question: are developers more productive, and by how much? Nobody can answer. "Code written by AI" counts characters inserted from suggestions, including ones later deleted. Thumbs-up comes from 0.3% of interactions. Weekly active users doubled because the assistant was enabled by default. Meanwhile the ML team's offline evaluation score improved 6 points with a new model, and online acceptance went down.

The team measured what was easy to count, not what the feature was for. This is the typical state of AI feature measurement: plenty of activity metrics, a few satisfaction signals, an offline evaluation that does not predict online results, and no answer to the question that decides whether the feature deserves its cost.

Measuring AI features well is hard for specific reasons. The output is variable, so "did it work?" is a distribution. Users interact with outputs in several ways (accept, edit, ignore, regenerate) that mean different things. The value often appears in a later task (code that ships, a ticket that stays closed), not in the interaction itself. Offline evaluations, which teams depend on to iterate quickly, measure quality on fixed inputs with rubrics that may not match what users value. And many AI metrics are ratios that can be moved by changing the denominator.

This lesson builds a measurement approach for the main kinds of AI feature: suggestion features (copilots), conversational assistants, support bots and agents. It covers a ladder of metrics from usage to business value; acceptance and persistence for suggestions, using GitHub's study of Copilot users as the real case; task completion for assistants and agents; deflection with quality for support; credible time-saved measurement; implicit signals; and the reasons offline evals and online metrics disagree, which ev8 introduced and this lesson turns into a checklist.

A warning that applies throughout: AI features are new enough that many organisations have no agreed metrics for them, and the vacuum fills with whatever numbers make the feature look good. Engineers who can propose an honest metric set, and explain why the flattering numbers mislead, are doing some of the most valuable product work available on an AI team.

Think about how you would judge whether a new junior colleague is helping a team. You could count how many emails they send (activity). You could count how many of their drafts colleagues use (engagement with their output). You could check whether the used drafts are still in the final documents a week later or were rewritten (quality). You could look at whether the team finishes projects faster (task outcome). And you could ask whether the business is better off: more clients served, fewer mistakes (value). Each step is closer to what matters, takes longer to observe, and is harder to attribute to the colleague alone.

AI features need the same ladder. The bottom rungs are easy and fast; the top rungs are what justify the feature. Good measurement uses all of them: bottom rungs for fast iteration and diagnosis, top rungs for decisions, and explicit validation that movements on the lower rungs predict movements on the higher ones.

The analogy breaks in one important way. A colleague's work is judged by a manager who sees context; an AI feature's outputs number in millions and are mostly unseen. So measurement relies on signals inferred from behaviour (did the user accept, edit, rephrase, give up?) and on sampling outputs for expert review, and both have to be designed into the product (ps9) rather than added later.

Different feature types live on different parts of the ladder. Copilots, features that offer suggestions inside a user's workflow (code completion, reply chips, writing suggestions), have a clean funnel at rungs 2 and 3: suggestions shown, accepted, kept. Assistants, which answer questions in a chat, have fuzzier engagement signals and need careful task-outcome definitions. Support bots have an established outcome (was the customer's issue resolved?) and a dangerous shortcut (deflection). Agents, which take multiple steps and act through tools, can often verify their own task outcomes (the tests pass, the booking is confirmed) and must be measured per task, including cost and human interventions. The body takes each in turn.

A reader who stops here should remember three rules. Measure on all five rungs, with each rung's metric clearly named. Make decisions on rungs 4 and 5, using experiments. And never trust a rung-2 ratio (acceptance rate, deflection rate, thumbs-up rate) without its numerator, denominator and a quality check beside it.

One more distinction runs through the lesson: metrics for iterating versus metrics for deciding. Iteration metrics must be fast, cheap and sensitive, so the team can compare two prompts in an afternoon; acceptance rate, an offline eval score and a judge's rating qualify. Decision metrics must be close to value and robust to gaming, so the company can decide whether the feature deserves its cost; resolution, task success and throughput qualify, measured in experiments. Teams get into trouble when they promote an iteration metric to a decision metric without checking that it predicts the outcome, which is the mistake the opening story's slides made.

2. Suggestions: the shown-accepted-persisted funnel

For a feature that offers suggestions inside a workflow, every interaction passes through a funnel. A completion opportunity arises (the user pauses typing). The system shows a suggestion or not. The user accepts it or not. The accepted text persists (is still there, unchanged or mostly unchanged) after some interval, or is edited away. Each step has a rate, and each rate can be gamed by changing the step before it.

GitHub's study of Copilot (Ziegler and colleagues, 2022) defined these measures precisely: shown rate (opportunities that resulted in a suggestion being shown), acceptance rate (shown suggestions accepted), persistence rate (accepted suggestions unchanged after 30, 120, 300 and 600 seconds) and a fuzzy version (mostly unchanged), plus volume measures per active hour such as accepted characters and accepted suggestions. The authors surveyed users about their perceived productivity using the SPACE framework (satisfaction and well-being, performance, activity, communication and collaboration, efficiency and flow) and received 2,047 responses they could match to usage data. In their data Copilot's acceptance rate was 27%.

The central finding: acceptance rate was a better predictor of perceived productivity than the more detailed persistence measures. All persistence measures correlated less well than acceptance rate, and persistence over shorter periods correlated better than over longer ones. The authors suggest acceptance rate can be used for coarse-grained monitoring of a code-completion system, and they note that acceptance varies significantly across users and over time; for example, it was relatively high at weekends and outside typical working hours.

Two cautions keep the finding in proportion. It is about perceived productivity, which matters for adoption but is not the same as measured time saved. And acceptance rate is a ratio. Consider two models:

A wins on acceptance rate and persistence rate; B delivers more surviving help per hour (30 versus 24). Whether B is better depends on what the extra 60 rejected suggestions cost the user in distraction, which a survey or an experiment on task time can measure. Reporting both a rate and a volume per active hour is the minimum; deciding on an outcome in an experiment is better.

3. Assistants: task completion and its proxies

For a chat assistant, the hardest part is defining success. A conversation ending is not success; the user may have given up. Useful definitions combine signals:

  • No reformulation: the user did not rephrase the same question within a few minutes.
  • No escalation: the user did not ask for a human or open a support ticket.
  • Positive follow-through: the user copied the answer, clicked a cited source, or took the action the answer described.
  • No repeat: the same user did not ask the same thing again within a day.

A resolved session can be defined as one with no reformulation, no escalation and no repeat, and validated against expert grading of a sample. Suppose experts grade 400 sessions: 300 judged resolved, 100 not. If the signal-based definition marks 290 as resolved, of which 260 are truly resolved, its precision is 260/290 = 90% and its recall 260/300 = 87%. Those two numbers tell you how far to trust the automatic metric, and they should be re-checked after major changes, because a new model can change how users behave around it.

4. Support bots: deflection with quality

Deflection rate, the share of contacts handled without reaching a human, is the metric support organisations most often cite for bots, and the most often misleading. Worked example: 10,000 contacts start with the bot in a week. 6,000 never reach a human, so deflection is 60%. But among those 6,000, 1,100 contact support again within 7 days through email or phone about the same issue, and 400 abandon after the bot's first answer and, in later analysis, churn at twice the normal rate. Resolved contacts are at most 6,000 − 1,100 − 400 = 4,500, 45% of contacts, and the true figure is lower still if some of the remainder were wrong answers users acted on.

The metric that should decide is resolution: contacts where the issue was solved with no repeat contact through any channel within a window, no reopened case, and acceptable satisfaction. Guardrails include customer satisfaction (CSAT) on bot conversations compared with human ones, complaints, and confirmed policy errors. A bot that is hard to escape will raise deflection and lower all of these, which is why deflection should be a diagnostic, never the goal. ps12 works through a case where deflection rose while satisfaction fell.

5. Agents: verified success and cost per successful task

An agent takes several steps, calling tools or acting in an environment, to complete a task: fixing a bug, booking travel, reconciling invoices. Agents have an advantage for measurement: many tasks have verifiable outcomes. A code change can be checked by tests; a booking by its confirmation and by whether it matches the request; a reconciliation by balances. Metrics follow:

  • Verified success rate: tasks whose outcome passes the check.
  • Human interventions: tasks where a person had to step in, and at which step.
  • Cost per successful task: total model and tool cost divided by successes, which penalises agents that fail often or loop. If an agent costs $0.40 per attempt and succeeds 50% of the time, each success costs $0.80; a cheaper agent at $0.25 per attempt with 25% success costs $1.00 per success.
  • Time to completion and steps per task, as diagnostics.
  • Harmful actions: irreversible wrong actions (a deleted file, a wrong payment), with a margin near zero.

Two cautions. Verification checks can be gamed: an agent that edits the tests to make them pass has "succeeded". Sample and grade successful tasks by hand, and make checks independent of what the agent can modify. And per-step success rates (tool calls that returned without error) are diagnostics for improving the agent, not evidence of value; value is measured per task, against a baseline of the user doing the task without the agent.

6. Time saved, measured credibly

"Time saved" is the most intuitive value claim and the easiest to get wrong. Self-reported savings (surveys asking "how much time does the tool save you?") reflect perception, which drives adoption but overstates or understates real effects in ways that depend on the user. Telemetry-based estimates ("each accepted suggestion saves the time to type it") assume the user would have typed exactly that, which ignores time spent reading and checking suggestions.

Credible estimates come from controlled comparisons. In a 2023 randomised trial by Peng and colleagues, developers asked to implement an HTTP server in JavaScript finished 55.8% faster with Copilot than without (about 71 minutes versus 161 on average), with a wide confidence interval, which is typical for small trials. In a field study by Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, the staggered introduction of an AI assistant to 5,179 customer-support agents raised issues resolved per hour by 14% on average, with a 34% improvement for novice and low-skilled workers and minimal impact on the most experienced. The pattern of larger gains for less experienced workers recurs in several studies and matters for measurement: average effects can hide that a feature helps some users a lot and others not at all.

For a product team, the practical version is an experiment on a throughput outcome: tickets resolved per agent-hour, pull requests merged per developer-week, documents processed per day, with the AI feature as the treatment. Converting time into money also needs care: saved minutes are only worth money if they turn into more output or less spending, so the outcome metric should be throughput or cost, not minutes.

The ambiguity means implicit signals must be validated, just like the resolved-session definition in section 3: grade a sample, compare with the signals, and use the signals that agree with the grading. Combining several signals is more robust than relying on one. And watch for shifts in meaning: if a redesign moves the copy button, copy rates change without any change in quality.

8. Why offline evals and online metrics disagree

Teams iterate on prompts and models using offline evaluations: a fixed set of inputs, outputs graded by rubric, often by an LLM judge (ev4). They launch based on online metrics. The two often disagree, and knowing why is a senior skill (ev8 introduced the problem). The main reasons:

Distribution shift. The eval set was written or sampled months ago; live traffic now includes new topics, longer inputs, other languages. A model better on the eval may be worse on what users now ask.

Rubric-value mismatch. The rubric rewards completeness; users want a short answer they can act on. A new model that writes longer, more complete answers wins the eval and loses online.

Latency and presentation. Offline evals ignore time. A better model that takes 3 seconds longer, or produces longer text that pushes the useful part below the fold, can lose online for reasons the eval cannot see.

Judge bias. LLM judges prefer certain styles (longer answers, answers from models similar to themselves, the first option shown). A model that matches the judge's taste can gain on the eval without gaining for users.

User adaptation. Users have learned how to work with the current model: how to phrase questions, which suggestions to trust. A new model, even a better one, breaks those habits, and early online results understate its value (a primacy effect, ps6), or the reverse.

Small online effects. A real improvement may be smaller than the experiment can detect (ps7), so "no online change" may be "no detectable change".

A worked example: a new model scores 81% versus 75% on the team's rubric eval. Online, acceptance of its answers falls from 42% to 41%. Investigation finds its answers are 60% longer, p95 latency is 1.8 seconds higher, and on a fresh sample of live questions graded by experts its advantage is 2 points, not 6. The eval set had over-represented complex questions where thoroughness helps. Fixes: refresh the eval from production samples, add a brevity criterion, include latency in the comparison, and only then rerun the experiment.

The structural defence is to track, across launches, how well offline gains have predicted online effects, exactly as ps2 recommends for any proxy. If the last eight model changes that won offline by more than 3 points produced online wins in only two cases, the eval is not predictive and needs rebuilding before it guides the next decision.

Keeping this plot up to date costs one row per launch: the offline delta, the online delta and its interval. After a dozen launches it tells you, better than any argument, how much weight the offline eval deserves in the next decision.

9. A complete metric set, worked

For an AI coding assistant in an internal developer platform (hypothetical, 3,000 developers):

Decision metric (experiments): pull requests merged per developer-week, randomised by team, four weeks, with a holdout kept for a quarter. Guardrails: reverts and incidents traced to changes with accepted AI code, review time per pull request (AI code that is harder to review shifts cost to reviewers), suggestion latency p95, cost per active developer. Iteration metrics (daily): shown rate, acceptance rate, accepted characters per active hour, persistence at 10 minutes. Quality sampling: weekly expert review of 100 accepted suggestions for correctness and security issues. Perception: a quarterly survey using a few SPACE dimensions, because perceived productivity predicts continued use. Validation: after each model change, compare offline eval delta, acceptance delta and merged-PR delta.

This set answers the finance lead's question at rung 5 (merged pull requests, a throughput measure), lets the team iterate daily at rungs 2-3, protects reviewers and production at the guardrails, and checks that the cheap metrics keep predicting the expensive one.

10. Building measurement into the product

None of these metrics exists unless the product logs the right events with the right identifiers. A workable minimum for an AI feature: one event per model call with a request ID, user and session IDs, feature name, model and prompt version, input and output token counts, latency and cost; one event per display (what was shown, where); one event per user action on the output (accept, edit with an edit-distance measure, copy, regenerate, dismiss, thumbs with reason); and outcome events joined by the same IDs (ticket reopened, pull request (PR) merged, document approved). With those, every metric in this lesson is a query; without them, every metric is a new engineering project.

Two design details matter. Log what was shown, not just what was generated, because generated-but-not-shown outputs inflate denominators. And log the versions of everything (model, prompt, retrieval index), because the most common question after a quality change is "what changed?", and the answer must be a lookup, not an investigation.

11. Reporting AI impact to leadership

The opening story ended with a question the team could not answer, so it is worth saying what a good answer looks like. A one-page impact report for an AI feature has four parts. Outcome: the rung-4 or rung-5 effect from the most recent experiment or holdout, with its interval ("merged pull requests per developer-week +6%, 95% interval +2% to +10%, from a four-week team-randomised experiment"). Cost: cost per active user and total monthly spend, against budget, and cost per unit of outcome ("about $9 per additional merged pull request"). Quality and risk: the graded-sample error rate, confirmed incidents and guardrail status. Trend: feature retention and the iteration metrics over time, with notes on what changed. What it leaves out is as important: no "percent written by AI", no raw thumbs-up rate without context, no adoption numbers inflated by default-on settings. A report like this lets leadership compare the AI feature with any other investment, which is ultimately the decision they need to make.

Goodhart's law applies to AI metrics too

Once acceptance rate, deflection or thumbs-up becomes a target for a team or an optimisation loop, it loses its link to value (ps3). Acceptance can be raised by showing suggestions only where they are easy; deflection by hiding the escalation button; thumbs-up by flattering answers. If a model is trained or selected on user approval signals, the effect can be stronger, because optimisation finds the cheapest route to approval. The OpenAI sycophancy rollback of April 2025, which ev8 and sf3 discuss, is the best-known public example of an approval signal pulling a model towards agreeable rather than helpful answers. Defences are the usual ones: counter-metrics, outcome metrics owned by someone else, and periodic expert review.

Who benefits matters as much as the average

Field evidence repeatedly finds larger gains for less experienced workers: Brynjolfsson and colleagues' 34% for novice support agents against minimal effects for the most experienced is a clear example. For a product, this means average effects can hide segments that gain a lot and segments that gain nothing or lose (experts distracted by suggestions). Pre-register segments by experience or tenure (ps7) and consider different defaults by segment, such as fewer, higher-confidence suggestions for experts.

Long-term effects: dependence and skill

Some effects of AI assistance appear only over months: users relying on the tool for things they used to know, junior staff learning faster (or more slowly) with it, code bases accumulating patterns the tool favours. Short experiments cannot see these. Long-term holdouts, cohort comparisons by start date and periodic skill or quality audits are the tools; in interviews, mentioning the possibility and how you would watch for it is enough.

LLM judges as measurement instruments

Grading outputs at scale usually needs an LLM judge. Treat it as a measurement instrument with known biases: it may prefer longer answers, the first option in a pair, and outputs in its own style (ev4). Calibrate it against human graders on a sample, report agreement, randomise order in pairwise comparisons, and re-check after changing the judge model. A judge that agrees with humans 85% of the time on your task is useful; one whose agreement is unknown is a source of confident noise.

Counterfactuals: would they have done it anyway?

Many AI metrics count things users might have done without the AI: a suggested reply the user would have typed anyway, an answer they would have found in search. The value is only the difference. Experiments measure it directly; without them, be cautious about crediting the feature with every accepted output. The same applies to agents: an agent that completes simple tasks users would have done in a minute adds less than one that completes hard tasks they would have abandoned.

Cost belongs in the success metric

For AI features, quality and cost trade off continuously: a larger model, more retrieved context, or more agent steps usually raise quality and cost together. Metrics such as cost per successful task or cost per resolved contact make the trade-off visible in one number. A change that raises success rate from 60% to 62% while doubling cost per attempt lowers cost-efficiency sharply: cost per success rises from 1/0.60 = 1.67 to 2/0.62 = 3.23 units. Whether that is worth it is a product decision, but it should never be hidden by reporting quality alone.

Surveys, used well

Perception matters: users who feel more productive keep using a tool, and GitHub's study shows that acceptance rate tracks perception. Surveys are the instrument, and they need care. Ask a few specific questions tied to the SPACE dimensions you care about (for example, "the tool helps me stay in flow"), sample users at random rather than relying on volunteers, ask at a consistent point (after a week of use, not on day one when novelty peaks), and track the same questions over time. Treat survey results as one rung of evidence, cross-checked with behaviour and outcomes, not as the measure of value.

Metric definitions for conversational and voice products

Multi-turn conversations and voice interfaces complicate the definitions. Where does a "task" begin and end in a conversation that drifts across topics? Segment conversations into tasks with a simple classifier and measure per task. In voice, users cannot easily copy or click sources, so implicit signals shift to interruptions ("no, I meant…"), repeat requests and hand-offs. The principle is the same: define success per task by an outcome, validate the automatic definition against human grading, and keep the definition fixed while you compare versions.

The real case: GitHub's Copilot productivity study

"Productivity Assessment of Neural Code Completion" (Ziegler, Kalliamvakou, Simister, Sittampalam, Li, Rice, Rifkin and Aftandilian; GitHub, presented at MAPS 2022, the Association for Computing Machinery's symposium on machine programming) is a rare first-party study of how to measure an AI feature. The problem it set itself is exactly this lesson's: commercial products aim to increase productivity but cannot measure it directly. The authors surveyed Copilot users on perceived productivity, designing the survey around the SPACE framework, and received 2,047 responses between 10 February and 6 March 2022 that they could match to the users' telemetry over the preceding four weeks.

They then asked which usage measures best predicted perceived productivity. Candidates included shown rate, acceptance rate, persistence (accepted completions unchanged after 30, 120, 300 and 600 seconds), fuzzy persistence, and volume measures per active hour. Acceptance rate of shown suggestions came out as the best predictor; every persistence measure was less well correlated, with shorter windows doing better than longer ones. In their data the acceptance rate was 27%. They also found acceptance rates varied significantly across the developer population and over time, including higher rates at weekends and outside typical working hours. Their conclusion is careful: acceptance rate can be used for coarse-grained monitoring of a neural code completion system.

What the study teaches a product team: define the funnel precisely before arguing about it; test candidate metrics against a measure of what you care about (here, perceived productivity) rather than assuming; and be explicit about what the winning metric is a proxy for. It also shows a limit: perceived productivity is not throughput, which is why GitHub and others ran separate controlled trials, such as the 2023 study by Peng and colleagues with its 55.8% faster task completion.

Failure modes in AI feature measurement

"Percent written by AI". Counting inserted characters from suggestions, including those later deleted, inflates impact. Use persisted code and outcome metrics.

Default-on adoption. Turning a feature on by default makes "active users" meaningless. Measure active use (users who accepted or invoked it), not exposure.

Eval set rot. The offline set stops resembling traffic; every model looks better offline and the same online. Refresh from production samples on a schedule.

Judge drift. The team changes the LLM judge model and scores shift for all candidates. Re-baseline whenever the judge changes, and keep a human-graded anchor set.

Celebrating deflection. A support bot's deflection rises after a change that hides the escalation button; satisfaction falls months later. Decide on resolution, not deflection.

The weekly AI quality review

Many teams that run AI features well hold a short weekly review with a fixed agenda: the dashboard's top-rung metrics and guardrails against their thresholds; a random sample of 20 to 50 outputs read aloud or graded together, including some from users who gave negative feedback; new failure patterns, each assigned an owner and added to the evaluation set; and any changes shipped that week with their measured effects. It takes under an hour and catches the slow failures that dashboards miss, such as a gradual shift in tone, a new kind of question the system handles badly, or a cost creep. In an interview, describing this ritual is a concrete way to show you know that measurement is a practice, not a one-off design.

Vendor-reported numbers

Vendors of AI tools publish impressive statistics: share of code written, hours saved, tickets deflected. Treat them as you would any metric from someone with an interest in the result: ask for the definition (is "code written" inserted or persisted?), the denominator (all users, or only active ones?), the comparison (against what baseline?), and whether the number came from an experiment. A vendor figure is a hypothesis to test in your own setting with your own outcome metric, ideally in a small randomised pilot before a company-wide rollout. The same scepticism applies to your own team's launch slides.

import random
random.seed(4)

def simulate(threshold, hours=2000, opps_per_hour=200):
    shown = accepted = persisted = 0
    for _ in range(hours * opps_per_hour):
        quality = random.random()                  # how good a suggestion would be
        conf = min(1, max(0, quality + random.gauss(0, 0.15)))
        if conf < threshold:
            continue                               # not shown
        shown += 1
        if random.random() < quality * 0.6:        # better suggestions accepted more
            accepted += 1
            if random.random() < 0.5 + 0.45 * quality:
                persisted += 1
    return shown / hours, accepted / hours, persisted / hours

print("threshold  shown/h  accepted/h  accept_rate  persisted/h  persist_rate")
for thr in [0.3, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9]:
    s, a, p = simulate(thr)
    print(f"{thr:9.1f} {s:8.1f} {a:11.1f} {a / s:12.1%} {p:12.1f} {p / a:13.1%}")

Measurement drill (10 minutes)

Notewise, a hypothetical document editor, launched "AI summarise" for long documents. After a month: 22% of weekly active users tried it, thumbs-up rate is 79%, and average summaries per user rose 30% week over week. Leadership asks whether it is working and whether to expand it to all documents (it currently triggers only on documents over 3,000 words). Propose how to measure it properly and what result would justify expansion.

Answer

What the current numbers say: adoption (22% tried) and a sparse satisfaction signal (thumbs from a small share of users). Summaries per user rising could be heavy use by a few users. None of these is an outcome.

Ladder: usage (weekly users of the feature among eligible users, and feature retention: of users who tried it in week 1, how many use it in week 4); engagement (summary opened, expand-to-full-document rate, copy rate); quality (weekly expert grading of 100 summaries for omissions and contradictions; contradiction rate as a counter-metric); task outcome (for documents shared for review: time from receiving the document to first comment or approval); value (retention of teams using it versus matched teams, measured properly in an experiment).

Experiment for expansion: randomise by team; treatment gets summaries on documents over 1,000 words, control keeps the 3,000-word threshold. Primary: time to first review action on documents between 1,000 and 3,000 words (triggered on users who open such a document). Guardrails: contradiction rate in graded samples, cost per active user (more documents means more calls), latency. Run four weeks and plot the effect by week for novelty.

Expansion bar: a pre-agreed reduction in time to first review on medium documents (say 10%), feature retention at week 4 of at least a set level (say 30% of triers), contradiction rate below the threshold on graded samples, and cost per active user within budget. If medium documents show no time saving, keep the threshold: short documents may simply not need summaries.
