A team of five engineers has a list of thirty ideas for next quarter. The sales lead wants a feature a big customer asked for. The head of product wants an AI assistant because competitors have one. The engineers want to fix the flaky data pipeline that breaks every other week. Support wants a better password-recovery flow. Someone has a clever idea for personalised onboarding. There is time for perhaps four of them. The planning meeting goes the way these meetings often go: the most senior and most persistent voices win, the pipeline fix is postponed again, and in three months nobody can say whether the chosen projects were the right ones because nobody wrote down what they were expected to achieve.

Prioritisation is where product sense meets scarcity. Every earlier topic in this track helps decide whether something is good; this one decides what to do first given that you cannot do everything. It needs three skills. Sizing: estimating, quickly and roughly, what an idea could be worth, with numbers someone can check. Comparing: putting ideas with different kinds of value on a common footing, honestly accounting for effort and uncertainty. And sequencing: choosing an order that tests the riskiest assumptions cheaply, delivers value early and does not block other work.

Interviewers test these directly. "How big is this opportunity?" wants a back-of-envelope calculation in two minutes. "You have three projects and one team, which first?" wants a structured comparison. "How would you build the first version?" wants a risk-first MVP plan. "The VP wants feature X, but you think it's wrong; what do you do?" wants a way to say no with reasons. Senior engineers are expected to do all of this, because they hold the effort estimates and know which ideas are technically risky.

This lesson covers opportunity sizing with worked ranges, Fermi estimation checked two ways, the impact/effort matrix, Intercom's RICE score worked on five projects, cost of delay, risk-first MVP sequencing for an AI feature, the pitfalls of each, and how to say no.

A word on mindset before the methods. Prioritisation is uncomfortable because every choice leaves good ideas undone and every estimate will turn out wrong in some way. The aim is not to be right about each number but to be explicit enough that wrong assumptions are found quickly and decisions improve over time. Teams that write their reasoning down and check it against results get better at this every quarter; teams that decide by argument stay where they are.

Prioritisation is an investment decision. The team's time is the capital, each idea is a possible investment with an uncertain return, and the goal is the best portfolio of returns for the time available. That framing brings three ideas with it. Returns should be compared per unit of capital: value divided by effort, not value alone. Uncertain returns should be weighed by their probability, and cheap ways to reduce uncertainty are worth paying for. And a portfolio needs a mix: safe bets that reliably pay something, and a few riskier ones that might pay a lot.

The analogy breaks in two places. Investors can usually sell a bad investment; software teams cannot get back the months spent on a failed project, though they can stop early if they planned checkpoints. And investments are usually independent, while product work is full of dependencies: the pipeline fix makes three future experiments possible, the assistant needs the new search index, and so on. Sequencing matters as much as selection.

Each stage uses a different tool. Sizing uses back-of-envelope arithmetic: reach × effect × value, with ranges. Scoring puts sized ideas on a common footing; RICE is the best-known formula, and cost of delay adds timing. Sequencing orders the chosen work to test the riskiest assumptions first and respect dependencies. Committing records the decision and its reasons, so it can be revisited with evidence rather than relitigated by opinion.

A reader who stops here should remember that the goal is expected value per unit of effort, that rough numbers with ranges beat precise-looking guesses, and that the first version of anything uncertain should be designed to answer its riskiest question as cheaply as possible.

A last framing note: much of what passes for prioritisation is really sizing that nobody did. When a team argues for an hour about two features, the argument is usually about unstated assumptions: how many users would see each one, how much it would change their behaviour, what that change is worth. Writing those three numbers down for each feature, even roughly, often ends the argument in minutes, either because one feature is clearly ten times bigger, or because the disagreement is revealed to be about a single assumption that a small test can settle.

Here is what a one-minute sizing sounds like in an interview, for "should we build a 'reorder last week's groceries' button?": "About 1 million weekly active shoppers; perhaps 30% reorder similar baskets weekly, so 300,000. If the button saves them two minutes and nudges 2% more of them to order in a given week, that is 6,000 extra orders a week. At $8 margin each, about $48,000 a week, $2.5 million a year. Effort is maybe two engineer-weeks. Even if I'm off by a factor of five, it's worth doing; the number I'm least sure of is the 2% nudge, which a quick experiment would measure." Every number is rough, every step is visible, and the conclusion survives the uncertainty. That is the skill.

Notice also what the example did not do: it did not try to be precise about the 30% or the $8, and it did not stop to ask for data. In an interview, asking for exact inputs before estimating looks like avoidance; estimating, then naming which input you would measure first, looks like judgement.

1. Opportunity sizing: reach × effect × value

Opportunity sizing estimates what a change could be worth before building it. The basic formula has three factors:

annual value ≈ reach per year × effect per reached unit × value per unit of effect

Reach is how many users, sessions or transactions the change touches. Effect is how much it changes each one: a conversion lift, minutes saved, a reduction in contact rate. Value per unit converts the effect into money or into the north-star metric: margin per order, cost per support ticket, lifetime value per retained user (ps5).

Worked example: a store considers adding one-tap wallet payments at checkout. Reach: 2,000,000 users start checkout each month; 35% are on phones with the wallet available, so 700,000. At the payment step, 8% of them abandon: 56,000 users a month. Effect: one-tap payment recovers some of those abandoners; base case a quarter, so 14,000 extra orders a month. Value: average order $45 at 30% margin is $13.50 of margin per order, so 14,000 × $13.50 = $189,000 a month, about $2.27 million a year.

The single number is less useful than the range. Repeat with low and high assumptions for the most uncertain factor, the recovery rate: low 10% gives about $0.91 million a year, high 40% about $3.63 million. Even the low case is large compared with a few engineer-weeks of work, so the idea is worth doing whichever case is true. That is the typical outcome of sizing: not a precise forecast but a robust ranking.

Two refinements matter. First, cannibalisation: some of the "recovered" orders would have happened anyway through another payment method later, so the true effect is smaller than the measured shift in payment methods. Second, ramp: adoption grows over months, so the first year is worth less than a full year at steady state.

2. Fermi estimation and the two-method check

When a number is unknown, Fermi estimation breaks it into factors that can each be estimated roughly, named after the physicist Enrico Fermi, who was known for such estimates. Errors in individual factors partly cancel, so the product is often within a factor of two or three of the truth, which is enough for prioritisation.

Worked example: how many password-reset support tickets does an app with 10 million registered users get each month? Monthly active users are perhaps 40% of registered, so 4 million. Perhaps 2% of monthly actives need a password reset in a month: 80,000 resets. Self-service email reset works for most; if 5% fail or get stuck and contact support, that is about 4,000 tickets a month.

Now check it with a second, independent method. If support handles about 40,000 tickets a month and account-access issues are typically around a tenth of tickets, that gives about 4,000. The two methods agree within a factor of two; had they disagreed by ten times, one of the assumptions would be wrong, and finding which is itself valuable. Interviewers like this check because it shows you know your estimate could be wrong and how you would find out.

Three habits keep Fermi estimates honest: track units at every step (users per month, not just "users"), round to one significant figure while estimating so the arithmetic stays easy, and say which factor is least certain, because that is the one to measure first.

3. Expected value and uncertainty

Most product ideas do less than their proponents expect; experimentation teams report that many changes fail to move the metric they targeted at all. So sizing should include a probability of success, turning the estimate into an expected value: a 30% chance of $2 million is worth $600,000 in expectation. This matters most when comparing a safe idea with a risky one.

Worked comparison: project A has a 90% chance of a $400,000 annual gain (expected $360,000); project B has a 20% chance of $5 million and an 80% chance of nothing (expected $1 million). B is worth more on average and is also much more likely to deliver nothing. Two responses improve on simply picking one. Reduce B's uncertainty cheaply before committing, for example with a fake-door test or a prototype that tells you whether the 20% is really 5% or 50%. And think in portfolios: a team should hold a mix of A-type and B-type bets, so that it delivers reliably and still has a chance at large wins.

The Bing ad-headline story (ps1) is the reminder that the probability estimates themselves are unreliable: an idea judged low priority for more than six months turned out to be the best revenue idea in Bing's history once tested. That argues for cheap tests of many ideas over confident ranking of a few.

4. The impact/effort matrix

The fastest first pass is a two-by-two grid: expected impact on one axis, effort on the other.

Quick wins (high impact, low effort) go first. Big bets (high impact, high effort) need deliberate planning with checkpoints. Fill-ins (low impact, low effort) are for slack time. Money pits (low impact, high effort) should be declined, politely and with reasons. The matrix's weakness is that it hides uncertainty and treats "impact" as one dimension; it is a sorting tool, not a decision tool.

5. RICE, worked

RICE, described by Sean McBride on Intercom's blog in January 2018, turns the matrix into a number by adding reach and confidence. Its four factors, as Intercom defines them:

  • Reach: how many people or events the project will affect in a defined period, such as "customers per quarter". Intercom's example: 500 customers reach a point in the signup funnel each month and 30% choose an option, so reach is 500 × 30% × 3 = 450 customers per quarter.
  • Impact: how much it moves the goal for each person reached, on a multiple-choice scale: 3 massive, 2 high, 1 medium, 0.5 low, 0.25 minimal.
  • Confidence: how sure you are about the estimates: 100% high, 80% medium, 50% low. It exists, in McBride's words, to curb enthusiasm for exciting but ill-defined ideas.
  • Effort: total person-months across product, design and engineering; whole numbers, or 0.5 for anything well under a month.

Score = Reach × Impact × Confidence / Effort. Applied to five of the opening example's projects (reach in users per quarter):

What the score hides is as instructive as the ranking. The assistant's score depends heavily on two judgement calls (impact 2, confidence 50%); at impact 1 it falls to 56,250, below the password flow. The custom feature scores almost nothing on RICE, but if the client is worth $1 million a year and will leave without it, RICE is measuring the wrong goal; reach-based scores undervalue concentrated value. And the pipeline fix is not on the list at all, because its value is indirect (section 9). RICE is useful precisely because it makes these assumptions visible enough to argue about.

6. Cost of delay: when timing matters

RICE ignores time. Cost of delay is the value lost for each week a piece of work is not delivered. Dividing it by the work's duration gives a ranking that favours short, valuable work and anything with a deadline.

Worked example: project X takes 2 weeks and its cost of delay is $20,000 a week; project Y takes 10 weeks at $50,000 a week. Ratio: X 10,000, Y 5,000. Do X first. Check: X then Y costs X's delay for 0 weeks and Y's for 2 weeks = $100,000 of delay; Y then X costs Y's delay for 0 and X's for 10 weeks = $200,000. Doing the short, valuable one first halves the total delay cost even though Y is worth more per week.

Cost of delay changes rankings in three situations: deadlines (a regulation taking effect, a seasonal peak where a feature is worth ten times more), decaying value (a competitor about to launch the same thing), and unblocking (a short project that makes three others possible, whose cost of delay includes theirs).

7. Sequencing: risk-first MVPs

Once a project is chosen, the order of work within it matters. An MVP, minimum viable product, is the smallest version that can test the project's key assumptions with real users. The principle is to test first the assumption most likely to kill the idea, using the cheapest test that would reveal it.

Cheap tests, in rough order of cost: data analysis (do users already do the workaround the feature would replace?); a fake door test (show the entry point, measure clicks, explain it is coming soon); a Wizard of Oz test (a person secretly does what the system will automate, so you learn whether users value the output before building the automation); a concierge version (a person openly does the service for a few users); then a real but narrow version for a slice of users.

For AI features the killer assumptions are usually three: the model's output quality on real inputs, whether users trust and act on it, and the cost per user at scale (ps8). A risk-first plan for the AI shopping assistant:

  1. Week 1: run a candidate model on 200 real customer questions from search and support logs; grade answers. If fewer than, say, 70% are acceptable, stop or rethink.
  2. Week 2: an internal prototype used by support staff on live questions; measure how often they use the answer as-is.
  3. Week 3: compute cost per active user from measured token use and the expected usage distribution; check against the margin per user.
  4. Weeks 4-6: a narrow production version for one product category, as an experiment on 5% of users, with conversion per visitor as the primary metric and cost per user as a guardrail.
  5. Only then: broaden categories, tune, scale.

Each step can end the project for the price of a week or two, instead of discovering the problem after eight person-months. That is also how to turn a risky big bet into something a cautious organisation can approve: not "fund the assistant" but "fund week 1, with a clear stop rule".

8. Saying no

Most ideas should not be built now, so a team that prioritises well says no often. How it says no determines whether stakeholders trust the process. A good no has four parts: the criteria (how ideas are ranked, agreed in advance); the trade-off (what the team would stop doing to take this on, and what that costs); what would change the answer (evidence of larger reach, a deadline, a cheaper approach); and a smaller alternative or a test that would produce that evidence. "Not now, because the wallet payment is worth about $2 million a year and this is worth about $100,000; if the client's renewal depends on it, bring that to the planning review and we will rescore it" is a no that keeps the relationship.

Two practices make no's stick. A decision log records what was decided, why, and when to revisit, so the same request is not relitigated every week. And a regular comparison of predicted with actual impact for shipped projects builds the credibility that makes future estimates (and future no's) persuasive.

9. Work that never appears on the feature list

Infrastructure, reliability and technical-debt work, such as the flaky pipeline in the opening story, rarely wins prioritisation meetings because its value is indirect. The fix is to size it in the same units as features. Suppose the pipeline breaks twice a month, each time costing two engineers two days and delaying experiments by a week. Fixing it saves about 8 engineer-days a month (roughly 0.4 person-months) and about two weeks of experiment delay a month. If the team's experiments produce, on average, one shippable win every six weeks (about nine a year), each worth about $50,000 a year, and the fix raises experiment throughput by a third, that is roughly three extra wins a year, about $150,000 of annual value. Now it can be scored: a person-month of effort for that return ranks well. ML teams in particular carry this kind of work (Sculley and colleagues' "hidden technical debt", ps8), and engineers are the ones who must translate it into numbers.

10. Sizing an AI feature

AI features need two extra terms in the sizing formula: adoption and marginal cost. A workable form is:

annual net value ≈ eligible users × adoption × effect per adopter × value per unit − adopters × usage × cost per use − fixed costs

Worked example for the AI shopping assistant: 3 million monthly visitors are eligible; perhaps 10% use the assistant in a month (adoption), 300,000. If assistant users convert 4% more often and an average visitor generates $6 of margin a month, the effect is 300,000 × $6 × 4% = $72,000 a month, about $864,000 a year. Cost: 300,000 users × 6 questions a month × $0.004 per question ≈ $7,200 a month, $86,000 a year. Fixed costs (a team of three for six months, evaluation, monitoring) might be $400,000 in year one. Year-one net is roughly $864,000 − $86,000 − $400,000 ≈ $380,000, with the 4% effect as the dominant uncertainty: at 1% it is negative. That tells you the risk-first plan must measure the conversion effect early, which is exactly week 4-6 in section 7. Notice also that model cost is a small term here; for many AI features the fixed costs of building and running the system dominate in year one.

11. Presenting a prioritised plan

A plan is persuasive when it shows its reasoning compactly. A one-page format: the goal for the period and the metric it will be judged on; the chosen projects with their sized value (range), effort, confidence and the riskiest assumption each will test first; the projects considered and not chosen, each with a one-line reason; the capacity reserved for reliability and debt; and the checkpoints at which the plan will be revisited. The "not chosen" list is the most important part for stakeholders, because it shows their requests were considered on the same criteria as everything else. In interviews, ending a prioritisation answer with "and here's what I would explicitly not do this quarter, and why" signals that you understand prioritisation is about trade-offs.

Estimates are biased, in known directions

Two biases are consistent enough to correct for. Effort is underestimated (the planning fallacy): people imagine the work going smoothly and forget integration, testing, edge cases and rollout. Impact is overestimated, especially by an idea's proponent. A team that records predicted and actual effort and impact for shipped projects can compute its own correction factors: if projects took 1.6 times their estimates and delivered half their predicted impact on average, those factors belong in the next quarter's scoring. Reference-class forecasting, estimating from the outcomes of similar past projects rather than from the details of this one, is the most effective single correction.

Comparing different kinds of value

A revenue feature, a retention feature, a cost saving and a risk reduction are measured in different units. Prioritisation needs a common currency, usually money per year, using the metric tree (ps2) and lifetime value (ps5) to convert. Where conversion is not credible (brand, strategic positioning), say so and decide that portion by judgement, rather than inventing a precise number. Mixed portfolios are often allocated in layers: a fixed share of capacity for reliability and debt, a share for committed customer work, and the rest ranked by expected value.

Option value and reversibility

Some work is valuable mainly because it creates options: an experiment platform that makes future tests cheap, a data pipeline that enables personalisation later, a small first version that tells you whether a big bet is worth making. These options are worth more when uncertainty is high and when the work is reversible or cheap to stop. Conversely, irreversible commitments (a long contract, a public API that customers will depend on, a data model that is hard to migrate) deserve more scrutiny than their RICE score suggests. Distinguishing "two-way door" from "one-way door" decisions (beh covers Bezos's terms) is part of sequencing.

Diminishing returns within a project

Within a project, value usually comes unevenly: the first 20% of the work delivers most of the benefit, and the last improvements are expensive. A ranking model's first version captures most of the gain over a heuristic (Rules of ML Rule 4: keep the first model simple); an AI assistant's first category covers the most common questions. Good sequencing ships the high-value part and then re-enters the remaining work into the next prioritisation round, where it must compete with new ideas instead of being finished by default.

Portfolio balance

A team that only takes safe, measurable bets drifts towards small optimisations of the existing product and misses larger opportunities. A team that only takes big bets often delivers nothing for long stretches. Many organisations aim for an explicit mix, such as most capacity on core improvements with measurable returns, a meaningful slice on larger bets with staged checkpoints, and a small slice on exploration. The exact split is less important than making it a deliberate choice that is reviewed against results.

The engineer's role

Engineers bring three things to prioritisation that others cannot: credible effort ranges, knowledge of technical risk (which projects have unknowns that could double their cost), and ideas for cheaper tests (a quick prototype, an offline analysis, a feature flag). Senior engineers are expected to contribute all three and to argue for infrastructure work in the same terms as features. In interviews, describing how you changed a roadmap by sizing an overlooked project or by proposing a cheap test that killed an expensive one is a strong behavioural answer.

Kill criteria and the sunk-cost trap

Every big bet should start with written kill criteria: the evidence that would stop it ("if week-1 grading finds fewer than 70% acceptable answers", "if the experiment shows less than a 1% conversion lift after four weeks"). Without them, projects continue because of what has already been spent rather than what remains to be gained, which is the sunk-cost fallacy. Kill criteria written in advance make stopping a normal outcome rather than a failure, and they free capacity for the next idea. A team that never kills a project is either very lucky or not looking.

How uncertainty compounds in a product of factors

A sizing estimate multiplies several uncertain factors, and their uncertainties compound. If each of three factors could plausibly be half or double your base guess, the product could be anywhere from an eighth to eight times the base case, a 64-fold range. That sounds hopeless, but in practice two things help. Errors partly cancel when they are independent, so the realistic spread is narrower than the extremes, which is why Fermi estimates often land within a factor of two or three. And the decision usually needs only to know which side of a threshold the value falls on. The practical move is to find the factor with the widest range and narrow it first, by measurement or a quick test, because that factor dominates the uncertainty of the whole estimate. The lab's simulation shows the same idea in ranking form.

The real case: RICE at Intercom

Sean McBride's 2018 post on Intercom's blog describes why the team built RICE. Prioritising a roadmap full of good ideas was hard, and personal favourites, pet ideas and projects that were exciting but ill-defined kept competing with less glamorous work that would affect more customers. The team wanted a framework that made them consider each factor deliberately and combine them consistently, and RICE was the result.

The post's examples show how the factors are meant to be used. Reach is counted from real product metrics wherever possible, "instead of pulling numbers from a hat": one project reaches 500 customers a month at a funnel step, of whom 30% choose a particular option, so 450 per quarter; another affects every customer who uses a feature in a quarter, 2,000; another has a one-time effect on 800 existing customers. Impact uses a fixed multiple-choice scale because it is hard to measure precisely. Confidence is a percentage chosen from three levels "to help avoid decision paralysis": in the examples, a project with good data on reach and effort but uncertain impact gets 80%, and one where reach and impact may be lower and effort higher gets 50%. Effort is in person-months, rough, in whole numbers. The post presents the resulting scores as a tool to inform priorities rather than a rule to follow blindly.

The lesson for engineers is that RICE's main value is social: it forces each estimate to be written down and argued about separately. When a project's score depends entirely on an impact of 3 and a confidence of 100%, the framework has done its job by making that visible.

Failure modes in prioritisation

Score inflation. Over time, everyone learns to give their project impact 2 and confidence 80%. Defence: calibrate scales with examples from past projects, and review the highest-scoring projects' assumptions explicitly.

Precision theatre. Spreadsheets with scores to three decimal places hide that the inputs are guesses. Defence: show ranges, and treat scores within a factor of 1.5 of each other as ties to be decided by other criteria.

Starving infrastructure. Reliability and debt work never wins on reach-based scores. Defence: a reserved capacity share or explicit sizing as in section 9.

No feedback loop. Predicted impact is never compared with actual impact, so estimates never improve. Defence: a quarterly review of shipped projects against their predictions.

Everything is a priority. Twelve "top priorities" for a team of five. Defence: a hard limit on concurrent projects, and an explicit "not doing" list published with the plan.

A planning cycle that works

Teams that prioritise well tend to follow a similar rhythm. Before planning, every candidate gets a one-paragraph brief with its sizing, effort range, confidence and riskiest assumption, written by whoever proposes it. Engineers review effort and technical risk; data scientists or analysts check reach and effect assumptions against real numbers. Scores are computed and the close calls discussed in a meeting focused on disagreements, not on every item. The plan is published with the "not doing" list and kill criteria for the big bets. Mid-cycle, checkpoints decide whether big bets continue. At the end of the cycle, each shipped project's predicted and actual impact are compared, and the team's correction factors are updated. Each step is small; together they replace argument with evidence, and they make the next cycle's estimates better than this one's.

Customer requests and the one-client trap

Requests from a single large customer are the most common source of money-pit projects, because the requester is important and the request is specific. Reach-based scores rank them near zero, which is often right and sometimes badly wrong. The honest way to size them is in the customer's own terms: the revenue at risk times the probability that this feature changes the renewal decision, compared with the effort, plus any value to other customers if the feature generalises. A request that protects $1 million of annual revenue with a 30% chance of mattering is worth $300,000 in expectation, which can justify a month of work; one that is "nice to have" for a customer who will renew anyway is not. Asking the account team for that probability, in writing, often settles the question.

Goal: score projects with RICE, then see how stable the ranking is when the uncertain inputs vary. About 15 minutes; Python 3.12 standard library.

  1. Save as rice.py and run it:
import random
random.seed(1)
projects = {   # reach/quarter, impact choices, confidence, effort range (person-months)
    "wallet":     (168_000, [0.5, 1, 2],   0.8, (0.5, 1.5)),
    "password":   (240_000, [0.25, 0.5, 1], 0.8, (0.5, 1.5)),
    "onboarding": (600_000, [0.5, 1, 2],   0.5, (3, 5)),
    "assistant":  (900_000, [1, 2, 3],     0.5, (6, 10)),
    "client":     (2_000,   [3],           1.0, (2, 4)),
}

def rice(reach, impact, conf, effort):
    return reach * impact * conf / effort

print("point estimates:")
point = {k: rice(r, imp[len(imp) // 2], c, sum(e) / 2) for k, (r, imp, c, e) in projects.items()}
for k, v in sorted(point.items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1]):
    print(f"  {k:11} {v:10,.0f}")

wins = {k: 0 for k in projects}
for _ in range(10_000):
    draw = {k: rice(r * random.uniform(0.7, 1.3), random.choice(imp), c, random.uniform(*e))
            for k, (r, imp, c, e) in projects.items()}
    wins[max(draw, key=draw.get)] += 1
print("share of simulations each project ranks first:")
for k, v in sorted(wins.items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1]):
    print(f"  {k:11} {v / 100:5.1f}%")

What to look for: the point estimates (middle impact, middle of the effort range) should reproduce the lesson's table exactly: wallet 134,400, assistant 112,500, password 96,000, onboarding 75,000, client 2,000. The simulation shows how often each project would rank first when reach, impact and effort vary within plausible ranges: if the wallet and the assistant split the first place between them, the ranking is not robust, and the right next step is to reduce uncertainty in the assistant's impact (the week-1 quality test) rather than argue about scores. Then narrow the assistant's impact choices to [1] (the test came back mediocre) and rerun; then add a deadline bonus to the password flow and see whether cost of delay would change your order.

Sizing and sequencing drill (10 minutes)

Pagewise, a hypothetical e-book subscription service with 1.5 million subscribers paying $10 a month, has two candidate projects for one team's quarter: (A) an AI "recap of what you read last time" shown when a reader reopens a book, and (B) fixing a bug that makes 4% of downloads fail on one Android device family, used by 15% of subscribers. Size both, choose, and outline the first three weeks of the chosen work.

Answer

(B) the bug. Reach: 15% of 1.5M = 225,000 subscribers. If 4% of their downloads fail and a fraction of affected users churn because of it, say an extra 0.5 percentage points of monthly churn among that group, that is about 1,125 subscribers a month, worth 1,125 × $10 × 12 ≈ $135,000 in annual revenue per monthly cohort. Over the first year, subscribers kept in month m pay for the remaining months, so the saving is about 1,125 × $10 × (11 + 10 + … + 0) = $742,500, roughly $0.7-0.8 million of revenue in year one (a rough figure; the churn assumption dominates). Effort: perhaps 2-4 engineer-weeks. Confidence is fairly high that fixing it helps; the size depends on the churn effect, which you can estimate from existing data by comparing churn of affected and unaffected users.

(A) the recap. Reach: readers who reopen a book after a gap of several days, say 40% of subscribers a month, 600,000. Effect: unknown; perhaps it raises reading sessions and slightly lowers churn. If it cut monthly churn by 0.1 points among those readers, that is 600 subscribers a month, about $72,000 in annual revenue per cohort, similar to or smaller than B, with low confidence and higher effort (quality evaluation, cost, design). Cost: a recap of perhaps 1,000 input tokens and 150 output tokens on a small model costs well under a cent; 600,000 readers × 4 reopenings a month is manageable, but needs computing.

Choice: B first: higher expected value per unit effort, higher confidence, and a known group of affected users. Run A's riskiest test in parallel cheaply.

First three weeks: week 1, reproduce the bug on the device family and quantify affected users and their churn versus similar users (confirms the size); week 2, fix and test on the device matrix; week 3, staged rollout with download-failure rate as the primary metric and crash rate as a guardrail. In parallel, for A: generate 200 recaps from real reading positions and grade them, and show a fake "Recap" button to 1% of returning readers to measure demand before building it.
