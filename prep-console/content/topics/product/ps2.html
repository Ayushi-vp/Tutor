A team running a news app decides that success means more page views. It is easy to measure, it rises when people read more, and the advertising team likes it. Over the next quarter they ship a redesign that buries the search box and splits long articles across several pages. Page views per user rise 18%. The team celebrates. Two months later, weekly returning users are down 6%, and user research finds people describing the app as "a maze". The redesign raised page views by making people work harder to get what they came for, and the metric rewarded it.

Nothing in this story required bad intentions. The team picked a metric that moves when users get value and when they are frustrated, then optimised it. Google's HEART paper uses almost exactly this example: a rise in page views for a feature "may occur because the feature is genuinely popular, or because a confusing interface leads users to get lost in it, clicking around to figure out how to escape." Microsoft's experimentation team found a bug in Bing that degraded search results and made queries per user rise over 10% and revenue per user over 30%; by the metrics on the dashboard, a broken search engine looked like a triumph.

Choosing metrics is where product goals become numbers that teams, models and launch reviews optimise. Get it wrong and every later step, from the model's training label to the experiment's verdict, inherits the error. Get it right and a team can make hundreds of decisions a year without arguing about what success means.

This lesson covers how to go from a goal to signals to metrics; the north-star metric and the tree of driver metrics beneath it, with a worked decomposition; guardrails and counter-metrics; the properties that make a metric good for decisions, including a sensitivity calculation showing why revenue makes a poor experiment metric; and the ratio-metric traps that catch experienced teams. The real cases are the HEART paper, Bing's puzzling outcome and two MSN experiments from Microsoft's "dirty dozen" of metric pitfalls.

A metric is a number computed from logged events or survey answers, defined precisely enough that two analysts would get the same value: what is counted (the numerator), over what (the denominator), in what time window, from which data source. A metric set is the handful of metrics a team uses to decide whether a change was good. Choosing a metric set is the step that turns "help users cook more" into something a dashboard, an experiment and a model can work with.

An analogy: running a restaurant. The owner's real goal is a restaurant people love and come back to. The best single measure of that is perhaps repeat visits per customer per month: it rises when food, service and value are good, and the owner cannot fake it easily. That is the north-star metric. Underneath it sit things the staff can influence directly: how long guests wait for a table, how long food takes, how often a dish is sent back, what share of first-time guests get a welcome. These are driver metrics (also called input metrics): each team can move its own, and together they produce the north star. Then there are things that must not get worse while chasing the north star: food-safety inspections, staff overtime, the cost of ingredients per plate. These are guardrails. Finally, if the kitchen is rewarded for speed, someone should watch dishes sent back, because the easiest way to cook faster is to cook worse. That is a counter-metric: chosen specifically to catch the most likely way a target could be gamed.

Where the analogy breaks: a restaurant owner can taste the food and watch the dining room. A product team with millions of users sees almost nothing directly; everything it knows comes through logged events and occasional surveys. That makes the choice of what to log, and how to turn it into metrics, far more consequential than it is for the restaurant. It also means the metrics can be corrupted in ways no diner would allow: bots, logging bugs, double-counted events, changes in what counts as a "session".

Three ideas carry most of the weight in this lesson. First, metrics come from goals through observable signals, not from whatever is easy to log. Second, a metric set has roles: one metric decides, a few protect, and the rest explain. Third, many metrics are ratios, and ratios can move because their denominator changed rather than because users behaved differently, which is the single most common way experienced teams misread results.

A fourth, quieter idea runs underneath: a metric is a tool for making decisions, and its quality depends on the decision it serves. A metric that is perfect for a quarterly business review (company-wide monthly users) can be useless for deciding whether a two-week experiment worked, because the experiment cannot move it detectably. A metric that is perfect for an experiment (clicks on a module per user) can be misleading on a dashboard if nobody remembers what it leaves out. The same team needs different metrics for different decisions, and naming which decision a metric serves is half of choosing it well.

Here is what the three roles sound like in a single sentence, which is roughly how you would say it in an interview: "We'll ship the new checkout if completed orders per active customer rise by at least 2% (the primary metric), as long as payment errors and page latency do not get significantly worse (guardrails) and refunds per order do not rise (the counter-metric for 'we made people buy things they did not want'); funnel steps will tell us where any change came from (diagnostics)." Notice that every metric has a role, a direction and a reason to be there. That sentence is the target output of this lesson.

1. From goal to signals to metrics

Google's HEART paper (Rodden, Hutchinson and Fu, CHI 2010) describes a three-step process that remains the clearest way to go from a goal to metrics. First, state the goal: what are users trying to accomplish, and what is this change trying to achieve? Second, list the signals: observable behaviours or attitudes that would indicate success or failure. Third, define metrics from those signals, precisely enough to put on a dashboard and test in an experiment.

The paper's tips are worth repeating because they are where teams go wrong. At the goal step, team members often disagree about what the goal is, and the process is a chance to reach agreement; and do not worry yet about whether you can measure it. At the signal step, choose signals that are "sensitive and specific to the goal": they should move only when the experience gets better or worse, not for unrelated reasons. Also, failure is sometimes easier to see than success: abandoning a task, pressing undo, rage-clicking. At the metric step, normalise raw counts (they rise with the user base) into ratios, percentages or per-user averages, and filter out traffic from automated sources.

Worked example: a calendar app adds "smart scheduling", which proposes meeting times. Goal: "help people schedule meetings with less back-and-forth". Signals of success: users accept a proposed time; the meeting is not rescheduled; the scheduling thread ends sooner. Signals of failure: users dismiss the proposals and pick manually; meetings proposed by the feature get moved within a day. Metrics: share of new meetings created from a proposal (adoption of the feature), median number of messages between the first request and a confirmed meeting (task success, the primary), and the share of feature-scheduled meetings rescheduled within 24 hours (a failure signal that works as a counter-metric). Notice that "number of proposals shown" never appears: it measures the feature's activity, not its value.

2. HEART and PULSE: a checklist of metric categories

The HEART paper contrasts two families. PULSE metrics are the business and technical health metrics most companies already track: Page views, Uptime, Latency, Seven-day active users and Earnings. They matter, but they are indirect measures of user experience and can be ambiguous; the paper notes that seven-day active users could in theory rise even with 100% weekly turnover of the user base.

HEART is a set of user-centred categories to choose from: Happiness (attitudes: satisfaction, perceived ease, likelihood to recommend, usually from surveys), Engagement (frequency, intensity or depth of use, reported per user), Adoption (new users of a product or feature in a period), Retention (users from one period still active in a later one) and Task success (efficiency, effectiveness, error rate). The paper's examples are concrete: Gmail measured engagement as the percentage of active users who visited on five or more days in the last week, and found it strongly predictive of longer-term retention; iGoogle saw satisfaction drop after a redesign and then recover, which suggested change aversion rather than a worse design; Google Finance used adoption and retention to separate crisis-driven new visitors from existing users checking their portfolios during the September 2008 market crash.

The useful habit is not to measure all five but to run through them deliberately and decide which apply. An enterprise tool used as part of someone's job may not need engagement at all (people use it because they must), but task success matters a lot. A new feature needs adoption; a mature product cares more about retention.

3. The north-star metric

A north-star metric is the single number a product organisation treats as the best measurable summary of the value it delivers to users. It exists to align many teams: if search, recommendations, notifications and onboarding all agree that, say, "weekly completed orders per active customer" is what matters, their local decisions point in the same direction.

Good north stars share five properties. They reflect value received by users (an order delivered, a meeting scheduled, a song listened to and saved) rather than effort spent (searches, clicks, page views), because effort can rise when the product gets worse. They are normalised or tied to a unit of value, so they do not rise merely because more users signed up. They lead revenue: when the north star rises, revenue tends to follow later, but the north star responds sooner and is less noisy. They are understandable by everyone in the company. And they decompose into drivers that individual teams can move.

Bad north stars usually fail on the first property. Time spent rewards both delight and confusion; queries per user rewards both curiosity and failed searches; messages sent rewards both conversation and spam. When an interviewer gives you an effort metric as the goal, the senior move is to propose a value-based version and to keep the effort metric as a diagnostic.

In product-type terms (hypothetical examples, since companies rarely publish their exact definitions): a marketplace's north star is often completed transactions per active buyer; a collaboration tool's is often weekly active teams performing the core action (editing a shared document, sending a message in a channel); a media product's is often engaged consumption (listening or watching time weighted by some satisfaction signal); a subscription utility's is often successful task completions per subscriber.

4. Driver metrics and metric trees, with a worked decomposition

A north star is too high-level for most teams to move directly, and when it changes you need to know why. A metric tree decomposes it into the factors that produce it. The most useful decompositions are multiplicative, because each factor is something a different team owns.

Worked example. Dishly is a hypothetical food-delivery app. Weekly orders can be written as:

orders = weekly active users × sessions per user × orders per session

Last week: 2,000,000 weekly active users × 3.0 sessions per user × 0.12 orders per session = 720,000 orders. This week orders fell to about 684,000, a 5% drop. The factors moved as follows: users 2,000,000 → 1,980,000 (−1%), sessions per user 3.0 → 3.03 (+1%), orders per session 0.12 → 0.114 (−5%). Check: 1,980,000 × 3.03 × 0.114 ≈ 683,950.

Because the log of a product is the sum of the logs, each factor's share of the change is its log change divided by the total log change. Here: ln(0.99) = −0.0101, ln(1.01) = +0.0100, ln(0.95) = −0.0513, total −0.0514. Users explain about 20% of the drop, sessions per user offset about 19%, and orders per session explain essentially all of it. The investigation should start at checkout and restaurant availability, not acquisition. This is the same move ps4 uses to diagnose metric changes; here it shows why trees are worth building in advance.

Trees can also be additive by segment: orders = orders from new users + orders from returning users, or the sum over cities. Additive decompositions answer "where" a change happened; multiplicative ones answer "which step". In practice you use both: first find the segment, then the step.

Driver metrics are what individual teams adopt as their targets. The onboarding team owns new-user activation, the search team owns orders per session, the notifications team owns sessions per user. The danger, which the next section addresses, is that each team then optimises its driver at the others' expense.

5. Guardrails and counter-metrics

A guardrail metric is one that is not the goal but must not get significantly worse. Microsoft's experimentation team describes guardrails as metrics "not clearly indicative of success of the feature being tested", which "we do not want to significantly harm when making a ship decision", with page load time as the classic example: almost any feature slightly increases it, so the question is how much is acceptable. Guardrails fall into four families:

  • User experience: latency, crash rate, error rate, accessibility regressions.
  • Business: revenue, cost per user, support tickets per thousand users.
  • Ecosystem: the other side of a marketplace or network (seller revenue, creator uploads, driver earnings).
  • Trust and safety: reports, blocks, policy-violation prevalence, unsubscribes.

A counter-metric is narrower. It is chosen for a particular primary metric to catch the most likely way that metric could be improved without delivering value. Examples:

For every guardrail, decide in advance what change would block a launch. "Must not get worse" is not a rule a launch review can apply; "p95 latency must not rise by more than 20 milliseconds, and crash rate must not rise by a statistically significant amount" is. ps7 covers how to handle borderline guardrail movements.

6. What makes a good decision metric

Six properties matter for a metric that will decide launches. A metric need not be perfect on all six; the point is to know where each candidate is weak.

Sensitive: a real effect of plausible size produces a statistically detectable change within the experiment's duration. Attributable: when it moves, the movement comes from the change being tested, not from seasonality, marketing campaigns or other teams' launches (randomised experiments help a lot here, because both arms see the same outside world). Hard to game: it is difficult to raise without delivering the value it stands for. Cheap and fast to compute: available daily from existing logs, not requiring a quarterly survey. Directional: everyone agrees which direction is good (time spent fails this). Normalised: per user or per unit, so arms of different sizes and periods of growth are comparable. Google's Rules of Machine Learning compresses several of these into Rule 13: "choose a simple, observable and attributable metric for your first objective".

Sensitivity is the property most often ignored, and it can be calculated. From math7, an experiment with n users per arm can detect, with 80% power at 5% significance, an absolute difference of about (1.96 + 0.84) × sqrt(2σ²/n) = 2.8 × sqrt(2σ²/n), where σ is the metric's standard deviation across users. Dividing by the mean gives the minimum detectable effect (MDE) in relative terms. Compare three candidate metrics for a shopping app with n = 50,000 per arm:

The arithmetic for revenue: 2.8 × sqrt(2 × 225 / 50,000) = 2.8 × 0.0949 = 0.266, which is 13.3% of $2.00. Revenue per user is heavy-tailed: a few large spenders produce a standard deviation many times the mean. Most product changes move revenue by a percent or two, so an experiment using raw revenue as its primary metric will come back "no significant change" almost every time. Winsorising, capping values at a high percentile, cuts the variance sharply (the capped row assumes a cap at $50 changes the mean slightly and the standard deviation a lot). Other options are using a conversion rate as the primary and revenue as a guardrail, or variance-reduction techniques such as CUPED (math7). The general lesson: before choosing a primary metric, estimate its MDE at the sample size you can afford.

7. Ratio-metric traps

Many important metrics are ratios: click-through rate (CTR, clicks over impressions), conversion rate, revenue per session, average page load time. A ratio can change because its numerator changed, because its denominator changed, or because the population it is computed over changed. Four traps follow.

Trap 1: the denominator moved. In an experiment described in Microsoft's "dirty dozen" paper (Dmitriev et al., KDD 2017), a module near the bottom of the home page of MSN (Microsoft's news and web portal) was moved higher, so users had to scroll less to reach it. The module's CTR fell by 40%. Checked separately, both parts rose: clicks on the module by 74%, the number of times it was displayed by 200%. A ratio of 1.74 / 3.00 = 0.58 is a 42% drop, matching the headline. Moving the module up showed it to many people who were less interested, so the share who clicked fell while the total benefit grew; whole-page CTR stayed flat and revenue rose. The experiment was a success that its main metric called a failure.

Trap 2: the population changed (metric sample ratio mismatch). In another MSN experiment, links opened in a new tab instead of the same tab. Average home-page load time rose 8.32%. The cause: the treatment had 7.8% fewer home-page loads, because users no longer pressed the back button to return (the home page was still open in the old tab), and back-button reloads are fast thanks to browser caching. Removing many fast loads from the average made the remaining average slower, without any page actually loading more slowly. When the denominator's population differs between arms, the ratio compares different things.

Trap 3: average of ratios versus ratio of totals. Take two users. User A saw 100 impressions and clicked 10 (10%). User B saw 2 and clicked 1 (50%). The ratio of totals is 11 / 102 = 10.8%; the average of ratios (per-user CTR, averaged) is (10% + 50%) / 2 = 30%. The first is dominated by heavy users; the second treats every user equally. If a change improves the experience of light users and slightly worsens heavy users, the two definitions can move in opposite directions. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions. Pick one deliberately, write it down, and keep it fixed across experiments.

Trap 4: wrong variance. When users are randomised but the metric's denominator is impressions or sessions (ratio of totals), the impressions are not independent: one user's sessions are correlated. Treating each impression as an independent observation underestimates the variance and produces false positives. The delta method or a bootstrap that resamples users (math7) gives correct intervals.

The practical rule for all four: for every ratio metric in a decision, also look at its numerator and denominator as per-user metrics, and check whether the denominator's population changed between arms.

8. Putting it together: a metric set

Before assembling the set, two choices remain that beginners often skip: the time window and the unit.

Time window. The same behaviour can be measured per day, per week or per experiment period, and the choice changes what the metric rewards. Daily metrics are noisy for products people use a few times a week: a recipe app's "cooked today" is mostly zeros. Weekly windows smooth out day-of-week patterns (many products have weekend peaks), so a change that shifts usage from Saturday to Sunday does not look like a win or a loss. For experiments, a common pattern is "per user over the whole experiment period": each user contributes one number, such as total orders during the three weeks, which keeps the unit of analysis equal to the unit of randomisation and simplifies the variance calculation.

Unit. Per user, per session, per page view and per request answer different questions. Per-session metrics can be gamed by changing what counts as a session (shorter timeouts create more, shorter sessions). Per-user metrics are the default for decisions because users are what experiments randomise and what businesses retain. When a metric is naturally per session (orders per session), compute it, but also report its per-user counterpart (orders per user), because a change that splits one long session into two short ones can raise sessions per user and lower orders per session while nothing real changed.

A worked check of why the unit matters: suppose a treatment shortens the session timeout from 30 to 15 minutes. Before, a typical user has 3.0 sessions a week and 0.36 orders, so 0.12 orders per session. After, the same behaviour is split into 3.6 sessions, so orders per session read 0.10, a 17% "drop", and sessions per user a 20% "rise". Orders per user, unchanged at 0.36, shows correctly that nothing happened.

Back to the calendar app's smart scheduling. A complete metric set reads: primary, median messages from first request to confirmed meeting, per scheduling thread (lower is better, task success); counter-metric, share of feature-scheduled meetings rescheduled within 24 hours; guardrails, calendar-page latency at the 95th percentile, weekly active users' next-week return rate, and support tickets about scheduling; diagnostics, proposal acceptance rate and the share of threads where proposals were shown. Each has a role, a direction and, for the guardrails, a blocking threshold. Note what this set does not contain: proposals shown (activity, not value) and total meetings created (rises with growth).

Composite metrics and the OEC

Microsoft's experimentation literature uses the term OEC (Overall Evaluation Criterion) for the metric that decides an experiment. Ideally it is a single metric; when the goal has parts that trade off, it can be a weighted combination, such as revenue per user minus a penalty proportional to ad load. A composite makes the trade-off explicit, so different teams' launches are judged consistently instead of being argued case by case. Its costs are that the weights encode a value judgement that someone must own and revisit, and that a composite can hide which component moved. The usual practice is to decide on the composite but always report its components next to it.

Validating a proxy across experiments, not across users

Most decision metrics are proxies for something slower: next-quarter retention, lifetime value. The tempting validation is to show that users with a high proxy also have high retention. That correlation is almost guaranteed and almost useless, because engaged users score high on everything. The test that matters is whether interventions that move the proxy also move the long-term outcome. With a history of past experiments, plot each experiment's effect on the proxy against its effect on the long-term outcome; a proxy is good if the points line up with a positive slope. Duolingo's former product lead describes a related approach: modelling how changes in each retention rate would compound into daily active users before choosing which one to target (ps5).

Leading and lagging indicators

A lagging indicator, such as 90-day retention, is close to what the business cares about but only measurable after a long delay. A leading indicator, such as the Gmail-style "visited on five or more of the last seven days", responds within days and predicts the lagging one. Experiments need leading indicators; strategy reviews need lagging ones. The work is in connecting them: an organisation that tracks only leading indicators drifts towards whatever is easy to move this week, and one that tracks only lagging indicators cannot run experiments at all.

Metric definitions are code, and they drift

"Weekly active user" sounds unambiguous until you ask: active doing what (opening the app, or doing the core action)? Counted by user account or by device? In which time zone does the week start? Are logged-out users included? Large companies keep a metric catalogue in which each metric's definition is versioned code with an owner, because two dashboards disagreeing by 3% can consume weeks of meetings. When a logging change alters a definition, mature teams back-fill or annotate the series so that the change is not mistaken for user behaviour. Interviewers like candidates who ask "how is that metric defined?" before reasoning about it.

Sensitivity as a design goal

Because the MDE scales with σ / mean and with 1 / sqrt(n), there are only three ways to detect smaller effects: more users, longer experiments, or lower-variance metrics. The third is often the cheapest. Options include capping outliers, using binary versions of continuous metrics ("did the user order at least once?" instead of "how many orders?"), measuring only users who were actually exposed to the change (triggered analysis, which removes users whose experience could not have changed and therefore dilute the effect), and adjusting for pre-experiment behaviour (CUPED, in math7). Microsoft and others report that teams spend real effort building metrics designed for sensitivity, because a metric that never reaches significance cannot guide decisions.

Effort metrics versus success metrics

A recurring pattern from Bing's puzzling outcomes: when users fail, they work harder, and many engagement metrics measure work. Queries per user rise when results are bad; time in a settings page rises when it is confusing; messages to support rise when a product breaks. The defence is to pair every effort metric with a success metric: queries per session with time to a successful click; support messages with resolution rate. When an effort metric jumps and the paired success metric does not, assume something got worse.

Where Goodhart's law starts

Once a metric becomes a target that teams or models optimise, it starts to lose its connection to the thing it measured, because optimisation finds the cheapest ways to move the number. A fuller treatment, including what happens when a model rather than a team does the optimising, is in ps3; here it is enough to say that counter-metrics, periodic re-validation of proxies, and rotating the specific driver targets are the practical defences.

Metrics for two-sided products

In a marketplace or a network, each side has its own north star, and the platform's real goal is a healthy exchange between them. A ride-hailing app's riders care about pick-up time and price; its drivers care about earnings per hour. A metric set that measures only riders will approve changes that quietly lower driver earnings, which shows up months later as fewer drivers, longer pick-up times and worse rider metrics. The standard practice is a primary metric on the side the change targets, plus at least one guardrail on the other side, plus a marketplace-health metric such as the share of requests fulfilled. Experiments on one side also leak to the other (riders in the treatment compete for the same drivers as riders in control), which is why ps6 treats marketplace experiments separately.

Survey-based metrics

HEART's Happiness category relies on attitudes, which logs cannot see: satisfaction, perceived ease, regret. Survey metrics catch failures that behaviour hides, such as people who keep using a product they dislike because switching is costly. Their weaknesses are low response rates, response bias (unhappy or very happy users answer more), and small samples per experiment arm. Practical patterns are a short in-product survey shown to a random sample (one question, a five-point scale), tracked as a weekly series rather than as an experiment metric; and, at larger scale, training a model to predict survey answers for everyone from behaviour, then validating it on held-back responses. YouTube describes doing exactly that for "valued watchtime" (ps3). The model's prediction then becomes a metric, with all the proxy caveats that implies.

The real case: Bing's puzzling outcome

In "Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments: Five Puzzling Outcomes Explained" (Kohavi, Deng, Frasca, Longbotham, Walker and Xu, KDD 2012), the first puzzle comes from Bing. A bug in an experiment's treatment showed users very poor search results. Two of the organisation's key metrics improved dramatically: distinct queries per user went up over 10% and revenue per user over 30%. The paper asks how Bing should evaluate experiments if a broken product can look like a big win, and answers that the long-term goals (query share and revenue) "do not align with short-term measurements in experiments. If they did, we would intentionally degrade quality to raise query share and revenue!"

The explanation is that degraded results force people to issue more queries and click more on ads, which the authors compare to raising prices in a shop: short-term revenue rises, but customers move to the competition over time. Their fix was a decomposition. Monthly distinct queries equal users per month × sessions per user × distinct queries per session. In a randomised experiment, users per arm are fixed by the design, so that term cannot be the target. Distinct queries per session should fall when search works well, because users find answers with fewer queries. The term that captures users choosing to come back is sessions per user, and that is what the paper recommends optimising. The later "dirty dozen" paper from the same group refers to sessions per user as "the key Bing metric".

The production lessons: a north star that rewards user effort can be raised by making the product worse; decomposing the top-level goal reveals which component reflects value; and when a change produces a dramatic win, check whether users are winning or merely working harder.

Failure modes you will see

Metric definition changes masquerading as behaviour. A logging library upgrade starts counting app foregrounds as sessions; sessions per user jump 7% overnight. Symptom: a step change on a single day across all segments, coinciding with a release. Defence: annotate deployments on metric charts and keep a data-quality metric (events per user per client version) that spots instrumentation changes.

Bots and internal traffic. Automated traffic inflates counts and dilutes ratios. A crawler that loads pages without clicking lowers CTR. The HEART paper lists filtering automated traffic as one of the challenges of log-based metrics. Defence: standard bot filters before metrics are computed, and monitoring of the share of traffic filtered.

Teams gaming drivers. When a team's bonus or roadmap depends on a driver metric, its members will find ways to move it, some of which do not help users. Symptom: the driver rises steadily while the north star is flat. Defence: counter-metrics owned by someone else, and reviewing driver metrics against the north star each quarter.

Too many metrics, no decision rule. An experiment scorecard with 200 metrics shows ten significant changes, some up, some down, and the launch meeting debates which matter. Defence: pre-declared primary and guardrails with thresholds; everything else is labelled diagnostic and cannot block or justify a launch on its own.

Tools and practice

Companies that run many experiments typically have a metric layer (a catalogue of definitions computed the same way for dashboards and experiments), an experimentation platform that computes the standard scorecard for every test (in10 designs one), automated checks for sample ratio mismatch, and metric review meetings where new metrics are proposed with evidence of sensitivity and of alignment with long-term outcomes. For an ML engineer, the relevant practice is to insist that the online metric for a model launch comes from that shared layer rather than a bespoke notebook, so results are comparable across launches.

How a metric review works

Mature experimentation organisations treat a new decision metric like a new API: someone proposes it with a written definition, evidence that it is sensitive (its MDE at typical experiment sizes), evidence that it is aligned (past experiments where it moved together with long-term outcomes), and a list of known ways it can be gamed. Reviewers check that it does not duplicate an existing metric under a new name, that its data source is reliable, and that it has an owner who will maintain it. Only then does it enter the standard scorecard. The process sounds bureaucratic, but it is cheaper than the alternative: two teams arguing about whose version of "active user" is right in the middle of a launch review.

Metric design drill

Trailhead is a hypothetical hiking app (3 million monthly users) launching offline maps for subscribers. The product manager (PM) proposes "map downloads per week" as the success metric. Propose a better metric set with roles, and estimate whether your primary metric can detect a 3% effect with 40,000 subscribers per arm.

Answer

Why downloads is weak: it measures activity, not value (people download maps they never use), it rises if the download button is made more prominent, and it is not normalised.

Goal: help subscribers navigate confidently where there is no signal, so they hike more with the app and stay subscribed.

Primary: recorded hikes per weekly active subscriber (a hike with GPS tracking for at least 1 km). Counter-metric: downloaded maps never opened within 30 days. Guardrails: subscription cancellations, app storage-related uninstalls, crash rate on map screens, support tickets about storage. Diagnostics: share of hikes that used an offline map; hikes in areas with poor signal.

Sensitivity: assume a mean of 0.9 recorded hikes per weekly subscriber with standard deviation 1.4. Absolute MDE ≈ 2.8 × sqrt(2 × 1.96 / 40,000) = 2.8 × sqrt(0.000098) = 2.8 × 0.0099 = 0.0277, which is 3.1% of 0.9. So 40,000 per arm is just about enough for a 3% effect over one week of data; running three or four weeks and using hikes over the whole period per subscriber lowers relative variance further, and limiting the analysis to subscribers who open the map screen (triggered analysis) helps more. If the expected effect were 1%, the answer would be "this metric cannot detect it at this size; use the share of hikes in low-signal areas, where the effect should be concentrated".
