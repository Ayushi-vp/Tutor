A short-video app trains its first ranking model to predict clicks. It works: click-through rate rises 9% in the experiment, and the model ships. Within a month the content team notices that the top of the feed is full of videos with shocking thumbnails and titles that the videos do not deliver on. Users click, watch four seconds, and leave. Click-through is at a record high and complaints are too.

The team switches the label to watch time. Now the feed fills with long compilations and endless clips that people half-watch late at night. Average watch time per user rises 12%. Survey scores fall, and a growing share of users say they "wasted time" on the app. The team has optimised two different labels, and each time the model faithfully delivered more of the label and less of what anyone wanted.

The engineers did nothing wrong technically. The models were well trained; offline metrics improved; the experiments were run properly. The failure was upstream, in the choice of what to optimise. A model is a powerful optimiser of whatever objective it is given, and the gap between the objective and the product goal is exactly where it will find its gains. This is the most consequential product decision an ML engineer makes, and it is often made in an afternoon by whoever writes the training pipeline.

This lesson is about closing that gap. It covers the labels you can actually observe and how far each sits from value; a worked example where three objectives pick three different winners; how production systems combine several predicted outcomes into one score, and how to set the weights; Goodhart's law in its four forms, with ranking examples; how to handle ecosystem goals like creator health; and YouTube's public account of moving from clicks to watch time to survey-based "valued watchtime", including the 20% drop in views it accepted along the way.

A note on scope: the examples are mostly feeds and search, because that is where the public evidence is richest, but the same chain applies to fraud models (what counts as fraud, and what does a false block cost?), ETA predictors (is the goal accuracy, or riders not being surprised?) and LLM assistants (is the goal a high rating, or the task actually done?). The last part of the body shows those translations.

Supervised models learn from labels: observed outcomes attached to past examples. A ranking model for a feed is trained on past impressions (times an item was shown) labelled with what happened: was it clicked, how long was it watched, was it liked. At serving time the model predicts those outcomes for new candidates, and the system sorts by a score built from the predictions. Everything the model "wants" comes from the labels and the scoring formula. It has no idea what the product is for.

An analogy: paying a sales team on commission. If you pay per sale, they sell, including to customers who will return the product next week. If you pay per sale net of returns, they become more careful. If you pay on customer satisfaction scores, some will start coaching customers on how to fill in the survey. Each pay scheme is a proxy for "build lasting customer relationships", and each is exploited in its own way. The fix in sales is the same as in ML: combine several measures, watch for gaming, and change the scheme when it stops tracking the real goal.

Where the analogy breaks: salespeople understand the intent behind their targets and many will honour it. A model has no intent to honour. It will exploit any regularity that raises its objective, including ones no human would think of, such as learning that videos uploaded at certain times by certain channels have inflated watch time because of autoplay. Models are more literal optimisers than people, which makes the objective more important, not less.

The core move of this lesson is to treat the chain explicitly. You decide the product goal and online metric (ps2). You choose labels that are as close to them as you can observe at useful volume. You combine several predicted labels into a score whose weights express what matters. You add rules the model should not have to learn, such as safety filters and diversity requirements. And you check, online, that optimising the score moves the product metric. When it does not, the fix is almost always further up the chain than the model.

A reader who stops here has the right picture: labels are proxies, every proxy has a gap, a single proxy is easy to exploit, and a production objective is a deliberate combination of several proxies plus constraints, judged online. The rest of the lesson makes each step concrete with numbers.

One more framing helps before the details. There are three distinct questions hiding in "what should the model optimise?", and they have different owners. What is valuable is a product question, answered with user research and the north-star metric. What can we observe, at what volume and delay is a data question, answered by the logging and the label pipeline. How should the observable signals be combined is a modelling and experimentation question, answered with calibrated heads, weights and online tests. Weak objectives usually come from answering only the second question ("we have click logs, so we predict clicks") and treating the first and third as settled.

It also helps to know what the model actually sees at training time, because that is where many objective problems hide. Each training row is one impression: the features of the user, the item and the context at the moment it was shown, plus the logged outcome. Impressions that were never shown have no row. Outcomes that happen later (the user came back tomorrow, the purchase was refunded next week) are only present if someone joined them onto the row, which requires a pipeline that waits for them. So the practical question "what can the model optimise?" is often answered by "what does the label pipeline join onto each impression, and how long does it wait?". If the answer is "clicks within 30 seconds", that is the objective, whatever the design document says.

1. The labels you can actually observe

For a content or commerce product, the outcomes you can log after an impression form a ladder, from dense and fast but far from value, to sparse and slow but close to it:

Two forces pull in opposite directions. Dense, fast labels give the model lots of training data and quick feedback, which is why first models nearly always use clicks. Sparse, slow labels sit closer to value but give little data and can be impossible to attribute to a single impression: a user returned next week because of the whole experience, not one video. Every production objective is a compromise between these forces.

All of these labels are also shaped by what the system chose to show (rec10). Items shown at the top are clicked more because of their position; items never shown have no labels at all. A model trained on these logs partly learns the old ranking's preferences. This matters for objectives because a label that seems to measure "user preference" also measures "what we showed".

2. The proxy gap, worked with numbers

Consider three candidate videos for one slot in a feed. Each has a predicted click probability, an average watch time for people who click, and the share of viewers who would rate it four or five stars in a survey.

VideoP(click)Watch if clickedRated 4-5 starsExpected watch per impressionExpected valued watch
A: shocking thumbnail, little content0.1020 s10%2 s0.2 s
B: clear 7-minute tutorial0.04400 s80%16 s12.8 s
C: 40-minute compilation0.06600 s30%36 s10.8 s

Expected watch per impression is P(click) × watch if clicked: 0.10 × 20 = 2, 0.04 × 400 = 16, 0.06 × 600 = 36 seconds. Expected valued watch weights that by the share of viewers who rate it highly: 2 × 0.1 = 0.2, 16 × 0.8 = 12.8, 36 × 0.3 = 10.8 seconds.

A click objective picks A. A watch-time objective picks C, by a wide margin. A valued-watch objective picks B. Same candidates, same predictions, three different winners. And the gap compounds: a feed ranked by clicks shows A-like videos all day, which teaches creators to make A-like videos, which changes tomorrow's candidates. The choice of objective shapes not just one ranking but what content exists on the platform.

3. Multi-objective scores: the value model

Because no single label equals value, most production rankers predict several outcomes and combine them. A multi-task model has shared layers and a separate output head for each outcome (rec6): P(click), expected watch time, P(like), P(share), P(not interested). The heads are combined by a value model, usually a weighted sum:

score = w_click·P(click) + w_watch·E[watch minutes] + w_like·P(like) + w_share·P(share) − w_neg·P(not interested)

The weights express how much each outcome is worth relative to the others. They are a product decision, not something the model learns. Changing them changes the ranking without retraining anything, which makes them a powerful and dangerous lever.

For Z: 0.08 + 2.2 + 5 × 0.015 + 10 × 0.003 − 20 × 0.02 = 0.08 + 2.2 + 0.075 + 0.03 − 0.4 = 1.985. The satisfaction-heavy weights are w_click = 1, w_watch = 0.5, w_like = 20, w_share = 40, w_neg = 40. For Y: 0.05 + 0.75 + 0.8 + 0.4 − 0.2 = 1.80; for Z: 0.08 + 1.1 + 0.3 + 0.12 − 0.8 = 0.80. Under the first setting Z ranks first; under the second, Y does. Nothing about the model changed.

A value model is only as good as its inputs' calibration: whether a predicted 0.04 like probability really means about 4 in 100 viewers like it (ml7). Because the score adds predictions, a head that over-predicts by a factor of two after a retrain silently doubles that outcome's weight. Ranking metrics such as AUC do not detect this, because they only care about order within a head. Teams that use value models check each head's calibration after every retrain, overall and in major segments.

4. Setting the weights

There are three sources of evidence for weights, usually used together.

Value per event. Where an outcome has a known business value, use it. Ads rankers do this naturally: expected revenue is P(click) × bid. For consumer engagement, direct values are rarer, but some exist, for example the estimated contribution of a follow to future sessions.

Regression on a satisfaction signal. Take a sample of impressions where users answered a satisfaction survey, and regress the survey score on the observed events. If the regression says a like adds 1.2 points of satisfaction, a share 1.5, each minute of watching 0.08 (up to a cap), and a "not interested" click subtracts 2.0, those coefficients suggest relative weights. Survey samples are small and biased, so treat the result as a starting point, not an answer.

Online tuning. Run experiments with several weight settings and pick the one that best moves the long-term online metric (next-week return, survey satisfaction) within guardrails. This is the final arbiter, because it measures the effect of the whole ranking on users. It is slow (each setting needs an experiment arm) and noisy, so teams usually tune only a few weights at a time.

Whatever the method, weights need an owner, a written rationale and a review schedule. They drift out of date when the model's heads change, when the product adds a new surface, or when user behaviour shifts.

5. Goodhart's law in four forms

Goodhart's law began as an economist's observation about monetary policy (Charles Goodhart, 1975) and is usually quoted in the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern's general phrasing: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." For ML objectives it is the central hazard. Manheim and Garrabrant (2018) distinguish four mechanisms, and each has a ranking example.

Regressional. The proxy is the goal plus noise. When you select the items with the highest proxy, you also select items with lucky noise, so the goal improves less than the proxy suggests. In ranking: the top-predicted items have prediction errors that are, on average, positive; realised engagement on the top slots is lower than predicted. This is mild and unavoidable; it is why offline gains shrink online.

Extremal. The proxy tracks the goal in the normal range of the data but not at the extremes an optimiser pushes towards. Watch time tracks value for videos between two and twenty minutes; at ten-hour compilations, it tracks compulsion. A model trained on normal data and asked to maximise will drift into exactly the region where the relationship breaks.

Causal. The proxy correlates with the goal because both are caused by something else, so intervening on the proxy does not move the goal. Users who share are happy, but adding share prompts raises shares without making anyone happier: sharing was a symptom of satisfaction, not its cause.

Adversarial. Other agents learn what the system rewards and produce inputs that trigger it. Creators adopt the thumbnail style the ranker rewards; sellers stuff keywords into listings; spammers learn which phrases pass the filter. This form only appears after launch, because it requires people to adapt to the system.

The practical defences map onto the four: expect regressional shrinkage and do not over-interpret offline gains; cap or transform proxies at extremes (cap watch-time credit per video); prefer labels that are causes of value, validated by experiments; and monitor content and supply-side changes after launch.

6. Ecosystem objectives: constraints and policy layers

Some goals do not belong to any single impression. Creator health (do new and small creators get enough exposure to keep uploading?), diversity of topics, freshness, and fairness of exposure all describe the whole ranking or the whole platform. Adding them as another weight in the value model is possible but awkward: the weight's effect varies as other predictions shift, and it is hard to audit.

The usual alternative is a constraint or a policy layer applied after scoring (rec12): "at least 10% of slots go to creators with under 1,000 followers", "no more than two videos from the same channel in the top ten", "items flagged by the safety classifier are removed regardless of score". Google's Rules of Machine Learning recommends a similar separation in Rule 15: keep spam filtering and quality ranking in a policy layer rather than mixing them into one model. Constraints are easy to explain, easy to audit, and their cost can be measured by experiment (how much does the primary metric fall when the new-creator floor rises from 5% to 10%?).

7. Delayed outcomes and surrogate labels

The outcomes that matter most, such as 90-day retention, lifetime value and loan repayment, arrive too late to train on directly; by the time you have the label, the world has changed. Teams use surrogate labels: short-term outcomes that predict the long-term one, such as 7-day return or first-week activity. Choosing a surrogate is exactly the proxy-validation problem from ps2: the surrogate must move with the long-term outcome across interventions, not just correlate with it across users.

A more sophisticated approach combines several short-term outcomes into a predicted long-term value, trained on historical users whose long-term outcomes are now known, and uses that prediction as the label. It reduces delay at the cost of one more model whose errors become the ranker's objective.

8. A procedure for choosing the objective

Pulling it together, for a new ranking or prediction system:

  1. Write the product goal and the online metric that will judge the launch (ps2).
  2. List observable labels with their frequency, delay and distortion; pick a primary label dense enough to learn from and a few secondary labels that catch its distortion (watch time to correct clicks, negative feedback to correct watch time).
  3. Train heads for each, check calibration, and combine in a simple value model with documented weights.
  4. Put platform-level goals (safety, diversity, creator exposure) in a policy layer as constraints.
  5. Run an online experiment judged on the product metric, with counter-metrics for each label's distortion.
  6. When offline gains stop translating into online gains, revisit the labels and weights before the model architecture, as Rule 38 of Rules of ML puts it: "don't waste time on new features if unaligned objectives have become the issue".

9. Worked back-chain: from next-week return to a ranking objective

Interviewers often give you the product metric and ask you to design the objective. Here is the reasoning, made explicit, for a short-video feed whose launch metric is next-week return rate (the share of this week's active users who are active next week).

First, ask what per-impression outcomes plausibly cause users to come back. Candidate causes: they found something they enjoyed (watch time on content they rate highly), they found something to follow (follows of new creators, which create a reason to return), and they did not have a bad experience (absence of "not interested" and reports). Candidate symptoms that do not cause return: clicks on their own, and very long sessions late at night that users later regret.

Second, check which outcomes you can log at volume. Clicks and watch time, yes. Follows and "not interested", at lower volume. Ratings, only from a survey sample, so you would predict them with a satisfaction model trained on survey responses.

Third, validate with history. Using, say, 40 past ranking experiments, regress each experiment's effect on next-week return against its effects on average watch time, follows per user, "not interested" rate and predicted satisfaction. Suppose watch time explains little once satisfaction is included, while follows and "not interested" explain a lot. That tells you which heads deserve weight.

Fourth, compose: score = P(click) × E[watch | click] × predicted satisfaction + w_follow × P(follow) − w_neg × P(not interested), with watch-time credit capped per video so ten-hour compilations cannot dominate. Fifth, test the composed objective against the current one with next-week return as the primary metric, and expect raw watch time to fall a little; decide in advance that this is acceptable. That is the full translation, and saying these five steps out loud is a strong answer to "what would your model optimise?".

10. Checking an objective before you launch it

Online experiments are the final test, but they are slow and expensive, so teams check a new objective offline first. Three checks are cheap and catch most surprises.

Look at what it ranks first. Re-rank a sample of real candidate sets with the new objective and read the top results. If the top ten for a typical user are all 40-minute compilations, you have found an extremal problem without an experiment. This sounds unscientific, but it catches glaring objective bugs quickly. Rule 23 of Rules of ML ("you are not a typical end user") is the caveat: your taste is not your users', so reading results complements measurement rather than replacing it.

Compare distributions. For the old and new objectives, compare the distribution of the top-k items by length, age, creator size, content category and predicted negative feedback. A new objective that shifts exposure heavily toward one category or away from small creators should be a conscious choice, not a surprise found in a launch review.

Estimate the effect counterfactually. With logs from a system that sometimes explored (showed items in randomised positions), you can estimate how the new ranking would have performed on the old traffic by reweighting logged outcomes (inverse propensity weighting, rec10). These estimates are noisy and only cover outcomes you logged, but they flag objectives that would obviously lose on a guardrail.

11. The same translation outside feeds

Fraud detection. The product goal is to stop fraud without blocking good customers. The label is usually "charged back within 90 days", which arrives late and only for transactions that were allowed (blocked ones have no outcome). The objective is not "maximise accuracy" but "minimise expected cost": each false block costs the lost margin plus the chance the customer leaves, and each missed fraud costs the chargeback plus fees. Writing the objective as cost lets you pick a threshold that is a product decision with a number on it, which hd4 develops.

ETA (estimated time of arrival) prediction. Training to minimise mean absolute error treats one minute early and one minute late the same, but riders are more upset by late arrivals than early ones. An asymmetric loss that penalises under-prediction more (hd20 describes one used in production) is a product decision encoded in the loss function.

LLM assistants. Thumbs-up rate is the dense, fast label, and it rewards answers that feel good: confident, agreeable, long. Task completion (did the user's problem get solved, did they not have to ask again) is the value. The objective for evaluating prompts or fine-tunes should weight task completion and correctness, with thumbs-up as a secondary signal and sycophancy checks as counter-metrics (ps10 and sf3).

Objective, metric and loss are three different things

Interviewers sometimes test whether you separate them. The product metric is what the launch is judged on (next-week return). The objective is what the ranking maximises (the value-model score). The loss is what each head minimises during training (log loss for P(click), a regression loss for watch time). A model can minimise its loss perfectly and still rank badly if the objective is wrong; an objective can be right and still fail if a head's loss rewards the wrong thing (for example, mean squared error on raw watch time lets a few hour-long sessions dominate training). Being explicit about all three, and about which one failed when something goes wrong, is a senior signal.

Predicting expected value directly

One elegant trick avoids a separate watch-time head. YouTube's 2016 recommendation paper (Covington, Adams and Sargin) describes training the ranking model with weighted logistic regression: clicked impressions are positives weighted by their watch time, unclicked impressions are negatives with weight one. The learned odds then approximate expected watch time per impression, so ranking by the model's output ranks by expected watch time. It is a clean way to optimise a continuous value with a classification model, and it shows how the choice of example weights is itself an objective choice: weight positives by satisfaction-adjusted watch time, and the same machinery optimises something different.

Weighted sums versus constrained optimisation

A weighted sum assumes outcomes are substitutes at a fixed rate: one share is worth exactly ten likes everywhere. Often the real preference is a threshold: "maximise watch time as long as negative-feedback rate stays below 1%". That is a constrained optimisation problem, and the standard technique (Lagrange multipliers, math11) turns the constraint into a weight that is adjusted until the constraint just holds. In practice teams approximate it by tuning the negative-feedback weight until the guardrail is met. Saying "this is really a constraint, and the weight is its multiplier" shows you understand why the weights are not arbitrary.

The objective shapes the data it learns from

A ranker trained on its own logs creates a feedback loop: what it promotes gets more labels, so it learns more about it and promotes it more (rec10). The objective decides what enters that loop. A click objective fills the logs with clicks on clickbait, so the next model is even better at clickbait. Exploration (showing some items for learning rather than for score), counterfactual weighting of logged data, and holding out a small randomly ranked slice are the standard counters. For objective design the lesson is that a bad objective gets worse over retrains, not just stays bad.

Reward models in LLM training are the same problem

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF, lm6) trains a reward model on human comparisons of responses, then optimises a language model to score highly on it. The reward model is a proxy for "what people want", and pushing hard against it produces reward hacking: longer answers, more flattering answers, confident tone (sf3 covers sycophancy). The mitigations rhyme with recommender practice: a penalty for drifting far from a reference model (Kullback-Leibler regularisation, the analogue of a constraint), several reward signals rather than one, and evaluation on fresh human judgements rather than on the reward model itself. An AI engineer who can draw the parallel between a feed's value model and an LLM's reward model is showing exactly the transferable judgement senior roles want.

Who should own the weights

In many organisations the value-model weights live in a config file edited by whichever engineer is tuning the ranker this quarter. That is a governance gap: the weights decide what millions of users see and what creators are rewarded for. Mature teams treat them like a product specification: changes go through review, are tested in experiments, and are recorded with the reason and the measured effect. In an interview, mentioning that weight changes need the same launch discipline as model changes is a small point that signals experience.

User agency as part of the objective

Explicit controls, such as "not interested", "show fewer like this", topic follows and the ability to reset recommendations, provide labels that are closer to intent than any implicit signal, and they give users a way to correct the system when it drifts. YouTube's 2021 post lists dislikes alongside likes and shares as signals, and notes that how much each signal matters depends on the individual user. Designing for explicit feedback is therefore both a product feature and an objective-quality investment.

Diminishing returns and capping

Most outcomes have diminishing value: the tenth minute of a video is worth less than the first, and the fifth follow of the day less than the first. A linear weight on raw watch time ignores this and hands the largest rewards to the longest items, which is the extremal failure in another form. Common fixes are capping the credit per item (count watch time up to five minutes), using a concave transform such as the logarithm of watch time, or crediting completion of a fraction rather than raw minutes. Each choice changes which content the system favours, so it belongs in the documented objective, not in a feature engineer's preprocessing script.

The real case: YouTube's changing objective

In September 2021, YouTube's vice president (VP) of engineering, Cristos Goodrow, published a first-party account of the signals behind its recommendations. Its history is a compact version of this whole lesson. Clicks came first, but the post says YouTube "learned back in 2011 that clicking on a video doesn't mean you actually watched it", using the example of someone searching for Wimbledon highlights who clicks on videos whose thumbnails suggest match footage but which turn out to be people talking about the match.

So YouTube "added in watchtime in 2012". The post is candid about the cost: "When we first incorporated watchtime into recommendations, we saw an immediate 20% drop in views. But we believed that it was more important for us to deliver more value to viewers." That is a launch decision that accepted a large loss on one metric (views, a click-like measure) for a better objective.

Then the post acknowledges the next gap: "not all watchtime is equal". The author describes staying up late watching random videos instead of learning something, and says YouTube did not want viewers regretting the time they spent. Its response was to measure "valued watchtime", time spent watching a video that you consider valuable, through surveys asking viewers to rate videos from one to five stars, with follow-up questions about why. Only highly rated videos count as valued watchtime. Because few people answer surveys, YouTube trained a model to predict survey responses for everyone, and checks it by holding back some survey responses from training and comparing predictions with them. The post also lists sharing, likes and dislikes as signals, with their importance depending on the individual user.

The engineering lessons are general. Objectives evolve as each proxy's gap becomes visible. Moving to a better objective can cost a lot on the old metric, and someone has to be willing to accept that. And survey-based satisfaction becomes usable at scale only through a predictive model, whose own accuracy must then be monitored like any other.

Failure modes in production

Silent head miscalibration. A retrain changes the like head's calibration; items with many predicted likes jump up the ranking; engagement shifts in ways nobody intended. Symptom: the distribution of value-model scores shifts after a retrain while no weight changed. Defence: calibration checks per head as a deployment gate.

Weight changes without experiments. An engineer bumps the share weight to "encourage virality" in a config change. Shares rise; so do reports of misleading content. Symptom: counter-metrics move on a day with no model release. Defence: weights live in reviewed config, and changes go through the experiment platform.

Label definition drift. The client app changes when it logs "watched", for example after autoplay starts rather than after the user taps. Watch-time labels inflate for autoplayed items, and the model learns to favour items that autoplay well. Defence: data-quality metrics on labels per client version, and label definitions owned like metric definitions.

Supply-side adaptation. Months after a successful launch, creators or sellers adapt to the objective, and content quality shifts. Symptom: the share of new content matching a pattern the ranker likes rises steadily. Defence: monitor the supply side (content types, title styles, length distributions), not just user metrics.

Debugging an objective problem

When a launch improves the label but not the product metric, a systematic approach saves weeks. First, confirm the experiment is valid (sample ratio, logging, exposure) before blaming the objective. Second, check the counter-metrics for each label's known distortion: abandoned starts for clicks, negative feedback and regret surveys for watch time. Third, compare the content that gained exposure with the content that lost it, by category, length, creator size and age; objective problems usually show up as a systematic shift. Fourth, look at calibration of each head in the treatment; a miscalibrated head changes the effective weights. Fifth, segment the product-metric effect by user type: new users often react differently from heavy users. Only after these checks is it worth changing the model itself.

Goal: see three objectives pick different items, and trace the trade-off between watch time and satisfaction as a weight changes. About 15 minutes; Python 3.12 standard library.

  1. Save as objectives.py and run it:
import random
random.seed(7)
items = []
for i in range(5000):
    quality = random.random()             # hidden true value, 0..1
    bait = random.random()                # how clickbaity the packaging is
    length = random.choice([1, 3, 8, 20, 45])   # minutes
    p_click = 0.02 + 0.08 * bait + 0.02 * quality
    watch = length * (0.15 + 0.6 * quality) * (1 - 0.5 * bait)  # minutes if clicked
    p_happy = min(1.0, 0.05 + 0.85 * quality - 0.3 * bait + 0.1 * random.random())
    items.append((p_click, p_click * watch, p_click * watch * max(p_happy, 0), quality))

def top(key, k=50):
    best = sorted(items, key=key, reverse=True)[:k]
    n = len(best)
    return [round(sum(x[j] for x in best) / n, 3) for j in range(4)]

print("objective        p_click  E[watch]  E[valued]  quality")
for name, key in [("click", lambda x: x[0]), ("watch", lambda x: x[1]), ("valued", lambda x: x[2])]:
    print(f"{name:15}", top(key))
for lam in [0, 0.5, 1, 2, 5]:
    r = top(lambda x: x[1] + lam * x[2])
    print(f"watch + {lam} * valued -> E[watch] {r[1]}, E[valued] {r[2]}, quality {r[3]}")

Objective design drill

Wayfare, a hypothetical travel-booking app, ranks hotels in search results by predicted click probability. Bookings per search are flat, but the share of bookings cancelled within 48 hours has risen from 6% to 9% since the ranker launched. Diagnose the likely objective problem and propose a new objective, with how you would set it and test it.

Answer

Diagnosis: a click objective rewards listings whose photos and headline prices attract clicks. A likely pattern is low headline prices that rise at checkout (fees) or flattering photos; users click, some book, then cancel when they see the real price or reviews. That is a proxy gap with an adversarial element: hotels learn what the ranker rewards.

New objective: predict several outcomes per impression: P(click), P(book | click), P(cancel within 48 h | book), and expected margin per booking. Score = P(click) × P(book | click) × (1 − P(cancel)) × margin, i.e. expected completed-booking value per impression, plus a small weight on P(click) to keep the model learning from dense data.
