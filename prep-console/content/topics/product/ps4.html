It is 9:10 on a Tuesday morning. A message lands in the team channel: "DAU down 10% yesterday?!" with a screenshot of a dashboard line falling off a cliff. Within twenty minutes there are four theories. The growth lead blames a competitor's launch. A product manager points at Monday's redesign of the home screen. Someone mentions that a marketing campaign ended on Sunday. An engineer suggests it might be the new notification throttling. Each theory has a champion, each champion starts pulling data to support it, and by lunchtime there are four half-finished analyses that disagree.

By the afternoon someone notices that the drop is entirely in one app version on one platform, that server-side traffic from that version fell far less than client-side events, and that the version's release notes include "migrated analytics library". Part of the "drop" is events that were never sent. The rest, it turns out, is real: the same release broke a login refresh, and some users were logged out. Neither the competitor, the redesign, the campaign nor the throttling had anything to do with it.

This is the normal shape of metric investigations that go badly: people start from causes they find plausible and look for confirming evidence, instead of starting from the data and narrowing down. The skill interviewers test with "DAU dropped 10%, why?" is not guessing the right cause. It is having a method that finds the cause quickly whatever it is, communicates progress clearly, and does not get fooled by measurement problems, coincidences or mix shifts.

This lesson gives that method. It covers pinning down the question; checking that the change is real and the data valid; separating internal from external causes; segmenting to localise the change, ranked by contribution; decomposing funnels; separating mix shift from rate change, including Simpson's paradox with the real 1973 Berkeley admissions numbers; and a complete worked investigation from alert to root cause with numbers at every step.

A metric change investigation starts with an observation ("this number moved") and ends with an explanation that is specific enough to act on ("Android version 8.4 broke login refresh for users whose token expired; it cost about 960,000 daily users; halt the rollout and ship the hotfix"). In between is a narrowing process: each step rules out large classes of explanation until one remains.

The best analogy is a mechanic diagnosing a car that "has lost power". A good mechanic does not start by replacing the part they suspect most. They first check whether the complaint is real and measured correctly: is the speedometer accurate, is the car carrying a heavy load, is it going uphill? Then they check the obvious external factors: was it a hot day, did the owner switch fuel? Then they localise: is the problem at all speeds or only when accelerating, in all gears or one? Then they follow the power path step by step from fuel to wheels to find where it is lost. Only then do they open the engine.

Where the analogy breaks: a car is one machine, while a metric aggregates millions of users who differ. A product metric can fall even though nothing broke for anyone, simply because the mix of users changed (more users from a group that naturally uses the product less). Cars do not have an equivalent of Simpson's paradox. That is why segmentation and mix analysis are central to product diagnosis in a way they are not to mechanics.

The order is the method. Clarifying first prevents investigating the wrong question. Checking reality and data validity next prevents investigating a measurement artefact, which is the cheapest class of cause to rule out and among the most common. The timeline gives a list of candidate causes. Segmentation and funnel decomposition narrow the change to a place and a step, which usually points at one candidate. Mix analysis checks that the change is not just a reshuffling of who is in the data. Confirmation turns a correlation into a cause.

A reader who stops here has the essential method: clarify, check it is real, check the data, list what changed, localise by segment and step, separate mix from rate, confirm, act. The body works through each step with numbers and then runs a full investigation.

One habit makes the method work under pressure: write the hypotheses down as a list with a status for each (open, ruled out, confirmed) and update it as you go. It keeps a group investigation from splitting into competing analyses, it makes progress visible to the people waiting for an answer, and in an interview it shows the interviewer exactly how you are narrowing the space. "Ruled out: competitor launch (drop is only on Android, competitor affects all platforms); open: Android release 8.4, logging change" is the kind of sentence that signals method.

It also helps to recognise the two shapes a metric change can take, because they point to different families of cause. A step change, where the metric is normal one day and different the next, almost always has a discrete cause with a timestamp: a release, a configuration change, an outage, a logging change, a holiday. The investigation is a search for that timestamp. A trend change, where the metric drifts over weeks, almost never has a single timestamp; it comes from something accumulating, such as weaker new-user retention, a slow shift in where users come from, or a competitor gradually taking share. The investigation is a decomposition of flows over time. Saying which shape you are looking at in the first two minutes of an interview, and why it changes your plan, is an easy way to show method.

1. Pin down the question

"DAU dropped 10%" is not yet a question you can investigate. Ask five things first.

What exactly is the metric? DAU, daily active users, can mean users who opened the app, users who were logged in, or users who did the core action. It can be counted by account or by device, in UTC or local time. Different definitions react differently to different causes: a login bug hits "logged-in active" but not "app opened".

How big, compared with what? Down 10% versus yesterday is meaningless if yesterday was a Sunday and the product is used for work. Down 10% versus the same weekday last week (week-over-week) removes the weekly cycle. Down 10% versus a forecast takes trend and seasonality into account.

Since when, and how fast? A step change on one day points to a discrete event: a release, an outage, a logging change. A gradual slide over weeks points to something that accumulates: worse retention, slower acquisition, a competitor.

Is it one metric or many? If DAU, sessions, orders and revenue all fell together, something affected the whole product or its measurement. If DAU fell but orders did not, either the lost users were low-value or DAU's measurement changed.

Who noticed, and how? An automated alert, an executive reading a dashboard, a support spike. Support tickets mentioning a specific error are gold.

In an interview, ask two or three of these, state assumptions for the rest, and say them back: "So daily logged-in users, measured in UTC, fell from about 10.0 million to 9.0 million on Monday versus the previous Monday, suddenly, and other metrics also dipped. I'll start by checking whether that is outside normal variation and whether the data is complete."

2. Is the change real?

Metrics wobble. A 10% move only means something relative to normal variation. If Monday DAU over the last twelve weeks ranged between 9.8 and 10.2 million, the week-over-week change has a typical spread of about ±1.5%, and a 10% drop is several times that range: real. If the same metric for a small product swings ±12% week to week, a 10% drop is unremarkable.

The quick calculation: take the last 8-12 week-over-week changes for the same weekday, compute their standard deviation, and express today's change in those units. A change of more than three standard deviations is very unlikely to be noise. This is the same logic as the z-test in math6, applied to a time series rather than an experiment.

Seasonality, regular patterns such as weekly cycles, holidays and school terms, is the next suspect. Compare with the same period last year: if DAU also dipped 8% in the same week last year because of a national holiday or the start of school holidays, the current drop is probably seasonal. Some seasonal events move dates from year to year (Easter, Lunar New Year, Ramadan, Diwali), so compare with the event, not just the calendar date. If the team has a forecasting model with weekly and yearly components, its prediction interval is the natural yardstick.

3. Is the data valid?

This step is where experienced investigators save the most time. Before asking what users did differently, ask whether the measurement changed. Common measurement causes:

  • Late or partial data. Events from mobile clients that were offline arrive hours later. A pipeline job that failed for one region leaves a partition empty. Yesterday's value computed at 6 a.m. is too low and climbs during the day. Check data completeness: events per hour by source, partition row counts, and whether the value still changes when recomputed.
  • Logging changes. A client release migrates the analytics library, renames an event, or changes when an event fires. The drop is then confined to that client version. Microsoft's "dirty dozen" paper calls a related problem telemetry loss bias: client events can be lost, and if a change affects loss rates, metrics move without behaviour changing. They suggest comparing client events with corresponding server events (Skype's client and server "call" events) or using event sequence numbers to estimate loss.
  • Definition and processing changes. A new bot filter, a deduplication fix, a change in what counts as "active", a time-zone change in the pipeline. Check the data platform's change log.
  • Upstream outages. If the logging service itself was down, user activity happened but was not recorded.

The most powerful single check is corroboration with an independent source. Client-side analytics and server-side request logs are produced by different code. If client events fell 10% but server requests fell 1%, most of the drop is measurement. If both fell 10%, users really did less. In the worked investigation below, this check splits the drop into a measurement part and a real part.

4. Build a timeline: internal and external causes

Once the drop is real and the data trusted, list what changed around the time it started. Split the list in two.

Internal causes are changes the company made: client and server releases (including staged rollouts), experiments started or ramped, configuration and feature-flag changes, marketing changes (a campaign ending, a budget cut, an ad account suspended), pricing changes, notification or email volume changes, outages and incidents, and partner changes (a payment provider switch, a new app-store listing).

External causes are outside the company's control: holidays and seasonal events, weather, major news or sports events, competitor launches or promotions, platform changes (an operating-system update, a browser change, an app-store ranking algorithm change, a change in how a search engine ranks your pages), regulation, and outages at providers you depend on.

Put both on a timeline next to the metric. Internal changes are easy to list if the company annotates dashboards with deployments and experiments; if not, building that list is the first task. External causes are harder to enumerate, but two tests help: does the drop appear in a whole region or market (external events tend to be geographic), and do industry-wide signals (app-store rankings, search trends, competitor traffic estimates) move too?

5. Segment to localise the change

Segmentation asks where the change happened. Split the metric by the dimensions most likely to align with causes: platform (iOS, Android, web), app version, country or region, new versus returning users, acquisition channel or traffic source, device type, and user tenure. A release affects one platform and version; an outage affects a region or a data centre; a campaign ending affects one channel; a holiday affects one country.

The key discipline is to rank segments by contribution to the absolute change, not by percentage change. Contribution = the segment's change in absolute terms, divided by the total change. Two cautions. First, the multiple-comparisons trap: with dozens of dimensions and hundreds of values, some slice will look extreme by chance, so start from likely dimensions and require the slice to explain a large share of the change. Second, segment shares themselves can move, which is the mix problem in section 7.

6. Decompose the funnel

Segmentation tells you where; funnel decomposition tells you which step. A funnel is a sequence of steps users pass through, with a conversion rate at each. For "logged-in daily active" on Android, a simplified funnel is: app opened → session token valid or refreshed → home screen loaded → active (any core action). Because the client-side events are under suspicion, build this funnel from server-side logs (app-open pings, authentication results, API calls). Per 100 Android users on 8.4 who opened the app on Monday, compared with 8.3:

As in ps2, a multiplicative funnel can be attributed through logs: ln(0.70 / 0.97) = −0.326 explains all of ln(67 / 92) = −0.317 (the other steps net to slightly positive, +0.009). The investigation now has a place (Android 8.4) and a step (session refresh).

The server-side funnel also splits the client-side shortfall in two. Server data says about 67 of every 92 expected actives were really active: 2.4M × 67 / 92 ≈ 1.75M real actives on 8.4, so the real loss is about 0.65M. The client-side count was 1.45M, so another 0.30M (about 17% of really active 8.4 users) are missing from client events: a measurement loss on top of the real one.

7. Mix shift and Simpson's paradox

Many metrics are rates: conversion, retention, click-through. An overall rate is a weighted average of segment rates, weighted by each segment's share of the population. So it can change for two reasons: the segment rates changed (a rate effect), or the shares changed (a mix effect, from a mix shift). Confusing the two leads to fixing products that are not broken.

Worked example. A shop's checkout conversion fell from 3.0% to 2.66% week-over-week. By platform:

PlatformShare beforeRate beforeShare afterRate after
Desktop33.3%5.00%20.0%5.10%
Mobile66.7%2.00%80.0%2.05%
Overall100%3.00%100%2.66%

Both platforms improved, yet the total fell. Check: before, 0.333 × 5.0 + 0.667 × 2.0 = 1.667 + 1.333 = 3.00%; after, 0.2 × 5.1 + 0.8 × 2.05 = 1.02 + 1.64 = 2.66%. An exact decomposition of the −0.34 point change:

Mix effect = Σ (change in share × old rate) = (−0.133 × 5.0) + (+0.133 × 2.0) = −0.667 + 0.267 = −0.40 points.

Rate effect = Σ (new share × change in rate) = 0.2 × 0.10 + 0.8 × 0.05 = 0.02 + 0.04 = +0.06 points.

Total −0.40 + 0.06 = −0.34. The product's conversion got slightly better on both platforms; the overall rate fell because the traffic shifted to mobile, perhaps because a social-media campaign brought in many mobile visitors. The right response is not to "fix checkout" but to ask whether the extra mobile visitors are worth their acquisition cost, and to report conversion by platform from now on.

This is Simpson's paradox in its product form: a trend that holds in every group reverses when the groups are combined, because the groups' sizes differ in a way related to the outcome. The most famous real case is graduate admissions at the University of California, Berkeley, in fall 1973. Of 8,442 men who applied, 44% were admitted; of 4,321 women, 35%. The gap was too large to be chance. But department by department, the picture changed: in the six largest departments, women's admission rates were similar to or higher than men's in most of them. Women had applied disproportionately to the departments with low admission rates, and men to departments with high ones. The full data showed 4 of 85 departments significantly biased against women and 6 against men, and the 1975 analysis by Bickel, Hammel and O'Connell in Science found a small but statistically significant bias in favour of women once department was accounted for.

Read down the women's columns: most women applied to C, D, E and F, where everyone's admission rate is low; most men applied to A and B, where rates are high. The overall 45% versus 30% is almost entirely a mix effect.

In product data, Simpson's paradox shows up whenever segment shares shift: traffic moving between platforms, between new and returning users, between countries with different baseline rates, or between experiment-exposed and unexposed users. The defence is to compute the mix and rate effects whenever an overall rate moves, and to choose a segmentation by asking which grouping is causally prior (the department applied to comes before the admission decision; the platform comes before conversion).

8. The worked investigation, end to end

Here is the full investigation for the hypothetical note-taking app Quillo, with the timeline, written as the status updates a senior engineer would post.

09:15, clarify. "DAU (logged-in users who opened at least one note, UTC day) was 9.00M on Monday versus 10.00M the previous Monday, −10%. Sessions and notes created are also down, by about 8%. Sudden: Sunday was normal."

09:30, real? "Monday week-over-week changes over the last 12 weeks ranged from −1.8% to +2.1%, standard deviation about 1.2%. This is around 8 standard deviations, and last year's same week was flat. Not noise, not seasonal."

09:50, data valid? "Data completeness normal: hourly event counts arrived on time from all regions, no failed pipeline jobs. Bot filter unchanged. Cross-check with server-side API requests: down 6.5%, not 10%. So part of the client-side drop may be measurement. Release log shows Android 8.4 started rolling out Monday 00:00 to 60% of Android users, with 'analytics SDK upgrade' and 'session handling refactor' in its notes."

10:20, timeline. "Internal: Android 8.4 rollout (Monday), a pricing-page experiment on web ramped to 50% (Friday), a marketing email paused (Saturday). External: no holidays in top markets; no competitor launches found; no app-store changes reported."

10:45, segment. "Android explains 95% of the drop (−0.95M of −1.00M). Within Android, the drop is entirely among users who received 8.4: about 2.4M expected, 1.45M active by client events, a 40% shortfall. Users still on 8.3 are normal. iOS and web are within normal range, which rules out the email pause and the web experiment as major causes."

11:15, funnel and measurement split. "Server-side funnel for 8.4: app opens per user are normal but the share of opens that end logged in fell from 97% to 70%; later steps convert normally. Login-refresh failures from 8.4 are up about 12×. Separately, about 17% of 8.4 users who were active by server logs have no client 'note opened' event at all, versus about 1% on 8.3, so the new analytics SDK is also dropping events."

11:40, confirm. "Dose-response: the shortfall tracks the rollout percentage by hour. Mechanism: the session refactor does not retry token refresh after a network timeout, so users whose token expired while offline are logged out. Engineering reproduced it. The logging loss is a known SDK batching issue."

12:00, act and size. "Rollout halted at 60%. Hotfix for token refresh in review; SDK batching fixed in 8.4.1. Of the 1.00M drop, roughly 0.65M is real (logged-out users; server-side corroborates), and roughly 0.3M is measurement (lost events from logged-in users). Users logged out are recoverable when they log in again; we will send a re-login prompt by push. Follow-up: add server-side DAU as a corroborating metric on the main dashboard, and a release gate that compares login-success rate on the new version against the old one at 5% rollout."

Notice what made this fast: the measurement check happened before the arguments, segmentation ranked by contribution found the place, the funnel found the step, and each update said what was known and what was still open.

9. Communicating during an investigation

Metric drops create anxiety, and anxious organisations generate theories. A short, regular status update with four parts calms that: what we know (with numbers), what we have ruled out, what we are checking next, and when the next update will come. Avoid naming a cause before step 8; say "leading hypothesis" until there is a mechanism and dose-response. In interviews, narrating the investigation as these updates is a strong way to show structure under pressure.

Base rates: what usually turns out to be the cause

Experienced investigators carry rough priors. Sudden step changes are most often measurement changes, releases or outages; external events are a smaller share, except around holidays. Gradual changes are most often acquisition-mix changes, retention changes in new cohorts, or slow drifts in measurement (a growing share of users on a client that logs differently). Saying these priors out loud in an interview ("for a sudden 10% drop, I'd weight measurement and releases highest") helps the interviewer see that your order of checks is deliberate. Priors guide the order of checks; they do not replace them.

Difference-in-differences when a change reached only some users

When a release, a policy or a regional change affected one group and not another, compare the change over time in the affected group with the change over the same period in the unaffected group. If DAU on Android 8.4 fell 40% relative to expected while Android 8.3 users were flat over the same days, the shared factors (day of week, news, weather) cancel, and the remaining difference estimates the effect. The key assumption is that the two groups would have moved in parallel without the change; check it by looking at their trends in the weeks before. Staged rollouts are valuable precisely because they create this comparison for free.

Anomaly detection on segments, not just totals

A large drop in a small segment can be invisible in the total: a payment method that fails completely in one country may move global conversion by 0.3%, within noise. Mature teams run anomaly detection, which forecasts expected values and flags those outside a band, on the key metrics by standard dimensions. The cost is alert volume: with 200 segment series at a 1% false-alarm rate each, two false alerts a day. Teams handle this by requiring a minimum absolute impact, alerting on a few critical segments, and routing segment alerts to dashboards rather than pagers.

Automated contribution analysis and its traps

Tools that automatically search a data cube for the dimension values that best explain a change are common and useful for speed. They share the multiple-comparisons problem: given enough slices, they always find something. They also tend to report correlated dimensions as separate findings (the drop is in Android, in version 8.4, in devices made by one manufacturer, and in one country, when all four describe the same users). Treat their output as a list of hypotheses ranked by contribution, then confirm the top one with a mechanism.

When the cause is a mix shift you caused

Mix shifts are often self-inflicted. A successful acquisition campaign brings in many new users who are less engaged than established ones; overall engagement per user falls even though no user's experience changed. Reporting per-cohort or per-tenure metrics alongside totals prevents a good campaign from being mistaken for a product problem. Conversely, a falling-off of new users can make per-user engagement rise, masking a growth problem. Google's HEART paper gives a version of this: during the September 2008 market crash, Google Finance used adoption and retention metrics to separate new visitors interested in the crisis from existing users checking their investments.

Two segments up, total flat

The "dirty dozen" paper describes a Bing ranking experiment in which users who saw a "deeplink" and users who did not both showed a statistically significant increase in sessions per user, while the combined population showed no significant change. The treatment moved some less-active users out of the deeplink group and into the other group, raising the average of both groups without changing anyone's behaviour. It is the same mechanism as Simpson's paradox, inside an experiment: segments defined by something the treatment can change are not stable groups. The rule is to segment by properties fixed before the change (country, platform at assignment, tenure), not by behaviours the change affects.

Gradual declines need flow analysis

A slow decline in active users is a change in flows: fewer new users, fewer of them sticking, more existing users leaving, or fewer lapsed users returning. Decomposing active users into these flows each week (ps5 covers the model Duolingo describes) turns "we're slowly shrinking" into "new-user next-week retention fell from 42% to 37% starting in March", which is investigable with the same segment-and-funnel method.

Checking external causes with outside data

External causes are hard to rule in or out from your own data alone, so it helps to know which outside signals exist. Public search-interest tools show whether interest in your product category moved at the same time; if searches for "grocery delivery" fell 15% across the country the same week your orders fell, the cause is probably not your product. App-store category rankings and third-party traffic estimates show whether competitors moved together with you. Public holiday calendars, weather records and major-event schedules explain regional dips. Status pages of the providers you depend on (cloud, payments, identity, push notifications) explain outage-shaped drops. None of these is conclusive alone, but an external cause that leaves no trace anywhere outside your company is unlikely.

The real case: Berkeley admissions, 1973

The Berkeley data is worth knowing in detail because it is the standard illustration of the trap at the centre of metric diagnosis. In fall 1973, the University of California, Berkeley's graduate admissions showed 44% of 8,442 male applicants admitted and 35% of 4,321 female applicants, a gap too large to be chance and a natural basis for concern about discrimination. When Bickel, Hammel and O'Connell analysed admissions department by department (published in Science in 1975), the apparent bias largely disappeared: women had applied in larger numbers to departments that admitted a small share of all applicants, men to departments that admitted a large share. Across all 85 departments, 4 were significantly biased against women and 6 against men, and the authors found a small but statistically significant bias in favour of women in the pooled, department-adjusted analysis.

Two lessons transfer to product work. First, an aggregate gap can be produced entirely by where people go, not by how they are treated once there; in product terms, by traffic mix rather than by product quality. Second, the right segmentation is the one that is causally prior to the outcome. Department choice happens before the admission decision, so it is legitimate to condition on it. Conditioning on something that happens after the treatment (like the deeplink example in the deep section) can create paradoxes rather than resolve them.

Failure modes in real investigations

Confirmation hunting. Each stakeholder looks for evidence for their favourite cause. Symptom: several analyses, each convincing, mutually inconsistent. Defence: one investigation lead, a written hypothesis list, and the standard order of checks.

Fixing the dashboard, not the data. A bad metric reading is "corrected" by excluding a day or a segment without understanding why it was wrong. Symptom: recurring unexplained gaps. Defence: every exclusion gets a ticket with a root cause.

Missing small-but-total failures. A complete failure in a small segment (one payment method in one country, one browser version) is invisible in totals but catastrophic for those users. Symptom: support tickets before any dashboard moves. Defence: segment-level anomaly detection and treating support-ticket spikes as a signal.

Declaring victory on recovery. The metric recovers on its own (users updated past the broken version), the investigation is closed, and the cause is never found. It returns in the next release. Defence: investigations close with a mechanism, not with a recovered chart.

Tooling that shortens investigations

Teams that diagnose quickly tend to have the same tools: dashboards annotated automatically with deployments, experiments, campaigns and incidents; key metrics precomputed by platform, version, country and tenure; a data-completeness panel showing event arrival by source; client-side and server-side versions of the most important metrics side by side; release gates that compare key funnel rates between new and old versions during a staged rollout; and a written runbook for the first hour. Each of these converts a step of the method from a manual query into a glance.

Telemetry loss in practice

The worked investigation's measurement split, part real drop and part lost events, is a pattern the "dirty dozen" paper documents at Microsoft. Client telemetry is sent from devices that go offline, run out of battery, or are killed by the operating system before a batch of events is uploaded, so some loss is always present; the problem arises when a change alters the loss rate, because then metrics move without behaviour changing. The paper's two practical estimators are worth remembering: compare a client event with a matching server event that has almost no loss (Skype's client and server "call" events), and have the client number its events so gaps in the sequence reveal how many were lost. Either turns "maybe it's logging" from a guess into a measured quantity.

The first-hour runbook

Teams that diagnose quickly usually write down the first hour so nobody has to improvise under pressure. A typical version: (1) confirm the metric definition and the comparison period; (2) check data completeness and pipeline status; (3) compare client-side and server-side versions of the metric; (4) pull the change log for the previous 48 hours, covering releases, experiments, flags, campaigns and incidents; (5) break the metric down by platform, version, country and tenure, ranked by contribution; (6) post a status update with what is known, what is ruled out and the next check. Only after these six does anyone propose a product cause. The runbook is boring on purpose: its value is that the common causes get ruled in or out in the first hour every time.

mix = sum((share1[k] - share0[k]) * rate0[k] for k in share0)
rate = sum(share1[k] * (rate1[k] - rate0[k]) for k in share0)
print(f"overall {o0:.4f} -> {o1:.4f}; mix {mix:+.4f}, rate {rate:+.4f}, sum {mix + rate:+.4f}")

What to look for: the platform breakdown should show Android with most of the contribution and the country breakdown India; neither alone pinpoints the cause, but together they point at Android in India, which you should confirm by printing that single cell. Note that India's percentage drop is large but its contribution depends on its size. In the second part, the mix effect should be about −0.0040 and the rate effect about +0.0006, summing to the overall change of about −0.0034. Then change the hidden cause to a small cell (web in DE) and see how a 45% drop in a small segment barely moves the total: that is why segment-level alerts matter.

Diagnosis drill (10 minutes)

Basketly, a hypothetical grocery-delivery app, reports that orders per weekly active user fell from 1.40 to 1.29 (−7.9%) week-over-week. Weekly active users rose 12% over the same week. Marketing launched a referral campaign giving new users $15 off their first order. Diagnose, with numbers, and say what you would report.

Answer

Clarify and validate: confirm the metric definition (orders per weekly active user, the week Monday-Sunday), that order data is complete (orders come from the server, so logging loss is unlikely), and that week-over-week variation is normally about ±2%; −7.9% is outside it.

Hypothesis: a mix shift from the campaign. New users order less per week than established ones in their first week, because many install, browse and leave.

Segment by tenure. Suppose last week there were 1.00M weekly active users, 0.10M new (10%) ordering 0.6 per week and 0.90M established ordering 1.49: overall 0.1 × 0.6 + 0.9 × 1.49 = 0.06 + 1.341 = 1.40. This week 1.12M users: 0.25M new (22.3%) at 0.62 and 0.87M established at 1.48: overall (0.25 × 0.62 + 0.87 × 1.48) / 1.12 = (0.155 + 1.288) / 1.12 = 1.29.

Decompose: mix effect = (0.223 − 0.10) × 0.6 + (0.777 − 0.90) × 1.49 = 0.074 − 0.183 = −0.109. Rate effect = 0.223 × 0.02 + 0.777 × (−0.01) = 0.0045 − 0.0078 = −0.003. Total about −0.11, matching 1.40 → 1.29. Almost all of the drop is mix.

Report: "Orders per active user fell 7.9%, almost entirely because the referral campaign added 150,000 new users who order less in their first week. Established users' ordering is flat (−0.7%, within noise). Total orders rose from 1.40M to 1.44M (+3%). The question to answer next is whether the new users retain and what the campaign cost per retained customer; I will report orders per user by tenure from now on." Also flag the established-user dip (1.49 → 1.48) as worth watching but not significant.
