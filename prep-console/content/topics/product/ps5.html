A consumer app's growth team has a great quarter on paper. Marketing doubles spend, app-store featuring brings a wave of installs, and signups hit two million, a record. At the end of the quarter, monthly active users are almost exactly where they started. The board asks where the two million people went.

The answer is that they came and left. Of every hundred people who installed, sixty signed up, forty finished onboarding, eighteen did the thing the app is for in their first week, and by week eight about six were still around. Meanwhile, some of the older users who had been active for months drifted away. New users poured into the top while existing users leaked out, and the level stayed flat. Nobody had been watching the shape of retention; everyone had been watching signups.

This is the most common growth mistake, and it is invisible in the metrics most dashboards lead with: totals of signups, installs and monthly active users. The tools that reveal it are older and simpler: funnels, which show where new users drop off; cohort tables, which show whether each group of users stays; retention curves, which show whether any group stays for good; and growth accounting, which shows whether the user base is growing because people stay or only because people keep arriving.

These tools matter to ML and AI engineers more than they might seem. Many models exist to improve a funnel step (search ranking improves search-to-purchase conversion) or retention (recommendations, notifications). Interviewers expect you to say which step or retention measure your model moves, and to reason about whether a change in a top-line number came from new users, current users or a change in mix. This lesson covers the definitions, the arithmetic with worked numbers, Duolingo's published growth model showing why current-user retention compounds, churn and lifetime, and a simple lifetime-value calculation including payback.

A final reason the topic matters in interviews: questions about AI features increasingly come with growth framing. "Our AI assistant has 30% weekly adoption; is it working?" cannot be answered without asking whether adopters keep using it (feature retention), whether they retain better in the product overall than comparable non-adopters, and whether that difference is causal. The vocabulary in this lesson is how you ask those questions precisely.

Think of a product's active users as water in a bucket. New users pour in from the top: that is acquisition. Holes in the bucket let users drain away: that is churn. The water level is the active user base. Growth happens when inflow exceeds leakage. Three things follow immediately. Pouring faster does not help if the holes grow as fast. Small holes matter enormously over time, because every drop that stays in the bucket keeps counting. And the cheapest way to raise the level is often to patch holes rather than to pour faster.

The analogy breaks in useful places. Water that drains out is gone, but users who leave can come back (resurrected users), sometimes because of a notification, a new feature or a friend. The holes are not the same for all water: new users leak much faster than users who have been around for months, so the bucket really has a leaky top layer and a sturdier bottom. And water is all the same, while users differ: one heavy user can be worth fifty light ones, which is why lifetime value matters and not just counts.

Four tools make the bucket visible. A funnel follows new users step by step from first contact to the core action, showing where they drop off before they ever become active. A cohort table groups users by when they started and tracks what share is still active week after week, showing whether newer groups stay better than older ones. A retention curve plots one cohort's share still active against time, and its shape (sliding to zero or levelling off) says whether the product has found lasting value for anyone. Growth accounting splits each period's active users into retained, new and resurrected, with churned users leaving, showing what the level's change is made of.

On top of these sit two money measures. Churn turns retention into an expected lifetime, and lifetime value (LTV) turns lifetime and margin into what a user is worth, which is the number that decides how much it is sensible to spend acquiring one. The body works through each with numbers.

A word on vocabulary before the details, because these terms are used loosely. "Retention" sometimes means "the share of a cohort active at week 4" and sometimes "the share of this week's users who come back next week"; the first describes a cohort over its life, the second describes the whole base in one period. "Churn" sometimes means users who stopped being active and sometimes customers who stopped paying. In interviews and in real analyses, say which one you mean, with the period and the definition of active, before you use the number. Half of the confusion in growth discussions comes from two people using the same word for different measurements.

Some bucket arithmetic makes the stakes concrete. Suppose a product loses 10% of its weekly active users every week. To stay flat it must add new or returning users equal to 10% of its base every week; to grow 1% a week it needs 11%. If the same product cut its weekly loss to 7%, the same 10% inflow would produce growth of about 3% a week. Small differences in leakage become large differences in trajectory within a quarter, because the effect accumulates every week. This is why experienced growth teams talk about retention first and acquisition second: acquisition buys users once, retention keeps paying.

The same arithmetic explains a puzzle new analysts meet: a product can have excellent retention among its long-time users and still shrink, because almost all of its leakage happens in the first two weeks after signup, where most new users leave before they ever become regulars. The bucket's top layer leaks fastest. Funnels and early cohort retention measure that layer; DAU/MAU and growth accounting measure the whole bucket. You need both views to know where to work.

1. Conversion funnels

A funnel is an ordered sequence of steps users pass through, such as install → sign up → finish onboarding → first core action → start trial → pay. For each step you report two numbers. Step conversion is the share of users at the previous step who reached this one. Cumulative conversion is the share of users who entered at the top who reached it. Both need a time window: "first workout within 7 days of install" is a funnel step; "first workout ever" is not, because the number keeps growing as you wait.

Worked example: Fitloop, a hypothetical fitness app, for one month's installs.

StepUsersStep conversionCumulative
Install100,000—100%
Sign up62,00062%62%
Finish onboarding41,00066%41%
First workout within 7 days18,00044%18%
Start free trial within 14 days7,20040%7.2%
Convert to paid3,60050%3.6%

Two definitional choices matter. Ordered funnels count a user at a step only if they passed the previous steps in order; unordered funnels count anyone who did both actions. Ordered is the usual choice for onboarding. And funnels should be computed per user, not per event: a user who opens onboarding five times is one user.

2. Choosing which step to improve

The tempting rule is "fix the step with the worst conversion". The better rule is to estimate the end-to-end gain of a realistic improvement at each step and compare it with the effort.

From the Fitloop funnel: improving onboarding completion from 66% to 72% adds 62,000 × 0.06 = 3,720 onboarded users. Downstream of onboarding, 3,600 of 41,000 currently become payers, 8.8%. If the extra users convert at that rate, that is about 327 extra payers a month. Improving first-workout conversion from 44% to 48% adds 41,000 × 0.04 = 1,640 first workouts; downstream of the first workout, 3,600 of 18,000 pay, 20%, so about 328 extra payers. The two improvements are worth about the same, even though one step converts at 66% and the other at 44%. The tie-break is effort, confidence and causality: is a better onboarding screen a two-week project or a quarter? Is there evidence that a first workout causes conversion, or do motivated people both work out and pay?

One caution makes all such estimates optimistic: the extra users you push through a step are marginal users, less motivated than those who already pass it, so their downstream conversion is usually lower than average. Discount the estimate (halving it is a common rough adjustment) or, better, measure it in an experiment.

3. Activation and the "aha moment"

Activation is the point where a new user first experiences the core value: a first workout completed, a first message answered, a first file shared. Teams often search their data for a behaviour in the first days that predicts long-term retention and make it the activation target. This is useful and dangerous. Useful, because it gives onboarding a concrete goal. Dangerous, because such behaviours are often symptoms of motivation rather than causes of retention. Duolingo's former product lead, writing about the streak feature, says the team found that users who reached a 10-day streak were much less likely to drop off, and immediately adds that "a lot of this was simply correlation and selection bias"; they treated it as a reason to invest in experiments on streaks, not as proof. That is the right posture.

4. Cohorts and cohort tables

A cohort is a group of users who started in the same period, for example everyone who signed up in the week of 6 January. A cohort table has one row per cohort and one column per age (week 0, week 1, week 2, …), and each cell is the share of the cohort active in that week of its life.

There are three ways to read it, and each answers a different question.

Along a row: one cohort's life. The 6 January cohort falls from 42% in week 1 to 26% by week 5 and is flattening: it loses only 1 point between weeks 4 and 5. (Its week-6 value sits in a dip week explained below.)

Down a column: cohorts compared at the same age. Week-1 retention is 41-43% for the first three cohorts and 47-48% for the 27 January and 3 February cohorts. Something improved for users who started from late January, perhaps a new onboarding flow. Because cohorts are compared at the same age, this comparison is not confused by older users being more loyal.

Along a diagonal: the same calendar week across cohorts. The bold cells all fall in the week of 17 February (week 6 for the 6 Jan cohort, week 5 for 13 Jan, week 4 for 20 Jan, and so on). Every cohort dips that week and recovers the next. That is a calendar event, such as an outage, a holiday or a broken notification, not a change in any cohort's quality. Without the diagonal reading, you might attribute the newest cohort's poor week 1 to a worse acquisition source.

5. Retention curves and their shapes

A retention curve plots one row of the cohort table: the share of a cohort active at each age. Two definitions are common. N-day (or "classic") retention counts a user as retained on day N only if they were active on exactly that day. Unbounded retention counts them if they were active on day N or any later day, which gives a smoother, always-decreasing curve and is less sensitive to which exact day someone happened to visit. Whichever you choose, state it and keep it fixed.

The shapes mean different things. A curve declining toward zero says no group of users found lasting value; every acquisition dollar eventually leaks away. A curve that flattens to a plateau says each cohort leaves behind a stable core; the plateau's height times the number of new users is how fast the core grows. A smile, a curve that dips and then rises, happens when users come back, typically in products with network effects (more of your friends join over time) or products that improve with accumulated data. When interviewers ask "is this product healthy?", the plateau is usually the first thing to look for.

6. Stickiness: DAU/MAU and beyond

DAU/MAU divides average daily active users by monthly active users. Because it equals the average number of days per month a monthly user is active, divided by about 30, it measures habit: 0.5 means the typical monthly user shows up about 15 days a month. Messaging products can be high; utilities used once a month (paying a bill) are naturally low, and that is not a problem for them. Compare DAU/MAU only between similar products or over time for the same product.

DAU/MAU averages away the distribution. A value of 0.3 could mean everyone uses it about 9 days a month, or that a third of users are daily and the rest come once. An L28 histogram (how many users were active on exactly 1, 2, …, 28 of the last 28 days) shows which, and its shape often has two humps: casual users at the left and a committed core at the right. Google's HEART paper describes a related measure: Gmail tracked the percentage of active users who visited on five or more days in the last week, and found it strongly predictive of longer-term retention.

7. Growth accounting and the quick ratio

Growth accounting splits each period's active users by where they came from: retained (active last period and this period), new (first period ever), resurrected (active before, but not last period) and, leaving, churned (active last period, not this one). For the month in the primer: 1,000,000 last month; 700,000 retained, 300,000 churned; 250,000 new and 80,000 resurrected arrive; 1,030,000 this month.

The quick ratio = (new + resurrected) / churned = 330,000 / 300,000 = 1.1. Above 1 the base grows; well above 1 (say 3) means healthy growth that does not depend on replacing a large share of users each period. A quick ratio of 1.1 with 30% monthly churn describes a business running hard to stand still. Improving retention of last month's users from 70% to 75% adds 50,000 users a month; matching that through acquisition would need 20% more new users.

8. Why current-user retention compounds: a two-state model

A simple weekly model makes the compounding visible. Each week N new users arrive. A share NURR (new-user retention rate) of them return next week and become "current" users. Each week, a share CURR (current-user retention rate) of current users return again. In steady state the number of current users C satisfies C = NURR × N + CURR × C, so:

C = NURR × N / (1 − CURR), and weekly active users = N + C.

With N = 100,000, NURR = 0.40 and CURR = 0.80: C = 40,000 / 0.20 = 200,000, so WAU = 300,000. Now improve each rate by 2 points. NURR 0.42: C = 42,000 / 0.20 = 210,000, WAU 310,000 (+3.3%). CURR 0.82: C = 40,000 / 0.18 = 222,222, WAU 322,222 (+7.4%). The same 2-point improvement is worth more than twice as much on CURR, because a retained current user is retained again next week and the week after; the factor 1 / (1 − CURR) amplifies it. The closer CURR is to 1, the stronger the amplification.

Duolingo's former product lead, Jorge Mazal, describes the same effect in a much richer model. Users are in exactly one state per day (new, current, reactivated, resurrected, at-risk weekly, at-risk monthly, dormant), the states add up to DAU, WAU and MAU, and the arrows between states are retention rates. The team simulated moving each rate by 2% per quarter for three years, and found CURR had "a gigantic impact on DAU—5 times the impact of the second-best metric", because "current users who stay active return to the same bucket". That analysis made CURR their north-star metric.

9. Churn and lifetime

Churn is the share of users or customers at risk at the start of a period who leave during it. For subscriptions, voluntary churn (cancelling) differs from involuntary churn (a card fails); the second is often a large and fixable share. For activity-based products, churn needs a definition of inactivity, such as "no activity for 30 days".

If monthly churn m is roughly constant, the expected customer lifetime is about 1 / m months: 5% monthly churn means about 20 months. Annual retention is (1 − m)^12: 0.95^12 ≈ 0.54, so about 46% of customers leave each year. These conversions are useful for sanity checks: a quoted "5% monthly churn" and "80% annual retention" cannot both be true for the same customers.

Real churn is not constant. It is highest in the first months and lower among survivors, because the customers who stay are disproportionately the ones who value the product. That is why average churn across all customers can fall over time even if nothing improves: the base becomes dominated by long-time survivors (another mix effect). Report churn by tenure.

10. Lifetime value and payback

Lifetime value (LTV) is the margin a customer generates over their relationship with the product. The simple formula, valid under constant churn and revenue:

LTV = ARPU × gross margin / monthly churn

where ARPU is average revenue per user per month. Example: a $10 monthly subscription with 80% gross margin and 5% monthly churn gives LTV = 10 × 0.8 / 0.05 = $160. Money later is worth less than money now; with a monthly discount rate d, LTV ≈ ARPU × margin / (churn + d); at d = 1%, 8 / 0.06 ≈ $133.

The figure is only meaningful next to CAC, the customer acquisition cost. If acquiring a subscriber costs $60, LTV/CAC ≈ 2.7 (undiscounted) and the payback period, the months of margin needed to repay acquisition, is 60 / 8 = 7.5 months. A business with long payback needs cash to grow, and if churn rises the payback may never arrive.

Cohort LTV replaces the constant-churn assumption with the cohort's actual retention curve. Suppose a cohort's monthly retention is 100% in month 1, then 70%, 58%, 52%, 48%, 45%, 43%, 41%, 40%, 39%, 38% and 37% over the first year. The sum is 6.11 months of an average customer's margin in year 1, worth 6.11 × $8 ≈ $49 in the first year, before later years. If CAC is $60, payback arrives only a few months into year 2, not at 7.5 months as the simple formula suggested. Using the real curve often changes a "profitable" acquisition channel into a marginal one.

11. Feature-level adoption and retention

The same tools work at the level of a single feature, which is how ML and AI features are usually judged. Feature adoption is the share of active users who used the feature at least once in a period (HEART's Adoption category, from Google's framework of Happiness, Engagement, Adoption, Retention and Task success). Feature retention is the share of users who used it in their first week of exposure and still use it in week 4. A feature with high adoption and low feature retention is being tried and abandoned: the launch announcement worked, the feature did not.

Worked example: an AI writing assistant in a document editor reaches 30% of weekly active users in its launch week. Four weeks later, of the users who tried it in week 1, 9% still use it weekly. That is a feature-retention curve sliding toward a low plateau, which says the feature delivers lasting value to a small group. The useful follow-ups are who that group is (segment by role or document type), whether the group retains better in the product overall than similar non-users (and whether that difference holds up in an experiment), and what the feature costs per retained user if it calls a model. ps10 takes this further for AI features specifically.

12. A growth diagnosis in five numbers

When an interviewer says "weekly active users have been flat for two months; what's going on?", growth accounting gives a fast structure. Ask for, or assume, five numbers per week: active users, new users, retained users, resurrected users and churned users. Then check three things in order. Is the inflow (new plus resurrected) falling? Then the problem is acquisition or reactivation: look at channels and campaigns. Is new-user retention (the share of last week's new users active this week) falling? Then the problem is onboarding or activation: look at the funnel and at which cohorts changed, reading the cohort table down its columns. Is current-user retention falling? Then the core experience got worse or a competitor is pulling users: segment by tenure and platform, and read the cohort table along its diagonals for calendar events. Flat totals often hide two of these moving in opposite directions, such as more new users but worse retention, which is exactly what a large acquisition campaign into a weak onboarding flow produces.

The retention you measure depends on your definition of "active"

If "active" means "opened the app", a notification that makes people open and immediately close it raises retention without anyone getting value. If it means "did the core action", the same notification may do nothing. Many teams track both: a broad activity definition for reach, and a narrow "valuable activity" definition for decisions. When a retention number moves, the first diagnostic question is which definition moved, and whether the broad one moved without the narrow one.

Survivorship in averages

Any average over currently active users is biased toward the people who stayed. Average session length "improving" over a year may simply reflect that short-session users churned. Cohort-based views (compare users at the same tenure) remove this survivorship. In interviews, when someone claims "engagement per user is up", asking "per user at the same tenure?" is a sharp follow-up that often reveals a mix effect (ps4).

Cohort definitions are a modelling choice

Cohort by signup date, first purchase date or first core action? By acquisition channel, country or platform as well? Each choice answers a different question. Cohorting by signup includes users who never activated; cohorting by first core action measures retention of activated users only, which looks better and is the right lens for the product team but not for the marketing team. Seasonal cohorts (January fitness signups, back-to-school learners) behave differently, so compare like with like across years.

Experiments on retention take patience

A change aimed at retention must be measured on retention, which arrives slowly: four-week retention needs at least four weeks after the last user enters the experiment. Teams therefore use leading indicators (week-1 retention, days active in the first week) to decide quickly, and keep a holdout, a small randomised group left without the change, to confirm the long-term effect months later. ps6 covers holdouts; the point here is that a week-1 lift that fades by week 4 is novelty, not retention.

Engagement depth versus breadth

Retention says whether users come back; it does not say how much they do. Two products with the same 30% week-8 retention can differ tenfold in value per retained user. Combining retention with depth metrics (core actions per retained user, the L28 histogram's right-hand hump) gives a fuller picture, and LTV ultimately combines them in money.

LTV for AI features: margin is no longer fixed

For conventional software, gross margin per user is roughly constant: serving one more user costs little. For a product whose core feature calls a large language model, every use has an inference cost, and heavy users cost most. A $20-a-month subscriber who uses an assistant heavily might cost several dollars a month in inference; a very heavy one can cost more than they pay. LTV must use margin after inference, by segment, and the levers change: usage limits, routing routine requests to smaller models, caching, or pricing by usage. A retention improvement among the heaviest users can raise retention and lower profit at the same time, which is why cost per active user becomes a guardrail for AI products (ps8 and ps9).

Network effects change the curve's shape

For communication and social products, a user's retention depends on whether people they care about are also active. Cohort retention can rise with age (the smile) as a user's contacts join. This also means retention experiments randomised by individual user underestimate effects that spread through the network, which is the problem LinkedIn's ego-cluster experiments address (ps6).

Incremental versus organic resurrection

Reactivation campaigns (win-back emails, discounts, push notifications to lapsed users) report how many recipients came back. Many would have come back anyway; the campaign's value is only the incremental returns. Measuring it needs a holdout: a random share of lapsed users who are eligible but not contacted. If 6% of contacted users return versus 4% of the holdout, the campaign's incremental effect is 2 points, a third of what the naive report claims. The same logic applies to notifications that "drive" sessions; ps12 has a worked case.

Benchmarks are mostly noise

Interviewers sometimes ask whether a given retention or DAU/MAU figure is good. Industry benchmarks circulate widely, but they mix product types, definitions of "active" and user populations, so they are a weak guide. A better answer compares the product with itself (cohort over cohort, before and after changes), with products that share its natural usage frequency (a tax app should not be compared with a messaging app), and with the economics: is retention high enough that LTV comfortably exceeds CAC? Saying this is more convincing than quoting a number.

The real case: Duolingo's growth model

In a 2023 essay in Lenny's Newsletter, Jorge Mazal, Duolingo's former chief product officer, describes how the company reaccelerated growth after he joined as head of product in late 2017. The first move was to focus on retention over acquisition, because all of Duolingo's new-user acquisition was organic and the team saw a "leaky bucket". The second was a model borrowed from Zynga and MyFitnessPal: weekly retention rates for current users (CURR, the chance a user comes back this week if they came in each of the past two weeks), new users (NURR) and reactivated users (RURR, reactivated user retention rate), later extended into a daily state model with resurrected users (SURR, resurrected user retention rate).

The state definitions are worth knowing as a template. New users: first day of engagement ever. Current users: engaged today and at least once in the prior six days. Reactivated: first day back after 7-29 days away. Resurrected: first day back after 30 or more days. At-risk WAU: inactive today but active in the prior six days. At-risk MAU: inactive for seven days but active in the prior 23. Dormant: inactive for 31 days or more. Every user who has ever used the product is in exactly one state each day, so DAU, WAU and MAU fall out of the model as sums.

The team simulated moving each transition rate by 2% a quarter for three years and found that CURR had five times the effect on DAU of the next-best rate, because current users who stay active stay in the same state and compound. CURR became the north-star metric. The essay credits leaderboards (which increased overall learning time by 17% and tripled the number of highly engaged learners, those spending at least an hour a day five days a week), renewed focus on push notifications and work on the streak feature, among other efforts. Over four years CURR rose 21%, which the author says represents a reduction in the daily churn of their best users of over 40%, and DAU grew 4.5 times. These are the author's figures; the method, not the exact numbers, is what transfers.

Failure modes

Counting reinstalls as new users. Device-based identity counts a returning user who reinstalls as new, inflating acquisition and deflating retention. Symptom: "new user" retention that looks oddly good. Defence: identity by account where possible, and a resurrected category.

Retention defined by notification opens. If any app open counts as active, push campaigns raise retention mechanically. Symptom: retention jumps on days with big pushes, without matching core actions. Defence: a "valuable activity" definition for decisions.

Cohort tables in calendar months with uneven lengths. February cohorts look worse at "month 1" because the month is shorter. Defence: use weeks or fixed day counts.

LTV used to justify any acquisition spend. Optimistic LTV from the simple formula (constant churn, no discounting, average margin) makes marginal channels look profitable. Defence: cohort LTV by channel, with discounting and margin after variable costs, compared with CAC by channel.

Instrumenting for cohorts

Cohort analysis needs a few things that ordinary event logging does not guarantee. Each user needs a stable identifier across devices and reinstalls, or cohorts fragment. Each user needs a recorded start event (signup, first core action) with a timestamp, stored once and never overwritten, so their cohort does not change when data is reprocessed. Events need an event time (when the user acted) as well as a processing time (when the server received it), because late-arriving mobile events otherwise land in the wrong week. And the definition of "active" must be versioned, so a change in definition shows up as an annotated break rather than as a fake retention shift. Teams that skip these discover the gaps when an executive asks for twelve-month retention and the data cannot answer.

Goal: build a cohort table and growth accounting from synthetic events, and check the two-state compounding result. About 20 minutes; Python 3.12 standard library.

  1. Save as cohorts.py and run it:
import random
from collections import defaultdict
random.seed(11)
WEEKS = 10
active = defaultdict(set)          # week -> set of user ids
start = {}
uid = 0
for w in range(WEEKS):
    new_users = 1000 + 50 * w
    nurr = 0.40 if w < 5 else 0.47  # onboarding improves from week 5
    for _ in range(new_users):
        start[uid] = w
        active[w].add(uid)
        alive = random.random() < nurr
        for later in range(w + 1, WEEKS):
            if alive:
                active[later].add(uid)
                alive = random.random() < 0.80   # CURR
            elif random.random() < 0.03:           # resurrection
                active[later].add(uid)
                alive = random.random() < 0.5
        uid += 1

print("cohort  size  " + " ".join(f"w{a}" for a in range(1, 6)))
for c in range(WEEKS - 1):
    members = [u for u, s in start.items() if s == c]
    row = [len([u for u in members if u in active[c + a]]) / len(members)
           for a in range(1, 6) if c + a < WEEKS]
    print(f"{c:6} {len(members):5}  " + " ".join(f"{r:.0%}" for r in row))

for w in range(1, WEEKS):
    prev, cur = active[w - 1], active[w]
    new = {u for u in cur if start[u] == w}
    retained = cur & prev
    resurrected = cur - prev - new
    churned = prev - cur
    print(f"week {w}: active {len(cur):5} retained {len(retained):5} new {len(new):5} "
          f"resurrected {len(resurrected):4} churned {len(churned):5} "
          f"quick {(len(new) + len(resurrected)) / len(churned):.2f}")

What to look for: week-1 retention in the cohort table should jump from about 40% to about 47% for cohorts from week 5 on (read down the w1 column); later columns should fall by roughly a factor of 0.8 per week plus a little resurrection. In growth accounting, retained + new + resurrected should equal active each week. Then change CURR from 0.80 to 0.82 and NURR back to 0.40 for all weeks, and compare the final week's active count with a run that raises NURR to 0.42 instead: the CURR change should matter more, as the two-state formula predicts.

Retention reasoning drill

Snapnote, a hypothetical note-taking app, has 400,000 weekly active users. Every week 60,000 new users arrive; 35% of them return the following week; current users return each week at 85%. The team can do one of two projects this quarter: a better onboarding that should lift new-user next-week retention to 40%, or a "daily review" feature expected to lift current-user retention to 87%. Which is worth more in the long run, and by roughly how much? What would you check before trusting the answer?

Answer

Model: steady-state current users C = NURR × N / (1 − CURR); WAU = N + C.

Today: C = 0.35 × 60,000 / 0.15 = 140,000, so WAU = 200,000 in steady state. The actual WAU is 400,000, so either the product is shrinking toward 200,000, or the simple model misses inflows (resurrected users) or overstates churn. First check: is WAU actually falling? If not, add a resurrection flow to the model.

Onboarding (NURR 0.40): C = 24,000 / 0.15 = 160,000, +20,000 current users (+14%).
