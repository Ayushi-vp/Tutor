A ride-hailing company tests a 15% discount for riders in one city. Half the riders, chosen at random, see the discount. Two weeks later the results look excellent: riders with the discount took 20% more rides than riders without it, and the revenue loss from the discount is more than covered. The company launches the discount to every rider in the city. Total rides do not move. The finance team asks where the 20% went.

It never existed at the city level. The city was short of drivers during busy hours. Discounted riders requested more, got matched first, and took rides that full-price riders would otherwise have taken. The experiment measured a transfer of scarce drivers from one group to the other, not new rides. Once everyone had the discount, there was no one to take rides from. The statistics were done correctly; the experiment design was wrong for the product.

This is why experiment design is a product decision, not a statistics exercise. The statistics (sample sizes, p-values, sequential tests, variance reduction) are covered in math6, math7 and in10, and the platform that runs them in in10. What those topics cannot decide for you is: what question the experiment should answer, which unit to randomise, which users to include, how long to run so that novelty and weekly cycles do not mislead you, whether users in the two arms affect each other, how to see effects that take months, and whether to run an A/B test at all. Each of these decisions depends on how the product works and how its users behave.

This lesson works through those decisions in order, with numbers: dilution and why triggering saves a hundredfold in sample size, a worked supply-constrained marketplace where a naive test shows +20% and the truth is zero, LinkedIn's ego-cluster method for network effects (and the 10 million members it needed), Microsoft's examples of novelty effects, long-term holdouts, and a clear list of when not to A/B test.

An online controlled experiment, or A/B test, randomly assigns units to a control (the current product) or a treatment (the change), and compares outcomes. Because assignment is random, the two arms differ only by chance and by the change, so a difference larger than chance can be attributed to the change. That is the whole logic, and it rests on assumptions that the product can break: that units are independent, that the measured period represents the future, and that the people in the test represent the people who will get the change.

An analogy: a farmer testing a new fertiliser. They split a field into plots, flip a coin for each plot, fertilise the heads, and compare yields at harvest. The farmer's design decisions map closely onto ours. How big should a plot be (the randomisation unit)? Should plots on the rocky hillside, where nothing grows anyway, count (the eligible population)? How long to wait: one growing season, or several, because fertiliser can burn soil over years (duration and long-term effects)? And what if fertiliser washes downhill from treated plots into control plots, making them grow better too (interference)?

The analogy breaks in two ways that matter. Plants do not notice they are being treated; users do, and their first reaction to something new (curiosity, or annoyance) is often not their lasting reaction. And plants do not talk to each other or compete for customers; users share social networks and compete for the same drivers, hotel rooms and ad slots. Much of this lesson is about those two differences.

Read the diagram as the order of questions to ask. First: can and should you randomise at all? Then: will units in different arms affect each other? If not, a user-level test with good triggering and enough duration is the default. If they will, randomise bigger units: regions, clusters of connected users, or whole markets in alternating time slots. Finally: will the effect take months to show, or change as users adapt? If so, keep a holdout. The body expands each step.

The one-sentence summary of the lesson: the statistics of an A/B test assume a world where units are independent, behaviour is stable, and the test population is the launch population; experiment design is the work of making the real product close enough to that world, or choosing a different design where it cannot be.

A few words of vocabulary used throughout. The arms of an experiment are its groups: control and one or more treatments. The allocation is the share of eligible units in each arm (50/50, or 90/5/5 when testing two variants cautiously). Exposure is the moment a unit actually experiences the difference between arms, which may be much later than assignment (a user assigned on Monday who first reaches checkout on Thursday is exposed on Thursday). An experimentation platform (in10) handles assignment, exposure logging and the statistical scorecard. It cannot decide for you which unit to assign, who counts as eligible, how long to wait, or whether the arms contaminate each other. Those are the decisions this lesson is about, and they depend on knowing how the product and its users behave.

It is also worth separating two purposes that get mixed up. Some experiments are for deciding: should this ship? Others are for learning: how sensitive are users to price, does a first workout cause retention? Decision experiments need pre-written launch rules and enough power for the minimum effect worth shipping. Learning experiments can be smaller, more exploratory and more numerous, but their results should not be quietly reused as launch decisions. Knowing which kind you are running shapes the design.

1. Start from the decision

An experiment exists to make a decision: ship or not, which of three variants, whether a model is worth its cost. Before designing it, write down three things. The hypothesis in product terms ("showing the delivery fee before checkout will reduce checkout abandonment without reducing orders"). The primary metric and guardrails (ps2). And the launch rule: the result that will make you ship, written before the data exists ("ship if orders per visitor do not fall by more than 0.5% and checkout abandonment falls"). ps7 covers launch rules in depth; the reason they come first here is that every design choice below (unit, population, duration) depends on what effect you need to detect for which metric.

A useful test of whether the decision is clear: can you say what you would do for each plausible result? If both "primary up 2%" and "primary flat" lead to "ship it", the experiment is not deciding anything and may not be worth running, or the real question is different (for example, whether a guardrail breaks).

2. Choosing the randomisation unit

The randomisation unit is what gets assigned to an arm. The common choices and their trade-offs:

UnitWhen it fitsMain risk
User (account or cookie)Default for anything users can noticeInterference between users; cookie churn splits a person across arms

DeviceApp changes before loginOne person, several devices, several arms

Two rules apply whatever the choice. First, the experience must be consistent for each unit: a user who sees the new design on Monday and the old one on Tuesday experiences neither. Second, the analysis must treat the randomisation unit as the independent observation. If you randomise by user but compute click-through rate per page view as if page views were independent, the variance is underestimated and false positives multiply (math7 covers the delta method that fixes this).

A worked example of choosing badly: a search team randomises a ranking change by session for logged-out users, because cookies are unreliable. Users who get the new ranking in one session learn that the top result is usually right and click it faster in their next session, even when that session is in control. Control's metrics improve from the treatment's influence, the measured difference shrinks, and the team concludes the change does little. A user-level (or device-level) assignment would have kept the learning inside the treatment arm.

3. Who to include: eligibility, triggering and dilution

The eligible population is the set of users who could possibly experience the change. A change to the checkout page can only affect users who reach checkout. Including everyone else adds people whose outcomes cannot differ between arms, and that has a large cost called dilution.

Worked example. Suppose 10% of weekly users reach checkout and the change raises conversion among them by 5% (relative). Among all users, the effect is about 10% × 5% = 0.5%. Required sample size scales roughly with one over the effect squared, so detecting 0.5% needs about (5 / 0.5)² = 100 times as many users as detecting 5%. Since all-user analysis has 10 times more users available than checkout-only analysis, it still takes about 10 times longer to reach the same power. Triggering, analysing only users who reached the point where the arms differ, recovers the full effect size.

Triggering has one trap. The trigger must be identical in both arms and not influenced by the treatment. If the treatment changes how many users reach checkout (say it adds a "checkout now" button on product pages), then "users who reached checkout" are different people in the two arms, and comparing them is biased. The fix is to trigger upstream (users who viewed a product page) or to log a counterfactual trigger in control: record when a control user would have seen the treatment's change, even though they did not.

After a triggered analysis, translate back to overall impact: triggered effect × trigger rate. A 5% lift among the 10% who reach checkout is a 0.5% lift in overall conversion, which is the number that belongs in the business case.

4. Duration: weekly cycles, novelty and primacy

Sample size from a power calculation (math7) sets a minimum. Product behaviour sets three more constraints.

Weekly cycles. Most products have strong day-of-week patterns, and the users active on weekdays differ from those active at weekends. A test that runs Tuesday to Friday measures weekday users. Running whole weeks, at least one and preferably two, makes the sample represent the full user base.

Novelty effects. Users engage with a change because it is new and noticeable, and the boost fades. Microsoft's "dirty dozen" paper describes an Edge browser experiment that showed a one-time "coach mark" prompting users with an empty list of top sites to add one. Over four weeks the treatment showed a 0.96% increase in whole-page clicks and a 2.07% increase in clicks on top sites, which looked positive. But the daily difference declined quickly; when the team separated the visit where users saw the coach mark from later visits, the first showed a large increase and the later visits showed no statistically significant change. The effect did not outlast the first visit, which saved the team from building more coach marks.

Primacy effects (change aversion) run the other way: users are used to the old design and perform worse at first, then recover. Google's HEART paper (ps2) gives an example: after a major iGoogle redesign, user satisfaction dropped initially and then recovered over time, which the team read as change aversion and kept the new design.

The practical tool is to plot the treatment effect by day, and by days since each user's first exposure. If the effect shrinks steadily, extend the test or keep a holdout before deciding; if it grows (users learning to use a feature), early results understate it. The decision should rest on where the curve is heading, not on its average over an arbitrary window.

5. Interference: when units affect each other

Standard A/B analysis relies on SUTVA, the stable unit treatment value assumption: each unit's outcome depends only on its own assignment. Products break it in two common ways.

Marketplaces and shared resources. When treated and control users compete for the same limited supply (drivers, hotel rooms, delivery couriers, ad budget, inventory), the treatment's effect on its own users partly comes out of the control group. Worked example: a city has capacity for 1,000 rides an hour at peak, and normally 1,000 riders request, so everyone is served. In the test, half the riders get a discount that makes them request 20% more: 600 treated requests and 500 control requests, 1,100 in all, above capacity. If the dispatch system serves requests in proportion, treated riders get 1,000 × 600 / 1,100 ≈ 545 rides and control riders ≈ 455. Per rider group, treatment took 20% more rides than control (545 versus 455): the naive estimate. After a global launch, 1,200 riders request and 1,000 are served: total rides do not change at all. The true market-level effect at peak is zero, the naive test said +20%, and the discount's cost is real. With spare capacity off-peak, the true effect would be positive, which is why the answer depends on the market's state.

Designs that respect market structure: switchback tests alternate the whole market between treatment and control in randomised time slots, so supply and demand interact as after launch; regional tests assign whole cities, with few units but no cross-arm competition; and for ads, budget-split designs give each arm its own share of each advertiser's budget so the arms cannot exhaust each other's money.

Social and communication networks. When a change affects what users create or send (a new sharing feature, a feed ranking that changes what people post), treated users' behaviour reaches their connections, who may be in control. The control group is partly treated, and the measured difference understates the full effect. Cluster randomisation assigns groups of connected users together so most interactions stay within an arm.

LinkedIn's ego-cluster design (Saint-Jacques and colleagues, 2019) is a practical version. It picks focal members called egos and assigns all of each ego's direct connections, the alters, to the same arm. Comparing egos whose connections were all treated with egos whose connections were all in control measures the "one-out" network effect: how much your connections' treatment changes your own outcome. The analysis is an ordinary two-sample t-test between egos, which existing platforms already support. The cost is traffic: the procedure identifies about 200,000 egos, and to get them about 10 million members must be assigned. The paper also recommends choosing the network that carries the effect (for many feed experiments, past feed impressions rather than all connections), because using all connections produces very large clusters.

6. Long-term effects and holdouts

Some effects only appear over months: users learning to ignore more frequent notifications, advertisers adjusting bids, sellers adapting listings, new users who joined during the test maturing. A holdout is a small random group, often 1% to 5%, kept without a change (or without all changes from a quarter) for a long period. Comparing the holdout with everyone else after three or six months measures the lasting effect.

Holdouts answer two questions short tests cannot. Did this change's effect persist, shrink or reverse? And what is the cumulative value of many launches whose individual effects were each near the noise floor? Teams sometimes find that the sum of claimed wins from a year of experiments is much larger than the holdout comparison, which reveals optimistic stopping, novelty or wins that did not last.

Holdouts cost something. The held-out users get an older product, which is a real cost if the changes are improvements; the old code path must keep working; and analysis over months must handle users leaving and joining. Teams typically use them for strategically important or adaptation-prone changes (notifications, ads load, pricing, ranking objectives), not for every launch.

7. When not to A/B test

Experiments are a tool, and some decisions are better made without them.

  • Too few units. An enterprise product with 150 customers cannot detect plausible effects. Use staged rollout, close monitoring and customer interviews.
  • Withholding is unacceptable. Safety, security, accessibility and legal fixes ship to everyone. Some pricing tests may be legally or ethically constrained by rules on fair pricing or discrimination; check before designing one.
  • Interference you cannot contain. A change to the whole matching system of a marketplace, or a product-wide redesign with strong network effects, may have no unit that isolates it. Use regional rollouts with a quasi-experiment, comparing launched and not-yet-launched regions over time (difference-in-differences, or a synthetic control as in ts11).
  • Effects too slow to measure. Brand changes, changes whose value appears over years. Use leading indicators with care, research and judgement.
  • Cheap and reversible. A copy change on a rarely visited settings page is not worth a two-week test. Decide, ship behind a flag, watch guardrails.
  • Obvious bugs and clear wins. Fixing a crash does not need a test; though a quick test can still catch unexpected side effects, which is why some teams test even fixes.

The alternatives are not "guess". A staged rollout (1%, 5%, 20%, 100% over days) with guardrail monitoring catches regressions. Quasi-experiments estimate effects without random assignment when a natural comparison exists. Interleaving (rec13) compares two rankers inside the same result list with far fewer users. And qualitative research answers "why" questions no metric can.

8. A worked design document

Prompt: a food-delivery marketplace (hypothetical "Dishly") wants to show the delivery fee on restaurant cards in search results, instead of only at checkout. The design, as it would appear in a short experiment plan:

Decision and hypothesis. Ship if showing fees early reduces checkout abandonment without reducing orders per searching user by more than 0.5%. Hypothesis: users currently abandon when they discover the fee; showing it early moves them to cheaper restaurants instead.

Unit. User. Fee display does not change supply or delivery capacity, so interference between users is weak; restaurants could see shifted demand (cheaper-fee restaurants gain), so add restaurant-side guardrails.

Population and trigger. Users who perform a search in a delivery area; triggered on first search result page view, logged identically in both arms.

Metrics. Primary: orders per triggered user. Guardrails: revenue per triggered user, average delivery fee per order, restaurant-level order concentration (do small restaurants with higher fees lose orders?), search latency. Diagnostic: checkout abandonment rate, share of orders from low-fee restaurants.

Size and duration. Suppose orders per triggered user per two weeks average 1.1 with standard deviation 1.6, and we want to detect a 1% change: n ≈ 16 × 2.56 / 0.011² ≈ 338,000 per arm. With 2 million triggered users a week, a 50/50 split reaches that in under a week; run two full weeks for weekly cycles and to see whether the abandonment effect is stable.

Risks to validity. Novelty is unlikely for a price display; primacy (users confused by new card layout) possible, so plot by day. Check sample ratio daily.

Long term. Keep a 2% holdout for eight weeks, because users may learn to prefer low-fee restaurants over time, which changes restaurant economics.

9. Experiments for ML models

Testing a new model adds a few twists to the design. First, a model can be run in shadow mode before any user sees it: it scores live traffic alongside the current model, and its predictions are logged but not used. Shadow mode catches crashes, latency problems, broken features and wildly different score distributions at no user risk, and it should precede the experiment rather than replace it, since it cannot show how users respond.

Second, for rankers, interleaving (rec13) mixes results from both models into one list per user and credits whichever model's items get clicked. It needs far fewer users than an A/B test to tell which ranker users prefer, which makes it a good filter for many candidate models; the winners then go to a regular A/B test, because interleaving cannot measure effects on retention or revenue.

Third, "the treatment" is a moving target when models retrain daily. The experiment compares two training pipelines, not two fixed models, so both arms must retrain on the same schedule and from data that does not leak between arms. A subtle trap: if both arms' training data comes from logs that the treatment influences, the control model starts learning from treatment-shaped data and the arms converge, shrinking the measured effect. Keeping each arm's training data separate, or at least checking how much the treatment shaped shared logs, protects against this.

Fourth, models carry model-specific guardrails: serving latency at the 95th and 99th percentiles, compute cost per request, and for LLM features, cost per active user and the rate of outputs flagged by safety filters. These belong in the launch rule alongside product metrics.

A/A tests and platform trust

Before trusting experiments on a new surface or platform, teams run A/A tests, in which both arms get the same experience. About 5% of metrics should look significant at the 5% level by chance; many more suggests the variance calculation is wrong (often a mismatch between randomisation and analysis units), and a sample ratio mismatch suggests a broken assignment or logging path. A/A tests are cheap insurance and a good interview answer to "how would you know your experimentation setup is trustworthy?".

Ramping as risk management

A new treatment usually starts at a small share of traffic (1-5%) for a day to catch crashes, broken logging and severe guardrail regressions, then ramps to the full experiment allocation. The early ramp is not for measuring the effect; it is for limiting the damage if something is wrong. Analyses should use only data from the stable allocation period, or account for the change in allocation, because mixing periods with different allocations can produce Simpson's-paradox-like distortions when the user mix shifts over time.

Overlapping experiments and interactions

Large platforms run many experiments on the same users at once, each randomised independently. Other experiments then act as extra noise but not bias, which is why overlap is usually safe and enormously increases capacity. The exception is interaction: two changes that conflict on the same page (two teams both moving the search box) or compete for the same resource. Platforms handle this with layers or mutual-exclusion groups for experiments on the same surface, and with interaction checks for pairs that might conflict. Interviewers sometimes probe whether you know overlap is normal rather than a flaw.

Peeking, sequential tests and fixed horizons

Checking results daily and stopping at the first significant p-value inflates false positives, because the p-value wanders over time (math7 quantifies this). Product teams want to stop early for clear harm and sometimes for clear wins. Two responses: fix the horizon in advance and only look at guardrails for harm before then, or use a sequential method (always-valid p-values, group-sequential boundaries) designed for repeated looks. The product decision is which one, and it should be in the plan before launch.

Heterogeneous effects: plan the segments in advance

Effects often differ by segment: new versus established users, platforms, countries. Looking at twenty segments after the fact will find some that differ by chance. Pre-register the two or three segments where you expect differences and why (new users might be confused by a change that established users ignore), and treat others as exploratory hypotheses for the next experiment. ps7 covers reading heterogeneous results.

Bias-variance trade-off in cluster and switchback designs

Bigger units reduce interference bias but cut the number of independent observations, which raises variance. A city-level test with 20 cities may be unbiased and nearly useless; a user-level test may be precise and biased. Switchback designs have their own trade-off in slot length: short slots give more units but more carryover (a delivery assigned at the end of one slot completes in the next), long slots the opposite. Experienced teams estimate both the expected bias (from simulations or past experiments comparing designs) and the expected variance, and pick the design with the smaller total error for the decision at hand.

Long-term effect estimation without long tests

Running every test for months is impractical, so teams estimate long-term effects from short-term data. One approach measures how users' sensitivity to a change evolves within a test and extrapolates; Google's ads team published such a method for ad load (Hohnhold, O'Brien and Tang, 2015, discussed in math7). Another builds a surrogate index: a model that predicts long-term outcomes from several short-term metrics, trained on past experiments with long-term data. Both rely on the past being a good guide; holdouts remain the check.

Ethics and trust

Users rarely know which arm they are in, so experimenters carry responsibility for what the treatments do to them. Most product tests are low-stakes (a button colour, a ranking tweak), but some are not: tests that change prices, that withhold safety features, that alter emotional content, or that affect vulnerable users. Mature organisations have a review step for such tests, ask whether the worst plausible treatment is acceptable to give to real people, cap exposure and duration, and stop early on clear harm. A good interview answer to "would you run this test?" sometimes includes "only with a review, a smaller allocation and a harm-based stopping rule".

Realistic effect sizes

Experienced teams know that most changes to a mature product move its key metrics by well under 1%, and that many ideas have no detectable effect at all. That is why sample sizes are large and why sensitivity work matters. It also means a test that shows +8% on a top-level metric from a small change deserves suspicion before celebration (Twyman's law, ps1). When you size an experiment in an interview, propose an effect size that is plausible for the change (a fraction of a percent for a ranking tweak to a mature feed, a few percent for a new feature measured on its triggered users) and say why.

The real case: LinkedIn's ego clusters

LinkedIn's 2019 paper "Using Ego-Clusters to Measure Network Effects at LinkedIn" (Saint-Jacques, Varshney, Simpson and Xu) starts from the problem in section 5: a network effect, where a feature affects not just the people who get it but their connections and followers, violates SUTVA, and a standard platform using user-level random assignment and a two-sample t-test can give highly biased results. The authors also note that network effects are not only a threat to validity but often the point: a new feed relevance algorithm is meant to make viewers share and post more, raising engagement downstream for the people who see that content.

Their design keeps the existing platform. For each ego, a coin decides whether all of the ego's connections get the treatment or all get control. Putting all egos in treatment and comparing by their connections' assignment gives the pure network effect; giving egos the same assignment as their connections gives the total effect. The final analysis is a two-sample t-test between egos. The procedure identifies about 200,000 egos, and the paper is explicit about the price: "in order to get about 200,000 egos, we need to treat 10 million individuals". It stops adding egos when the average loss rate of recent egos (alters already used by earlier clusters) reaches about 25%. It recommends defining the network by the channel that carries the effect (past feed impressions for many feed experiments) rather than all connections, because the connection graph gives very large clusters. The approach allowed LinkedIn to run such experiments dozens of times a year at low setup cost.

The lessons for design: name the interference channel before choosing the unit; prefer designs that reuse standard analysis; and budget for the power cost, which is large.

Failure modes in practice

Triggering on a post-treatment condition. A new recommendation widget is analysed on "users who clicked a recommendation"; treatment shows more clicks, so its triggered group includes less-engaged users; the per-user metrics look worse. Symptom: a sample ratio mismatch in the triggered population. Defence: trigger on exposure upstream of the change, with counterfactual logging in control.

Session-level randomisation of a learnable change. As in section 2, users carry learning across sessions. Symptom: effects that shrink as the test runs, in both arms.

Ending on a holiday. A two-week test that spans a national holiday measures holiday behaviour. Defence: check the calendar when scheduling, and avoid launching or concluding on atypical weeks.

Celebrating marketplace tests. User-level tests of pricing, dispatch or ranking in supply-constrained marketplaces routinely overstate effects, as in the worked example. Defence: for any change that alters demand for shared supply, require a market-level design or an explicit argument that supply is not constrained.

Holdouts that quietly end. Teams set up a holdout, then a migration removes the old code path and the holdout disappears without anyone analysing it. Defence: holdouts get an owner, an end date and a scheduled analysis.

Questions a design review asks

Teams with strong experimentation practice review experiment plans before launch, and the questions are predictable. What decision will this make, and what is the launch rule? Is the randomisation unit the same as the analysis unit, and if not, how is variance computed? Who is eligible, and is the trigger logged identically in both arms? Is the trigger downstream of anything the treatment changes? Is the test powered for the minimum effect worth shipping, and over how many full weeks? Could treated units affect control units, through a network, shared supply or shared budget? Which segments will be examined, and were they chosen in advance? Is there a plan for novelty (plot by days since exposure) and for long-term effects (a holdout)? What are the stopping rules for harm? Answering these in the plan, before data arrives, is most of what separates trustworthy experiments from expensive noise.

Goal: reproduce how interference in a supply-constrained market biases a user-level test, and see a switchback design recover the market-level effect. About 15 minutes; Python 3.12 standard library.

  1. Save as market.py and run it:
import random
random.seed(5)
CAPACITY = 1000          # rides per hour at peak
BASE_DEMAND = 1000       # requests per hour without discount
LIFT = 0.20              # discount raises a rider's request rate by 20%

def hour(share_treated):
    t_req = int(BASE_DEMAND * share_treated * (1 + LIFT))
    c_req = int(BASE_DEMAND * (1 - share_treated))
    total = t_req + c_req
    served = min(total, CAPACITY)
    t_rides = served * t_req / total if total else 0
    return t_rides, served - t_rides, served

# 1) user-level A/B: 50% of riders treated, per-group rides compared
t, c, s = hour(0.5)
print(f"user A/B: treatment {t:.0f}, control {c:.0f}, naive lift {t / c - 1:+.0%}")

# 2) global launch vs no launch
print(f"no launch: {hour(0.0)[2]:.0f} rides; full launch: {hour(1.0)[2]:.0f} rides")

# 3) switchback: alternate whole market by hour, 40 hours
slots = [random.random() < 0.5 for _ in range(40)]
on = [hour(1.0)[2] for x in slots if x]
off = [hour(0.0)[2] for x in slots if not x]
print(f"switchback: on {sum(on) / len(on):.0f}, off {sum(off) / len(off):.0f} rides per hour")

# 4) now give the market spare capacity and repeat
CAPACITY = 1300
t, c, s = hour(0.5)
print(f"spare capacity: naive lift {t / c - 1:+.0%}; launch {hour(1.0)[2]:.0f} vs {hour(0.0)[2]:.0f}")

What to look for: with capacity 1,000 the user-level test should show about +20% for treatment (about 545 versus 455) while full launch and no launch both give 1,000 rides: the true market effect is zero. The switchback comparison should show on and off hours equal. With spare capacity (1,300), the naive lift and the launch effect should agree (+20%: 1,200 versus 1,000). Then set capacity to 1,100 and see the naive test overstate a partly real effect. Write one sentence on how you would know, before running a test, whether your market is supply-constrained.

Design drill (10 minutes)

Huddle, a hypothetical team-chat product with 40,000 paying organisations, wants to test AI-generated "catch-up summaries" of unread channels. The product manager (PM) proposes a user-level A/B test for one week with "messages read per user" as the metric. Critique the design and propose a better one with numbers.

Answer

Problems: (1) Unit: colleagues in the same channel would be split across arms; a summary might change how treated users reply (fewer "what did I miss?" messages), which control colleagues see, so interference within organisations. Also a team might discuss "the new summary" and confuse control users. (2) Metric: messages read could fall when summaries work (people read the summary instead), so its direction is ambiguous; it measures effort, not value. (3) Duration: one week cannot separate novelty from lasting use of summaries.

Better design: randomise by organisation (40,000 units is plenty for many metrics) and trigger on users with at least 50 unread messages when they open the app. Primary metric: weekly active days per triggered user, or time from opening the app to first message sent (task success: getting back up to speed). Counter-metrics: summary dismissal rate and "jump to message" clicks from summaries that land on content the summary got wrong (a quality signal). Guardrails: messages sent per user (collaboration should not fall), cost per active user (each summary calls a model), latency. Diagnostic: share of users who open summaries in week 1 versus week 4.

Size and duration: organisation-level randomisation means the variance must be computed by organisation (or with cluster-robust methods), which reduces power compared with 40,000 independent users; check the minimum detectable effect with the platform's organisation-level variance. Run four weeks, plot the effect by days since first exposure, and keep 5% of organisations as a holdout for two months to measure lasting value against the inference cost.
