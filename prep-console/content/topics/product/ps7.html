The launch review for a new feed-ranking model starts well. The primary metric, sessions per user, is up 1.2% and statistically significant. Then the questions begin. Page latency at the 95th percentile rose 40 milliseconds. Revenue per user is down 0.4%, not significant. Sessions in Brazil are down 3%, significant. Two secondary metrics that nobody planned to look at are up. The product manager wants to ship. The infrastructure lead says latency is a guardrail and it moved. The Brazil team asks why their users should get a worse product. Someone asks whether 1.2% is even real, given how many metrics were examined. After an hour the meeting ends with "let's run it another week", which is what happens when there is no agreed way to decide.

None of this was unforeseeable. Every experiment that matters produces some conflicts: a primary metric that moves with a guardrail that also moves, segments that react differently, results near the significance boundary. What separates teams that decide well from teams that argue is that they settled, before the data arrived, how each kind of conflict would be resolved. When a guardrail moves 0.5%, they already know whether that is acceptable. When a segment is harmed, they know which segments count. When a result is borderline, they know whether to replicate.

Interviewers probe this skill with exactly the follow-ups from that meeting: "what if the guardrail moves 0.5%?", "the primary went up but revenue went down, do you ship?", "how would you know this isn't a false positive?", "one country got worse, what do you do?". A strong candidate answers with a method, not an opinion.

This lesson gives the method. It covers the trust checks that come first, practical versus statistical significance read from confidence intervals, why "not significant" on an underpowered metric is not reassurance, borderline results and replication, primary-versus-guardrail conflicts resolved with exchange rates and non-inferiority margins, heterogeneous effects across segments, long-term effects, writing launch criteria in advance, and a one-page decision memo. The real cases are four Microsoft experiments from the "dirty dozen" paper, each a pitfall that cost or nearly cost a wrong decision.

An experiment scorecard is a table of metrics, each with an estimated difference between treatment and control, a confidence interval and a p-value. Reading it well means answering three questions in order. Can I trust this experiment at all? For each metric that matters, how big is the effect, and how sure am I? And given those effects and uncertainties, what should we do?

A good analogy is a court applying written law. A judge decides cases using rules written before the case arose: what counts as an offence, what evidence is needed, what penalty applies. That is what makes decisions consistent and hard to manipulate; nobody gets to invent a new law after hearing the evidence. Launch criteria play the role of the law: written before the experiment, they say which metric decides, how big an effect is needed, how much harm to a guardrail is tolerable, and what happens otherwise. The scorecard is the evidence. The launch review applies the one to the other.

The analogy breaks because product decisions are more forgiving than verdicts. Most launches are reversible, so the cost of a wrong call is bounded; a judge cannot easily undo a sentence, but a team can roll back a feature. That changes the standard of proof: for reversible, low-cost changes you can ship on moderate evidence with monitoring, while for costly or irreversible changes you need stronger evidence or a replication. Matching the strength of evidence to the cost of being wrong is the core judgement.

The matrix is the shape of every launch rule. The primary metric's result is classified against a minimum worthwhile effect (not just zero); each guardrail is classified against a margin of acceptable harm (not just "did it move"); and each combination has a pre-agreed action. The body explains how to classify results using confidence intervals, how to set margins and exchange rates, and how segments and long-term effects modify the call.

A reader who stops here should remember three habits: trust checks before metrics; intervals compared with pre-agreed thresholds rather than p-values compared with 0.05; and decisions recorded in a memo that someone could audit a year later.

One more framing helps in interviews. When an interviewer gives you a scorecard and asks "ship or not?", they are rarely testing arithmetic. They want to see whether you ask what the launch criteria were, whether you read intervals rather than stars, whether you notice which guardrails are underpowered, whether you treat a surprising number with suspicion, and whether you can make a decision with conditions attached rather than freezing. A short answer that does all five, ending in a clear "ship, with these conditions" or "don't ship, and here is the next experiment", is the target.

To make the habits concrete, here is a scorecard read in about a minute. Results: primary +0.8% (95% CI +0.3% to +1.3%), minimum worthwhile +0.5%; crash rate +0.02 points (CI −0.01 to +0.05), margin +0.05; revenue +0.2% (CI −0.6% to +1.0%), margin −1.0%; sample ratio 50.02/49.98, within chance. Reading: trust check passes. The primary's estimate clears the bar but its interval starts below it, so the result is a probable but not certain win. Crash rate's interval sits inside its margin: pass. Revenue's interval sits above −1.0%: pass. Decision under a typical rule: ship, because the guardrails pass and the primary is more likely than not above the bar for a reversible change; note in the memo that the lower bound is below the bar and check the effect in a holdout. Every step of that reading is mechanical once the thresholds exist; the judgement went into setting them beforehand.

1. Trust checks come first

Every metric in a scorecard is meaningless if the experiment itself is broken. Before reading results, check:

  • Sample ratio. Are the arms the size the design intended? A mismatch beyond chance (tested with a chi-squared test, in10) signals a bug in assignment, logging or filtering. Microsoft's "dirty dozen" paper (KDD 2017, a major data-mining conference) describes an experiment on MSN, Microsoft's news portal, that showed a puzzling drop in engagement along with a sample ratio mismatch; the bot filter was labelling the most engaged treatment users as bots because the treatment made them so active. After fixing the filter, the mismatch disappeared and the result reversed to a big engagement increase.
  • Exposure and triggering. Was the trigger logged the same way in both arms (ps6)?
  • Incidents. Were there outages, data-pipeline failures or other launches during the run that might affect one arm?
  • Duration. Did the test run full weekly cycles, and does the effect look stable by day?
  • Plausibility. Is any effect too large for the change? Twyman's law, the rule of thumb that any figure that looks interesting or different is usually wrong, says to look for a bug first.

Only when these pass do the metrics deserve interpretation.

2. Practical versus statistical significance

A p-value answers a narrow question: if the change had no effect, how surprising would this result be? Statistical significance (p below 0.05, conventionally) says the effect is probably not zero. Practical significance asks whether it is big enough to matter, given what the change costs to build, run and maintain. The tool that answers both at once is the confidence interval: the range of effects consistent with the data.

Before the experiment, set the minimum worthwhile effect, the smallest lift that justifies shipping. Suppose it is +0.5% on sessions per user for a ranking change that adds serving cost. Four results:

Case B is the trap for people who read only p-values: significant, and still not worth shipping on this metric alone. Case C is the trap for people who read "not significant" as "no effect": the data is equally compatible with a worthwhile gain. Case D is the only one that justifies saying "it doesn't work", because the interval excludes everything worthwhile. Reading intervals against the minimum worthwhile effect turns four ambiguous results into four clear decisions: ship, don't ship (or ship for other reasons), extend, abandon.

3. "Not significant" on an underpowered metric is not reassurance

A metric is underpowered when the experiment is too small to detect effects of the size that matter. The "dirty dozen" paper gives a precise example from MSN: total page views per user rose 0.5%, not statistically significant. For a mature business, 0.5% of page views is meaningful. Was it safe to assume no impact? The confidence interval was about ±5%, and the experiment could only detect a change of 7.8% or more with 80% power. The result said almost nothing either way.

This matters most for guardrails. A guardrail that is underpowered passes by default: it will almost never show a significant regression, so the scorecard looks clean even when real harm is possible. The paper's recommendation is to do a power analysis in advance for the decision metric and the guardrails, and allocate enough traffic for changes that are small but meaningful to the business.

Underpowering has a second consequence, the winner's curse: when an underpowered metric does reach significance, the estimate is likely to be exaggerated, because only the experiments where noise pushed the estimate up crossed the threshold. Booking the observed lift of an underpowered win as the expected impact overstates it.

4. Borderline results and replication

A p-value near 0.05 is weak evidence, especially when many metrics were examined and the metric rarely moves. In a Bing experiment, one of the key evaluation metrics, which tries to capture user satisfaction and is a leading indicator of retention and which very few experiments improve, rose with p = 0.029. Bing runs a certification run (a "certification flight") whenever key metrics move positively: an independent repeat of the experiment. The repeat, with double the traffic, showed no statistically significant change.

The general policy: for important decisions resting on a borderline result, especially on hard-to-move metrics, replicate before launching. Replication costs a week or two of traffic; launching a false positive costs the complexity of the change forever, plus the false belief that the idea works, which then shapes the roadmap.

The multiple-metrics problem makes borderline results more common than intuition suggests. With 40 metrics and no real effects, about 2 will show p below 0.05 by chance. With 40 metrics and 10 segments, about 20. Treating every significant cell as a finding guarantees false discoveries. The defence is a small, pre-declared set of decision metrics and segments, with everything else labelled exploratory.

5. Primary versus guardrail: exchange rates and non-inferiority

The hardest calls come when the primary metric wins and a guardrail loses. There are two principled ways to resolve them, both decided in advance.

Exchange rates. If the organisation has measured how much one metric is worth in units of another, the conflict becomes arithmetic. Suppose past latency experiments (deliberately slowing pages for a small group) showed that each 100 ms of added p95 latency costs about 0.5% of sessions per user. Then a 40 ms regression costs about 0.2%, and a ranking change that adds +1.2% sessions nets about +1.0%: ship, and open a ticket to recover the latency. Exchange rates need to be measured, owned and reused, not invented in the launch meeting.

Non-inferiority margins. When no exchange rate exists, each guardrail gets a non-inferiority margin: the largest harm that is acceptable. The guardrail passes only if its confidence interval lies entirely above minus the margin, that is, if the experiment can rule out harm bigger than the margin. This answers the interviewer's favourite follow-up, "what if the guardrail moves 0.5%?":

Guardrail result (revenue per user)95% CIMargin −1.0%Verdict
−0.5%−0.9% to −0.1%CI above −1.0%Pass: real but acceptable harm, record it
−0.5%−1.4% to +0.4%CI crosses −1.0%Not passed: cannot rule out harm beyond margin; extend or accept risk explicitly
−1.3%−1.9% to −0.7%CI below −1.0% in partBreach likely: do not ship as is

The second row is the underpowered case from section 3: the experiment did not see harm, but it could not have seen it. The honest options are to run longer, reduce variance (CUPED, math7), or have someone with authority accept the risk in writing.

Some guardrails are absolute: safety incidents, legal compliance, data-loss rates. Their margin is effectively zero, and a breach blocks the launch whatever the primary says.

6. Segments: heterogeneous treatment effects

A heterogeneous treatment effect is one that differs across segments. Averages can hide important harm. The "dirty dozen" paper describes a Bing experiment on a new ad auction and placement algorithm intended to raise revenue by improving ad quality while keeping the number of ads roughly constant. Overall it looked successful: revenue +2.3%, ads per page −0.6%. Segmented by page type, it was not: on "dup" pages (pages reloaded when users press back after clicking a result) ads per page fell 2.3%, while on original result pages, the more important ones, ads per page rose 0.3%. The experiment had not met its goal of holding ad load constant where it mattered.

Rules for reading segments. Pre-register the segments that matter for the decision and why (new users, the largest markets, the main platforms, the page types the change targets). Treat any other segment difference as a hypothesis for a new experiment, because with many segments some will differ by chance. Segment only by properties fixed before treatment (country, platform at assignment, tenure), never by behaviours the treatment can change (ps4 shows how that creates paradoxes). And decide in advance what you will do if a pre-registered segment is harmed: fix and retest, ship with the segment excluded, or accept with a written rationale.

For the opening example, the Brazil decline of 3% matters only if Brazil was a pre-registered segment or the effect is large enough to survive a multiple-comparisons correction. If it does, the next step is a mechanism: does the new ranker rely on a feature that is sparse or broken for Portuguese content?

7. Long-term effects in the decision

A two-week experiment measures two weeks of behaviour. For changes prone to novelty, adaptation or slow harm (notifications, ads load, prices, ranking objectives that change content supply), the launch decision should include how long-term effects will be checked. The usual structure is a conditional launch: ship to most users, keep a small holdout, agree a readout date (often 6-12 weeks), and agree the rollback rule in advance. Google's Rules of Machine Learning makes the underlying point in Rule 39: launch decisions are a proxy for long-term product goals, so the short-term metrics used to decide are only as good as their link to the long term.

8. Writing launch criteria in advance

Launch criteria compress everything above into a short document written before the experiment starts. A template:

  1. Decision: what we will decide (ship variant B or not).
  2. Primary metric and minimum worthwhile effect: sessions per user, +0.5%.
  3. Guardrails and margins: p95 latency ≤ +20 ms; revenue per user non-inferior at −1.0%; crash rate no significant increase; safety reports margin 0.
  4. Exchange rates in force: 100 ms p95 ≈ −0.5% sessions.
  5. Segments that can block: new users (first 28 days), Android, top five markets.
  6. Duration and power: two full weeks; powered at 80% for +0.5% on the primary and for the revenue margin.
  7. Actions: the decision matrix from the primer, filled in for this test.
  8. Long-term check: 2% holdout, readout at week 8, roll back if sessions in holdout comparison are below +0.2%.

Writing it takes an hour. It prevents most of the arguments in the opening story, and it makes the launch review short: apply the rules, discuss only what the rules did not foresee.

9. The decision memo

After the decision, write it down. A decision memo is one page: the decision in one sentence; the hypothesis and the pre-registered criteria (linked); trust checks passed; results for the primary metric and every guardrail with intervals; pre-registered segments; trade-offs accepted and why; risks and how they will be monitored; follow-ups with dates. Example, for the opening story under the criteria above:

"Decision: ship ranker v7 to 98% of users with a 2% holdout. Primary: sessions per user +1.2% (95% CI +0.7% to +1.7%), clears the +0.5% bar. Guardrails: p95 latency +40 ms (breaches the +20 ms margin; under the agreed exchange rate it costs about 0.2% sessions, net about +1.0%); a latency fix is required before full holdout readout and is tracked in ticket X. Revenue per user −0.4% (CI −1.1% to +0.3%): non-inferiority at −1.0% not established; accepted by [owner] given the primary gain, with revenue as a holdout readout metric. New users and Android: no significant difference. Brazil −3.0% (CI −5.5% to −0.5%): Brazil was not pre-registered; investigating a hypothesis that Portuguese-language features are sparse; follow-up experiment planned. Readout: week 8 holdout analysis on sessions, revenue and notification disables."

The memo makes the trade-offs visible, names who accepted which risk, and gives the future reader the evidence. It is also, word for word, the kind of answer that scores well when an interviewer hands you a scorecard and asks "ship or not?".

10. Reading results for ML and LLM launches

Model launches add a few specific checks to the reading. First, offline-online consistency: did the online effect have the sign and rough size the offline evaluation predicted? A model that gained 3 points of offline AUC and shows nothing online is telling you that the offline metric or label does not track the product goal (ps3), and that is worth recording even if you do not ship. Second, cost and latency are first-class guardrails: a new model that wins on engagement but doubles serving cost needs an exchange rate between engagement and compute, just like latency. Third, slice quality: models often help on average and fail on specific slices (a language, a content type, new users with little history); pre-register the slices where the model is weakest offline. Fourth, for LLM features, read a sample of outputs from the treatment alongside the metrics. Metrics such as thumbs-up rate and task completion can look fine while a small share of outputs are harmful or fabricated, and a few confirmed incidents may breach a zero-tolerance guardrail that no aggregate metric would reveal. The drill below is an example.

Finally, models retrain. The model that won the experiment is not the model that will run in three months. A launch decision for a model is really a decision about a training pipeline, so the memo should say what retraining cadence was tested and what monitoring will catch a later version that loses the gain.

11. Communicating uncertain results

Stakeholders want a yes or no, and many results are "probably" or "not yet". Three practices help. Lead with the decision and its conditions, then the evidence: "we're shipping with a holdout; the gain is probably between 0.3% and 1.3%". Express uncertainty as ranges of business impact rather than p-values: "between 30,000 and 130,000 extra weekly sessions" is easier to act on than "p = 0.002". And separate what is known from what will be learned: "the long-term effect on retention will be measured at week 8, and we will roll back if it is below X". Executives tend to trust teams that say what they do not know and when they will know it, and to distrust teams whose every result is a confident win.

In interviews, the same communication pattern works when you are asked to interpret a result: give the decision first, then the two or three numbers that drive it, then the caveat and how you would resolve it. It shows you can be decisive and honest about uncertainty at the same time, which is most of what "senior judgement" means in this context.

12. A second worked call: neutral quality, lower cost

Not every launch is about winning on the primary metric. Suppose a team replaces a large model with a distilled smaller one for an AI search-answer feature. The goal is cost, so the pre-registered criteria say: ship if answer helpfulness (the share of answers rated helpful in an in-product prompt) is non-inferior at a margin of −1.0 point, task completion (users who did not reformulate the query within two minutes) is non-inferior at −0.5 points, p95 latency does not rise, and cost per thousand answers falls by at least 30%.

Results after two weeks: helpfulness −0.4 points (95% CI −0.9 to +0.1); task completion −0.2 points (CI −0.6 to +0.2); p95 latency −120 ms; cost per thousand answers −58%. Reading: helpfulness passes (its interval lies above −1.0); task completion does not quite pass, because its lower bound of −0.6 crosses the −0.5 margin; latency and cost clearly pass.

The call: under the written rule, task completion is inconclusive, not failed. Options are to extend the test a week to narrow the interval, or to ship with explicit acceptance of the risk. Given that the change is reversible within an hour (switch the model back) and saves 58% of a large cost line, a reasonable decision is to ship to 90% with a 10% control kept for two more weeks, with an owner accepting the task-completion risk in writing and a rollback rule if the extended comparison shows harm beyond −0.5. That is a defensible, auditable decision, made in minutes, because the criteria existed. Without them, the same results would have produced a long argument about whether "−0.2" matters.

Decisions under uncertainty: expected value, not certainty

A launch decision is a bet. Shipping a change whose true effect is uncertain has an expected value: the probability-weighted gain if it works, minus the probability-weighted loss if it does not, minus the cost of maintaining it. Reversible changes have bounded downside (you can roll back after detecting harm), so they can ship on weaker evidence; irreversible ones (a pricing change that resets customer expectations, a data migration, removing a feature people depend on) need stronger evidence. Senior answers make this explicit: "this is reversible within a day, so I would ship on this evidence with monitoring" or "this is hard to undo, so I want a replication".

Shrinking estimates toward what usually happens

Most changes to a mature product have small effects, and the distribution of past experiment results shows it. A Bayesian reading of a new result combines the observed estimate with that prior: an observed +3% on a metric where past changes rarely exceed +0.5% is probably partly noise, and the best forecast of its real impact is lower than +3%. Teams that forecast business impact from experiments increasingly shrink estimates this way, which directly counters the winner's curse and makes roadmaps more honest.

Composite criteria and their governance

An OEC (Overall Evaluation Criterion) that combines metrics with weights (ps2) turns many launch arguments into arithmetic, much like an exchange rate. The cost is governance: the weights must be owned, revisited when the product changes, and protected from being adjusted to make a particular experiment pass. A sign of trouble is a composite whose weights changed in the same week as a contentious launch.

Ship to learn versus ship to win

Sometimes the right decision is to ship a change that did not win, because it enables learning or future work: a new infrastructure path that is neutral today but makes ten future experiments possible, or a simplification that removes code. That is legitimate if it is called what it is and evaluated against non-inferiority margins rather than as a "win". It becomes illegitimate when neutral results are relabelled as wins to protect a team's roadmap.

Organisational traps

Three biases recur in launch reviews. Sunk cost: "we spent six months on this" is not evidence that it works. HiPPO (highest paid person's opinion): seniority overriding pre-agreed criteria undermines every future experiment's credibility. Metric shopping: searching the scorecard for something that improved. Pre-registered criteria, decision memos with named owners for accepted risks, and periodic audits of past decisions against holdouts are the structural defences. In an interview, describing how you would keep a launch review honest when a senior leader wants to ship is a strong senior signal.

When the primary metric was wrong

Occasionally an experiment reveals that the pre-registered primary metric does not capture the goal: it moved in a direction that clearly reflects confusion rather than value, for example. The right response is not to swap metrics for this decision after seeing the data, but to decide this test on its original criteria (often "inconclusive"), fix the metric through the normal metric review, and rerun. That feels slow; it is much faster than a culture in which every launch renegotiates its metrics.

Choosing among several variants

Experiments with many variants (five headlines, four model versions) magnify the winner's curse: the best-looking variant is partly best because of luck, so its observed lift overstates its true lift, and the more variants you test, the larger the overstatement. Two habits help. Treat the multi-variant test as a screen, then confirm the winner in a fresh head-to-head test against control. And when the variants are close, prefer the simplest or cheapest among those whose intervals overlap with the best, since the data cannot really tell them apart.

Stopping early, in both directions

Stopping for harm is usually right and should be allowed by the plan: if a guardrail shows a large, clear regression on day three, continuing exposes users to harm for no benefit, and the cost of a false alarm (stopping a test that was fine) is small. Stopping for a win is riskier, because early estimates are noisy and the peeking problem inflates false positives (math7). The asymmetry is deliberate and worth saying in an interview: harm-based stopping rules can be liberal, win-based ones must be conservative or use a proper sequential method.

Effect sizes in money

Translating intervals into business impact makes trade-offs comparable across teams. A +0.3% to +1.3% interval on sessions, multiplied by sessions per week and a value per session, becomes a range in money per year; the same for a latency cost or an inference bill. Decisions that weigh a revenue range against a cost range are easier to make and to explain than decisions that weigh percentages of different metrics. The conversion factors are exactly the exchange rates from section 5, so building them pays off twice.

The real case: four Microsoft pitfalls

Microsoft's "A Dirty Dozen: Twelve Common Metric Interpretation Pitfalls in Online Controlled Experiments" (Dmitriev, Gupta, Kim and Vaz, KDD 2017) draws on thousands of experiments across Bing, MSN, Office, Skype, Xbox, Edge and other products. Four of its pitfalls map directly onto this lesson.

Underpowered metrics. In an MSN experiment, page views per user rose 0.5%, not significant. The interval was about ±5%, and only changes of 7.8% or more were detectable with 80% power, so "no significant change" said nothing. The paper recommends power analysis in advance for the decision metric and guardrails.

Borderline p-values. A Bing experiment improved a key satisfaction metric, one that very few experiments move, with p = 0.029. Bing's practice is to rerun experiments whose key metrics move positively; with double the traffic, the effect was not significant. The paper notes that repeated A/A experiments regularly show metrics crossing the 5% boundary by chance.

Heterogeneous effects. A Bing ad auction change looked successful (revenue +2.3%, ads per page −0.6%) until segmentation by page type showed ads per page rising 0.3% on the important original result pages and falling 2.3% on reloaded "dup" pages. The goal of holding ad load constant had not been met.

Twyman's law. On MSN, replacing a link to the Outlook.com web mail site with a button that opened the desktop mail app raised overall navigation clicks 4.7%, clicks on the button 28% and clicks on the adjacent button 27%. Retention and satisfaction metrics showed no significant change, and daily clicks on the mail button fell rapidly. The team concluded users were confused, clicking repeatedly and trying nearby buttons, and shut the experiment down mid-way to avoid dissatisfaction.

Together they show the same lesson from four angles: a scorecard number is not a decision until you know its power, its fragility, its distribution across segments and whether it measures what you think.

How launch reviews work in practice

Organisations that run many experiments usually separate routine and contentious decisions. Routine launches, where the pre-registered criteria give a clear answer, are approved by the owning team with the scorecard attached. Contentious ones go to a review with an experimentation expert who checks trust, power and interpretation. Some organisations require a decision memo for any launch that accepts a guardrail regression, naming who accepted it. Periodically, teams audit past launches against holdouts to calibrate how often their "wins" were real. These processes look bureaucratic from outside; inside, they are what lets a company run thousands of experiments a year without each one becoming a debate.

Failure modes

The scorecard with stars. Dashboards that colour metrics green or red by p-value encourage reading stars instead of intervals. Symptom: launches justified by "everything green". Defence: show intervals against thresholds.

Ship and forget. Conditional launches whose holdout readouts never happen. Symptom: holdouts quietly removed in a migration. Defence: readouts with owners and dates in the memo.

Accumulated guardrail drift. Each launch costs 5 ms of latency, within margin; fifty launches later, the product is 250 ms slower. Defence: budgets for guardrails across launches, not only per launch.

Post-launch verification

A decision is not finished at launch. Mature teams compare the metrics after full rollout with what the experiment predicted: if the experiment said +1.2% sessions and the whole-population metric shows no change a month later (after accounting for seasonality and other launches), something about the experiment did not generalise, such as interference between users, novelty or a population difference between the test and launch. The holdout makes this comparison clean. Recording these predicted-versus-realised comparisons over many launches tells a team how much to trust its experiments, and therefore how much evidence to demand next time.

Goal: apply a written launch rule to experiment results mechanically, including non-inferiority checks. About 15 minutes; Python 3.12 standard library.

  1. Save as decide.py and run it:
from statistics import NormalDist
Z = NormalDist().inv_cdf(0.975)

def ci(est, se):
    return est - Z * se, est + Z * se

RULE = {"primary": ("sessions", 0.5),            # minimum worthwhile effect, %
        "margins": {"revenue": -1.0, "latency_ms": 20.0}}

def decide(res):
    lo, hi = ci(*res["sessions"])
    if lo >= RULE["primary"][1]:
        primary = "win"
    elif hi < RULE["primary"][1]:
        primary = "loss_or_too_small"
    else:
        primary = "inconclusive"
    guards = {}
    rlo, rhi = ci(*res["revenue"])
    guards["revenue"] = "pass" if rlo > RULE["margins"]["revenue"] else (
        "breach" if rhi < RULE["margins"]["revenue"] else "inconclusive")
    llo, lhi = ci(*res["latency_ms"])
    guards["latency_ms"] = "pass" if lhi < RULE["margins"]["latency_ms"] else (
        "breach" if llo > RULE["margins"]["latency_ms"] else "inconclusive")
    return primary, guards

scenarios = {
    "clean win":        {"sessions": (1.2, 0.25), "revenue": (-0.1, 0.2), "latency_ms": (5, 3)},
    "latency breach":   {"sessions": (1.2, 0.25), "revenue": (-0.1, 0.2), "latency_ms": (40, 4)},
    "underpowered rev": {"sessions": (1.2, 0.25), "revenue": (-0.5, 0.46), "latency_ms": (5, 3)},
    "too small":        {"sessions": (0.3, 0.10), "revenue": (0.0, 0.2), "latency_ms": (2, 3)},
}
for name, res in scenarios.items():
    print(f"{name:17}", decide(res))

What to look for: "clean win" should give a win with both guardrails passing; "latency breach" a win with latency breached (the case for an exchange rate or a fix); "underpowered rev" a win with revenue inconclusive, because its interval (about −1.4% to +0.4%) crosses the −1.0% margin even though the estimate is only −0.5%; and "too small" an effect that is significant but below the bar. Then add a rule: an exchange rate where each 100 ms costs 0.5% sessions, and print the net primary effect for the latency case. Finally, halve the revenue standard error and watch "inconclusive" become "pass", which is what running the test longer buys you.

Lumen, a hypothetical reading app, tested an AI "explain this passage" button. Pre-registered criteria: primary reading minutes per user, minimum worthwhile +1%; guardrails: subscription cancellations non-inferior at +0.3 percentage points, cost per active user at most +$0.05, content-safety reports margin zero. Results after two weeks: reading minutes +2.1% (CI +0.6% to +3.6%); cancellations +0.1 pp (CI −0.1 to +0.3); cost per active user +$0.07 (CI +$0.06 to +$0.08); safety reports: 2 confirmed incidents of the explanation inventing quotes. Reading-minute lift by day: +3.5% in week 1, +0.9% in week 2. Make the call and write the two-sentence memo summary.

Answer

Primary: the interval's lower bound (+0.6%) is below the +1% bar, so it is not a clear win by the criteria, and the decay from +3.5% to +0.9% suggests novelty: the lasting effect may be below the bar.

Guardrails: cancellations pass non-inferiority (the upper bound +0.3 pp equals the margin; borderline, treat as just passing or extend). Cost breaches: $0.07 with interval entirely above $0.05. Safety: two confirmed fabricated quotes against a zero margin is a breach by itself.

Call: do not ship. Fix the safety issue (ground explanations in the passage text and block quotation marks around text not present in the passage), reduce cost (a smaller model or caching explanations of popular passages), and rerun for four weeks to measure the lasting effect after novelty.

Memo summary: "Not shipping the explain button: two confirmed fabricated-quote incidents breach the zero-tolerance safety guardrail, cost per active user exceeds its margin ($0.07 vs $0.05), and the reading-time lift is decaying (week 2 +0.9%) below the +1% bar. Next: grounded explanations with a cheaper model, rerun for four weeks with the same criteria."
