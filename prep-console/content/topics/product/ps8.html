A support team receives about 50,000 tickets a day and spends a lot of time routing them to the right queue. An ML team proposes a ticket classifier. Six months later it ships: a fine-tuned model with 91% routing accuracy, a feature pipeline, a labelling workflow and an on-call rotation. In the launch review, someone asks what the old system's accuracy was. Nobody knows exactly. A quick check finds that a set of 40 keyword rules, written by a support lead years ago, routed 84% correctly. The model added 7 points, at the cost of six engineer-months and a permanent maintenance burden. It might still be worth it, but nobody had asked before building.

The opposite mistake happens too, and it has become more common with large language models. A team adds an LLM to produce a summary of every customer conversation, for every user, on every page load. It is impressive in demos. A month after launch, finance flags the bill: the feature costs far more per year than the incremental revenue anyone can attribute to it, because most summaries are generated and never read. The model works; the product economics do not.

Both teams skipped the same question: should this be ML, or an LLM, at all? It is a question about baselines (what does the simplest approach achieve?), error tolerance (what do mistakes cost?), economics (what is a useful output worth compared with what it costs to produce?), latency (does a model fit the response-time budget?), and ownership (build it, buy it, or call an API?). Senior engineers are expected to ask it before building, and interviewers test it directly with prompts like "would you use an LLM for this?" and "how would you know the AI feature is worth it?".

This lesson gives a structured way to answer. It covers heuristic baselines and Google's first rules of ML; turning error costs into a required operating point; the value-per-query versus cost-per-query calculation with October 2026 prices; latency budgets; the build-versus-buy-versus-API decision with a crossover calculation; when classic ML beats an LLM and vice versa; and a complete worked "is the AI feature worth it" model with a break-even acceptance rate.

Every product decision that could use machine learning has a ladder of options, from cheapest and simplest to most capable and most expensive. At the bottom are rules and heuristics: hand-written logic such as "route tickets mentioning 'refund' to billing" or "rank by number of purchases last week". Next is classic ML: a model such as logistic regression or gradient-boosted trees trained on labelled examples of structured data. Then LLM APIs: a hosted large language model called with a prompt, paid per token. Then fine-tuned or distilled models: a model trained further on your own examples, often smaller and cheaper per call. At the top, self-hosted or custom models: running models on your own hardware, or training your own. Each step up buys capability and costs more to build, run and maintain.

A good analogy is staffing a task in a company. You can write a checklist that anyone can follow (a rule). You can hire a junior specialist and train them on past examples (classic ML). You can bring in an expensive, versatile consultant who understands almost anything you explain to them but bills by the hour (an LLM API). You can train a junior person specifically to do what the consultant does on your most common cases (distillation and fine-tuning). Or you can build an in-house expert team (self-hosting). Nobody hires a consultant to sort the mail by postcode, and nobody writes a checklist for negotiating a complex contract. The skill is matching the option to the task's difficulty, volume and stakes.

Where the analogy breaks: software options cost money per use in very different ways. A checklist costs nothing per use; a consultant bills every hour; an in-house team costs the same whether busy or idle. And an LLM consultant answers in seconds at any scale, which no human consultant can, so tasks that were never worth a human's time (summarising every conversation) become possible, and the question becomes whether they are worth even a fraction of a cent.

The decision rests on six questions, each covered in the body. Is there a learnable pattern, and data? If rules capture it, use rules; if not and there is no data, an LLM's general knowledge may be the only option. What do errors cost? This sets how accurate the system must be, and whether a person must review outputs. Is value per query higher than cost per query? At the volume you expect, including errors. Does it fit the latency budget? Build, buy or call an API? And are there constraints (regulation, explainability, data residency) that rule options out?

A reader who stops here should hold two ideas: start at the bottom of the ladder and climb only with evidence, and treat "is the AI worth it?" as an arithmetic question about value per query, cost per query and error costs at real volume, which the body works through with numbers.

It is worth saying plainly why this question is harder now than it was a few years ago. Before capable LLM APIs, adding a model to a product meant collecting labelled data and training something, which was slow enough that teams naturally asked whether it was worth it. Today a prototype that summarises, classifies or drafts can be built in an afternoon, and it often looks magical in a demo. The cost of trying has collapsed; the cost of running at scale and the cost of errors have not. That shift moves the hard part of the decision from "can we build it?" to "should it exist in this form, at this price, with these failure modes?", which is a product question that engineers are now expected to answer.

1. The six questions, in order

Before choosing an approach, answer six questions about the problem. Each can end the discussion early.

Is there a pattern that simple rules cannot capture? If ten keyword rules route 84% of tickets correctly, the question becomes whether the remaining 16% justify a model. If no simple rule works (judging whether a product photo matches its description), a model is needed.

Is there data to learn from? Classic ML needs labelled examples, typically thousands for a simple classifier. If you have none, an LLM's pretrained knowledge (used with a prompt and a few examples) may be the only practical option at first, and its outputs can later become training data.

What do errors cost? Every model makes mistakes. The cost of each type of mistake determines the accuracy needed and whether a human must stay in the loop (section 3).

Is value per query higher than cost per query? At the volume the product will see, including the cost of errors (sections 4 and 7).

Does it fit the latency budget? A step that must finish in 50 milliseconds cannot call a model that takes two seconds (section 5).

Are there constraints that rule options out? Regulations that require explainable decisions (credit, hiring, insurance in many jurisdictions), data that cannot leave a region or the company, or audit requirements. These can make a simpler, explainable model the only acceptable choice.

2. Heuristic baselines

A heuristic is a hand-written rule or formula that approximates the decision. Google's Rules of Machine Learning, a widely used guide written by Martin Zinkevich, opens with Rule 1: "Don't be afraid to launch a product without machine learning." Its reasoning: machine learning requires data, and a model borrowed from another problem will likely underperform basic heuristics; "if you think that machine learning will give you a 100% boost, then a heuristic will get you 50% of the way there." Its examples are worth remembering because they are so plain: rank apps in a marketplace by install rate; filter spam by blocking publishers who have sent spam before; rank contacts by most recently used.

A baseline does four jobs. It ships in days and delivers value immediately. It sets the bar a model must beat by enough to justify its cost. It forces the team to build logging and metrics (Rule 2 is "first, design and implement metrics"), which any later model needs. And it often reveals that the real problem is different from the one imagined: the ticket-routing rules fail mostly on tickets written in Spanish, so the fix might be a language detector rather than a new classifier.

Heuristics also have a natural end. Rule 3 says "choose machine learning over a complex heuristic": once the rules grow into dozens of interacting special cases that nobody can maintain, a model trained on data is easier to improve. The best transitions keep the heuristic's logic as input features to the model, so its knowledge is not lost.

Measuring a baseline is usually quick. For classification, label a random sample of a few hundred cases and compute the rules' accuracy, precision and recall. For ranking, run the heuristic as the control in an experiment. For generation tasks, the baseline might be a template ("Your order #1234 will arrive on Tuesday") whose quality can be rated alongside model outputs.

3. From error costs to an operating point

Most ML decisions are made by comparing a model's score with a threshold. Where to put the threshold, and whether any model is good enough, follows from what errors cost. Write down, for one decision, the value of a correct action and the cost of each error type.

Worked example: a payments company considers a fraud model that blocks risky transactions. Blocking a legitimate payment (a false positive) costs about $40: the lost margin on the sale plus an estimate of the customer's reduced future spending. Letting fraud through (a false negative) costs about $200: the chargeback, fees and the goods. The model outputs a probability p that a transaction is fraud. Blocking has expected cost (1 − p) × $40 (if it was legitimate); allowing has expected cost p × $200 (if it was fraud). Block when p × 200 > (1 − p) × 40, which gives p > 40 / 240 ≈ 0.17. The threshold is set by the costs, not by convention.

At that threshold, the model's precision and recall (ml7) determine its value. Suppose that at p > 0.17 it catches 70% of fraud (recall) and 30% of the transactions it blocks are actually fraud (precision). Per 100,000 transactions with a 0.5% fraud rate (500 fraudulent): it blocks 350 frauds, saving 350 × $200 = $70,000, and blocks 350 / 0.30 − 350 ≈ 817 legitimate payments, costing 817 × $40 ≈ $32,700. Net about $37,300 per 100,000 transactions compared with no model. If the current rules achieve, say, $25,000, the model adds about $12,300 per 100,000, which tells you how much it is worth spending to build and run it.

Error costs also decide whether a human must stay in the loop. When an error is cheap and reversible (a misrouted ticket is re-routed in a minute), let the model act. When it is expensive or irreversible (sending a refund, giving medical advice, publishing content), use the model to suggest and a person to decide, at least until the error rate on reviewed samples is known and low. That changes the economics: value then comes from time saved for the reviewer, not from full automation.

4. Value per query versus cost per query

For LLM features, every call has a visible marginal cost, so the basic economics fit on one line. For each request:

net value = P(output used) × value if used − P(harmful error) × cost per error − cost per call

Cost per call is input tokens × input price + output tokens × output price, plus any retrieval or tool calls. At October 2026 list prices from Anthropic's pricing page, Claude Haiku 4.5 costs $1 per million input tokens and $5 per million output tokens, and Claude Sonnet 5.5 costs $2 and $10. Examples:

Discounts change these by large factors: batch processing (for work that can wait) is priced at half; cached prompt prefixes are much cheaper to re-read (ae2 covers both). Prices change over time, so recheck them before any real decision.

Value if used is the hard part. For internal tools, it is often time saved: an agent who earns a fully loaded $30 an hour costs $0.50 a minute, so a draft that saves two minutes is worth $1.00. Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond's field study of an AI assistant for 5,179 customer-support agents found it increased issues resolved per hour by 14% on average, and by 34% for novice and low-skilled workers, with minimal effect on the most experienced; that is the kind of evidence that grounds a value estimate. For consumer features, value is usually indirect: higher retention or conversion, which must be measured in an experiment and converted to money with lifetime value (ps5). P(output used) is the acceptance or read rate, which you can estimate from a pilot.

The support example: value 0.40 × $1.00 = $0.40 per ticket if 40% of drafts are accepted, cost $0.008, so net about $0.39 before error costs, roughly $19,500 a day at 50,000 tickets. The break-even acceptance rate is $0.008 / $1.00 = 0.8%. Cost is not the constraint here; quality and risk are.

The consumer example looks very different. A free email app considers summarising threads for 50 million daily users, two summaries per user per day. At $0.003 per summary on Haiku 4.5, that is 100 million × $0.003 = $300,000 a day, about $110 million a year. Suppose 60% of summaries are read, and the value of a read summary, measured as its effect on retention converted to ad revenue, is $0.002. Value per summary is 0.6 × $0.002 = $0.0012, against a cost of $0.003: the feature loses $0.0018 per summary, about $65 million a year. The levers are then design changes, not model tweaks: summarise only long threads (most threads are short), generate on demand when a user taps rather than for every thread, cache summaries, use batch pricing for overnight digests, or put the feature in a paid tier where value per user is higher.

Now change one input: the model. Anthropic's newest small model, Claude Haiku 5.5, lists $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens for prompts up to 100,000 tokens, a tenth of Haiku 4.5's price. The same 1,500-in, 300-out summary then costs $0.00015 + $0.00015 = $0.0003. Value per summary is still $0.0012, so the feature now nets about +$0.0009 per summary, roughly $90,000 a day or $33 million a year, provided the cheaper model's summaries are good enough to keep the read rate and the retention effect. Nothing about the product changed; a price and a model choice flipped a $65 million annual loss into a gain. Two lessons follow. Every cost assumption needs a date and a model name, because prices move. And model choice is a product lever: the question "which is the cheapest model whose quality meets the bar on our evaluation set?" often matters more than any prompt improvement.

5. Latency budgets

A latency budget is the time a step may take within an end-to-end target. A search results page that must load in 300 milliseconds might give ranking 50 milliseconds to score 500 candidates. A generative model that takes a few hundred milliseconds just to start producing output, and seconds to finish a paragraph, cannot sit in that path.

That does not rule out LLMs; it changes where they sit. Four patterns are common. Precompute: generate summaries, tags, embeddings or labels offline and serve them from a store; the request path only reads. Asynchronous: start the model when the user arrives and show its output when ready (an "AI overview" that appears a second after the results). Stream: send tokens as they are generated, so the first words appear quickly even if the full answer takes seconds. Distil: train a small, fast model to imitate the LLM on the specific task and use it in the path. Matching the pattern to the latency budget is part of the product design, because users experience the result.

The API-versus-self-host crossover is worth computing. Take the ticket classifier at 50,000 calls a day, $0.0016 per call on an API: about $80 a day, $29,000 a year. Self-hosting a small open model on one GPU rented at an assumed $4 an hour costs $96 a day for the GPU alone, before redundancy (you need at least two for availability, so about $70,000 a year), plus a share of an engineer's time to run it. The crossover depends on volume, traffic shape (spiky traffic wastes reserved GPUs), and model size, so compute it with your own numbers. Non-cost factors (data that cannot leave your infrastructure, the need to control model versions, latency from co-location) can decide it regardless.

7. A worked "is the AI feature worth it" model

Prompt: Ledgerly, a hypothetical accounting SaaS with 20,000 business customers, wants an AI feature that reads uploaded receipts and invoices and proposes the bookkeeping entry (vendor, amount, tax, category), which the user approves or edits. Today users type entries by hand.

Volume. Customers upload about 60 documents a month each: 1.2 million documents a month.

Cost per document. An image-plus-text request with about 2,500 input tokens (the document image and instructions) and 200 output tokens on Haiku 4.5: $0.0025 + $0.001 = $0.0035. Add $0.0005 for storage and processing: $0.004. Monthly model cost: 1.2M × $0.004 = $4,800.

Value per document. Manual entry takes about 90 seconds; reviewing a correct proposal takes 15 seconds; fixing a wrong one takes 60 seconds. Users' time is not the company's money, so value must come through the business: Ledgerly expects the feature to reduce churn among small businesses who find bookkeeping tedious. Convert time into an assumed value of $0.40 per minute saved for the customer, of which the company captures some share through retention and pricing; to keep the model simple, assume the company captures $0.02 per minute saved.

Accuracy. In a pilot, 80% of proposals are fully correct, 15% need a small edit, 5% are wrong enough to redo.

Time saved per document = 0.80 × (90 − 15) + 0.15 × (90 − 40) + 0.05 × (90 − 60 − 90) seconds. The last term assumes a wrong proposal costs the user 60 seconds to notice and clear, then the full 90 seconds to type, a net loss of 60 seconds. That gives 60 + 7.5 − 3 = 64.5 seconds, about 1.08 minutes.

Captured value per document = 1.08 × $0.02 ≈ $0.0215, against a cost of $0.004: net about $0.0175 per document, about $21,000 a month or $250,000 a year, before engineering, evaluation and support costs. With one engineer-year of build and ongoing maintenance at, say, $200,000 a year all-in, the feature is modestly positive in year one and better in later years.

Sensitivity. The result depends on accuracy through the time saved. If the fully-correct share fell to 50% and the wrong share rose to 20%, time saved would be 0.5 × 75 + 0.3 × 50 + 0.2 × (−60) = 37.5 + 15 − 12 = 40.5 seconds, value $0.0135, net $0.0095 per document: still positive but halved. The model makes the experiment's job clear: measure the correct and wrong shares in production and the churn effect, because those two numbers decide whether the feature pays.

Notice what the model did: it turned an argument about whether AI receipts are "worth it" into two measurable quantities (accuracy mix and value captured per minute saved) and a clear statement of which one is most uncertain. That is the output interviewers want.

8. Classic ML, LLM, or both

Classic ML fits when inputs are structured (numbers, categories, IDs, counts), labelled data is plentiful or cheap to collect, volume is high and latency tight. Fraud scores, ranking, ETA prediction, demand forecasting and churn prediction are classic ML problems, and an LLM in their core path would be slower, costlier and usually less accurate.

LLMs fit when inputs are unstructured language, documents or images; labelled data is scarce; tasks vary widely (a support assistant answering anything); or output must be generated (drafts, summaries, explanations). They also fit early in a product's life, when you do not yet know the task well enough to collect labels.

Hybrids are common and often best. Use an LLM to label a large dataset, check a sample by hand, then train a small classifier that serves at a fraction of the cost and latency (distillation). Use a classic model to decide whether to call the LLM (only summarise threads predicted to be long and important). Use an LLM to extract structured features from text that then feed a classic model. In interviews, proposing a hybrid with a reason ("the LLM labels, the small model serves") shows you are thinking about cost and latency, not just capability.

Total cost of ownership

Token costs are the visible part. The rest of an LLM feature's cost of ownership includes: building and maintaining evaluation sets (they go stale as the product changes); prompt and pipeline changes when a provider deprecates a model version or changes its behaviour; monitoring for quality drift, abuse and cost spikes; safety filtering; human review where outputs are checked; retrieval infrastructure if the feature uses documents; and engineers on call. Sculley and colleagues' "Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems" (NeurIPS 2015) made the classic version of this point for ML: the model code is a small fraction of the system, and the surrounding glue, configuration and monitoring carry ongoing cost. For a small feature, these fixed costs can exceed token costs for years.

Costs move over time, in both directions

The price of a given level of model capability has tended to fall, and teams sometimes justify uneconomic features by assuming it will keep falling. It is safer to design for the current price and treat falling prices as upside. Costs also rise from inside the product: contexts grow as features accumulate (more history, more retrieved documents), heavy users increase their usage, and agents that call the model repeatedly multiply tokens per task. A feature that costs $0.003 per call at launch can cost $0.02 a year later without any price change. Budgets per feature and per user, with alerts, catch this.

Option value and the data flywheel

Sometimes a feature that is marginal today is worth building because it creates data that makes later features possible: user edits to AI proposals become labelled training data, which supports a fine-tuned model that is cheaper and better. That option value is real but easy to overstate. Make it concrete: how many labelled examples will the feature generate per month, what model could they train, and what would that model save? If the answers are vague, treat the flywheel as a bonus, not a justification.

Explainability and regulation

In many jurisdictions, decisions about credit, employment, insurance and housing come with legal duties: explaining adverse decisions, testing for discrimination, keeping records. A documented scorecard or gradient-boosted model with reason codes is easier to explain and audit than an LLM's judgement. The EU AI Act also classifies many such uses as high-risk, with obligations on data, documentation and human oversight (sf11 covers it). The product decision is often to keep the regulated decision in a simple, auditable model and use LLMs around it: extracting information from documents, drafting explanations that a person checks.

The tail of error costs

Average error cost can hide rare, very expensive errors: a chatbot that promises a refund policy that does not exist, a summary that omits a critical medical detail, an agent that deletes data. One such error can cost more than a year of the feature's value, and its probability is hard to estimate from a small pilot. That is why high-stakes features keep humans in the loop, constrain the model's actions (it can propose, not execute), and design the product so the worst case is bounded. Including a rough tail term ("a 1-in-100,000 chance per call of a $50,000 error is $0.50 per call") in the economic model often changes the answer more than any token price.

Opportunity cost of the team

The scarcest resource is usually not money but the team's time. Six engineer-months on a model that adds 7 points of routing accuracy are six months not spent on something else. A good "should we build this?" answer compares the feature's expected net value with the best alternative use of the same people, which is the subject of prioritisation (ps11).

Cascades and routing

Many production features do not use one model for every request. A cascade sends each request to a cheap model first and escalates to a larger one only when the cheap model is unsure or the request is hard; a router picks the model up front from features of the request. If 80% of requests can be handled by a model costing a tenth as much, the blended cost falls to 0.8 × 0.1 + 0.2 × 1 = 0.28 of the all-large-model cost, a 72% saving, as long as the routing decision itself is cheap and the escalation catches the cases that matter. The product judgement is in the escalation rule: route on the cost of being wrong, not only on difficulty, so that high-stakes requests (a refund, a medical question) go to the stronger model or to a person even when they look easy.

The real case: Google's first rules of ML

Google's "Rules of Machine Learning", by Martin Zinkevich, collects lessons from many ML products into 43 rules. The first four are about whether and how to start, and they read like this lesson's summary. Rule 1: don't be afraid to launch a product without machine learning; ML requires data, a heuristic often gets about half the benefit, and if ML is not absolutely required, don't use it until you have data. Rule 2: first, design and implement metrics; track as much as possible in the current system, because it is easier to get permission early and you will want historical data later. Rule 3: choose machine learning over a complex heuristic; a simple heuristic gets a product out the door, but a complex one is unmaintainable, and an ML model is easier to update and maintain once you have data. Rule 4: keep the first model simple and get the infrastructure right; the first model provides the biggest boost, so it need not be fancy, but the pipeline, data and evaluation must be solid.

The rules predate today's LLM APIs, and their logic transfers directly. For an LLM feature, "launch without ML" becomes "measure the template or rule-based version first"; "design metrics first" becomes "log acceptance, edits and errors from day one"; "complex heuristic" becomes "a prompt with forty special-case instructions that nobody dares to edit", at which point fine-tuning or a better-structured system is cheaper to maintain; and "keep the first model simple" becomes "start with a small model and a simple prompt, and invest in evaluation".

Failure modes in practice

Demo-driven launches. A feature is approved on a handful of impressive examples. In production, the distribution of inputs is broader and messier, and quality on the long tail is much worse. Defence: evaluate on a random sample of real inputs before deciding (ev2).

Generate-for-everyone. Outputs are generated eagerly for every item, most of which are never seen. Symptom: cost grows with page views, not with use. Defence: generate on demand, cache, and measure the read rate.

LLM in place of a lookup. A model is asked for facts the company already has in a database (order status, prices), producing occasional confident errors. Defence: retrieve facts from systems of record; use the model for language, not as a database.

Unpriced errors. The business case counts time saved but not the cost of wrong outputs that reach customers. Symptom: complaints and refunds rising after launch. Defence: an explicit error-cost term in the model and a guardrail metric for it.

Vendor lock-in surprises. A provider deprecates a model version; the replacement behaves differently and breaks prompts. Defence: an evaluation suite that runs on any candidate model, and an abstraction layer that makes switching a configuration change.

Cost incidents

LLM features produce a kind of incident that classic software rarely has: a bill that jumps overnight. Common causes are a prompt change that doubled the context (someone added the full conversation history), a retry loop that resends failed requests indefinitely, an agent that calls the model in a cycle, abuse by a few accounts using the feature as a free general-purpose model, and a traffic spike from a product launch. The defences are the same as for any resource: per-feature and per-user budgets with alerts, rate limits, dashboards of tokens per request by feature and version, and a kill switch that degrades the feature gracefully. Treat cost per active user as a guardrail in experiments (ps7), so that a change that quietly doubles it does not ship as a "win".

def cost_per_call(in_tok, out_tok, in_price, out_price, extra=0.0):
    return in_tok * in_price / 1e6 + out_tok * out_price / 1e6 + extra

def time_saved_s(correct, edit, wrong, manual=90, review=15, edit_t=40, clear=60):
    return correct * (manual - review) + edit * (manual - edit_t) + wrong * (-clear)

def net_per_doc(wrong, edit=0.15, capture_per_min=0.02, cost=None):
    correct = 1 - edit - wrong
    value = time_saved_s(correct, edit, wrong) / 60 * capture_per_min
    return value - cost

c = cost_per_call(2500, 200, 1.0, 5.0, extra=0.0005)       # Haiku 4.5 list prices
print(f"cost per document ${c:.4f}")
for w in [0.0, 0.05, 0.2, 0.4, 0.44, 0.6]:
    print(f"wrong {w:4.0%}: net per doc ${net_per_doc(w, cost=c):+.4f}")

docs_per_month = 1_200_000
print(f"monthly net at the pilot (5% wrong): ${net_per_doc(0.05, cost=c) * docs_per_month:,.0f}")

"Would you use an LLM?" drill (10 minutes)

Shelfwise, a hypothetical online bookshop with 3 million monthly visitors, wants to (a) auto-tag 2 million catalogue books with genres and themes, and (b) answer customer questions about orders in a chat widget, about 8,000 conversations a day. For each, say whether you would use rules, classic ML, an LLM or a hybrid, and estimate the cost.

Answer

(a) Tagging the catalogue. A one-off batch job plus a trickle of new books: no latency constraint, and errors are cheap (a slightly wrong tag) and fixable. An LLM is a good fit because descriptions are unstructured and there are few labels. Cost: about 800 input tokens (title, blurb, tag list) and 60 output tokens per book on Haiku 4.5 at batch pricing (half of $1/$5): 800 × $0.5/1M + 60 × $2.5/1M = $0.0004 + $0.00015 = $0.00055 per book, about $1,100 for 2 million books. Hand-check a random 500 for quality; use the results as labels for a small classifier if new books arrive in large volume.

(b) Order questions. Most questions are about order status, delivery dates and returns, facts that live in the order system. Rules and retrieval answer them: identify the order, look up its status, reply from a template. An LLM helps with understanding varied phrasing and writing a natural reply, but it must not invent facts. Design a hybrid: an LLM extracts the intent and order number and writes the reply from data fetched from the order system; policy answers come from approved text; anything else goes to a person. Cost: about 3 turns per conversation at roughly 2,000 input and 150 output tokens each on Haiku 4.5: 3 × ($0.002 + $0.00075) ≈ $0.008 per conversation, about $66 a day. Value: if half of conversations are resolved without an agent and an agent conversation costs $3, the saving is 4,000 × $3 = $12,000 a day. The cost is not the issue; the error guardrail (no invented delivery dates or refund promises) is, which is why the facts come from the system of record.
