Imagine an online shop with 5 million products. A data scientist trains an excellent model: given one shopper and one product, it predicts how likely the shopper is to buy it, using hundreds of signals about both. It is accurate, and on a single server it takes about one millisecond to score one product for one shopper. The product team asks for the obvious thing: when a shopper opens the home page, score every product and show the top 20.

Do the arithmetic. Five million products at one millisecond each is 5,000 seconds, a little over 83 minutes, for one page view. Even spread across 100 servers it is 50 seconds. The shop receives thousands of page views every second. Brute force is not slow; it is impossible by a factor of millions.

The obvious workaround, precomputing everyone's top 20 every night, fails differently. The shopper who just searched for running shoes still sees last night's list of kitchen gadgets. The product that went on sale at noon appears tomorrow. A brand-new product never appears at all, because no one has bought it yet and the nightly job only knows history.

Every large search engine, video feed, music recommender and shop solves this the same way. It does not ask one model to look at everything. It builds a funnel: very cheap methods look at the whole corpus and keep a few thousand plausible items; a moderately expensive model trims those to a few hundred; the expensive, accurate model orders the few hundred; and a final stage arranges the page and enforces the rules the business must keep. YouTube, Instagram, Netflix, Pinterest, Amazon and every web search engine describe some version of this design. It is also the skeleton of almost every machine-learning system design interview at those companies: "design the feed", "design search for this marketplace", "design video recommendations".

This lesson is the map for the rest of the track. You will learn what each stage is for and what it optimises, why recall lost at the top can never be recovered further down, how the stages talk to each other, where offline, nearline and online computation fit, and how to turn "200 milliseconds at 10,000 queries per second" into per-stage deadlines and a server count. Later topics open each box: classical and embedding retrieval (rec2-rec5), ranking models (rec6-rec7), metrics (rec8), features and data (rec9), biases (rec10), exploration (rec11), re-ranking (rec12), experiments (rec13) and LLMs (rec14).

A useful picture is a company hiring for one role with 10,000 applications. Nobody runs a full day of interviews for 10,000 people. A recruiter skims each CV for thirty seconds and keeps 300. A phone screen of 30 minutes keeps 40. A full interview loop of a day keeps 5. A hiring committee then looks at the 5 as a group, checks budget and team balance, and makes offers. Each step spends more effort per candidate on fewer candidates, and each uses information the earlier step could not afford to gather.

A recommender has the same shape, with the same consequences. The cheap step must be generous, because anyone it rejects is gone for good; a brilliant engineer whose CV was skipped never reaches the interview. The expensive step must be accurate, because it decides who is actually chosen. And the final step looks at the group, not at individuals: a committee would not hire five people with exactly the same skills even if each was the top-scoring candidate.

The analogy breaks in one important way. Job candidates apply; items do not. A recommender has to go and find candidates itself, from several different directions at once: "items similar to what this user liked", "items popular in this region today", "items from creators this user follows", "items whose text matches the query". Each direction is a candidate source, and their union is the pool for ranking. A second break: a hiring loop takes weeks, while the whole recommender funnel must finish in a fraction of a second, so the time each stage may take is as carefully planned as its accuracy.

In one sentence per stage. Candidate generation (also called retrieval) uses methods cheap enough to touch the whole corpus, an inverted index for words, a nearest-neighbour search over embeddings, precomputed "people who liked X also liked Y" tables, popularity lists, and returns a few hundred to a few thousand items; its job is recall, getting the good items into the pool. Pre-ranking is an optional small model that cuts thousands to hundreds. Ranking is the expensive model that predicts what the user will do with each item, using everything known about the user, the item and the moment, and orders them; its job is precision at the top. Re-ranking arranges the page as a whole: it spreads out near-duplicates, mixes topics and freshness, reserves slots for new items. Business rules enforce what must be true regardless of scores: the item is legal in this country, not blocked by this user, not already purchased, and the page contains the contracted ads.

Two habits of thought follow from this picture and run through the whole track. First, always ask which stage a change touches and what that stage optimises: a new embedding model is a recall change, a new feature is a precision change, a diversity rule is a page change. Second, remember that stages are coupled. The ranker learns from what users did with pages the old funnel produced, so every stage shapes the training data of every other. Most of the hard problems in this field, from position bias to cold start, come from that coupling.

1. The vocabulary of a request

Before opening the boxes, fix the words. An item is anything that can be shown: a product, a video, a song, a post, a document, a job listing. The corpus is every item that could in principle be shown, from thousands (a small catalogue) to billions (web pages, Pinterest pins). A user is the person the response is for, possibly logged out and known only by a device. A query is text (or an image, or a voice request) the user typed; recommendation has no query. The context is everything about the moment: time, device, location, the page the user is on, the item they are looking at now.

One call from the app to the service is a request, and the service returns a ranked list, often called a slate when it is shown as a page. Each item actually displayed to the user is an impression. Whatever the user then does is engagement: a click, a 40-second watch, a like, a purchase, an "add to cart", a skip after two seconds, a "not interested". Engagement events are joined back to the impressions that caused them, and those pairs become the training data for the models. That loop, show, observe, learn, show again, is why the system shapes its own data, a theme rec10 develops at length.

Two numbers describe load. QPS (queries per second) is how many requests arrive each second; a service with 50 million daily users who each load 10 pages has 500 million requests a day, about 5,800 per second on average and two to three times that at the evening peak. Latency is how long one request takes, and it is always described by percentiles: the p50 (median) is what a typical request sees, the p99 is the time 99% of requests beat. Budgets are set on p99, because a user who opens the app 50 times a day hits the slowest 1% every other day, and because averages hide stalls.

2. Why one model cannot do it: the arithmetic

The funnel exists because of a three-way conflict between corpus size, cost per scored item, and the time and money available per request. Write it as an inequality. If the corpus has N items and a model costs c seconds of compute per item, scoring everything costs N × c per request. With Q requests per second, the fleet must supply N × c × Q seconds of compute every second, that is, that many busy cores.

Plug in the shop: N = 5,000,000, c = 1 ms, Q = 10,000. That is 5,000,000 × 0.001 × 10,000 = 50 million cores. A large data centre has a few hundred thousand. Now try a cheap model, a dot product between a 64-number user vector and a 64-number item vector: 64 multiplications and additions, about 20 nanoseconds with vector instructions. Exhaustive: 5,000,000 × 20 ns = 100 ms per request on one core, 1,000 cores at 10,000 QPS. Feasible, but wasteful; an approximate nearest-neighbour index (lm10) finds most of the top 1,000 by touching only a few thousand vectors, under 5 ms. The cheap model can look at everything; the expensive model cannot.

The trick is that the cheap model and the expensive model do not need to agree on everything. They only need to agree on which items are clearly not worth considering. Of 5 million products, perhaps 4,990,000 are obviously wrong for this shopper right now: wrong category, wrong language, wrong price range, never bought by anyone like them. A cheap method can throw those away with little risk. The expensive model is needed only for the fine distinctions among the remaining few thousand: which of 300 plausible running shoes will this shopper actually buy?

The same arithmetic explains why the ranking stage is limited to hundreds rather than thousands, and why the expensive model's per-item cost is the main lever on the hardware bill. If the ranker costs 0.25 ms of CPU per item, ranking 300 items costs 75 ms of CPU per request; ranking 3,000 costs 750 ms, ten times the servers. The whole design is an exercise in spending compute where it changes the answer.

3. Candidate generation: recall from several directions

The first stage answers "what could possibly be good here?" It is judged by recall: of the items that should end up near the top of the final list, what share made it into the candidate pool? Precision matters little at this stage: a pool of 2,000 in which 1,900 are mediocre is fine, as long as the 20 great ones are in it.

Retrieval must be cheap per item, which forces a structural rule: the item's representation cannot depend on the user. Whatever the method stores about items, a posting list in an inverted index, an embedding vector, a row in a co-occurrence table, must be computed ahead of time, independent of who is asking, so it can be indexed. The user side (the query, the user's embedding, their recent items) is computed per request and used to probe the index. This rule is why retrieval cannot use the rich features the ranker uses, such as "how many of this creator's videos has this user finished": that number depends on both user and item and cannot be indexed for every pair.

No single method finds everything, so real systems run several candidate sources and take the union. Typical sources, each covered later in the track:

  • Lexical retrieval (rec2): an inverted index maps each word to the items that contain it; a query's words pull matching items scored by BM25. Essential for search, product codes and names.
  • Embedding retrieval (rec4): a two-tower model maps users (or queries) and items into the same vector space; an ANN index returns the items closest to the user's vector. Captures broad taste and meaning.
  • Item-to-item co-occurrence (rec3): "items engaged with in the same sessions as the items this user just engaged with". Precomputed tables; strong for "more like this".
  • Graph and social sources: items from followed creators, items liked by friends.
  • Popularity and trending: what is popular in this region, language or category today. Robust fallback for new users.
  • Freshness: recently published items, to give new content a chance (rec11).

Each source returns a scored list, but scores from different sources are on incomparable scales (a BM25 score of 14 and a cosine similarity of 0.62 cannot be compared), so the merge usually works with quotas: each source contributes up to a fixed number of items, the union is de-duplicated, and an item found by several sources is noted (being found twice is itself a useful feature for the ranker). A worked merge for one request: embedding search returns 800, co-occurrence 500, follows 300, trending 200, fresh 200, for 2,000 in total. After de-duplication there are 1,640 distinct items, because 360 were found by two or more sources. After eligibility filters (out of stock, blocked seller, already purchased) 1,410 remain. Those go on to pre-ranking.

Quotas are a product decision dressed as a technical one. Giving the fresh source 200 slots guarantees new items a chance at being ranked, at some cost in average quality. The right quotas come from measurement: for each source, how often do items that only it contributed end up shown and engaged with? A source that contributes many unique, engaged items earns its slots; one whose items are all also found by others, or are never chosen, is costing latency for nothing.

4. Pre-ranking: a cheap model to protect the expensive one

Between 1,400 candidates and a ranker that can afford 300 sits an awkward gap. Truncating each source's list by its own score is crude, because sources' scores are not comparable and none of them used the rich features. So many systems add a pre-ranker (also called a light ranker or first-pass ranker): a small model, often a two-tower network or a small tree ensemble, that uses a subset of the ranker's features and costs perhaps a twentieth as much per item.

The cleanest way to train a pre-ranker is to make it imitate the ranker, a technique called distillation: run the full ranker offline on large candidate sets, record its scores, and train the small model to reproduce the ranker's ordering. A pre-ranker trained this way has one clear job, "keep what the ranker would have chosen", and its quality is directly measurable as the share of the ranker's top items it keeps. Instagram's Explore system, described below, does exactly this in its first pass.

The pre-ranker is optional. With a small corpus or a fast ranker, retrieval can hand 500 items straight to ranking. It earns its place when the candidate pool is large relative to what the ranker can afford.

5. Ranking: precision with everything you know

The ranker answers "of these few hundred, which will this user value most, and in what order?" It can use the features retrieval could not: cross features that depend on user and item together (the user's past engagement with this creator, the similarity between this item's topic and the user's last session), fresh item statistics (this video's click rate in the last hour), and context (device, time, network). rec6 covers the models, from logistic regression and gradient-boosted trees to deep networks with embedding tables; rec9 covers where the features come from.

A modern ranker rarely predicts just one thing. It has several heads, outputs that each predict one outcome: probability of click, expected watch time, probability of like, of share, of "not interested". A single click head would learn to love clickbait. The heads are combined by a value model, a formula such as score = E[watch seconds] + 15 × P(like) + 30 × P(share) − 50 × P(hide), whose weights encode what the product wants and are tuned with experiments (rec13). For the formula to make sense, each head must be calibrated: a predicted 0.1 must mean the event happens about 10% of the time (ml15).

The ranker's output is a score per item and an order. Its quality is measured with ranking metrics, such as nDCG and MRR (rec8), on held-out logged data, and ultimately with online experiments.

6. Re-ranking: from a list of items to a good page

The ranker scores items one at a time, as if each were the only item shown. But a page is a set. If the top ten scores are ten videos from the same creator, or five near-identical news clips about one story, the page is worse than a slightly lower-scoring but varied page, because the user's interest in the fifth clip about the same story is much lower than the model, which never saw the other four, predicted.

Re-ranking fixes this by building the page greedily: at each slot it picks the item with the best score after a penalty for similarity to items already placed. It also handles freshness boosts, exploration slots (a position reserved for a new or uncertain item, rec11), spacing (no two items from one seller adjacent), and blending of different content types such as ads, videos and posts. rec12 covers the algorithms, maximal marginal relevance (MMR) among them, and the multi-objective trade-offs.

7. Business rules and filters: what must always hold

Some constraints are not preferences to be traded off but rules: the item must be licensed in the user's country; the user blocked this seller; the user already bought this washing machine and does not need another; the page must contain the two ad slots the sales team sold; content flagged by the integrity team must not appear for teenage accounts. A model trained on engagement can learn none of these reliably, and some it must never be allowed to trade away for engagement.

Where a filter goes is an engineering decision with a clear rule: apply a filter as early as it can be applied exactly and cheaply, and check again at the end as a guarantee. If 30% of the catalogue is unavailable in a country and retrieval ignores that, 30% of the candidate budget is wasted on items that will be thrown away, and on some requests the page comes back short. So regional availability is pushed into retrieval (separate indexes per region, or a filter applied during the index search, which db15 discusses for vector indexes). Filters that need per-user data the index cannot hold, such as "already purchased", are applied after retrieval, with over-fetching to compensate: if a filter removes 20% on average, fetch 25% more. And a last check before the response catches anything that changed between the index build and now, such as an item taken down five minutes ago.

8. Losses multiply: recall through the funnel

The most important quantitative fact about a funnel is that each stage's mistakes are permanent for later stages. Measure each stage against the same reference: the items the final ranker would place in its top 10 if it could score the entire eligible corpus (or, practically, a very large candidate set on a sample of requests). Call that set the ideal top 10.

A worked example for one surface:

StageKeepsRecall of ideal top 10 at this stageCumulative survivors
Candidate generation (2,000)80% of ideal items0.808.0 of 10
Eligibility filters98% (wrongly drops 2%)0.987.8
Pre-ranking (to 300)90% of what reaches it0.907.1
Rankingorders what it gets—7.1 ideal items available

So 0.80 × 0.98 × 0.90 ≈ 0.71: only about 7 of the 10 items the ranker would most like to show ever reach it. The other three slots are filled with its best alternatives, which are worse by the ranker's own judgement. This has three consequences. First, ranker improvements are capped by what retrieval supplies; a team that only works on the ranker will see diminishing returns. Second, the biggest loss is usually at the top, so recall work on retrieval is often the best investment. Third, stages must be evaluated against the downstream reference, not by their own metrics: a pre-ranker with a lovely offline AUC (area under the ROC curve, ml7) that disagrees with the ranker on which items are best is a bad pre-ranker.

The reference set is measured with a shadow or sampled-expansion job: for 0.1% of requests, retrieve 10,000 candidates instead of 2,000, run the full ranker on all of them offline (not in the request path), and record its top 10. Then check which stages would have kept each. This is how you find out that the fresh source contributes 12% of ideal items, or that a filter is broken.

9. The latency budget: 200 ms at 10,000 QPS, worked

Suppose the product requirement is: the home feed must return within 200 ms at the 99th percentile, measured at the service, at a peak of 10,000 requests per second. (Time on the public internet to the phone is a separate budget owned by the client and edge teams.) Here is how to turn that into stage deadlines and hardware.

Step 1: draw the dependency graph. Some work depends on other work; some does not. Fetching the user's features and running retrieval sources can start immediately and in parallel (the embedding source needs the user vector, which needs the user features, so it starts slightly later). Merging needs all sources. Fetching candidate features needs the merged list. Pre-ranking needs features; ranking needs pre-ranking; re-ranking needs scores; hydration (fetching titles, thumbnails, prices for the final items) needs the final list. That gives a critical path: request handling → user features → embedding retrieval → merge and filter → candidate features → pre-rank → rank → re-rank and rules → hydration → response.

Step 2: assign per-stage budgets. A plausible allocation, each number a p99 target for that stage:

StageBudget (ms)Notes
Parse request, auth, routing8in-data-centre network included
User features10one key-value read, about 4 KB
Retrieval (sources in parallel)40 from start (30 for the ANN after the user vector)hard deadline; late sources dropped
Merge, de-duplicate, filter5in memory
Candidate features (1,400 items)15local cache for hot items plus one batched read

Rank 30045batched model inference

Critical path≈ 153leaves ≈ 47 ms slack

Why leave 47 ms unused? Because the p99 of a sum is not the sum of p99s in a simple way: the slow cases of different stages are usually independent, so adding p99s overstates the typical slow request, but queueing delays under load, garbage-collection pauses and the occasional retry are correlated across stages and appear exactly at peak. Slack keeps a small regression in one stage from breaking the target. Teams usually hold it centrally and grant it to a stage only with evidence.

Step 3: compute concurrency and capacity. Little's law says the average number of requests in flight equals arrival rate times average time in the system: 10,000 per second × 0.15 s (typical, not p99) = 1,500 concurrent requests, which sizes thread pools and connection pools. Hardware comes from CPU time, not wall time. Suppose ranking costs 0.15 ms of CPU per item (a mid-sized network, batched); 300 items is 45 ms of CPU per request. At 10,000 QPS: 10,000 × 0.045 = 450 cores busy continuously. Servers are run at roughly 50-60% utilisation so that queueing delay stays small and a peak or a lost machine does not tip them over, so plan about 450 ÷ 0.55 ≈ 820 cores, roughly 26 machines of 32 cores, plus spare capacity to survive losing a zone. Pre-ranking: 1,400 × 7 µs ≈ 10 ms of CPU per request, 100 busy cores. Retrieval: an ANN search of about 3 ms of CPU, 30 busy cores; tiny by comparison. The ranker dominates, which is why "how many items to rank" and "how big a model" are the two biggest cost decisions.

Step 4: deadlines and fallbacks. Every stage needs a rule for when it is late. Retrieval sources that miss the 40 ms deadline are dropped for this request. If candidate features are missing, use the defaults the model saw in training for missing values and mark the request. If the ranker misses its deadline, serve the pre-ranker's order. If everything fails, return a cached, non-personalised popular list for the user's region. The page might be a little worse; it is never late and never empty.

10. Fan-out and the tail

One more piece of arithmetic changes designs. When a request fans out to many servers in parallel, for example an index split into 40 shards (pieces, each holding part of the corpus), the response must wait for the slowest shard. If each shard independently exceeds its own p99 on 1% of calls, the chance that at least one of 40 does is 1 − 0.9940 ≈ 0.33. A third of requests are slowed by some shard's tail. With 10 shards it is 1 − 0.9910 ≈ 0.096; with 50, about 0.39. In other words, the shard's rare case becomes the request's common case. Dean and Barroso's "The Tail at Scale" made this widely known.

Three practical responses follow. Prefer replication over sharding when the index fits in one machine's memory: each request then touches one server, not 40. Use hedged requests: if a shard has not answered by its p95, send the same request to another replica and take whichever answers first, which costs about 5% extra load and removes most of the tail. And allow partial results: if one shard of 40 is late, return the other 39 shards' candidates; retrieval is about recall, and losing 2.5% of the pool for one request rarely matters.

11. Offline, nearline and online

Not everything happens inside the request. Netflix's 2013 description of its architecture introduced a useful three-way split that most teams now use. Offline computation runs on a schedule over large data with no latency pressure: training models, computing embeddings for every item, building the ANN index and inverted index, aggregating 90-day user statistics. Nearline computation reacts to events within seconds or minutes but is not part of any request: a streaming job updates "items this user engaged with in the last 30 minutes" after each click, or embeds a newly uploaded video and inserts it into the index. Online computation is the request path: reading features, probing indexes, running models, building the page.

The design rule: keep the online path to lookups and model scoring; push every computation to the slowest lane that still meets its freshness need. A user's 90-day favourite genres can be offline; their last three clicks must be nearline; the score of this item for this request must be online. Getting this wrong costs either freshness (the shopper who just looked at shoes still sees gadgets) or latency and money (recomputing 90-day aggregates on every request).

12. Search, feeds and recommendations: same skeleton, different emphasis

Search adds a query-understanding step at the front: spelling correction, tokenisation, detecting that "apple" means the fruit on a grocery site and the company on an electronics site, expanding "tv" to "television" (rec5). The query is by far the strongest signal, so lexical and semantic match to the query dominate retrieval, and personalisation is usually a ranking feature rather than the main driver. Users judge search harshly on the first few results, and some queries have one right answer (a product code, an exact title).

Recommendation on an item page ("more like this") starts from the item being viewed: item-to-item sources dominate. Home feeds start from the user and the moment: recency, freshness and the last few minutes of behaviour matter a lot, the page is long and scrolled, and the slate's variety matters more. Notifications and emails are recommendations with a much higher cost of a bad item (the user may turn them off) and a decision of whether to send anything at all.

In an interview, naming the surface and what is different about it, before drawing boxes, is a strong signal: it shows you know which stage will need the most care.

Stage misalignment and the selection problem

Every stage after the first is trained on data the earlier stages selected. The ranker is trained on impressions, which are items that survived retrieval, pre-ranking and the previous ranker. It has never seen a label for an item that retrieval never surfaces, so it has no idea how it would rank them; this is a form of sample selection bias. In the other direction, retrieval is often trained on engagement with shown items, which the ranker chose, so retrieval learns to imitate the ranker's past taste rather than to find good items the ranker has not yet been offered. Two practical mitigations: train retrieval on engagement from all surfaces (YouTube's 2016 paper trained on watches from everywhere, including embedded players, not only on its own recommendations), and add random or exploratory candidates in a small share of traffic so the ranker sees labels outside the current funnel's comfort zone.

Distillation makes stages agree

A pre-ranker trained on raw engagement labels and a ranker trained on the same labels will disagree, because they see different features. Disagreement wastes the ranker: the pre-ranker drops items the ranker would have loved. Distilling the pre-ranker from the ranker, training it on the ranker's scores or pairwise preferences over large candidate sets, aligns it with the stage it serves, and makes its quality directly measurable as recall of the ranker's top-k. The cost is a training pipeline that must run the full ranker over many more items than are served, which is cheap offline relative to the gain. The same idea is sometimes applied to retrieval, with care: a retriever that only imitates the ranker inherits the ranker's blind spots.

Spending the budget: more candidates or a bigger model?

With a fixed compute budget per request, you can rank more candidates with a smaller model or fewer candidates with a bigger one. The answer depends on where the loss is. If funnel recall against a large-set reference is already 95%, more candidates add little and a better model wins. If recall is 70%, a better model is polishing an incomplete pool. Measure both curves (quality versus candidate count, quality versus model size) on a sample before arguing. Many teams find the knee of the candidate curve between 500 and 2,000 for ranking, but that is a property of their retrieval, not a law.

Precomputed recommendations versus real-time

Some surfaces can be computed ahead of time per user: a weekly playlist, a daily email, a "because you watched X" row refreshed nightly. Precomputation turns online cost into offline cost and removes latency risk, but freezes the result. A common hybrid precomputes a large candidate list per user offline (hundreds of items), then re-ranks it online with fresh context and recent behaviour. The trade-off is freshness: a precomputed list cannot react to the last five minutes, which on a feed is exactly what users notice.

The p99 of a pipeline is not the sum of p99s

If two sequential stages each have p99 = 20 ms with independent slow cases, the pipeline's p99 is less than 40 ms, because both being slow at once is rare (about 1 in 10,000 for both above their p99). Budgeting by adding p99s is therefore conservative for independent noise. But the causes of tail latency in practice are often shared: a host under CPU pressure slows every stage running on it; a garbage-collection pause stalls the whole process; a burst of traffic queues everything. These correlated causes make the sum of p99s less conservative than it looks, which is the real reason for central slack. Measure the end-to-end distribution directly, and trace slow requests to see which stage was slow.

Replicas, shards and where the index lives

An embedding index for 20 million items at 128 dimensions in 8-bit integers is about 2.6 GB plus a graph of similar size: it fits in memory on one machine, so replicate it and send each request to one replica. A web-scale inverted index or a billion-item embedding index does not fit, so it is sharded by document, and every query fans out to every shard. Sharding by document means every shard returns its own top-k and a coordinator merges them; it keeps shards independent but multiplies fan-out. Sharding by term (each shard holds some words' posting lists) reduces fan-out per query but makes multi-term queries cross shards. Search engines nearly always shard by document, for load balance and simplicity, and fight the tail with replicas, hedging and partial results.

Degraded modes are part of the design

A recommender almost never fails completely; it degrades. A feature store returning defaults, a retrieval source timing out, an index a day stale: each produces a worse page that looks normal. Three practices separate mature systems. Every fallback is counted per request and graphed. Requests served in a degraded mode are marked in the impression logs so the next model is not trained on degraded pages as if they were normal. And degraded modes are exercised on purpose in tests, so the first time the "ranker down, serve pre-ranker order" path runs is not during an outage.

The public case: Instagram Explore's funnel

In November 2019 Meta's AI blog described the system behind Instagram Explore, the grid of photos and videos from accounts a user does not follow. It is a clean real example of every stage in this lesson. Candidate generation starts from accounts the user has interacted with, finds similar accounts using account embeddings (they call the embedding approach ig2vec, trained on sequences of accounts users interact with in a session, like words in a sentence), and pulls recent media from those accounts. Filters then remove ineligible content, including policy-violating material and spam.

Ranking happens in three passes. The first pass is "the distillation model", which "mimics the combination of the other two stages, with minimal features" and "picks the 150 highest-quality and most relevant candidates out of 500". The second pass is a lightweight neural network with the full set of dense features, picking 50. The final pass is a deep neural network with dense and sparse features that picks the 25 for the first page of the Explore grid. The post reports that the system extracts 65 billion features and makes 90 million model predictions every second. The predictions are combined with a value formula of the form wlike × P(like) + wsave × P(save) − wnegative × P(negative action), with saves able to count for more than likes, and a final step adds diversity: posts from the same author or the same seed account are down-ranked with a penalty factor so one person does not fill the grid.

Every idea in this lesson appears: multiple candidate sources, eligibility filtering early, a distilled cheap stage that imitates the expensive ones, cost per item rising as counts fall, multi-outcome value scoring and page-level rules at the end.

How funnels fail in practice

A source silently stops contributing. A schema change makes the co-occurrence source return empty lists for half of users. Latency improves slightly, nothing errors, and engagement drops 1-2% over a week, attributed to "seasonality". The defence is per-source dashboards: candidates returned, unique contribution, and share of shown and engaged items per source, with alerts on sudden changes.

A deadline set too tight. The embedding source's p99 creeps from 25 to 42 ms after an index grows; with a 40 ms deadline it is dropped on 3% of requests, which are disproportionately the heaviest users with the largest histories. The effect shows up as worse engagement among power users. Track drop rate per source, not just latency.

Training on degraded traffic. During a feature-store incident, a third of requests used default features for six hours. The impression logs record the defaults, the nightly training run learns that "default value" correlates with low engagement, and the next model performs worse on new users, who legitimately have default values. Marking degraded requests and excluding or down-weighting them in training prevents this.

Filter after retrieval with no over-fetch. A new regional licensing rule removes 40% of a catalogue in one country. Retrieval still returns 500 candidates, 200 are removed late, and the ranker sees a thin pool. Pages in that country become repetitive. Over-fetching, or moving the filter into the index, fixes it.

What to monitor

For each stage: p50, p99 and p99.9 latency; timeout and fallback rates; items in and items out. For each candidate source: candidates returned, unique contribution after de-duplication, share of final impressions and of engaged impressions. For the funnel as a whole: recall of a large-set reference, measured on a sampled shadow job daily. For the page: empty or short pages, duplicate rate, number of distinct creators or categories per page. For capacity: CPU utilisation of ranking hosts at peak, queue depth, and the cost per thousand requests. A dashboard with these lets an on-call engineer answer "is this a retrieval, ranking or page problem?" in minutes.

Knobs people actually turn

Candidate counts per source and in total; the pre-ranker's cut-off; the ranker's model size and batch size; per-stage deadlines; the over-fetch factor for late filters; hedging thresholds for sharded retrieval; cache lifetimes for hot item features. Each knob trades quality for cost or latency, and each change should be an experiment with its quality and cost measured together.

Goal: see why funnels work, how recall multiplies, and how fan-out creates tail latency, by simulation. Python 3.12, standard library only (random, statistics).

  1. Create rec1_lab.py. Build a corpus of 100,000 items, each with a hidden "true value" v ~ Normal(0, 1). The ideal page is the 10 items with the highest v.
  2. Model three scorers of increasing accuracy and cost: retrieval sees v + Normal(0, 1.0) at cost 1 unit per item; pre-ranking sees v + Normal(0, 0.5) at 20 units; ranking sees v + Normal(0, 0.2) at 400 units. (Draw each stage's noise once per item.)
  3. Run the funnel: retrieval scores all items and keeps the top R; pre-ranking keeps the top 300 of those; ranking picks the top 10. Print for R in 500, 1,000, 2,000, 5,000, 20,000: how many of the ideal 10 reached each stage, and the total cost. Average over 20 random seeds.
  4. Compare with "rank everything": ranking alone on all 100,000 items. How close does the funnel get, and at what fraction of the cost?
  5. Fan-out: model one shard's latency as 5 ms plus an exponential with mean 2 ms, and with probability 1% add 30 ms (a pause). For 1, 10 and 40 shards per request, simulate 100,000 requests where request latency is the maximum over its shards. Print p50 and p99 for each.
  6. Add hedging: if a shard has not answered by 12 ms, send a second copy with fresh randomness and take the faster. Recompute p99 for 40 shards and the extra load.

What to look for. In step 3, the number of ideal items reaching ranking should climb steeply from small R and then flatten: that knee is the candidate count you would choose. The final page should keep most, but not all, of the ideal items, and the loss should be mostly at retrieval. In step 4 the funnel should cost a few percent of brute force while giving up a little quality. In step 5, the p99 for 40 shards should be dominated by the 30 ms pause even though each shard pauses only 1% of the time, and even the p50 should rise noticeably with more shards. Step 6 should cut the 40-shard p99 sharply for a modest increase in requests sent.

Design: the funnel and budget for marketplace search

A second-hand marketplace has 40 million active listings in 12 countries, 25% of listings are only visible in their own country, and search traffic peaks at 8,000 QPS. Product wants results in 250 ms p99 at the service, personalised by the user's past views, with sold listings never shown. The current system runs BM25 retrieval of 1,000 listings, ranks them all with a gradient-boosted tree model (0.08 ms CPU per listing), and applies "sold" and "country" filters at the end. Users complain that searches for a specific phone model in small countries show few results, and that the system is expensive. Sketch the funnel, budget and fixes.

Answer

Diagnose first. Filtering country and sold status at the end means the 1,000 retrieved listings include many invisible ones. In a small country, a query like "iPhone 13 128GB" may retrieve 1,000 listings of which most are in other countries, leaving a thin page: that is the complaint. Cost: ranking 1,000 listings at 0.08 ms is 80 ms of CPU per request; at 8,000 QPS that is 640 busy cores, about 1,150 at 55% utilisation, mostly spent scoring listings that are then thrown away.

Funnel. Query understanding (spelling, model-number normalisation, category intent) → retrieval from two sources in parallel: BM25 on title and description, and an embedding source for paraphrases ("cheap iphone" matching "iPhone 11 good condition"), each with the country and status filter applied inside the index (a per-country index partition, or a filter on the posting lists and ANN search) → merge to about 1,200 → pre-rank with a small distilled model to 300 → gradient-boosted tree (GBDT) ranker with personalisation features on 300 → re-rank for seller diversity (no more than two listings per seller in the top 20) and freshness → final check that nothing sold in the last minutes.

Budget at p99. Parse and query understanding 15 ms; retrieval 40 ms deadline (both sources in parallel); merge 5; features 20; pre-rank 10; rank 300 × 0.08 = 24 ms of CPU, about 30 ms with feature transforms; re-rank 10; sold-check and hydrate 25. Critical path about 155 ms, leaving about 95 ms slack against 250 ms, which is healthy for a sharded search index with fan-out.

Capacity. Ranking now costs 24 ms of CPU per request: 8,000 × 0.024 = 192 busy cores, about 350 provisioned, a third of before. Pre-ranking at roughly 5 µs × 1,200 = 6 ms per request adds 48 busy cores. The index of 40 million listings is likely sharded; keep shard count modest and hedge requests after the shard p95.

Check it worked. Measure, for small countries, results-per-query and zero-result rate before and after; measure funnel recall against a sampled job that ranks 5,000 country-filtered candidates; run an A/B test on search conversion (purchases or messages to sellers per search).
