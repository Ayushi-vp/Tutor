A news app ranks articles by a click model. Every morning, the top article on the home screen gets 10% of visitors to click it; the article in fifth place gets 2%. The team trains tomorrow's model on today's clicks, and tomorrow's model, unsurprisingly, learns that the articles that were at the top are the good ones. It puts them, or ones like them, at the top again. The clicks confirm it. After a few months, an editor notices that a certain kind of article, long investigative pieces, has almost disappeared from the top three, even though readers who open them spend twelve minutes reading. They were never placed high enough to be clicked enough to be placed high.

That is the central problem of this lesson: a recommender learns from data that it produced itself. What users clicked depends on what was shown, where it was shown and to whom. So the data contains the system's own past decisions mixed in with users' real preferences, and a model trained naively on it learns both, unable to tell them apart. This produces several named biases. Position bias: things higher on the page get more attention. Selection or exposure bias: things never shown have no data at all. Popularity bias: already-popular items get more exposure and so look even more popular. Together they form feedback loops that can entrench the old system, starve new and niche items, and, some worry, narrow what users see.

The good news is that the tools to separate preferences from the system's own influence are well understood, borrowed from causal inference and statistics. If you know, or can estimate, the probability with which the system showed each item at each position, you can reweight the data so that it behaves like data from a randomised experiment. That idea, inverse propensity weighting, powers unbiased learning to rank and counterfactual evaluation: estimating offline how a new ranker would have performed, using logs from the old one.

This lesson covers each bias with its mechanism, works inverse propensity weighting with real position click-through rates, shows how propensities are measured with randomised swaps, follows Joachims and colleagues' unbiased learning to rank from theory to a live search engine, computes counterfactual estimates (IPS and self-normalised IPS) by hand on six logged events, and ends with popularity bias, feedback loops and the honest state of the evidence on filter bubbles. Exploration, which generates the data these corrections need, is rec11's subject.

Imagine judging which restaurants in a city are best by counting how many diners each has. The restaurants on the main square are always full; the ones down side streets are half empty. Does that mean the square's restaurants are better? Partly, perhaps, but mostly they are easier to see. Tourists walk past them first. If you wanted to know which restaurants diners actually prefer, you would compare how many people choose a restaurant given that they walked past it. A side-street restaurant that only 100 people pass and 30 of them enter is more appealing than a square restaurant that 2,000 pass and 300 enter.

A recommender's page is a city where the top positions are the main square. Clicks per impression are like diners per restaurant: they mix appeal with visibility. The examination hypothesis makes the split precise: a user clicks an item only if they look at it (examine it) and like what they see, so

P(click) = P(examined | position) × P(clicked | examined, item)

The first factor is about the page; the second is about the item. To learn about items, divide out the first factor. That is inverse propensity weighting: count each click as worth 1 / P(examined) clicks, so a click on position 6, where only 20% of users look, counts five times as much as one on position 1, where everyone looks.

The analogy breaks, usefully, in two places. In a city you can count passers-by; on a page you cannot directly observe whether a user examined position 4. Examination probabilities must be estimated, usually by running small randomised experiments that move items between positions. And a city's restaurants do not move; a recommender rearranges the page for every request based on its beliefs, which is why its data is so entangled with those beliefs. Breaking the entanglement requires the system to deliberately add some randomness and record the probabilities, the theme that connects this lesson to exploration in rec11.

Why not simply run an A/B test for every question? Because experiments are slow and scarce: each one takes weeks of traffic and answers one question about one change. Debiasing logged data lets a team learn from all the traffic it already serves, and counterfactual evaluation lets it screen dozens of candidate models offline, sending only the promising ones to the expensive online test. The two are complements: randomised experiments give unbiased answers to few questions; propensity-corrected logs give approximately unbiased answers to many.

One sentence summary to carry through the lesson: logged data tells you what happened under the old policy; to learn what would happen under a new one, you need to know how likely the old policy was to produce each thing you observed. That likelihood is the propensity, and almost every technique below is a way to estimate it or use it.

1. The loop: why recommender data is not like other data

Most supervised learning assumes training examples are drawn independently from the same distribution as future data. Recommender data violates this in a specific way. The system chooses which items to show (the action), in what order, to which users (the context), and only then observes outcomes (the reward: click, watch, purchase). The probability distribution over actions given context is the logging policy. Every logged example is therefore (context, action chosen by the logging policy, reward), and the reward is only observed for the action that was taken.

This is the structure of a bandit problem (rec11 builds on it): you see the outcome of the arm you pulled, never of the arms you did not. Three consequences follow. Outcomes depend on presentation (position, layout), not only on the item. The distribution of items in the data is set by the logging policy, not by users. And the next model is trained on data from this one, so its errors and preferences propagate. Each named bias below is one of these consequences.

2. Position bias and the examination model

Position bias is the best measured of the biases. Joachims and colleagues' 2005 eye-tracking and click studies of web search found that users scan results largely from the top down, look at the first one or two results far more than lower ones, and click the top result much more often even when the order of the top two results is secretly swapped. The click on the top result is partly a vote for relevance and partly a reflection of trust in the ranking and of attention.

The examination model (also called the position-based model) formalises it. For an item i shown at position k:

P(click | i, k) = p_k × r_i

where pk = P(examined | position k) is the position propensity, the same for every item, and ri = P(click | examined, item i) is the item's relevance-driven click probability. Propensities are usually expressed relative to position 1 (p1 = 1). The model is a simplification: in reality examination also depends on the items above (a great result at position 1 means fewer people look further, which the "cascade" model captures), on layout (grids, carousels, autoplay) and on device. But the position-based model is the workhorse for correction, and it is often good enough.

Worked example. Use illustrative propensities p = (1.0, 0.6, 0.4, 0.3, 0.25, 0.2) for positions 1-6. Item A was shown 10,000 times at position 1 and clicked 800 times: raw click rate 8%. Item B was shown 10,000 times at position 4 and clicked 300 times: raw 3%. A naive model concludes A is far better. Under the examination model, A's relevance-driven rate is 800 / (10,000 × 1.0) = 8%, and B's is 300 / (10,000 × 0.3) = 10%. B is the more appealing item; it was handicapped by being shown where fewer people look. Equivalently, each of B's clicks is worth 1/0.3 = 3.33 "examined-and-clicked" events, and each of A's is worth 1.

This is inverse propensity weighting (IPW). In training, every click (or every example) is weighted by 1/pk for the position k it was shown at. In expectation, the weighted click count for an item equals the number of clicks it would have received if every impression had been examined, which is proportional to ri, its relevance.

The cost: variance. B's estimate rests effectively on 10,000 × 0.3 = 3,000 examinations, not 10,000, so it is noisier. At position 20 with a propensity of 0.1, each click gets weight 10, and a handful of lucky clicks can swing an item's estimate. Practical systems clip weights at a maximum (for example 1/p ≤ 10), which introduces a little bias but bounds the variance.

3. Estimating propensities with randomised interventions

We cannot see examination, so propensities are estimated from experiments in which an item's relevance is held fixed and only its position changes. The cleanest is the swap intervention. On a small share of traffic, take the ranker's top item and swap it with the item at a random position r. Because the same item (with the same relevance) now appears at r instead of 1, the ratio of its click rate at r to its click rate at 1 estimates pr/p1: relevance cancels out. Average over many queries and items.

Joachims, Swaminathan and Schnabel did exactly this for their live experiment: for 7 days, before presenting each ranking, they swapped the top-ranked document with the document at a uniformly random rank j from 1 to 21, and used the ratio of click rates to estimate pj/p1, smoothing the noisy estimates by interpolating with overall click rates by position. The resulting propensities decayed with rank, with the smallest around 0.12.

Swapping costs some user experience (a worse result at the top for some users), so it runs on a small share of traffic for a limited time. Alternatives reduce the cost. RandPair-style interventions swap only adjacent or nearby pairs, a smaller harm. Intervention harvesting uses naturally occurring variation: the same item appears at different positions for the same query across different rankers (A/B test arms) or days, which acts like a free randomisation when the variation is unrelated to the item's relevance. Click models fit examination and relevance parameters jointly to logs with repeated queries. And neural approaches learn a position tower jointly with the ranker: YouTube's multi-task ranker (rec6) added a shallow tower fed with position and device features whose output is added to the main logit during training, then dropped at serving, so the main tower is trained as if positions were equal.

4. Unbiased learning to rank

Combine the pieces and you can train a ranker on clicks without inheriting position bias. Joachims and colleagues (WSDM 2017) set this up as counterfactual empirical risk minimisation. Imagine the loss you would compute if you knew true relevance: for each query, a rank-based loss summed over the relevant documents. With clicks you only see some relevant documents, those that were examined. But if each clicked document's contribution is divided by the propensity of the position where it was shown, the expected value of this propensity-weighted loss equals the loss with full relevance information. Minimising it learns a ranker for relevance, not for the old presentation order, without needing queries to repeat (unlike click models) and without randomising every ranking.

They implemented it as a propensity-weighted ranking SVM (a pairwise linear ranker, close in spirit to RankNet in rec7, with the pairwise loss weighted by 1/p of the clicked document's position). On synthetic click data generated from the Yahoo! LTR dataset, it recovered most of the quality of training on true labels, and degraded gracefully when propensities were misspecified. On a live search engine for scientific articles, trained on 21 days of clicks under the hand-tuned production ranker with propensities from the 7-day swap experiment, it was compared using interleaving (rec13): against production it won on 87 queries, lost on 48 and tied on 83; against a naive click-trained ranking SVM it won 95, lost 60, tied 102. Both differences were statistically significant.

The same correction applies to any learning-to-rank loss: LambdaMART (rec7) with propensity-weighted lambdas, neural rankers with weighted softmax losses. XGBoost's learning-to-rank tutorial, for instance, includes position-debiasing options for click data.

5. Selection bias: what was never shown

Position bias concerns how items were shown; selection bias (or exposure bias) concerns whether they were shown at all. Logged data contains outcomes only for items the logging policy chose. An item the old ranker scored low is shown rarely, collects few interactions, and remains scored low. The data is missing not at random (MNAR): whether an outcome is observed depends on what the system believed about it, which is correlated with the outcome itself.

The same structure appears in explicit ratings: people rate films they chose to watch, which they expected to like, so observed ratings skew high (rec3). Schnabel and colleagues' 2016 paper "Recommendations as Treatments" treated recommendation exactly like a medical study with non-random treatment assignment: each rating's propensity is the probability that the user rated (was "treated" with) that item, estimated from data, and training and evaluation are reweighted by 1/propensity so that the observed ratings stand in for the full matrix.

Exposure propensities in a recommender are the probabilities that each item was shown to the user, which depend on the whole funnel (retrieval, ranking, re-ranking, rules). They are only known if the system has a stochastic component and logs its probabilities, which a deterministic top-k ranker does not. That is the fundamental reason mature systems add controlled randomness (exploration slots, randomised tie-breaking, small perturbations) and log the probabilities: without them, the propensity of every unshown item is zero and nothing can be learned about it from logs.

6. Counterfactual evaluation: estimating a new policy from old logs

The same reweighting answers a different question: how would a new ranker have performed, had it been serving? rec8 explained why ordinary offline metrics on logged data are biased towards the logging policy. Counterfactual (or off-policy) evaluation fixes this when the logs record propensities.

Simplify to one slot: in each logged event, the logging policy π0 showed one item a from some candidates in context x, with probability π0(a | x), and observed reward r (1 for click, 0 otherwise). A new, deterministic policy π would choose π(x). The inverse propensity scoring (IPS) estimate of π's average reward is:

IPS = (1/n) Σ over events:  r × 1[π(x) = a] / π0(a | x)

Events where the new policy agrees with what was logged count, weighted by how unlikely the logging policy was to take that action; disagreeing events count zero. In expectation over the logging policy's randomness, this equals the new policy's true average reward, provided every action the new policy takes had a non-zero probability under the old one.

The logging policy's own average reward: 4 clicks / 6 events = 0.67. IPS for the new policy: (2 + 0 + 0 + 5 + 3.33 + 0) / 6 = 10.33 / 6 = 1.72. That is an impossible click rate: one event with a small propensity (event 4, weight 5) dominates. This is IPS's well-known weakness: unbiased on average, with very high variance on small samples.

Self-normalised IPS (SNIPS) divides by the sum of the weights of matching events instead of the number of events: 10.33 / (2 + 2 + 5 + 3.33) = 10.33 / 12.33 = 0.84. It is slightly biased but always lies within the range of rewards and has much lower variance. Clipping caps weights (say at 4), which would turn event 4's weight from 5 to 4. Doubly robust estimators add a reward model's prediction for every event and use IPS only to correct the model's error, accurate if either the model or the propensities are good. The effective sample size, (Σw)² / Σw², here 12.33² / (4 + 4 + 25 + 11.1) = 152 / 44.1 ≈ 3.4, says these six events carry the information of about three unweighted ones; always report it.

Bottou and colleagues' 2013 paper on computational advertising at Microsoft showed this machinery working at production scale: by randomising some ad-placement decisions slightly and logging probabilities, they could estimate offline, with confidence intervals, the effect of changes to the ad system that had never been deployed.

For full rankings, the action space is enormous (every permutation of items), so exact matching almost never happens. Practical estimators work per position or per item, using the position-based model (an item's probability of being shown at each position under the old and new policies) or assume the reward decomposes across slots. These estimators are an active research area; in production they serve as a filter before online tests, alongside interleaving.

7. Popularity bias and the rich-get-richer loop

Popularity bias combines the previous mechanisms over time. Popular items are retrieved by more sources and ranked higher, so they get more impressions; more impressions mean more clicks and more training data; more data means more confident and usually higher scores; so they are shown even more. Meanwhile the long tail and new items get few impressions, so their estimates stay uncertain and usually low. Some of an item's popularity reflects genuine broad appeal, and some reflects accumulated exposure; naive training cannot tell the two apart.

Several earlier lessons met specific channels of this bias: in-batch negatives in retrieval (rec4), where the logQ correction compensates; inner-product scoring with large vector norms for popular items (rec3, rec4), where normalisation helps; and position bias, which gives top-ranked popular items extra clicks. The cumulative effect is measured at the system level: the Gini coefficient of impressions across items, the share of impressions going to the top 1% of items, catalogue coverage, and the time a new item takes to reach some threshold of exposure (rec8 defines these). Watching them over months shows whether the loop is tightening.

Mitigations act at each stage: exploration traffic that gives uncertain items impressions (rec11); propensity-weighted training so popular items' many clicks are not over-counted; retrieval sources dedicated to fresh and long-tail items; re-ranking with exposure constraints or diversity (rec12); and long-term holdouts that measure what the loop does to user satisfaction and the catalogue over months (rec13).

8. Filter bubbles: what is and is not known

The "filter bubble" concern is that personalisation narrows each user's information diet to content similar to what they already consume, with possible effects on the users and on society. Mechanistically, it is a user-level feedback loop: the model recommends more of what you engaged with; you engage with what you are shown; the model becomes more confident.

Simulation studies show the mechanism can operate: Chaney, Stewart and Engelhardt (RecSys 2018) simulated recommenders retrained on data from their own recommendations and found this "algorithmic confounding" increased the homogeneity of users' behaviour and reduced utility compared with an ideal recommender. Empirical evidence on real platforms is more mixed, depends on how narrowing is measured (diversity of recommendations, of consumption, of opinions) and on the platform, and has changed as platforms changed their algorithms; some studies find recommendations expose users to more variety than they would choose themselves, others find narrowing in specific settings.

The engineering position that survives scrutiny: do not assume either answer for your system; measure it. Track per-user diversity of recommendations and of consumption over time (for example, the entropy of the category distribution of what each user watched each month), compare against a holdout with a less personalised or more diverse policy, and design in calibration and diversity (rec8, rec12) where narrowing appears, especially where the content has civic or health consequences.

9. A debiasing checklist for a new system

Pulling the lesson together, a team building or inheriting a recommender can work through a short list. Log the presentation: every impression's position, surface, layout and device, and the model version, so position effects can be modelled at all. Add controlled randomness and log its probabilities: an exploration slot, randomised tie-breaking among near-equal scores, a small swap experiment; each random decision's probability goes in the log. Estimate propensities per surface and re-estimate after interface changes. Correct training: inverse propensity weights (clipped) or a position tower removed at serving, compared against each other on randomised traffic. Correct evaluation: counterfactual estimates with effective sample sizes for candidate models, interleaving for fast online checks (rec13), and ordinary metrics only on randomised traffic when possible. Watch the loop: concentration and coverage metrics over months, new-item exposure, per-user diversity, and a long-running holdout.

None of these steps requires exotic modelling. They require deciding, early, that the system's own influence on its data is a first-class engineering concern, because retrofitting propensity logging after years of deterministic serving leaves all of that history uncorrectable.

Why IPW is unbiased: one line of algebra

For an item at position k, let C be the indicator of a click and E of examination, with C = E × R where R is the relevance-driven click. Under the examination model E and R are independent given the item and position, so E[C / pk] = E[E] × E[R] / pk = pk × r / pk = r. The weighted click is an unbiased estimate of relevance, whatever position the item was shown in. The derivation also shows the assumptions: examination depends only on position (not on the items above it, which the cascade model relaxes), the propensity is known, and it is not zero. If position 30 is never examined, no reweighting can recover information about items shown there.

Trust bias and other violations of the simple model

Users do not just examine top results more; they also trust them more, clicking a top result they would have skipped lower down. This trust bias breaks the clean factorisation, because the second factor now depends on position too. Corrections model it with position-dependent false-positive and false-negative click rates. Grid layouts (two-dimensional examination), carousels (horizontal swipes), autoplay and rich snippets all need their own propensity models. The lesson is not that correction fails, but that propensities should be estimated per surface and layout, and re-estimated when the interface changes, because a redesign can shift attention patterns overnight and silently invalidate weights.

Doubly robust estimation in one paragraph

A direct method estimates a new policy's reward by training a reward model on logs and averaging its predictions for the new policy's choices; it has low variance but inherits the model's bias, especially for actions rarely logged. IPS has no model bias but high variance. The doubly robust estimator combines them: for each event, take the model's predicted reward for the new policy's action, then add an IPS correction for the model's error on the logged action when the policies agree: DR = (1/n) Σ [ r̂(x, π(x)) + 1[π(x) = a] × (r − r̂(x, a)) / π0(a | x) ]. If the reward model is accurate, the correction terms are small and variance is low; if the propensities are accurate, the correction removes the model's bias. It is consistent if either is right, which is where the name comes from, and it is the default choice in many off-policy evaluation libraries.

The bias-variance trade-off in off-policy estimators

IPS is unbiased with variance that grows with the squared importance weights. Clipping trades bias for variance; self-normalisation does too, more gently; doubly robust estimators reduce variance when the reward model is decent. A useful diagnostic is how far the new policy is from the logging policy: if they agree on most decisions, estimates are stable; if the new policy mostly takes actions the old one rarely took, no estimator will be reliable, and an online test is the only honest option. This is why logging policies with a little randomness everywhere (rather than a lot in a few places) make far more future policies evaluable.

Exposure is a decision, so log its probability

The single most valuable practice this lesson implies is mundane: whenever any stage of the system makes a random choice (an exploration slot, a randomised tie-break, a sampled candidate, a swap experiment), log the probability with which it made it. Without that number, the data cannot be reweighted later, and the randomness was wasted. With it, every future model can be trained and evaluated with corrections. Systems that log propensities from the start accumulate an asset; systems that add them later can only use data from that point on.

Debiasing through features versus through weights

Two families of methods address position bias. Weighting methods (IPW, unbiased LTR) change how much each example counts. Feature methods give the model the bias variables (position, device, layout) during training and neutralise them at serving, such as YouTube's shallow position tower or simply feeding position as a feature and setting it to a constant (often position 1) at inference. Feature methods have lower variance and need no explicit propensities, but rely on the model separating position effects from relevance effects correctly, which can fail when position and relevance are strongly correlated in the logs (the best items are always on top). Randomised data helps both: it breaks that correlation.

Long-term loops are slower than experiments

Many feedback effects play out over weeks or months: a creator who gets no exposure stops uploading; a user who sees narrower content gradually engages less. A two-week A/B test measures the first-order effect of a change and misses these. Long-running holdouts (a small group kept on an older or more exploratory policy for months) and ecosystem metrics (creator retention, catalogue coverage, new-item success) are the tools for seeing them; rec13 covers their design.

The public case: unbiased learning to rank on a live search engine

Joachims, Swaminathan and Schnabel's WSDM 2017 paper "Unbiased Learning-to-Rank with Biased Feedback" is a clean, complete example of the lesson's ideas moving from theory into a running system. The theory: treat learning to rank from clicks as counterfactual empirical risk minimisation, with click models providing propensities, so the propensity-weighted training objective is an unbiased estimate of the objective with full relevance labels, even when queries do not repeat. The method: a ranking SVM whose pairwise loss for each clicked document is weighted by the inverse of its position's propensity.

The deployment was on a live search engine for scientific articles, using a linear scoring function over a 1,000-dimensional vector of query-document features. The production ranker had a hand-crafted weight vector. Clicks from 21 days of production traffic provided training data. To estimate propensities, the authors ran a 7-day randomised intervention: for each query, swap the top-ranked document with the document at a uniformly random rank j between 1 and 21, and estimate pj/p1 from the ratio of that document's click rate at j to its rate at 1, smoothed with overall click rates by position; the estimates decayed with rank to a minimum around 0.12, and ranks beyond 21 were assigned p21.

The trained propensity-weighted ranker was compared with production and with a naive click-trained ranking SVM using balanced interleaving, which mixes two rankers' results on one page and credits clicks to the ranker that contributed each result (rec13). Against production: 87 query wins, 48 losses, 83 ties (two-tailed sign test p = 0.001). Against the naive click-trained ranker: 95 wins, 60 losses, 102 ties (p = 0.006). On synthetic experiments built from the Yahoo! LTR data, the method was robust to click noise and to moderately misspecified propensities. The case shows the whole chain: small randomised interventions to measure bias, propensity-weighted training, and an online comparison method that itself resists position bias.

How bias handling fails in production

Stale propensities. A redesign moves from a list to a two-column grid; position propensities from the old layout are still used. Corrections are now wrong, sometimes worse than none. Re-estimate propensities after every layout change, per surface and device.

Exploding weights. Propensities for deep positions are tiny; a few clicks there receive weights of 50 and dominate training batches, producing unstable models. Clip weights and monitor the distribution of weights per batch.

No logged probabilities. The team wants to evaluate a new policy counterfactually, but the system is deterministic and never logged propensities; the estimator is undefined for anything the old policy did not show. Add controlled randomness and log probabilities now; the payoff comes later.

Correlated randomisation. "Random" exploration that depends on something correlated with outcomes (only for new users, only at night) biases the corrected estimates. Randomise independently of user and item features, or log and model the dependence.

Popularity creep. Over a year, the share of impressions going to the top 1% of items rises from 30% to 45% with no single launch responsible. Each change was small and positive in its two-week test. Ecosystem metrics on a dashboard, reviewed monthly, and a long-term holdout catch this.

What to monitor

Click-through rate by position over time (a shift indicates changed attention patterns); propensity estimates per surface with their confidence intervals and last re-estimation date; weight distributions in training; share of traffic with randomisation and logged probabilities; off-policy estimates for candidate models with effective sample sizes; concentration metrics (Gini of impressions, top-1% share, coverage, new-item exposure time); and per-user diversity trends against a holdout.

Goal: simulate position bias, correct it with inverse propensity weighting, and run IPS and SNIPS on a simulated logging policy. Python 3.12, standard library only.

Design: debiasing a job-recommendation ranker

A jobs marketplace shows 10 job listings per page to job seekers. Its ranker is trained on "applied" clicks from its own traffic. Recruiters complain that new listings get almost no applications for their first week; analysts find the top 2% of employers receive 40% of all impressions, up from 25% a year ago; and a new ranker that looked 6% better offline on nDCG lost its A/B test. Propose a plan covering data collection, training, evaluation and monitoring.

Answer

Diagnosis. Three loops. Position bias: applications concentrate on top slots, and the model learns that whatever the old ranker placed high is good. Exposure bias: new listings start with no data, are ranked low, get few impressions and therefore few applications, confirming the low score. Popularity concentration: large employers with many listings and strong brand pull dominate exposure, rising year on year. The failed launch is the rec8 pattern: offline nDCG on logged applications rewards imitating the old ranker.

Data collection. Run a swap intervention on 1-2% of traffic (top result swapped with a random position 1-10) for two weeks, per device and layout, to estimate position propensities. Add an exploration policy: one of the 10 slots, on 10% of page views, filled by sampling from relevant new or under-exposed listings with known probabilities. Log, for every impression, the position, the propensity of that position, and for exploration slots the sampling probability.

Training. Weight applications by 1/p(position), clipped at 10, in the ranker's loss (or add a position tower removed at serving and compare). Use a smoothed prior for new listings' engagement features so they are not judged on a handful of impressions.

Evaluation. Counterfactual estimates (SNIPS with effective sample size) of each candidate ranker on exploration and swap traffic; interleaving against production for fast directional checks; then an A/B test whose primary metric is applications that lead to a recruiter response (a quality signal less driven by position), with guardrails on new-listing applications in the first week and employer concentration.

Monitoring. Top-2% employer impression share and Gini, time to first 10 applications for new listings, CTR by position, propensity re-estimation after any layout change, and a three-month holdout on the old policy to measure long-term effects on job seekers' success and recruiter retention.
