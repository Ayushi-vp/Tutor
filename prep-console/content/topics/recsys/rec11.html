A music service adds 100,000 new tracks every day. Its recommender, trained on billions of past plays, is excellent at recommending tracks that already have plays. A new track has none. Its collaborative-filtering vector does not exist, its click-through rate is undefined, and the ranker, trained to predict engagement from engagement history, scores it with whatever its default features suggest, usually low. So the track is not recommended, so it gets no plays, so it stays unknown. Somewhere in that daily batch are the next season's hits, and the system is structurally unable to find them.

The same happens to people. A new user signs up and the system knows their country and their phone. Every model built on interaction history has nothing to say. If the first session is generic and dull, many new users never come back, and the system never learns anything about them. These are the two halves of the cold-start problem.

Underneath cold start sits a more general problem that applies even to established users and items: the system only learns about what it shows (rec10). If it always shows what it currently believes is best, it never discovers that something else is better. Every recommendation is therefore a small decision between exploiting current knowledge and exploring to improve it. Too little exploration and the system freezes around its early beliefs; too much and users get a worse product today. The mathematics of this trade-off is the multi-armed bandit problem, and its algorithms, ε-greedy, upper confidence bounds and Thompson sampling, are among the most practically useful tools in recommendation.

Interviewers love this area because it tests judgement. "How would you recommend to a brand-new user?" and "how would you give new items a fair chance?" have no single right answer, but strong answers share a shape: use every prior you have, explore deliberately rather than by accident, bound the cost, log the probabilities, and measure both the short-term price and the long-term benefit. The algorithms make that shape concrete.

This lesson starts with practical cold-start strategies for new items and new users, then builds bandits from scratch: regret, ε-greedy, UCB1 and Thompson sampling, worked over three arms for several rounds. It then adds context (contextual bandits and LinUCB), shows how to evaluate bandits offline without bias using random traffic, follows Yahoo!'s news-recommendation case, and finishes with how exploration is actually implemented in a large ranked feed: exploration slots, uncertainty-driven boosts and logged probabilities.

The name comes from a gambler facing a row of slot machines ("one-armed bandits"), each paying out with an unknown probability. Every pull earns a reward and reveals a little about that machine. The gambler wants to win as much as possible over an evening. Pulling only the machine that has paid best so far might mean missing a better one that had an unlucky first few pulls; pulling every machine equally wastes money on bad ones. A good strategy tries machines it is unsure about, especially ones that might be good, and gradually concentrates on the best.

A recommender faces the same choice every time it fills a slot. The arms are the candidate items (or layouts, or rows). The reward is a click, a play, a purchase. The gambler's uncertainty is the system's uncertainty about each item's appeal, which is large for new items and small for items with millions of impressions. A bandit algorithm turns that uncertainty into a decision rule.

Three rules capture most of the field. ε-greedy: usually play the best-looking arm, but with small probability ε play a random one. Upper confidence bound (UCB): play the arm whose optimistic estimate (its average plus a bonus for being little-tried) is highest; uncertainty is treated as potential. Thompson sampling: for each arm, imagine a plausible true value drawn from your current belief about it, and play the arm whose imagined value is highest; arms get played in proportion to the chance they are actually best.

The analogy breaks in ways that matter for recommenders. Slot machines have a fixed payout; items' appeal changes (news ages in hours, songs trend and fade). There are three machines in the story and millions of items in a catalogue, so pure bandits over all items are hopeless; bandits are applied over a few candidates the models already like, or over model-predicted values with uncertainty. And the gambler's choice affects only the gambler; a recommender's choices shape users' tastes and creators' incentives. Still, the core idea transfers intact: direct exploration to where uncertainty and potential are both high, and log what you did.

It is worth separating two questions that the word "exploration" mixes together. One is what to explore: which items or options are worth spending traffic on, which depends on uncertainty and on how good they might be. The other is how much to explore: what share of traffic, which positions, which users, which depends on the cost the product can bear. Bandit algorithms answer the first question well. The second is a product decision, settled by measuring the cost against long-term benefits, and no algorithm can make it for you.

Cold start is the most extreme case of that idea. A new item is an arm with no pulls; a new user is a context the models have never seen. The practical strategies in the first part of the lesson are about getting good priors, informed first guesses from content and context, so that exploration starts from a sensible place rather than from zero.

1. Cold start for new items

A new item lacks three things: interaction counts (so engagement features are empty), a collaborative representation (no matrix-factorisation vector, no trained ID embedding) and the trust that comes from history. Each has a remedy.

Content-based representation. Everything known about the item at upload time can place it: title and description text through a text encoder, the thumbnail and frames through an image or video encoder, the audio through an audio model, the creator, category, language, duration and price. A two-tower item tower trained with these features (rec4) gives a new item a vector immediately, so retrieval can surface it to users whose vectors are nearby. Yi et al. assign never-seen IDs to shared hash buckets so the content features carry the vector until the ID embedding is learned.

Priors for engagement features. A new item's click rate is undefined; feeding 0 tells the ranker "nobody clicks this". Better: a smoothed rate with a prior from similar items, (clicks + α × prior) / (impressions + α) (rec6, rec9), where the prior comes from the creator's history ("this creator's videos average 6% CTR"), the category, or a model that predicts engagement from content. Add an explicit "impressions so far" feature so the ranker knows how much evidence stands behind the rate.

Guaranteed exploration. Even with good content features, a new item needs real interactions to confirm or correct the prior. Systems give each new item an exploration budget, for example its first 500 impressions to users predicted to match it, delivered through exploration slots or a temporary score boost. The budget ends when the item's estimates are confident (enough impressions for a narrow posterior) or it has clearly underperformed. hd3 described this for a video platform: new uploads inserted into the index within minutes, with an exploration budget.

Fast updates. The faster interaction counts and embeddings update (nearline counters, frequent retraining, rec9), the shorter the cold period. An item that gets 500 impressions in its first hour should be ranked on its own data by the second hour.

2. Cold start for new users

A new user is not a blank: the system knows the context of the signup. Country and language, device and operating system, the time, the acquisition channel (an ad for cooking videos, a link shared by a friend, an app-store search for "football highlights") and the first screen they land on are all informative. A model trained on new users' early sessions can predict their likely interests from this context alone.

Onboarding. Asking new users to pick a few interests (genres, artists, topics) gives a strong, explicit signal. It also costs friction: every extra step in signup loses some users. Common practice is a short, skippable selection whose choices seed the user vector (by averaging the embeddings of chosen artists, say) and the candidate sources.

A diverse first slate. The first page for a new user has two jobs: be good enough that they stay, and reveal their preferences. Ten popular items from the same category fail the second job; ten varied, broadly appealing items succeed at both. This is exploration at the user level: each click on the first page is far more informative than a click on the hundredth.

Fast fold-in. After the first interactions, update immediately: session features capture the last few actions within seconds (rec9), a user tower that reads recent history produces a new vector on the next request, and matrix-factorisation fold-in (rec3) solves the user's vector from their handful of interactions in microseconds. The weight on population priors should fall as personal evidence accumulates, which a model trained with "number of interactions so far" as a feature learns automatically.

3. The explore-exploit trade-off and regret

Formalise the problem. There are K options (arms). Each time step t, the system chooses one arm at and receives a reward rt (1 for a click, 0 otherwise) drawn from that arm's unknown distribution with mean μa. The goal is to maximise total reward over T steps. The best possible strategy, knowing all the μ, would always play the best arm with mean μ*. Regret is the reward lost by not doing so:

Regret(T) = T × μ*  −  Σ over t of μ(a_t)

A strategy that never explores can lock onto a suboptimal arm forever, so its regret grows linearly with T. A strategy that explores uniformly forever also has linear regret, because it keeps paying for bad arms. Good algorithms achieve regret that grows only logarithmically with T: they explore a lot early, then less and less, spending exploration mostly on arms that might still be best. Lai and Robbins showed in 1985 that logarithmic growth is the best achievable in general, which gives a benchmark for comparing algorithms.

Throughout this section, use a running example: three candidate headlines for a promoted article, arms A, B and C, after some traffic. A was shown 20 times with 2 clicks; B 50 times with 4 clicks; C only 5 times with 1 click. Total plays t = 75. Observed click rates: A 0.10, B 0.08, C 0.20.

4. ε-greedy

The simplest strategy: with probability 1 − ε, play the arm with the highest observed mean (exploit); with probability ε, play an arm chosen uniformly at random (explore). With ε = 0.1 on the example, 90% of the time the system plays C (highest mean 0.20, from five plays), and 10% of the time a random arm, so each arm gets about 3.3% of plays from exploration.

Its virtues are simplicity and robustness, which is why it is everywhere. Its weaknesses are clear in the example. The greedy choice rests on C's five plays; one more non-click drops C's mean to 0.17, and two drop it to 0.14, flipping the leader. Exploration is undirected: B, with fifty plays and a mean of 0.08, gets as much exploration as C, about which we know almost nothing. And with constant ε the system keeps exploring at the same rate forever, so regret grows linearly. Decaying ε (for example εt = min(1, c/t)) fixes the last issue but needs tuning. In recommender practice, ε-greedy survives mainly as a simple "random slot" policy whose uniform randomness is valuable for debiasing (rec10), even if it is not the most efficient learner.

5. UCB1: optimism in the face of uncertainty

The upper confidence bound idea is to act as if each arm is as good as it plausibly could be. For each arm, compute an upper confidence bound on its mean; play the arm with the highest bound. Arms with few plays have wide confidence intervals and therefore high bounds, so they get tried; as they are tried, their bounds shrink to their true means. Auer, Cesa-Bianchi and Fischer's UCB1 uses:

UCB(a) = mean(a) + sqrt( 2 ln t / n_a )

where na is the arm's plays and t the total. On the example, ln 75 = 4.317:

  • A: 0.10 + sqrt(8.63 / 20) = 0.10 + 0.657 = 0.757
  • B: 0.08 + sqrt(8.63 / 50) = 0.08 + 0.416 = 0.496
  • C: 0.20 + sqrt(8.63 / 5) = 0.20 + 1.314 = 1.514

UCB1 plays C. The bonus dominates: C's bound is high because it has been tried only five times, not because of its mean. Notice the scale: the bonuses (0.4-1.3) are far larger than the click rates (0.08-0.20). UCB1's bonus is derived for any reward in [0, 1] and does not know that click rates are small, so it explores very generously at these counts. Variance-aware variants (UCB-V, KL-UCB) use the observed variance or the Bernoulli structure and give much tighter bounds for low rates. UCB1 is deterministic given the history, which is a disadvantage when feedback is delayed or batched: every request between updates gets the same choice.

6. Thompson sampling, worked over several rounds

Thompson sampling (from William Thompson's 1933 paper, rediscovered in the 2000s) keeps a full probability distribution, a posterior, for each arm's mean, and makes decisions by sampling from it. For click-or-not rewards, the natural posterior is a Beta distribution. Start each arm at Beta(1, 1), which is uniform on 0 to 1 ("no idea"); after s clicks and f non-clicks, the posterior is Beta(1 + s, 1 + f). Its mean is (1 + s) / (2 + s + f) and its spread shrinks as s + f grows. The rule:

  1. For each arm, draw one random value from its posterior.
  2. Play the arm with the highest draw.
  3. Observe the reward and update that arm's posterior (add 1 to the first parameter for a click, to the second for no click).

On the example, the posteriors are A: Beta(3, 19), mean 0.136, standard deviation 0.072; B: Beta(5, 47), mean 0.096, SD 0.040; C: Beta(2, 5), mean 0.286, SD 0.160. Here are five rounds, with draws given (in a real system they come from a random number generator):

10.11 / 0.09 / 0.31CnoC: Beta(2, 6) (0.250, 0.144)
20.16 / 0.10 / 0.12AyesA: Beta(4, 19) (0.174, 0.077)
30.14 / 0.12 / 0.35CyesC: Beta(3, 6) (0.333, 0.149)
40.13 / 0.08 / 0.21CnoC: Beta(3, 7) (0.300, 0.138)

Read the pattern. C, the least-tested arm, won three of five rounds, not because its mean was always highest but because its wide posterior often produced the highest draw. In round 2 its draw happened to be low (0.12), and A, whose posterior overlaps C's, won and got a click. B, well-tested and probably the worst, never won: its narrow posterior around 0.10 rarely produces a draw above the others. Each update narrows the played arm's posterior: C's SD went from 0.160 to 0.138 in three plays. Over hundreds of rounds, C's posterior will settle near its true rate; if that is high, C will win almost every draw; if low, it will lose almost every draw and stop being explored.

This is the key property: Thompson sampling plays each arm with probability equal to the posterior probability that it is the best arm. Exploration goes where it can change the decision, and fades automatically. It is also randomised, which helps with delayed feedback (requests between updates get varied choices) and gives every choice a probability that can be logged for later debiasing, though computing that probability exactly requires extra work (for example by simulation).

Chapelle and Li's 2011 empirical study compared Thompson sampling with UCB variants on simulations and on display-advertising and news-recommendation data and found it competitive with or better than the alternatives, while being simple to implement. Agrawal and Goyal later proved logarithmic regret bounds for it. It is the most common default bandit in industry.

7. Contextual bandits and LinUCB

The headline example ignored who the user is. In recommendation, the best arm depends on context: a cooking article for one user, a football one for another. A contextual bandit observes a feature vector x (user features, time, device, and arm features) before each choice, and an arm's expected reward is a function of x.

LinUCB (Li, Chu, Langford and Schapire, 2010) assumes each arm's expected reward is linear in the context: E[r | x, a] = θa · x. For each arm it keeps the matrix Aa = Daᵀ Da + I and the vector ba = Daᵀ ca, where Da stacks the contexts in which the arm was played and ca the rewards; the ridge-regression estimate is θa = Aa−1 ba (the same ridge solution as ALS in rec3). It plays the arm maximising:

θ_a · x  +  α × sqrt( xᵀ A_a⁻¹ x )

The second term is the standard deviation of the prediction in the direction of this context: large when the arm has rarely been played in contexts like x, small when it has been played often in similar contexts. α sets how optimistic to be. The structure is UCB, but the uncertainty is per context, so an article well tested on sports fans but never shown to cooking fans gets an exploration bonus only for cooking fans. A Thompson-sampling version draws θa from a Gaussian posterior with covariance proportional to Aa−1 instead of adding a bonus.

Modern systems generalise this: the reward model is a neural network (often the ranker itself), and uncertainty comes from an ensemble of models, dropout, a Bayesian last layer, or count-based estimates of how much data supports each prediction. The decision rule stays the same: score = predicted value + exploration bonus, or score = a sample from the predictive distribution.

8. Evaluating bandits offline with replay

How do you compare bandit algorithms without running each one on live users? Logs from a deterministic production system cannot answer it: whenever the candidate algorithm would choose differently from what was logged, the outcome is unknown (rec10). Li and colleagues' solution was to log a random bucket: a small share of traffic in which the article shown was chosen uniformly at random from the pool. Then replay the logged events in order: for each event, ask the candidate algorithm which arm it would choose given its history so far; if it matches the logged (random) arm, record the reward and let the algorithm learn from it; otherwise skip the event. Because the logged choice was uniform and independent of everything, the retained events are distributed exactly as if the algorithm had been running live; the estimate of its click rate is unbiased. The cost is data: with K arms, only about 1 in K events is retained.

The same paper used this method to evaluate LinUCB and baselines on Yahoo!'s front page, described below. When logging is random but not uniform, inverse propensity weighting with the logged probabilities generalises replay (rec10).

9. Exploration in a large ranked feed

Production recommenders rarely run a textbook bandit over the whole catalogue. Exploration is built into the funnel instead.

Exploration slots. Reserve one or two positions per page (or a probability per page) for exploration. Fill them from a candidate pool of uncertain items, new items, items with few impressions in this user's segment, or items from categories the user has not engaged with, chosen by Thompson sampling or UCB on the ranker's predicted value plus an uncertainty estimate. Place exploration where it is seen but costs little (positions 3-8 rather than position 1). Log the selection probability and position for every exploratory impression.

Score boosts. Instead of separate slots, add an exploration bonus to uncertain candidates' scores in the ranker or re-ranker (rec12), for example proportional to 1/sqrt(impressions), decaying as data accumulates. This is UCB inside a ranker. It is less transparent than slots and its probabilities are harder to log, so randomise a little and log what you can.

Budgets and caps. Limit how much of each user's feed is exploratory (no one should get a page of experiments) and how many impressions any item receives from exploration. Measure the cost with a holdout group that receives no exploration: the short-term engagement difference is the price, and the long-term difference (catalogue coverage, new-item success, retention) is the benefit.

Bandits at the top of the stack. True bandits remain the right tool for small, discrete, fast-changing decisions: which of five headlines or thumbnails to show for an article, which promotional banner to show, which of a dozen home-screen rows to place first. Spotify described a contextual bandit (BaRT) personalising which shelves and explanations appear on its home page, with exploration built into production serving.

10. Uncertainty estimates for a neural ranker

UCB and Thompson sampling need an uncertainty for each candidate's predicted value. With Beta counts that is free; with a deep ranker it must be estimated. Four practical approaches, from cheapest to most principled. Count-based: treat the number of impressions the item (or the user-segment × item pair) has received as a proxy, with uncertainty proportional to 1/sqrt(impressions + prior count); crude but robust and easy to explain. Ensembles: train several rankers with different random seeds or data subsets; the spread of their predictions is the uncertainty, and sampling one member per request is a form of Thompson sampling. Dropout at inference: keep dropout active at serving and run a few passes; the spread approximates uncertainty, at extra compute cost. Bayesian last layer: treat the final linear layer's weights as Gaussian, as LinUCB does for the whole model, and compute the predictive variance from the layer's input; cheap at serving and principled for the part of the model it covers.

Whatever the method, check that the uncertainty is meaningful: items with few impressions should have wider intervals, and predictions with wide intervals should be wrong more often. A miscalibrated uncertainty sends exploration to the wrong places just as a miscalibrated probability distorts a value model (rec6).

Why Thompson sampling explores the right amount

The probability that Thompson sampling plays arm a equals the posterior probability that a is the best arm. That is a principled allocation: an arm that is almost certainly worse than the leader is played almost never; an arm that has a 30% chance of being the best is played about 30% of the time. As evidence accumulates, these probabilities concentrate on the best arm and exploration fades. UCB achieves a similar effect deterministically through confidence bounds. Both achieve logarithmic regret, but Thompson sampling's randomisation makes it robust to delayed and batched feedback, which is the normal case in recommenders, where rewards arrive seconds to days later and model updates happen in batches.

Priors are where domain knowledge enters

Beta(1, 1) says "any click rate from 0 to 1 is equally likely", which is absurd for a feed where rates are 1-10%. A uniform prior makes every new arm look like a potential 50% winner and over-explores. A prior fitted to similar items, for example Beta(2, 38) for a category averaging 5% click rate, is equivalent to having already seen 40 impressions with 2 clicks, so a new item starts near the category average with moderate uncertainty. Priors from content models do the same per item. Getting priors right is the single biggest practical improvement to bandit performance in recommendation, and it is exactly the cold-start problem from the first section viewed through a Bayesian lens.

Non-stationarity

Bandit theory usually assumes fixed reward rates. News, trends and seasonal content violate this: an article's appeal decays over hours, a song's rises and falls over weeks. A posterior built on all history becomes overconfident and stops exploring an arm whose value changed. Remedies: discount old observations (multiply both Beta parameters by a factor below 1 each hour, which keeps the mean and widens the spread), sliding windows, change-point detection that resets an arm's posterior, or including time features (item age, time of day) in a contextual model. Yahoo!'s news setting, where articles enter and leave the pool constantly, is a case where the arm set itself changes, so new arms' priors matter as much as old arms' updates.

The value of information

A different way to think about exploration asks how much a single impression is worth as information. Showing a new item with an uncertain 2-12% click rate to one user produces an expected reward now (its mean, say 7%) and a reduction in uncertainty that improves all future decisions about that item, for every user who might see it. The more future traffic the item could receive, the more that information is worth. This explains several practical rules: explore items with large potential audiences more than niche ones, explore early in an item's life (when there is the most future to benefit), and explore less near the end of an item's life (news a day old, a seasonal product after the season). Formal "knowledge gradient" and Gittins-index methods compute this trade-off directly; in practice, horizon-aware budgets approximate it.

Exploration has users on the other end

Exploration means showing some users something the system believes is worse. That has ethical and product dimensions. Caps per user, avoiding exploration in sensitive contexts (health, finance, children's surfaces), and excluding items that fail integrity checks from exploration pools are standard. The flip side is also ethical: without exploration, new creators and niche content are structurally excluded, and the system's early biases become permanent (rec10). Teams should be able to state their exploration budget and its measured cost.

Bandits and A/B tests are complements

A/B tests fix allocation in advance to estimate an effect with controlled error; bandits change allocation adaptively to maximise reward. Adaptive allocation breaks the assumptions of standard significance tests (the sample sizes depend on the results), so bandit results should not be read as A/B test results. A common combination: use a bandit to allocate traffic among many short-lived variants (headlines), and A/B tests for lasting decisions about algorithms and policies, including the decision to use the bandit itself (math7 discusses bandits versus A/B tests from the statistics side).

Exploration in retrieval, not just ranking

An item that retrieval never returns cannot be explored by the ranker. Dedicated candidate sources for fresh and under-exposed items (rec1), random sampling of a few candidates from the eligible pool, and retrieval with perturbed user vectors (adding noise to the query embedding, a form of Thompson sampling in embedding space) push exploration down the funnel. Without them, ranker-level exploration only explores among items retrieval already likes.

The public case: LinUCB on Yahoo!'s Today Module

Li, Chu, Langford and Schapire's 2010 WWW paper is the classic production case for contextual bandits. The Today Module was the most prominent panel on Yahoo!'s front page, featuring news articles from a small, constantly changing pool chosen by editors. Collaborative filtering was a poor fit: articles live for hours, so there is never much history per article, and the pool changes too quickly. The authors framed article selection as a contextual bandit: arms are the articles in the pool; the context is a feature vector built from user features (demographics, behavioural categories, reduced by clustering) and article features; the reward is a click.

The evaluation is as important as the algorithm. Yahoo! ran a random bucket: a small fraction of front-page traffic in which the featured article was chosen uniformly at random from the pool. The authors collected events from this random bucket in May 2009, over 33 million events in total, using the first day's roughly 4.7 million events to tune parameters and the rest for evaluation. Using the replay method (keep only events where the algorithm's choice matches the random logged article), which they proved unbiased, they compared LinUCB with context-free bandits (ε-greedy, UCB) and with variants that used context in other ways. The abstract reports a 12.5% click lift for their approach compared with a standard context-free bandit, with the advantage growing when data was scarcer, the cold-start regime where context matters most.

The case shows three ideas working together: context-aware exploration (LinUCB's per-context uncertainty bonus), a deliberately randomised logging policy that made unbiased offline evaluation possible, and the value of evaluating exploration algorithms on the metric that matters (clicks over time) rather than on prediction accuracy.

How exploration fails in production

Exploration without logging. A team adds random slots but does not log which impressions were exploratory or their probabilities. The traffic is spent; its value for debiasing is lost. Log the probability with every exploratory decision from day one.

Uniform priors. A Beta(1, 1) prior on every new item over-explores obviously weak items and floods exploration slots with them; users notice the quality drop. Use priors from content or category.

Exploration that never ends. An item that has had 10,000 exploratory impressions with a clearly poor click rate is still being explored because its pool membership was based on age, not on uncertainty. Exit rules based on posterior confidence fix it.

Stale posteriors. Trending content's posteriors, built over a week, stop the system from noticing that an item's appeal collapsed this morning. Discount old evidence.

Hidden cost. Exploration's short-term cost is never measured, so it is cut during the next engagement push. Keep a no-exploration holdout and report both the cost and the long-term benefit (new-item success, catalogue coverage, retention).

What to monitor

Share of impressions that are exploratory, per surface and per user; engagement on exploratory versus exploitative impressions; time from item upload to first 100 impressions and to a confident estimate; share of new items that "graduate" to normal ranking; posterior widths for active items; for bandit-controlled decisions, traffic allocation per arm over time and replay estimates on random buckets; and the holdout comparison of long-term metrics.

What to look for. Greedy should sometimes lock onto a worse arm (high variance across seeds). ε-greedy's regret should keep growing linearly. UCB1 should explore heavily at first (its bonus is large for small click rates) and then flatten; Thompson sampling should usually have the lowest regret, and the informed prior should reduce early regret. After the swap, plain Thompson sampling should take a long time to notice; the discounted version should recover faster. The replay estimate should be close to the online result, using about one third of the logged events.

Design: cold start and exploration for a podcast app

A podcast app has 3 million monthly listeners, 400,000 shows, and 2,000 new shows per day. New shows get almost no listens in their first month; 70% of listening goes to the top 1,000 shows. New users churn at 55% within their first week. Product asks for a plan to help new shows and new users without hurting overall listening by more than 1%. Propose the strategies, the exploration mechanism, and how you will measure cost and benefit.

Answer

New shows. Embed each new show at ingest from its description, episode titles and transcripts (speech-to-text plus a text encoder), category, language and host's previous shows, using the item tower so retrieval can match it to listeners with nearby vectors. Set engagement priors from category and host history (for example Beta(2, 18) on "listened over 10 minutes" for a category averaging 10%). Give each new show an exploration budget of, say, 300 impressions to well-matched listeners in its language, delivered through one exploration slot on the home page's "new for you" row, selected by Thompson sampling over the ranker's predicted value with posterior uncertainty from impression counts. Shows graduate when their posterior SD falls below a threshold or after the budget; underperformers exit early.

New users. Use signup context (country, language, acquisition campaign, device) to predict initial interests; offer a skippable pick-three-topics screen; show a diverse first home page (several categories, a mix of popular and well-reviewed niche shows); fold in each listen within seconds via session features and re-rank. Measure the first-week funnel: first listen, second-day return, seventh-day return.

Exploration mechanics. At most one exploratory item per row and two per page; only items passing quality and policy checks; log selection probabilities and positions. Use the exploratory impressions for propensity-weighted training (rec10) and to update new shows' statistics nearline.

Measuring cost and benefit. A/B test with three arms: control (no exploration slots), exploration at 1 slot per page, exploration at 2.
