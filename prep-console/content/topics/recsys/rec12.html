A sports fan opens a video app the evening after a big match. The ranker has done its job well: for this user, every highlight clip of the match has a predicted watch probability above 90%. So the top of the feed is: the official highlights, a fan's re-upload of the official highlights, the five best goals, the post-match interview, the manager's press conference, another re-upload, a tactical breakdown, two more goal compilations. Each item, judged alone, is an excellent recommendation. As a page it is poor. The user watches one or two and scrolls past the rest, and the cooking channel they also love, and the comedy special released that afternoon, are nowhere to be seen.

The ranker cannot fix this, because it scores items one at a time. It does not know that the second clip is a copy of the first, or that interest in the fifth clip about one match is far lower than in the first, or that the business has promised every new creator a chance, or that an ad must appear in slot 5, or that the same seller should not fill the first three rows of a marketplace. Those are properties of the page, not of items, and they are the job of the re-ranking stage and the business logic that runs with it (rec1).

Re-ranking is easy to under-design. Many systems start with a few heuristic rules ("no more than two items per creator", "remove items with identical titles"), add more over time, and end up with an unexplained tangle of rules that fight each other and the model. YouTube's 2018 paper on diversification gives a striking illustration: three reasonable heuristics, fuzzy deduplication, a sliding-window rule and a smooth score penalty, each reduced their key user-satisfaction metric in live tests, while a principled model of diversity increased it and raised watch time by about half a percent.

In interviews, re-ranking is where a design shows maturity. Weak answers end at "sort by the ranker's score"; strong ones say what the page must also achieve (variety, freshness, fairness, ads, rules), how each is expressed (penalties, boosts, constraints), in which order they run, what each costs in relevance, and how the trade-off will be measured online. Being able to compute an MMR step or an exposure share on the whiteboard makes those claims concrete.

This lesson builds the re-ranking stage properly: greedy page construction; maximal marginal relevance worked by hand on six videos; determinantal point processes and YouTube's results; freshness; near-duplicate removal; fairness of exposure, computed for two groups of sellers with position weights; calibrated re-ranking; blending ads and other content types; and how to manage several objectives at once with weights, constraints and trade-off curves.

Think of putting together a dinner party menu. Each dish on its own has a score: how much the guests will enjoy it. The five highest-scoring dishes might be five rich pasta dishes, each excellent, together a disaster. A good host picks the best dish first, then asks "given what's already on the menu, what adds the most?", and continues. A light salad scores lower than a third pasta dish in isolation, but adds far more to this particular menu. The host also has rules that override taste: one guest is vegetarian, the budget is fixed, the sponsor's wine must be served.

Re-ranking is that host. It receives the ranker's scored candidates and builds the page one slot at a time, asking at each step which remaining candidate adds most to the page as it stands, after accounting for similarity to what is already there, freshness, fairness and constraints. The general pattern is greedy page building:

page = []
while page not full:
    for each remaining candidate c:
        adjusted(c) = score(c) − redundancy(c, page) + boosts(c) − costs(c)
    add the candidate with the highest adjusted score that satisfies all rules

Read the loop as a recipe with four ingredients. The score is what the ranker believes the item is worth on its own. The redundancy term subtracts value the page already provides: the more the candidate resembles items already placed, the less it adds. The boosts add value the ranker does not see, such as freshness, exploration of an uncertain new item, or a promotion the business has committed to. The costs subtract value the page would lose, such as the user-experience cost of an ad. Rules then act as filters on the choice: a candidate that would break a cap or a spacing rule is skipped for this slot even if its adjusted score is highest. Almost every technique in this lesson is a way to define one of those four terms or one of those rules well.

Because the loop runs over a few hundred candidates and about twenty slots, it is cheap: a few thousand similarity computations per request, well under ten milliseconds. Its cost is not compute but design: deciding the terms, their weights, and the rules, and keeping them coherent as the product grows.

The analogy breaks in two helpful ways. A host knows the guests; re-ranking must estimate how much similar items reduce each other's value, and that estimate is a model to be learned and tested, not a fixed fact. And a dinner party happens once; a feed is refreshed many times a day, so re-ranking also shapes what users learn to expect and what data the next model is trained on (rec10). A page that always shows one interest teaches the model that the user has one interest.

A good mental division: the ranker answers "how good is each item for this person now?", re-ranking answers "what is the best page made of these items, given everything else we care about?". The second question involves the first, plus redundancy, freshness, fairness, ads and rules. Keeping the two separate lets each be measured and tuned on its own.

1. Why the page is not the sum of its items

The ranker's score for an item estimates its value if shown, assuming the rest of the page does not matter. That assumption fails in predictable ways. Redundancy: two near-identical items split the user's interest; seeing one makes the other almost worthless. Satiation: interest in a topic declines with each additional item about it on the same page. Coverage: users often have several current interests, and a page that serves only one misses value. Position interactions: an item's value depends on where it lands and what is above it. Each of these means the value of a page is less than the sum of its items' scores when items are similar, which is exactly what diversity-aware re-ranking corrects.

In principle, one could rank whole pages: score every possible page and pick the best. With 300 candidates and 20 slots that is astronomically many pages. In practice, re-ranking uses greedy construction (add the best next item given the page so far), which is fast and, for objectives with diminishing returns (the technical term is submodular), provably close to optimal. All the methods below are variations on greedy construction with different definitions of "best next item".

2. Maximal marginal relevance, worked

Maximal marginal relevance (MMR), introduced by Carbonell and Goldstein in 1998 for search results and summarisation, is the simplest principled diversity rule. At each step, every remaining candidate c gets:

MMR(c) = λ × rel(c)  −  (1 − λ) × max over selected s of sim(c, s)

and the highest is added. λ between 0 and 1 sets the trade-off: λ = 1 is plain ranking by relevance; λ = 0.5 weighs novelty equally with relevance. The similarity function can be the cosine of item embeddings, shared categories, shared creators or any combination.

Worked example. Six candidate videos with relevance scores from the ranker: v1 football 0.95, v2 football 0.93 (a re-upload of v1), v3 football 0.90, v4 cooking 0.85, v5 comedy 0.80, v6 cooking 0.78. Similarities: v1 and v2 are near-duplicates, 0.95; other pairs on the same topic 0.8; pairs on different topics 0.1. Use λ = 0.7 and build four slots.

  • Slot 1 (nothing selected, so no penalty): scores are 0.7 × relevance: v1 0.665, v2 0.651, v3 0.630, v4 0.595, v5 0.560, v6 0.546. Pick v1.
  • Slot 2: v2 = 0.7 × 0.93 − 0.3 × 0.95 = 0.651 − 0.285 = 0.366; v3 = 0.630 − 0.3 × 0.8 = 0.390; v4 = 0.595 − 0.3 × 0.1 = 0.565; v5 = 0.560 − 0.03 = 0.530; v6 = 0.546 − 0.03 = 0.516. Pick v4 (cooking).
  • Slot 3: each candidate's penalty is now its maximum similarity to v1 or v4. v2 0.366, v3 0.390 (closest to v1), v5 0.530, v6 = 0.546 − 0.3 × 0.8 = 0.306 (close to v4). Pick v5 (comedy).
  • Slot 4: v2 0.366, v3 0.390, v6 0.306. Pick v3.

The page is v1, v4, v5, v3 instead of v1, v2, v3, v4. The near-duplicate never appears; three interests are covered in the first three slots. The sum of raw relevance on the page drops from 3.63 to 3.50 (about 4%), which is the "price" of diversity if one believes the ranker's isolated scores; whether users actually prefer the diverse page is measured online.

MMR's weaknesses: the penalty considers only the single most similar selected item, not how many similar items are already there; λ is a hand-tuned global constant; and similarity must be defined well, or the penalty pushes in irrelevant items. It remains a strong, easy baseline, and is widely used in RAG to diversify retrieved chunks (lm9).

3. Determinantal point processes

A determinantal point process (DPP) gives a principled score to a whole set. Build an N × N kernel matrix L for the N candidates: the diagonal entry Lii encodes item i's quality (for example its score squared), and the off-diagonal Lij encodes quality times similarity. The DPP assigns each subset Y of candidates a probability proportional to the determinant of the submatrix LY. Determinants have the right behaviour: for two items with qualities q1, q2 and similarity s, the submatrix is [[q1², q1q2s], [q1q2s, q2²]] and its determinant is q1²q2²(1 − s²): large when both are high quality and dissimilar, near zero when s approaches 1 (near-copies). Unlike MMR, the determinant accounts for all pairwise similarities in the set at once.

Finding the highest-probability set of size k is NP-hard in general, but a greedy algorithm (add the item that most increases the determinant) works well, and efficient implementations exist (Chen, Zhang and Zhou, 2018). The kernel's parameters can be learned from data: choose them to maximise the probability of the sets users actually engaged with.

YouTube's version. Wilhelm and colleagues (CIKM 2018) placed DPP re-ranking late in YouTube's pipeline, with a typical input of N = 100 videos, each with a personalised quality score q from the ranker and a sparse embedding from the video's topical content. Their kernel was Lii = qi² and Lij = α qiqj exp(−Dij / 2σ²), with Dij a distance between embeddings (Jaccard distance over sparse topic tokens worked best) and α, σ learned by grid search on homepage data: α scales all similarities up or down. To order a feed of several hundred videos, they repeatedly asked the DPP for a high-probability set of about a dozen videos, placed those next, and continued with the rest, because similarity between items far apart in the feed matters less than between neighbours.

The live results (about one week of experiments on the mobile homepage) are the public case below: every heuristic they tried lowered "satisfied homepage watchers", while DPPs raised it by 0.63% and overall watch time by 0.52%.

4. Freshness

Freshness matters differently by surface: crucial for news, live events and social feeds, modest for films, irrelevant for reference documentation. Three layers handle it.

In the ranker: age features (hours since publication, with interactions) let the model learn how much users value recency in each context. YouTube's 2016 paper's "example age" feature was a version of this for training (rec4).

In re-ranking: an explicit boost that decays with age, such as score × (1 + β × e−age/τ). With β = 0.3 and τ = 6 hours, a video an hour old gets a 25% boost, a 6-hour-old video 11%, a day-old video 0.5%. Explicit boosts are useful when product goals (keeping the feed feeling alive, supporting new uploads) exceed what engagement-trained models learn, and because they are transparent and easy to tune. They must be tested, since a strong boost floods the feed with new but weak content.

In supply: a fresh-content retrieval source (rec1) and sometimes a reserved slot, because no boost can promote an item that retrieval never returned. Exploration (rec11) overlaps here: new items are both fresh and uncertain.

5. Near-duplicate removal

Duplicates come from re-uploads, cross-posts, the same product listed by several sellers, syndicated news articles and template content. They waste slots and make a page look broken. Detection uses content fingerprints (perceptual hashes for images and video frames that change little under resizing and re-encoding; MinHash signatures over word shingles for text, ev11), embedding similarity above a threshold, and metadata (same product identifier, canonical URL, source ID). Candidates are clustered, and only the best member of each cluster is placed, ideally with the others linked ("also sold by 3 other sellers").

Order matters. Deduplicate after ranking, so the best representative is chosen with full information; and before diversity re-ranking, so diversity penalties are not spent on copies. Overly aggressive "fuzzy" deduplication can remove items that are related but distinct (two different goals from the same match), which is one reason YouTube's fuzzy-dedup heuristic slightly hurt their metric.

6. Fairness of exposure

Rankings allocate attention, and attention is valuable to whoever is ranked: sellers, creators, job seekers, news outlets. Because attention falls steeply with position (rec10), small score differences become large exposure differences. Singh and Joachims (KDD 2018) formalised fairness of exposure: define each position's exposure weight (for example 1/log2(rank + 1), or measured examination propensities); a group's exposure is the sum of the weights of the positions its items occupy; fairness can then require exposure proportional to the group's merit (relevance) rather than to whatever the sort order happens to produce.

Worked example. A marketplace ranks 10 listings: five from large sellers (group A, average relevance 0.84) and five from small sellers (group B, average 0.80). Sorting by relevance puts all of A at ranks 1-5 and B at 6-10. Exposure weights 1/log2(rank + 1) are 1.000, 0.631, 0.500, 0.431, 0.387 for ranks 1-5 (sum 2.948) and 0.356, 0.333, 0.315, 0.301, 0.289 for ranks 6-10 (sum 1.595). Group A gets 2.948 / 4.543 = 65% of exposure while holding 4.2 / 8.2 = 51% of the total relevance. A 5% relevance gap became a 30-point exposure gap.

Alternating the groups (A at ranks 1, 3, 5, 7, 9; B at 2, 4, 6, 8, 10) gives A 1 + 0.5 + 0.387 + 0.333 + 0.301 = 2.521 and B 0.631 + 0.431 + 0.356 + 0.315 + 0.289 = 2.022: shares of 55.5% and 44.5%, much closer to the relevance split, at the cost of moving some slightly better items down. Practical systems implement this as a constraint in greedy re-ranking ("if group B's running exposure share falls below 0.9 × its merit share, place the best B item next") or by amortising over time (fair on average across many requests rather than within each page).

Two cautions. Merit is usually the model's predicted relevance, which may itself be biased by feedback loops (rec10): small sellers may be under-scored because they were under-shown. Exposure fairness should therefore be paired with exploration. And groups must be defined carefully and legally; fairness work involving protected attributes needs legal and policy review, not just engineering.

7. Calibrated re-ranking

rec8 defined calibration of a list: whether its mix of categories matches the user's interests. Steck's method re-ranks greedily to maximise (1 − λ) × total relevance − λ × KL(user's category mix ∥ list's category mix). For a user with 70% romance and 30% action history, it keeps adding romance while romance is under-represented relative to 70% and switches to action once action falls below 30%. It is the targeted form of diversity: not "as varied as possible" but "as varied as this user is". It particularly protects minority interests, which plain relevance ranking tends to crowd out.

8. Blending ads and other content types

Many feeds mix several kinds of items: organic posts, ads, recommended accounts, short videos, promotional modules. Each comes from its own pipeline with its own scores, which are not on a common scale. Blending decides which goes where.

The simplest approach is fixed slots: an ad at positions 5, 12, 19. It is predictable and easy to sell, but ignores context: an irrelevant ad at position 5 costs the user experience more than a relevant one. The principled approach puts everything into one currency. For each ad, the value is its expected revenue (bid × predicted click or conversion probability, from a calibrated model, rec6). The cost is the expected loss in user value from displacing the organic item that would otherwise occupy that slot, plus any long-term effect (users who see more ads return slightly less often). An ad is inserted where value exceeds cost by a margin, subject to constraints: maximum ad load per page, minimum spacing between ads, no ad in the first slot, per-advertiser frequency caps. The exchange rate between revenue and user value is a business decision, informed by long-running experiments that vary ad load and measure retention.

The same logic applies to non-ad modules ("people you may know", promotions): each needs a value estimate and a cost, or it ends up placed by politics rather than evidence.

9. Several objectives at once

By now the page serves many goals: engagement, satisfaction, diversity, freshness, creator fairness, revenue, safety. There are three standard ways to combine them.

Scalarisation: one weighted score, as in the value model (rec6) plus re-ranking terms. Simple and fast, but the weights are hard to interpret and must be retuned when models or content change.

Constraints: optimise the main objective subject to floors and caps for the others ("maximise expected engagement subject to at least 30% of exposure for small creators, at most 15% ad load, at least three categories in the top ten"). Constraints are easier to explain and to guarantee, and greedy re-ranking enforces them naturally. They can be infeasible on some requests (not enough small-creator candidates), so define fallbacks.

Pareto analysis: run several settings (say five values of a diversity weight) and plot each objective against the others. The Pareto frontier is the set of settings where no objective can improve without another getting worse; choosing a point on it is a product decision, made with the curve in view rather than one number. Experiments can map the frontier online with a few arms.

In all three, the metrics that define success should include long-term outcomes (rec13): many re-ranking goals, such as diversity, freshness and fairness, show their value over weeks in retention and ecosystem health, not in the next click.

10. Search results versus feeds

Re-ranking looks different on a search results page. The query states a specific intent, so diversity is mostly about ambiguity: for "jaguar", show the car, the animal and the guitar if the query-understanding model (rec5) is unsure, a practice called intent-aware diversification, which allocates top slots in proportion to the probability of each interpretation. For a clear query ("jaguar f-type price"), diversity should be minimal, and deduplication matters more (several dealers listing the same car). Freshness matters for news-like queries and hardly at all for reference ones. Seller caps and exposure fairness apply as in feeds, but the cost of moving a highly relevant result down is higher, because the user asked for it.

Feeds, by contrast, have no stated intent, are scrolled through, and are visited many times a day, so variety across the page and across visits matters more, and satiation across sessions becomes relevant (do not show the same article a user scrolled past this morning). Many feeds therefore track recently shown items per user and penalise or exclude them, a form of cross-request diversity that per-page methods cannot express.

11. Evaluating re-ranking changes

Re-ranking changes are notoriously hard to evaluate offline. Logged data contains engagement only with pages the old re-ranker built; a new page with different items and order has no labels for its new parts (rec10's selection problem), and the value of diversity shows up in session length and return visits, not in per-item clicks. Offline, report the page-level metrics (distinct topics and creators, duplicate rate, exposure shares, freshness share, calibration KL) and the relevance cost (sum or nDCG of ranker scores on the page), which together trace the trade-off curve. Then rely on online experiments with session and long-term metrics, using several arms to map the curve, and keep long-running holdouts for settings like ad load whose effects accumulate slowly.

Submodularity: why greedy works

Many page-quality objectives have diminishing returns: adding an item to a page that already covers its topic adds less than adding it to a page that does not. Functions with this property are called submodular. For monotone submodular objectives under a size constraint, the greedy algorithm (always add the item with the largest marginal gain) is guaranteed to reach at least 1 − 1/e ≈ 63% of the optimum, and in practice usually much more. Coverage-style objectives (how many of the user's interests are represented, weighted by their strength) are submodular, and the log-determinant of a DPP kernel is submodular too, which is why greedy DPP inference performs well. MMR is a heuristic with similar spirit but without the guarantee.

Learning the diversity trade-off

Hand-tuned λ values and penalties are brittle. Better approaches learn them. DPP kernel parameters can be fitted to the sets users engaged with, as YouTube did by grid search and later with "deep Gramian" kernels learned by a neural network (which looked strong on their metric but changed the ranking enough to disturb secondary business metrics, so it was not yet deployed). Another approach makes the ranker itself list-aware: features about the page so far (how many items from this topic are already above) let the model learn satiation directly, at the cost of scoring sequentially. Slate-level models (rec14 mentions generative approaches) predict the value of whole pages and are an active research area.

Exposure fairness over time, not per page

Enforcing fairness constraints on every single page is costly: some requests have few good candidates from the protected group. Amortised fairness relaxes it: across many requests (per day, per query type), each group's cumulative exposure should track its cumulative merit. The re-ranker keeps running totals and nudges harder when a group falls behind, which costs much less relevance than strict per-page balance. Singh and Joachims discuss both per-ranking and amortised formulations; production systems usually prefer amortised ones.

Diversity across time, not only within a page

Users see many pages a day. A page can be perfectly varied while the day's feed is not: the same three topics rotate through every refresh, or an article the user scrolled past at breakfast reappears at lunch. Cross-request diversity uses per-user memory of recent impressions: penalise items shown recently without engagement, cap how often a topic or creator appears per day, and decay these penalties over hours. It costs a fast per-user store of recent impressions (often the same session store that feeds the ranker, rec9) and it matters most on surfaces users open many times a day.

Rules as code with owners

Business rules accumulate. A rule added for a launch, a legal requirement in one country, a partner contract, an incident fix: years later, nobody knows why "no more than one item from category 47 above position 8" exists. Treat rules as code: each has an owner, a reason, a creation date, a test, a metric for how often it fires and what it changes, and a review date. Log for every response which rules fired. Many teams discover that a third of their rules no longer fire at all and another third conflict with newer ones.

Position-aware placement

Greedy construction fills slots in order, implicitly assuming the most valuable slot is first. On many layouts that is false: grid layouts put two or three items in the first row; carousels hide items beyond the first few; autoplay makes the first video dominant. Placement can use measured position propensities (rec10) as slot values: the best item goes to the slot with the highest examination probability, and constraints such as "no ad in the most-viewed slot" refer to measured attention rather than index numbers. When the layout changes, the re-ranker's notion of "top" must change with it, or carefully tuned diversity and fairness constraints act on the wrong positions.

The order of operations is a design decision

Filters, deduplication, diversity, freshness, fairness, ads and rules interact, and their order changes the result. A useful default: hard eligibility filters first (they define the candidate set); deduplication next (one representative per cluster); then a single greedy pass that combines the value score with diversity, freshness and exploration terms while enforcing constraints (per-seller caps, fairness floors, ad spacing); then final validation (nothing ineligible, page length met). Doing these as separate passes, each re-sorting the list, is how rule tangles start: a later pass silently undoes an earlier one.

The public case: DPPs on YouTube's homepage

Wilhelm, Ramanathan, Bonomo, Jain, Chi and Gillenwater's CIKM 2018 paper describes diversifying recommendations on the YouTube mobile homepage feed, where the system must order a few hundred videos for each user. Before DPPs, diversity was handled by heuristics in the serving layer. The authors explain why that is fragile: heuristics are "impoverished models" of diversity, the retrieval and ranking layers are unaware of them, and tuning many interacting heuristics requires repeated, exponentially growing searches over their parameters.

They ran live experiments of about one week each, measuring "satisfied homepage watchers", a metric counting sessions that originate from the homepage and last a significant time. The heuristics all lost: fuzzy deduplication −0.05%, a sliding-window rule −0.26%, and a smooth score penalty (decaying each video's score by its similarity to previously placed ones) −0.41%. The DPP re-ranker, with the RBF-style kernel over ranker quality scores and Jaccard distances between sparse topic-token embeddings, applied in windows of about a dozen videos over an input of around 100 candidates in the base version, gained +0.63% on the same metric and +0.52% in overall watch time, which the authors call "quite a significant jump over the baseline". After the mobile success, DPP diversification was deployed on all surfaces, including TV, desktop and Live. A learned "deep Gramian" kernel scored +1.72% on the satisfaction metric in experiments but changed rankings enough to affect secondary business metrics and had not been deployed at the time of writing.

One more detail is instructive: for some parameter settings, direct interactions on the homepage fell, yet site-wide engagement rose, with more activity in the related-videos panel and longer sessions starting from the homepage. Diversity's value showed up downstream, not in homepage clicks, which is why re-ranking must be judged on session and long-term metrics.

How re-ranking fails in production

Rule tangles. Dozens of heuristic rules, applied in sequence, undo each other and make the page's behaviour inexplicable. Consolidate into one greedy pass with explicit terms and constraints, log rule firings, and delete dead rules.

Over-diversification. A strong penalty with a coarse similarity function pushes in irrelevant items; engagement drops; the team concludes diversity is bad. Map the trade-off curve before deciding, and use fine-grained similarity.

Fresh-content floods. A freshness boost tuned during a busy news week overwhelms the feed during a quiet one with weak new items. Make boosts depend on the quality of fresh candidates, not just their age.

Ad-load creep. Each quarter's revenue target nudges ad load up by a small, individually acceptable amount; retention slowly erodes. A long-term ad-load holdout and a ceiling owned by the product, not the revenue team, prevent it.

Infeasible constraints. A fairness floor cannot be met on requests with few eligible items from the group; the re-ranker fills slots with poor items or times out. Define what happens when constraints are infeasible (relax, amortise, or skip) and monitor how often it occurs.

Who owns the page

Re-ranking is where many teams' interests meet: ranking wants engagement, ads wants revenue, creator and seller teams want fair exposure, trust and safety wants guarantees, product wants freshness and variety, partnerships want contractual placements. Without an explicit owner and a shared objective, each team adds its own rule and the page becomes a negotiated settlement nobody can reason about. Mature organisations make one team own the re-ranking stage, express every request as a term or constraint in one framework, require an experiment for every change to it, and publish a regular report of the trade-offs being made, so that decisions like "one more ad slot" are made with their measured cost in view.

What to monitor

Per page: number of distinct topics, creators and sellers in the top N; duplicate rate; share of fresh items; ad load and spacing violations; how often each rule and constraint fired. Over time: exposure shares by group versus merit shares, catalogue coverage, calibration KL (rec8). Effect: engagement, session length and return rate for re-ranking changes, with long-term holdouts for ad load and diversity settings. Latency: re-ranking time per request (should be a few milliseconds for a few hundred candidates).

Design: the re-ranking stage for a news app

A news app's ranker orders 400 candidate articles per request for a feed of 30. Complaints: the same story appears five times from different outlets; small local publishers get almost no traffic; breaking news takes 20 minutes to appear; the ad team wants an ad every 4 items instead of every 6. Design the re-ranking stage, its order of operations, and how you will tune and evaluate it.

Answer

Order of operations. (1) Eligibility and safety filters (licensing, user blocks, misinformation labels). (2) Story clustering: group articles about the same event using text embeddings and named-entity overlap within a time window; keep the best article per cluster for the main feed (choosing by ranker score with a bonus for the outlet the user prefers), and attach the others as "more coverage". (3) One greedy pass building 30 slots with adjusted score = value score − MMR-style penalty on topic similarity (λ tuned) + freshness boost decaying over hours (stronger for clusters with a sharp recent rise in articles, a breaking-news signal) + small exploration bonus for uncertain new articles. Constraints during the pass: at most two articles per outlet in the top 15; an amortised exposure floor for local publishers relative to their predicted relevance; ads by value versus cost with minimum spacing.

Breaking news latency. The 20-minute delay is a supply problem: add a streaming fresh-articles retrieval source that indexes new articles within a minute, nearline engagement counters, and the freshness boost; re-ranking alone cannot place articles retrieval never returns.

Ads.
