A recommendation team has a new ranker. Offline it is 3% better on nDCG@10 (rec8). The team runs an A/B test on 1% of users for two weeks, and the result is +0.3% in watch time with a confidence interval of ±0.5%: inconclusive. They run it for two more weeks at 5% of users: +0.15% ± 0.2%, still inconclusive. Meanwhile four other promising rankers wait in the queue, each needing a month of a large share of traffic. At this pace the team can evaluate about ten ideas a year, most results are ambiguous, and nobody knows whether the slow trickle of launches is helping users in the long run.

This is the normal state of ranking experimentation in a mature product. Effects of a single ranking change are small (a fraction of a percent on the metrics that matter) compared with the natural variation between users, so A/B tests need millions of users and weeks of time. Effects also change over time: users react to novelty, adapt to better or worse recommendations, and the system's own feedback loops (rec10) play out over months. In marketplaces and social products, treated and untreated users affect each other, which breaks the basic assumption of an A/B test.

The tools to cope are well established, and this lesson covers them in the order a team uses them. First, the arithmetic of A/B tests for ranking, so you know how much traffic a question costs (ml16 and math7 cover the statistics; here we apply them). Then interleaving, which compares two rankers within the same result list and, in Netflix's measurements, needed over 100 times fewer subscribers than their most sensitive A/B metric to find the better ranker. Then metric choice: primary, secondary and guardrail metrics that do not reward clickbait. Then time: novelty and learning effects, and the long-term holdouts that revealed, in a well-known Google study, that low-quality ads gradually teach users to ignore ads. Finally interference: what goes wrong when users share inventory or influence each other, and the cluster, switchback and two-sided designs that address it.

Interview questions here are rarely about formulas alone. "How would you test this new ranker?" invites a design: which stage first, which unit to randomise, which metric decides, which guardrails block, how long to run, how to recognise novelty, and what changes in a marketplace. Being able to estimate the sample size on the spot and to walk through an interleaved list by hand turns a generic answer into a credible one.

The aim is a practical experimentation programme for a ranking team: fast pruning of many ideas, careful measurement of a few, and a long-term view that keeps short-term wins from accumulating into long-term losses.

Imagine a wine shop deciding between two suppliers. One way is to give half the customers wine from supplier A and the other half wine from supplier B for a month, then compare how much each half bought. Customers differ enormously (some buy a bottle a year, some a case a week), so the difference between suppliers is buried in the difference between customers, and you need a lot of customers to see it. Another way is a blind tasting: give each customer one glass from each supplier, side by side, and ask which they prefer. Each customer compares the two directly, so their personal taste for wine in general cancels out, and a few dozen tastings may settle the question. But a tasting only tells you which wine people prefer in the moment; it cannot tell you whether customers will buy more wine over the year, or keep coming back.

That is the difference between an A/B test and interleaving for rankers. The A/B test gives each user one ranker and compares groups of users: unbiased and able to measure anything, including long-term behaviour, but noisy because users differ. Interleaving gives each user one list that mixes both rankers' results and sees which ranker's items the user engages with: very sensitive, because each user compares both, but limited to relative preference in the moment.

The analogy breaks in two places that matter. In a tasting, both glasses are poured side by side; in a ranked list, items occupy different positions, and position strongly affects engagement (rec10). Interleaving methods must therefore make sure each ranker is equally likely to supply each position, which is what the coin flips in team-draft interleaving do. And wine customers do not affect each other's choices much; users of a marketplace or a social network do, which is the interference problem at the end of the lesson.

A second useful distinction is between what a test can detect and what a launch will do. A test detects the difference between arms during the test, among the users in it, with everyone else unchanged. A launch changes the experience for everyone, permanently. The two coincide only when users do not affect each other, behaviour does not change with time, and the test population represents everyone. Much of this lesson is about the cases where they do not coincide, and about designs that bring the test closer to the launch.

One more contrast is worth holding: an A/B test answers "what happens if we launch?" and interleaving answers "which ranker do users prefer when both are available?". The first question is the one the business must answer; the second is cheaper and, for rankers, usually points the same way. Using the cheap question to choose which expensive question to ask is the central idea of the staged approach.

The overall picture used by mature teams is a staged funnel of evidence, much like the ranking funnel of rec1: cheap offline metrics prune many ideas; interleaving prunes the survivors quickly on small traffic; A/B tests measure the few best carefully on the metrics that matter; and long-term holdouts check that the sum of many small launches is actually good.

1. The A/B test for a ranking change, and what it costs

The basic design (ml16): randomly assign users (not requests, so each user has a consistent experience) to control (current ranker) or treatment (new ranker); run for a fixed period; compare a pre-chosen metric between arms with a statistical test; check that the split is the expected 50/50 (a sample ratio mismatch means the assignment or logging is broken and the result cannot be trusted).

The cost is set by variance. For a proportion metric such as click-through rate p, a widely used rule of thumb for 80% power at a two-sided 5% significance level is n per arm ≈ 16 σ² / δ², where σ² = p(1 − p) and δ is the absolute effect to detect (math7 derives the exact version). Take a feed with a 5% click rate and a target of detecting a 1% relative lift, so δ = 0.0005: n ≈ 16 × 0.05 × 0.95 / 0.0005² = 16 × 0.0475 / 0.00000025 ≈ 3.0 million users per arm. For a 0.5% relative lift, four times as many: 12 million per arm. Ranking changes on mature products often have true effects of this size, which is why teams run tests on large shares of traffic for weeks.

Three things make it cheaper. Variance reduction: CUPED (math7) uses each user's pre-experiment value of the metric as a covariate, removing the part of the variance explained by who the user is; reductions of a third or more in required sample size are common for engagement metrics. More sensitive metrics: metrics closer to the change (engagement with the ranked surface) are less noisy than site-wide metrics, though less directly tied to value. Interleaving, below, for comparing rankers.

Two design details matter for ranking specifically. Randomise by user, and keep users in their arm for the whole test, because rankers have memory effects (yesterday's recommendations shape today's behaviour). And make sure the whole pipeline is consistent per arm: if the new ranker changes candidates, the re-ranker and logging must handle both, and caches keyed by user must not leak one arm's results into the other.

2. Interleaving, worked

Interleaving puts both rankers in front of the same user at the same time. The design challenge is fairness with respect to position: if ranker A always supplied the top item, A would win because of position bias, not quality. Team-draft interleaving (Radlinski and colleagues) solves it like picking teams for a game: in each round, a coin flip decides which ranker picks first; each ranker then adds its highest-ranked item not already in the list; repeat until the list is full. Each item is labelled with the "team" that picked it.

Worked example. Ranker A's list: a, b, c, d, e. Ranker B's list: b, f, a, g, c. Build six slots.

  • Round 1, coin says A first. A picks its top item, a (team A). B picks its highest not yet chosen: its top is b, so b (team B).
  • Round 2, coin says B first. B's highest unchosen: b is taken, so f (team B). A's highest unchosen: a and b are taken, so c (team A).
  • Round 3, coin says A first. A picks d (team A). B's highest unchosen: b, f, a are taken, so g (team B).

The user sees a, b, f, c, d, g with teams A, B, B, A, A, B. Each team has three items, and across many users each position is equally likely to belong to either team, so position bias cancels in expectation. If this user plays b and d, each team gets one credit: a tie for this user. Another user who plays only f gives B the win. For each user, the ranker with more credited engagement wins; ties are recorded.

Aggregate across users. Suppose 1,000 users engaged: A won for 520, B for 430, and 50 tied. A simple preference measure is (A wins − B wins) / users = (520 − 430) / 1,000 = 0.09. A sign test on the non-tied users treats each as a coin flip under the null hypothesis of no preference: z = (520 − 430) / √(520 + 430) = 90 / 30.8 ≈ 2.9, significant at the 1% level. A thousand engaged users can be enough, where the equivalent A/B test might need millions, because each user's general activity level cancels out of their own comparison.

Netflix's 2017 engineering post describes using a variant of team-draft interleaving for rows of titles on the homepage, with preference measured as which ranker produced the greater share of hours viewed within the interleaved row. Comparing interleaving with A/B tests on the same pairs of rankers using bootstrap subsampling, they found interleaving "can require >100× fewer subscribers to correctly determine ranker preference even compared to the most sensitive A/B metric", and that the interleaving preference correlated strongly with their most sensitive A/B metric across many rankers tested against production.

Variants exist: balanced interleaving (an older method, used in Joachims's unbiased-LTR evaluation, rec10), probabilistic interleaving, and multileaving, which compares more than two rankers in one list. All share the same strengths and limits.

3. What interleaving cannot do

Netflix's post lists the limits plainly. Interleaving is a relative measurement: it says users preferred A's items to B's in a merged list, not how much a launch of A would change retention, revenue or total viewing. Building it is engineering-heavy: the merged list must go through the same downstream logic, and business logic applied after ranking (deduplication, diversity re-ranking, rec12) can change which team's items survive and bias the comparison, requiring consistency checks. And it is short-term: it measures preference on the visits during the test, not learning effects over months. The standard answer is a two-stage process: interleaving to prune many candidates quickly, then an A/B test, now affordable at larger sample sizes per arm because there are fewer arms, to measure the absolute and longer-term effect of the best few.

4. Choosing metrics: primary, secondary, guardrails

An experiment's metrics should be chosen before it starts, in three tiers.

The primary metric (or overall evaluation criterion, OEC) is what the decision rests on. It should reflect user value, be sensitive enough for the test's size, and resist gaming. Click-through rate fails the last test: clickbait, larger thumbnails or moving items up all raise it. Better candidates: successful sessions (sessions containing at least one satisfying engagement such as a watch longer than a threshold), engagement from satisfied interactions, purchases or conversions, return visits within a week. Many teams validate their OEC historically: across past experiments, which short-term metric best predicted the long-term outcome they care about?

Secondary metrics explain the result: clicks, watch time, likes and hides per surface, distribution of engagement by content type, by user segment. They are not decision criteria; testing many of them invites false positives (math7's multiple-comparisons problem).

Guardrail metrics must not get worse beyond a threshold, regardless of the primary result: latency and errors; hides, reports and complaints; diversity and catalogue coverage (rec8); exposure for new creators or small sellers (rec12); ad revenue where the change should be neutral. Guardrails are often tested as non-inferiority: "we are confident the drop, if any, is smaller than 0.5%". A ranker that raises watch time by 1% and raises reports by 10% should not ship.

5. Time: novelty, learning and the long term

Users are not static. A visibly changed recommendation surface attracts exploration: people click new kinds of items out of curiosity, producing a novelty effect that fades. Conversely, some changes produce effects that grow: users gradually learn that a surface has become more useful and visit it more (or that it has become annoying and avoid it). These learning effects can take weeks or months.

Two practices detect them. Cohort analysis: plot the treatment effect not by calendar date but by days since each user first entered the experiment. A curve that starts high and decays is novelty; one that starts low and climbs is learning; a flat curve suggests the measured effect is stable. Longer tests and holdouts: when a curve has not flattened, the test is not done.

Google's 2015 KDD paper "Focusing on the Long-term: It's Good for Users and Business", by Hohnhold, O'Brien and Tang, is the classic demonstration that short-term results can mislead. Studying ads on Google search, they developed experiment designs to measure long-term user learning (described below) and found ads blindness and sightedness: users' propensity to click ads changes with the quality of ads they have previously seen. Showing more, lower-quality ads raised revenue in the short term but taught users to ignore ads, eroding it over time. Their applications included a change to the ads auction that increased the weight of ad quality, and a 50% reduction of the ad load on Google's mobile search interface.

6. Long-term holdouts and surrogate metrics

A team that ships 20 changes a year, each tested for two weeks, never measures their cumulative effect over a year. A long-term holdout keeps a small, random share of users (say 1-2%) on the system as it was at the start of a period, or without a class of features (no exploration slots, an older ad load), for many months. Comparing them with everyone else measures the cumulative long-term effect of everything launched, including learning effects and feedback loops (rec10), on retention and ecosystem metrics. The cost is real (those users get an older product), so holdouts are small, time-limited and refreshed.

Because long-term outcomes such as 6-month retention cannot be waited for in every test, teams build surrogate metrics: short-term metrics validated, on past experiments and holdouts, to predict the long-term outcome. Google's paper did exactly this, building a model that predicted long-term ad engagement from short-term measurements of ad quality. A surrogate is only as good as its validation; re-check it periodically against holdout results.

7. Interference: when users affect each other

The A/B test's core assumption is that one user's treatment does not affect another user's outcome (the stable unit treatment value assumption). Ranking systems break it in several ways.

Shared inventory. In a marketplace (rooms, rides, jobs, limited stock), if treatment users get better rankings, they book the best listings, leaving control users with worse ones. The treatment looks better partly because it took from control; launched to everyone, the effect would be smaller. The bias can even reverse the sign of the conclusion.

Supply-side reactions. Creators, sellers and hosts respond to the demand they see. A ranker that sends more traffic to new creators in the treatment arm changes those creators' behaviour (they upload more), which then affects control users who see the same creators.

Social spillovers. In social products, a treatment that makes treatment users share more changes what their friends, including control users, see.

Designs that reduce interference:

  • Cluster randomisation: randomise whole groups whose members interact mostly with each other (cities in a ride-hailing market, social clusters found by graph partitioning). Interference stays within an arm. Cost: far fewer independent units, hence much less power.
  • Switchback experiments: alternate an entire market between treatment and control over time slots (for example every hour, randomised), used when users cannot be separated, as in dispatch and pricing. Carry-over between adjacent slots must be handled, for example by discarding transition periods.
  • Two-sided randomisation: randomise both buyers and sellers, which allows estimating how large the interference bias is from the cells where treated buyers meet untreated sellers.
  • Budget or supply splitting: in ads and marketplaces, give each arm its own share of the limited resource (each advertiser's budget split between arms), so arms do not compete for it.

8. Running many experiments at once

A ranking organisation runs dozens of experiments simultaneously: retrieval, ranking, re-ranking, UI, ads. Giving each its own exclusive slice of users would leave too little traffic per test. Google's overlapping experiment infrastructure (Tang and colleagues, KDD 2010) organised experiments into layers: experiments in the same layer (changing the same component) are mutually exclusive, while experiments in different layers are randomised independently, so each user can be in one experiment per layer. Interactions between layers are assumed small and checked occasionally. Platform hygiene matters as much as design: A/A tests (both arms identical) confirm that the platform does not produce false differences, sample-ratio checks run on every test, and metric definitions are shared.

9. Experimenting on each stage of the funnel

Experiments on different funnel stages (rec1) need slightly different handling. A retrieval change alters which candidates the ranker sees; its effect depends on the ranker, so test it with the production ranker, and add funnel diagnostics to the experiment (share of shown items from the new source, recall against the ranker's choices). Interleaving at the retrieval level is awkward, because the two candidate sets feed one ranker; teams usually A/B test retrieval changes directly. A ranking change is the natural fit for interleaving followed by A/B. A re-ranking change (diversity, freshness, ads, rules) affects the page as a whole and its value often appears in session and long-term metrics, so it needs A/B tests with longer durations and holdouts; interleaving is unsuitable because re-ranking acts on the whole page. Changes to features or data pipelines should first be validated for training/serving parity (rec9), then tested like ranking changes.

10. Reading a result and deciding

The decision rule should be written before the test starts. A typical one: ship if the primary metric's 95% confidence interval excludes zero in the positive direction, no guardrail is breached beyond its non-inferiority margin, there is no sample ratio mismatch, and the cohort curve has stabilised. Results that are positive but inconclusive are common for ranking; the options are to extend the test (if the cohort curve is stable and power was the problem), to run a larger second test, or to judge the change on secondary evidence (interleaving, offline gains) with explicit acknowledgement of the risk. Results that are positive on the primary metric but mixed on secondary metrics deserve a look at what changed: a ranker that raises watch time by showing longer videos is different from one that raises it by finding better ones. And every decision, positive or negative, should be recorded with its stage-by-stage numbers, so the organisation learns which offline and interleaving signals predict A/B outcomes.

11. A staged programme, end to end

Put together, a healthy ranking experimentation programme has four stages, each cheaper and less conclusive than the next. Offline evaluation (rec8) with counterfactual estimates where propensities exist (rec10) screens dozens of ideas. Interleaving on a few percent of traffic for a few days prunes the survivors to the best two or three. A/B tests with a pre-registered OEC, guardrails, power analysis, variance reduction and cohort analysis measure absolute effects over weeks. Long-term holdouts measure the cumulative effect of everything over months and validate the surrogate metrics the A/B tests rely on. After each launch, record the effect at every stage; over time this calibrates how much each stage's results predict the next, which is the most valuable dataset an experimentation team owns.

Why interleaving is so sensitive

In an A/B test, the variance of the difference between arms includes the variance between users: a heavy user and a light user differ by orders of magnitude in engagement, and that difference has nothing to do with the rankers. Interleaving makes each user their own control: the outcome per user is a comparison between the two rankers' items shown to that same user at the same time, so user-level variation largely cancels. Only the within-user noise of which items they happened to engage with remains. That is the same principle as a paired design in statistics (ev7's paired bootstrap), applied inside a single page.

Credit assignment in interleaving

Team-draft interleaving credits each engagement to the team that contributed the item. Complications arise when both rankers would have contributed an item (it appears high in both lists): team-draft gives it to whichever picked it first, which is random by the coin flips, so it is fair in expectation. Engagements that are not per-item (session length, return visits) cannot be credited at all, which is another reason interleaving measures only immediate preference. Netflix's choice of "share of hours viewed within the interleaved row" is a sensible per-item, value-weighted credit for a video service.

The long-term methodology in Google's study

Hohnhold, O'Brien and Tang compare several designs for measuring learning effects. In the post-period method, after a long treatment period the treatment is switched off and both groups receive the same experience; any remaining difference in behaviour is what users learned during the treatment, isolated from the treatment's direct effect. The cookie-cookie-day design compares groups that entered the treatment at different times, measuring, on each day, users with different lengths of exposure, which separates learning from seasonal and system changes. The general lesson for recommender teams: to measure learning, you must separate "the treatment's effect while it is on" from "what users have learned from having had it", and that requires designs that switch treatments off or stagger them.

Sequential testing and peeking

Ranking teams watch dashboards daily, and stopping a test early when it looks significant inflates false positives (math7 showed peeking can raise a nominal 5% error rate several-fold). Sequential designs with always-valid confidence intervals or group-sequential boundaries allow monitoring and early stopping for clear harm or clear benefit without that inflation. For ranking, early stopping for harm (guardrail breaches) is especially valuable; early stopping for benefit should be rarer because of novelty effects: an early win may be curiosity.

Counterfactual estimates as a pre-test

Where the logging policy randomises and logs propensities (rec10), off-policy estimators can estimate a candidate ranker's performance before any live traffic, and some teams use them as a stage between offline metrics and interleaving. They are most reliable when the candidate stays close to production (most of its choices were plausible under the logging policy), which is also when online tests are least likely to show large effects. Their main value is ruling out clearly worse candidates cheaply and calibrating expectations; a positive counterfactual estimate is a reason to test, not to launch.

Heterogeneous effects and who a ranking change helps

An average effect of +0.3% can hide +2% for new users and −0.5% for heavy users. Ranking changes often have such heterogeneity, because they shift which content is shown to whom. Pre-registered segment analyses (new versus established users, heavy versus light, country, device) catch it; trawling dozens of segments after the fact finds spurious effects. Some teams estimate heterogeneous effects with causal machine-learning methods, but the practical minimum is a handful of pre-declared segments with multiple-comparison corrections.

Experiment duration and weekly cycles

Behaviour differs by day of week (weekend viewing, weekday commuting), so tests should cover whole weeks, usually at least two, even when power is reached sooner; a test that runs Monday to Thursday measures a different population of sessions than the product serves. Holidays and major events (a sports final, a product launch by a competitor) can swamp ranking effects for days; check whether the treatment effect is stable across them rather than discarding them silently, and avoid starting tests on unusual days.

Bandits versus experiments, revisited

rec11 noted that bandits allocate adaptively to maximise reward, which complicates inference. For ranking, a pragmatic split is: bandits for many short-lived variants (thumbnails, headlines, promotional modules), interleaving and A/B tests for rankers and policies. Adaptive experiments that shift traffic towards a winning arm during a test exist and can reduce the cost of testing bad variants, but their estimates need corrections for the adaptive allocation, and long-term effects still need a fixed holdout.

The public case: Netflix's two-stage experimentation with interleaving

In November 2017, Netflix engineers Joshua Parks, Juliette Aurisset and Michael Ramm described how Netflix accelerated testing of personalisation algorithms. The homepage of the service, then serving over 100 million members, is built from many rows, each ordered by a ranking algorithm. As those algorithms matured, the post explains, discerning wins in A/B metrics required increasingly large sample sizes and long durations, which limited how many ideas could be tested.

Their response was a two-stage process. The first stage, a fast pruning step, uses interleaving: a variant of team-draft interleaving in which a coin flip decides which ranker contributes the first title to a row and the rankers then alternate, each adding its highest-ranked title not already present. Member preference is measured by which ranker produced the greater share of hours viewed within the interleaved row, with viewing credited to the contributing ranker. The second stage is a traditional A/B test on the few best algorithms, powered with larger samples per cell to measure longer-term member behaviour such as retention.

They validated the first stage on two requirements. Sensitivity: running interleaving and an A/B test in parallel on the same pair of rankers and using bootstrap subsampling to compare the sample sizes needed, they found interleaving "can require >100× fewer subscribers to correctly determine ranker preference even compared to the most sensitive A/B metric". Alignment: across many rankers tested against production, the change in interleaving preference showed "a very strong correlation" with the change in their most sensitive A/B metric. They also stated the limits: interleaving is engineering-intensive, business logic can interfere and needs automated consistency checks, and it is a relative measure that cannot directly measure retention, which is why the A/B stage remains.

How ranking experiments go wrong in production

Sample ratio mismatch. A bug in the new ranker causes some treatment requests to time out and fall back to control's results, or logging drops some treatment events; arms end up 50.4/49.6 instead of 50/50 on millions of users, which is statistically impossible by chance. Every metric is now suspect. Check SRM automatically and stop the analysis until it is explained.

Shared caches. Recommendations cached per user leak between arms when a user's assignment changes, or a shared item-feature cache warmed by treatment traffic speeds up control. Key caches by experiment arm.

Interleaving bias from downstream logic. A diversity re-ranker applied after interleaving removes more of one ranker's items because that ranker tends to propose similar items; the comparison measures re-ranker interaction, not ranker quality. Apply consistency checks and, where possible, interleave after all logic.

Novelty misread as a win. A two-week test shows a lift that is entirely novelty; the launch's long-term effect is negative. Cohort plots and holdouts catch it.

Interference ignored. A marketplace ranking test shows +4% bookings in treatment, but total bookings across both arms did not change: treatment took bookings from control. Check total outcomes across arms and use cluster or switchback designs for supply-constrained products.

Culture and process

Experimentation programmes fail as often through process as through statistics. Common problems: launching on secondary metrics when the primary is flat ("but clicks went up"), re-running a test until it is significant, changing the primary metric after seeing results, and teams with revenue targets owning their own guardrails. The defences are boring and effective: pre-registration of metrics and decision rules, a central experimentation review that checks SRM, power and guardrails before launch, published results including failures, and a long-term holdout owned by a team without a stake in any individual launch.

What to monitor in an experimentation platform

Per experiment: SRM, exposure counts, pre-period balance on key metrics, primary and guardrail metrics with confidence intervals, cohort effect curves. Platform-wide: A/A false-positive rate, number of concurrent experiments per layer, launch decisions and their stage-by-stage effects (offline, interleaving, A/B, holdout). Long-term: holdout versus production on retention, engagement and ecosystem metrics, reviewed quarterly.

Design: testing a new ranker for a food-delivery app's home feed

A food-delivery app wants to test a new restaurant ranker on its home feed. Restaurants have limited kitchen capacity at peak times; couriers are shared across all orders in a city. Offline, the ranker is 5% better on nDCG@10 of orders. The team proposes a two-week user-level A/B test with orders per user as the primary metric. Critique the plan and propose a better one.

Answer

Problems. (1) Interference: treatment users who get better rankings will order from the best restaurants and use couriers, increasing wait times and sold-out items for control users in the same city; user-level comparison overstates the effect (treatment gains partly come from control losses). (2) Orders per user is a reasonable primary metric but misses satisfaction (late deliveries, cancellations) and supply effects (restaurant overload). (3) Two weeks may capture novelty but not learning (users discovering new favourite restaurants changes ordering over months). (4) No pruning stage: if several ranker variants exist, testing each this way is slow.

Better plan. Stage 1: interleaving on the home feed with a small share of users to compare variants quickly on order and click preference; interleaving shares supply within a user's list too, but a small traffic share limits supply effects, and it is only a pruning signal. Stage 2: a switchback or city-cluster experiment for the best variant: randomise cities (or city-hour slots in a switchback with discarded transition periods) so supply competition stays within an arm. Primary metric: completed orders per active user (or per city-hour), with guardrails on delivery time, cancellations, sold-out rate, restaurant acceptance rate and small-restaurant order share. Use CUPED with each city's pre-period orders to recover power lost to the small number of clusters. Run four weeks with cohort analysis, and keep a long-term holdout of a few cities for the quarter. Compare the cluster estimate with a parallel user-level estimate to measure how much interference bias the user-level design would have had.
