A product team at an online shop watches a demo. A user types "something to keep my coffee hot on long hikes that won't leak in my backpack", and a large language model, given the shop's top results, explains which three flasks fit, why, and which one to avoid because its lid design leaks when tipped. It is the best search experience anyone in the room has seen. Then the engineering lead does the arithmetic. The shop's search receives about 5,000 queries per second at peak. The demo took about two seconds and used a few thousand tokens. At current prices for a small, fast model, doing this for every query would cost on the order of a million dollars a day and would make every search ten times slower than the latency budget.

That tension is the theme of this lesson. Large language models bring abilities that the classical stack lacks: they understand long, vague, natural-language requests; they know a lot about the world (that "keeping coffee hot" means vacuum insulation); they can compare candidates and explain trade-offs; they can read messy product pages and extract clean attributes; they can judge relevance roughly as well as trained human labellers. And they are slow and expensive per call, compared with the microseconds and fractions of a cent per thousand items that retrieval and ranking cost.

So the question is never "should we use LLMs in search and recommendation?" but "where in the stack does their ability justify their cost, and how do we capture most of the benefit without paying the per-request price?" The answers that have emerged in research and production are fairly consistent. Use LLMs offline, where cost is paid once per item or per label rather than per request: enriching content, generating training data and relevance labels. Use them on small candidate sets for high-value queries, as re-rankers. Use them as teachers whose judgements are distilled into small, fast models. Use them in new interfaces, such as conversational recommenders, where traffic is lower and the value per interaction is higher. And use new retrieval paradigms, such as generative retrieval with semantic IDs, where they solve real problems like cold start.

This is also where interviews for AI-engineering roles at search and recommendation companies increasingly go: "how would you use an LLM to improve our search?" is a test of exactly this judgement. A strong answer names a specific placement, estimates its calls per second, cost per day and latency, explains how it will be grounded and evaluated, and proposes how to distil it if it works. A weak answer puts a frontier model in the request path and stops.

This lesson works through each: LLM re-rankers (RankGPT's listwise prompting, sliding windows and permutation distillation), generative retrieval (DSI and TIGER's semantic IDs), conversational recommenders, LLM-generated features and content, LLM relevance labels as used at Bing, LLM query understanding, and finally the cost and latency arithmetic at 10,000 queries per second that decides where all of this can run. All provider-specific numbers are as of October 2026 and change often.

Think of a large research library with a team of fast clerks and one brilliant but expensive scholar. The clerks (retrieval and ranking) can pull and sort thousands of books a second using catalogues and well-practised rules. The scholar can read a patron's rambling question, understand what they really mean, read ten books side by side and say which one answers it best and why, but takes several minutes and charges by the hour. Sending every patron to the scholar is impossible. A well-run library uses the scholar in three ways: to train the clerks (the scholar labels examples and writes better catalogue entries, offline), to handle the hardest cases (a short list of books for a difficult question), and to run a premium desk for patrons who want a conversation.

That is the map of LLMs in search and recommendation:

Read the diagram from top to bottom as a gradient of affordability. The top row runs once per item or per label, in batch, where an LLM's cost is divided by the number of requests that later benefit; a few thousand dollars can improve every search for months. The middle row runs per request but can be narrowed: query understanding only for rare or long queries, re-ranking only for the top few candidates and only when it changes the answer. The bottom row is a different product, an assistant that a small share of users choose, where each interaction is worth more and can carry an LLM's full cost. Most of the engineering judgement in this lesson is deciding which row a proposed use belongs in, and moving it upwards when it starts in the wrong place.

The analogy breaks in two ways that matter. The scholar is reliable; an LLM sometimes confidently invents facts, so anything it produces that users will see, or that models will learn from, must be grounded and checked. And unlike a scholar, an LLM can be copied: its judgements on millions of examples can be used to train a small, fast model that imitates it. That second fact, distillation, is the single most important pattern in this lesson: the expensive model teaches; the cheap model serves.

A practical way to judge any proposed LLM use is to ask three questions. How many calls per day will it make, and at what cost per call? How long can each call take before it breaks a latency budget? And what happens when it is wrong? Uses that run once per item, tolerate minutes of latency and are checked before reaching users are easy wins. Uses that run per request on many items, inside a 200 ms budget, with outputs shown directly to users, are the hardest, and usually need distillation, caching or a narrower scope.

1. What LLMs add, and what they cost

Three capabilities make LLMs attractive here. Language understanding: they interpret long, vague, compositional requests ("a gift for my dad who likes gardening but has a bad back") that keyword and embedding retrieval handle poorly. World knowledge: they know that "keeping coffee hot" implies vacuum insulation, that a film is a sequel, that two products are compatible. Comparison and explanation: given several candidates, they can judge which best fits and say why.

The costs are equally concrete. As of October 2026, Claude Haiku 4.5 costs $1 per million input tokens and $5 per million output tokens, and Claude Sonnet 5.5 $2 and $10; the batch API halves these for work that can wait, and cached prompt prefixes cost a tenth of the input price on most models. A call has latency of hundreds of milliseconds to seconds: time to first token plus generation time for the output. Compare a two-tower retriever (microseconds of compute per item) or a ranker (a fraction of a millisecond per item on CPU). Every design in this lesson is shaped by that gap of several orders of magnitude.

2. LLM re-rankers: pointwise, pairwise and listwise

The most direct use is re-ranking the top of a list, the job a cross-encoder does in rec7. There are three ways to prompt an LLM for it.

Pointwise: for each candidate, ask "Is this passage relevant to the query? Answer yes or no" and rank by the probability of "yes" (or ask for a 0-3 grade). It is simple and parallel, but scores from separate calls are not well calibrated against each other, and it uses one call per candidate.

Pairwise: ask "Which of these two passages better answers the query?" for pairs, then aggregate. It uses the model's strength at comparison but needs many calls (up to n² for n candidates, or n log n with a sorting algorithm), and suffers from position bias: the model may prefer whichever passage appears first (ev4).

Listwise: give the model the query and a numbered list of candidates and ask for the order, for example "[3] > [1] > [5] > [2] > [4]". One call ranks many candidates, and the model compares them all in context. Sun and colleagues' RankGPT (EMNLP 2023) showed that this works remarkably well: properly instructed ChatGPT and GPT-4 delivered results competitive with, and in GPT-4's case better than, state-of-the-art supervised re-rankers on TREC and BEIR benchmarks, and the authors built a new test set (NovelEval) from recent knowledge to check that the results were not due to the models having memorised benchmark data.

Sliding windows. The context window limits how many passages fit in one prompt. RankGPT re-ranked BM25's top 100 passages using a window of 20 and a step of 10, working from the end of the list to the front: first order passages 81-100, then 71-90, and so on to 1-20. Because each window overlaps the previous one by 10, a strong passage found near the bottom is carried upwards window by window, like a bubble rising. The cost is about nine calls per query for 100 passages, each with 20 passages of text.

Failure modes specific to LLM re-rankers: sensitivity to the initial order of candidates in the prompt (the model partly trusts the order it is given); malformed or incomplete permutations (missing or repeated identifiers, which must be repaired by code); occasional refusals or commentary instead of a ranking; and variability across model versions. Evaluation must be run for each model and prompt change, on your own queries, with metrics from rec8.

3. Distillation: the LLM as teacher

RankGPT also showed the way around the cost. The authors generated permutations with ChatGPT for a set of queries and trained small specialised models to reproduce them, using a pairwise loss on the LLM's orderings (rec7's RankNet-style loss applied to the teacher's preferences). Their distilled 440M-parameter model outperformed a 3-billion-parameter supervised model on the BEIR benchmark. A student of that size can run as a cross-encoder within a production latency budget.

The general pattern applies everywhere in this lesson. Use the LLM where its judgement is best (comparing candidates, understanding intent, assessing relevance) on a large, carefully chosen set of examples offline; train a small model on its outputs; serve the small model; periodically refresh the teacher's labels on new data and new query types. The teacher's cost becomes a training cost, paid once per example, and the serving cost stays close to that of a normal cross-encoder.

4. Generative retrieval and semantic IDs

A more radical idea replaces the retrieval index itself. In standard retrieval, a model embeds the query and an ANN index finds nearby item embeddings (rec4). In generative retrieval, a sequence-to-sequence transformer reads the query (or the user's history) and generates the identifier of the relevant item, token by token, with constrained decoding so only valid identifiers can be produced. The index lives inside the model's parameters. Tay and colleagues' Differentiable Search Index (DSI, NeurIPS 2022) demonstrated this for documents, training one transformer both to map document text to its identifier and to map queries to identifiers.

The key design choice is the identifier. Arbitrary numbers ("item 48213") give the model nothing to generalise from: it must memorise each item separately. Semantic IDs fix this by building each item's identifier from its content. Rajput and colleagues' TIGER (NeurIPS 2023) embedded each item's text with a pre-trained encoder, then compressed the embedding with a residual-quantised variational autoencoder (RQ-VAE): the first codebook assigns the embedding to its nearest code (a coarse cluster), the second codebook quantises the remaining error, the third the error after that. An item's semantic ID is the tuple of codebook indices, such as (12, 201, 7). Items with similar content share leading codes, like a postcode in which the first part names the region and later parts the street.

TIGER then trained a transformer to take a user's history as a sequence of semantic IDs and generate the semantic ID of the next item. It reported significant improvements over earlier sequential recommenders on its datasets and, importantly, better retrieval for items with no prior interaction history: because a new item's semantic ID comes from its content, the model can generate it even though it has never seen that item in training, a direct attack on cold start (rec11).

The practical obstacles are real. New items need semantic IDs computed and, ideally, the model needs to learn them; codebooks must be updated as the catalogue drifts; decoding with beam search to return hundreds of candidates is slower than an ANN lookup; and the infrastructure is less mature. Most production systems in 2026 still use embedding retrieval with ANN as the backbone, with semantic IDs appearing as features, as compact item representations shared across models, or as an additional candidate source evaluated by its marginal contribution in the funnel (rec1).

5. Conversational recommenders

LLMs make a different interface practical: recommendation through dialogue. A user says "I want a laptop for video editing, under 1,500 euros, not too heavy"; the system asks "Do you edit 4K footage?"; then presents three options with trade-offs and refines when the user says "the battery matters more than the screen". The LLM handles the conversation: understanding requests, asking good clarifying questions (preference elicitation), comparing options and explaining.

What it must not do is invent the options. A catalogue of millions of items, with prices and availability changing by the minute, cannot live in a prompt, and an LLM asked to recommend products from memory will cheerfully name models that do not exist or quote outdated prices. The working architecture is grounded: the LLM translates the conversation into structured constraints and a search query (category = laptop, price ≤ 1,500, weight ≤ 1.6 kg, GPU class ≥ X), the existing search and ranking stack returns real, in-stock items with current data, and the LLM presents and explains those items, citing their actual attributes. This is retrieval-augmented generation (lm9) with the product catalogue as the corpus and the ranker in the loop, often implemented with tool calls (lm16).

Evaluation is about task success, not fluency: did the user find and buy (or save) something suitable, how many turns did it take, how often were constraints violated or attributes misstated? Because conversational traffic is a small fraction of total search traffic and each conversation has high value, LLM costs that would be impossible for every search box query can be acceptable here.

6. LLMs for features and content, offline

The least glamorous use is often the most valuable: running LLMs offline over the catalogue to improve what every other component sees.

Item enrichment: extract normalised attributes from messy product titles and descriptions ("Men's Trail Runner GTX 2 - Size 10 - Blk" becomes brand, model, gender, size, colour, waterproof = yes), write concise summaries, assign items to the taxonomy, generate tags for videos from transcripts, and identify duplicates across sellers. These become lexical fields (rec2), filter attributes (rec5), item-tower features (rec4) and ranker features (rec6).

Synthetic queries: for each item or document, generate queries a user might type to find it. These pairs train retrievers where query logs are thin (new markets, new item types, long-tail items). Spotify's podcast search (rec5) used a sequence-to-sequence model to generate synthetic queries as one of its training sources; LLMs make this easier and more varied. The quality risks are the same as for any synthetic data (ev10): filter, deduplicate and validate against real behaviour.

Cost is per item, not per request. Enriching 10 million items with about 500 input tokens and 100 output tokens each through the Claude batch API with Haiku 4.5 ($0.50 and $2.50 per million tokens, half the standard price) costs 10 million × (500 × $0.0000005 + 100 × $0.0000025) = 10 million × $0.0005 = about $5,000, once, plus a little for new and changed items each day. That is small next to the value of better attributes for every search and recommendation.

The risks: hallucinated attributes (an LLM declares a jacket waterproof because jackets often are), biased or inconsistent tagging, and silent changes when the generating model or prompt changes, which shift feature distributions under a trained ranker (training/serving skew, rec9). Mitigations: prefer extraction over generation (instruct the model to only use the source text and to output "unknown"), validate samples against human review, version the generator and regenerate the whole catalogue consistently when it changes, and mark LLM-derived fields so their effect can be measured.

7. LLM relevance labels

Ranking models need labelled examples to train and evaluate (rec7, rec8), and human labelling is slow and expensive. LLMs can produce relevance labels at scale. The approach mirrors LLM-as-judge for generative outputs (ev4), with the same biases to watch: position, verbosity, sensitivity to prompt wording.

Thomas, Spielman, Craswell and Mitra's SIGIR 2024 paper describes how Bing does this, and is this lesson's public case (see the production section). The essential discipline: use a small set of high-quality labels, in Bing's case preferences from real searchers about results for their own queries, to select the model and prompt; then label at scale; then monitor with ongoing human re-labelling of samples. Labels produced this way feed ranker training (as targets or as features for distillation) and offline evaluation, letting teams measure many more queries and systems than human labelling allowed.

8. LLMs in query understanding

Query understanding (rec5) is a natural fit for language models: rewriting a vague query into a precise one, expanding it with related terms, extracting attributes, classifying intent and handling long natural-language queries. The obstacle is latency: query understanding sits at the start of the critical path with perhaps 15-20 ms in total. The patterns that work: cache LLM rewrites for head queries (the same million queries cover a large share of traffic), generated in batch and reviewed; run a small fine-tuned model, distilled from an LLM, for real-time understanding of everything else; and invoke a live LLM only for the rare long or zero-result queries, where an extra few hundred milliseconds is better than a useless result page, possibly asynchronously (show initial results, then refine). Hypothetical-answer generation for dense retrieval (embed an LLM-written answer instead of the question) fits the same pattern: valuable for long questions, too slow for every query.

9. Cost and latency at 10,000 QPS, worked

Take rec1's service: 10,000 queries per second, 200 ms p99 budget. Suppose we want an LLM to re-rank the top 20 results per query.

Tokens per query. 20 passages or product descriptions of about 100 tokens each, plus a 300-token instruction and query: about 2,300 input tokens. Output: a permutation of 20 identifiers, about 60 tokens.

Cost per query with Claude Haiku 4.5 ($1 input, $5 output per million, October 2026): 2,300 × $0.000001 + 60 × $0.000005 = $0.0023 + $0.0003 = $0.0026. With Sonnet 5.5 ($2, $10): $0.0052. Prompt caching helps little here, because the passages differ per query; only the 300-token instruction is shared.

Cost per day at 10,000 QPS: $0.0026 × 10,000 × 86,400 = about $2.25 million per day with Haiku, about $4.5 million with Sonnet. Even if 60% of queries hit a cache of re-ranked head queries, Haiku costs about $0.9 million per day.

Comparison. A distilled cross-encoder scoring the same 20 pairs per query is 200,000 pairs per second; if one GPU handles about 2,000 such pairs per second, that is about 100 GPUs, roughly $4,800 per day at $2 per GPU-hour. The LLM costs several hundred times more.

Latency. An LLM call with a few thousand input tokens and 60 output tokens takes, very roughly, a few hundred milliseconds to over a second end to end, far beyond a 200 ms total budget with perhaps 50 ms for ranking. Running it would require asynchronous presentation or a much larger latency budget.

The conclusion is not "never use an LLM online" but "be precise about where". Re-rank with an LLM for a small, high-value slice (long natural-language queries, a premium assistant, enterprise search with tens of queries per second), cache aggressively, trigger only when the first-stage ranker is uncertain, and distil its judgements into a cross-encoder for everything else.

10. Evaluating LLM components

Every LLM component should be evaluated like any other model, with metrics that match its role. Re-rankers: nDCG@k and MRR on labelled queries (rec8), sliced by query type, compared with the distilled student and the existing cross-encoder, plus a measurement of sensitivity to candidate order (shuffle inputs and measure output change). Enrichment: precision of extracted attributes against human review, with special attention to fabricated attributes. Labels: agreement with high-quality human labels per slice (ev5's kappa), and stability across prompt paraphrases. Conversational recommenders: task success and constraint violations on scripted and real conversations (ev8's online evaluation applies). And every component needs an online test (rec13) before it ships, because offline gains from LLMs vanish online for the same reasons as any other model's.

11. A placement checklist

When someone proposes "add an LLM to search", walk through five questions. Volume: how many calls per second at peak, and can that be reduced by restricting to a query class, triggering on uncertainty, or caching? Latency: is the call on the critical path, and if so does it fit the stage's budget (rec1); if not, can it run asynchronously or offline? Cost: tokens per call times price times calls per day, compared with the value per call (a booking, a purchase, a retained user) and with the cost of a distilled alternative. Failure: what happens when it is wrong, slow or unavailable: is there a fallback, and are outputs grounded and validated before users or models consume them? Measurement: which offline metric and which online experiment will show it helps (rec8, rec13)? Uses that pass all five tend to be offline enrichment, labelling and teacher roles, narrow re-ranking, and opt-in conversational surfaces; uses that fail usually fail on volume and latency, and the fix is almost always distillation or a narrower scope.

Why listwise prompting suits LLMs

Pointwise relevance scores from an LLM are produced in separate calls, each with its own implicit scale, so "7 out of 10" for one passage and "7 out of 10" for another may not mean the same. Listwise prompting removes the scale problem: the model only has to compare items it can see together, a task closer to how language models reason over context. It also reduces calls: one call per window instead of one per passage. The cost is that output must be parsed into a valid permutation and the method inherits the model's position biases within the window, which is why RankGPT-style systems repair outputs in code and why running a few windows with different initial orders and aggregating can improve stability.

Permutation distillation in more detail

Training a student on an LLM's permutations can use any learning-to-rank loss from rec7. RankGPT used a RankNet-style pairwise loss: for every pair of passages where the teacher placed i above j, the student is penalised by log(1 + e−(si − sj)). The student never sees the LLM's reasoning, only its orderings, which is enough. Two practical points: the teacher's orderings should cover the kinds of candidates the student will actually see (from your own first-stage retriever, not random passages), and teacher noise should be reduced by averaging several teacher runs or by keeping only pairs the teacher ranks consistently.

Semantic IDs as a shared vocabulary

Beyond generative retrieval, semantic IDs have a useful property for large organisations: they give every model a compact, content-derived token vocabulary for items. Instead of each model learning its own huge, sparse ID embedding table (rec6's memory problem), models can embed the few codebook tokens of each item, sharing statistical strength across similar items and handling new items naturally. The trade-off is that items with identical content but different behaviour (two versions of a song with very different audiences) need extra disambiguation, which pure content-derived IDs cannot provide.

LLM labels and the circularity problem

If an LLM labels relevance and the same or a similar LLM powers the re-ranker being evaluated, the evaluation can reward agreement with the LLM rather than usefulness to people: a form of self-preference (ev4). Bing's approach guards against this by anchoring prompt selection and monitoring on preferences expressed by real searchers about their own queries, which are independent of any model. Any team using LLM labels should keep a human-grounded evaluation set that never touches LLM labels, and check periodically that conclusions agree.

Prompt caching and batching, and their limits here

Prompt caching cuts the cost of repeated prefixes to a tenth of the input price on most Claude models (a twentieth on Opus 5.5, as of October 2026), which matters for conversational surfaces with long stable instructions and growing histories. It helps little for per-query re-ranking, where the bulk of the input, the candidate passages, differs on every call. The batch API halves prices for work that can wait up to a day, which suits enrichment, labelling and synthetic query generation exactly and suits nothing in the request path. Matching each use to the pricing mechanism that fits it is part of the design.

When an LLM sees user data

Putting a user's history into an LLM prompt for personalised explanations or conversational recommendation raises privacy and governance questions that the classical stack, which uses aggregate features, partly avoids: what is sent to which provider, how long it is retained, whether it can appear in outputs to others, and how sensitive attributes are handled. Production designs minimise what is sent (recent relevant items rather than full history), use providers and settings with appropriate data-handling guarantees, and filter outputs.

Scaling laws for recommenders and LLM-style models

A separate line of work applies LLM-like architectures, not LLMs themselves, to recommendation: large transformers trained on sequences of user actions, with item tokens rather than words (rec6's deep section mentioned this). Their appeal is that quality may keep improving with scale as in language modelling. Their cost profile is that of the ranker (they score candidates for each request) rather than of an LLM API call, so the cost arithmetic of section 9 does not apply directly; instead they compete for the ranker's latency and GPU budget.

The public case: LLM relevance labels at Bing

Thomas, Spielman, Craswell and Mitra's paper "Large Language Models Can Accurately Predict Searcher Preferences" (SIGIR 2024) describes how Bing moved much of its relevance labelling from crowd workers to LLMs. The problem it starts from is familiar to any search team: evaluating and tuning a search engine needs relevance labels at scale, ideally from real searchers who know what they wanted, but in practice from third-party labellers who may misunderstand needs (for example, failing to recognise that a query is navigational), and whose quality must be managed with auditing and training, with ongoing problems of mistakes, bias and adversarial workers.

Their alternative: collect careful feedback from real searchers (Bing's ground-truth corpus held, at the time, about 2.5 million preference pairs from real searchers, in about ten languages and from about fifty countries, stratified by attributes such as language, recency and authority), use it to select an LLM and prompt that agree with searchers' preferences, and then let the LLM produce labels at scale. On TREC data with trained assessors' labels, they found LLMs as accurate as human labellers and as useful for identifying the best systems and hardest queries, while also finding that performance varied with prompt features and "unpredictably with simple paraphrases", which reinforces the need for high-quality gold labels. At Bing, they report that LLMs proved more accurate than any third-party labeller, including staff, much faster end to end, higher in throughput and many times cheaper, letting Bing measure many more results with greater sensitivity; Bing had been using LLMs, in conjunction with expert human labellers, for most of its offline metrics since late 2022.

The monitoring is as instructive as the result. Every week a stratified sample of recent LLM labels is re-labelled by trained assessors and disagreement rates and patterns are tracked; a fixed set of queries is re-labelled daily and should not change; disagreements are investigated by a metrics team, and, the authors note, are resolved in favour of the LLM about as often as in favour of the humans. The case captures the lesson's main pattern: LLMs used offline, at scale, where their per-call cost is paid once per label, anchored and monitored against human judgement.

How LLM components fail in production

Latency creep. An LLM step added to query understanding for "only 2% of queries" grows as the trigger is loosened; p99 latency rises; the fallback to non-LLM results fires silently. Budget LLM calls explicitly, with rate limits and timeouts.

Silent model changes. A provider model update or a prompt edit changes enrichment outputs or labels; features and evaluation shift without any code change in the ranking system. Pin model versions, regenerate consistently, and diff outputs on a fixed sample before switching.

Hallucinated attributes. An enrichment prompt fills in missing attributes plausibly; filters return items that do not match; customers complain. Restrict to extraction from source text, output "unknown" when absent, and audit samples.

Ungrounded conversational answers. An assistant recommends a product that is out of stock or quotes an old price. Ground every recommendation in live catalogue data via tools, and validate outputs against it before display.

Cost surprises. A conversational surface's average conversation grows from 4 to 12 turns with long histories; cost per session triples. Track tokens per session and cost per successful outcome; use caching for stable prefixes and smaller models for routine turns.

What to monitor

For each LLM component: calls per second, tokens per call, cost per day and per useful outcome, p50 and p99 latency, timeout and fallback rates, malformed-output rates, and model and prompt versions in use. For quality: agreement of LLM labels with human samples per slice, attribute precision from audits, re-ranker metrics on a fixed labelled set re-run after any change, and for conversational systems task success and constraint-violation rates.

Goal: simulate a listwise re-ranker with sliding windows, distil it into a pairwise student, and do the cost arithmetic. Python 3.12, standard library only; a scripted function stands in for the LLM, so everything runs offline.

  1. Create rec14_lab.py. Generate 50 queries, each with 100 candidates having a hidden true relevance in [0, 1] and a noisy first-stage score (relevance plus Gaussian noise with SD 0.3). Order candidates by first-stage score.
  2. Write fake_llm_rank(window): it returns the window ordered by relevance plus small noise (SD 0.1), but with a position bias: add 0.05 × (window length − index) / window length to each item's perceived score, so it partly trusts the order it was given.
  3. Implement sliding-window re-ranking with window 20 and step 10, from the end of the list to the front, and count calls per query (expect 9). Compute nDCG@10 (rec8) with gains equal to relevance for first stage, one full pass, and the sliding-window result.
  4. Shuffle each query's candidates before re-ranking and compare nDCG@10 with re-ranking from the first-stage order, to see the position bias.
  5. Distil: from the sliding-window outputs, build pairs (i above j) for the top 30 positions; train a linear student on two features you give each candidate (the first-stage score and a noisy copy of relevance with SD 0.2) with a RankNet loss for 20 epochs. Evaluate the student's nDCG@10 on 20 held-out queries.
  6. Cost: write a function that takes QPS, passages, tokens per passage, prompt tokens, output tokens and prices, and reproduces about $0.0026 per query and $2.25 million per day for Haiku 4.5 at 10,000 QPS; then compute the enrichment cost for 10 million items with batch prices (about $5,000).

What to look for. Sliding-window re-ranking should beat the first stage clearly, and a single pass over only the top 20 should miss relevant items that started lower. Shuffled inputs should give somewhat different (often slightly worse) results, showing the position bias. The distilled student should recover a large share of the teacher's gain at essentially no per-query cost. The cost function makes the per-request versus per-item difference concrete.

Design: adding LLMs to a travel site's hotel search

A travel site handles 3,000 hotel searches per second at peak (destination, dates, guests, optional free text). It has 1.5 million hotels with messy descriptions in many languages and inconsistent amenity data. Leadership wants "AI search": users type "quiet boutique hotel near the old town with a rooftop bar, good for a long weekend with my partner". Budget: the AI features must cost less than $40,000 per day and must not raise p99 latency of the main search above 400 ms. Design it.

Answer

Offline enrichment (the biggest win). Run a cheap model over all 1.5 million hotel descriptions and reviews through the batch API to extract normalised amenities (rooftop bar, quiet rooms, adults-only, walking distance to landmarks), a short neutral summary per language, and tags such as "romantic" or "family", extraction-only with "unknown" allowed. At about 1,500 input and 200 output tokens per hotel with Haiku batch prices ($0.50/$2.50 per million): 1.5 M × (1,500 × 0.0000005 + 200 × 0.0000025) = 1.5 M × $0.00125 ≈ $1,900, once, plus a small daily refresh. Audit a sample of 2,000 hotels for attribute precision. Index the attributes as filters and fields, and fine-tune the hotel retriever on real queries plus LLM-generated synthetic queries.

Query understanding. Most searches are structured (destination and dates) and need no LLM. For free-text queries (say 5% of traffic, 150 per second), parse with a small fine-tuned model distilled from an LLM into constraints (area: old town; amenities: rooftop bar; style: boutique, quiet; trip type: couple) within about 30 ms, with LLM rewrites cached for frequent phrasings.

Re-ranking. For free-text queries only, re-rank the top 20 hotels with an LLM listwise prompt using enriched attributes and review snippets, asynchronously: show the standard results immediately and update the order (with short "why this hotel" explanations grounded in extracted attributes) when the LLM returns. Cost: about 2,500 input and 300 output tokens with Haiku ≈ $0.004 per query; 150 per second × 86,400 ≈ $52,000 per day, over budget, so cache by (parsed constraints, destination) for head combinations (assume 40% hits) and trigger only when the first-stage ranker's top scores are close: at about 60 LLM calls per second, ≈ $21,000 per day.
