In the early days of online shopping, a bookshop had a problem the shop assistant used to solve by intuition. A customer buys a book on beginner rock climbing. What should the website suggest next? A content rule, "other books with 'climbing' in the title", produces a list of more climbing books, many redundant, and misses the things climbers actually buy next: a guide to knots, a first-aid manual, a book about a famous expedition. Nothing in the text of a rock-climbing book says "people who buy this also want wilderness first aid". But the order history says it, loudly, thousands of times.

That observation is the whole idea of collaborative filtering: let the behaviour of many people reveal what goes with what, and who is like whom, without needing to understand the items at all. It works for books, films, songs, products and posts, in any language, for items whose appeal no feature describes. It is why a recommender can learn that a particular indie band appeals to the same listeners as a particular podcast, a connection no metadata would suggest.

It also comes with problems that shape the whole field. The data is a gigantic table that is more than 99% empty. Most of what we observe is implicit, a click or a play rather than a rating, so we never see a clear "no". New items and new users have no history at all. And whatever is popular gets recommended, gets clicked and becomes more popular.

This lesson builds collaborative filtering from the ground up: the user–item matrix and why its empty cells are unknown rather than zero; neighbourhood methods, worked with cosine similarity; matrix factorisation, the idea that won the Netflix Prize, trained both by stochastic gradient descent and by alternating least squares, with one ALS step worked by hand on a 3×3 matrix; and the adjustments for implicit feedback, confidence weights, negative sampling and pairwise ranking. Its embeddings are the direct ancestors of the two-tower models in rec4.

Picture a giant spreadsheet. Each row is a person, each column is an item, and each cell records what that person did with that item: a star rating, a purchase, how many times they played a song. Almost every cell is blank, because each person has touched only a tiny part of the catalogue. Collaborative filtering is the job of guessing which blank cells would be high if the person encountered that item, and recommending those.

There are two ways to make the guess. The first is to look for neighbours. To guess whether Priya will like a film, find films similar to ones Priya liked, where "similar" means "liked by the same people"; or find people whose past ratings resemble Priya's and see what they thought. This is like asking friends with similar taste for advice. It is easy to explain, and with clever precomputation it scales well.

The second way is to find hidden factors. Suppose every film could be described by a few numbers, such as how much action it has, how much romance, how arty it is, and every person by how much they enjoy each of those. Then a person's interest in a film is roughly the sum of (their love of action × the film's action) + (their love of romance × the film's romance) + ..., a dot product. Matrix factorisation learns such numbers for every person and every film purely from the filled-in cells, without being told what the factors mean. Often the learned factors line up loosely with genres or with "mainstream versus niche", but they are whatever best explains the data.

The spreadsheet analogy breaks in an important place. A blank cell in a spreadsheet is just blank. In a recommender, blanks are not random: people rate what they chose to watch, and they chose because they expected to like it. And with implicit data, a blank might mean "never saw it" or "saw it and scrolled past". How you treat the blanks is the single most consequential modelling decision in collaborative filtering, and much of this lesson is about it.

Both approaches share one assumption worth saying out loud: the past predicts the future. If a thousand people who bought a tent also bought a stove, the next tent buyer probably wants a stove. That is usually true, which is why collaborative filtering works, and occasionally false in ways that matter: tastes change, seasons change (nobody needs the tent-and-stove pairing in January), and the system's own recommendations shape what people buy, so the "past" it learns from is partly its own doing. Keep that in the back of your mind; rec10 returns to it.

Two facts make the factor approach powerful. It compresses: 50 million users and 5 million items with 64 factors each is 3.5 billion numbers instead of 250 trillion cells. And it generalises: a user who rated only ten films still gets a sensible vector, because each of those films' vectors was learned from thousands of other people.

1. The user–item matrix and the meaning of a blank

Formally we have m users, n items and a matrix R whose entry rui records user u's interaction with item i. Two kinds of data fill it. Explicit feedback is a stated preference: 1-5 stars, thumbs up or down. It is clean in meaning but scarce, because most people never rate. Implicit feedback is behaviour: purchases, clicks, plays, minutes watched, add-to-cart. It is plentiful, but its meaning is fuzzy: a play may be a mis-tap, a purchase may be a gift, and there is no explicit "no".

The matrix is extremely sparse. The Netflix Prize dataset had about 100 million ratings from roughly 480,000 users on 17,770 films: 100 million ÷ (480,000 × 17,770) ≈ 1.2% filled, and that was a dataset of active raters. A shop with 50 million customers, 5 million products and 4 billion interactions is 4 × 10⁹ ÷ 2.5 × 10¹⁴ = 0.0016% filled.

The key conceptual point is what a blank means. With explicit ratings, a blank is unknown, and it is missing not at random: people rate films they chose to watch, which they chose because they expected to like them, so observed ratings skew high, and unrated films skew towards ones people expected to dislike. A model that treated blanks as zero ratings would learn that everything unrated is terrible. So explicit-feedback models fit only observed entries. With implicit data, a blank is a weak, ambiguous negative: the item was either not wanted or never noticed. Implicit models therefore include blanks, but with low weight. Keep this distinction in mind; every method below takes one side or the other.

2. Neighbourhood methods: item-to-item similarity

The most direct collaborative method says: two items are similar if the same people engage with both. To make that a number, represent each item by its column in the matrix, a vector with one entry per user, and compare columns. For binary data (did the user engage or not), the standard measure is cosine similarity: the number of users who engaged with both items, divided by the square root of the product of the two items' user counts. Dividing by the counts matters: without it, a bestseller that everyone buys would be "similar" to everything.

A worked example with five customers of an outdoor shop and four items: A (tent), B (sleeping bag), C (camping stove), D (a novel).

A and C share u1: 1 / √6 = 0.408. A and D share u4: 1 / √6 = 0.408. B and D, C and D share nobody: 0.

To score candidates for a user, sum each candidate's similarity to the items the user already has. Customer u2 has A and B. Candidate C scores cos(C, A) + cos(C, B) = 0.408 + 0.816 = 1.225. Candidate D scores 0.408 + 0 = 0.408. Recommend the stove, which matches intuition: tent-and-sleeping-bag buyers buy stoves. Production systems weight the user's items by recency and strength (a purchase over a view), keep only each item's top 50-200 neighbours, and normalise scores.

The user-based variant compares rows instead of columns: find users similar to u2, then recommend what they bought. It works, but at scale item-based usually wins, as Amazon's 2003 paper explained: item-to-item similarities are more stable (an item's audience changes slowly, while a user's similarity to others shifts with every purchase), they can be computed offline into a table of each item's top neighbours, and serving is then a few table lookups: for each of the user's recent items, fetch its neighbours, merge and score. The method also explains itself: "because you bought a tent".

The weaknesses are visible in the example. The novel D has only one co-purchase with anything; similarities from one or two shared users are noise. Real systems require a minimum co-occurrence count, shrink low-support similarities towards zero, and still find that neighbourhoods are thin for the long tail of rarely bought items. Matrix factorisation addresses exactly that.

3. Matrix factorisation: the model

Give each user u a vector xu of k numbers and each item i a vector yi of k numbers, where k (the number of latent factors) is small: 20 to 256. Predict the interaction as their dot product:

r̂(u, i) = x_u · y_i = x_u1·y_i1 + x_u2·y_i2 + ... + x_uk·y_ik

In matrix terms, the whole m × n matrix is approximated by X Yᵀ, where X is m × k (users' vectors as rows) and Y is n × k. That is a low-rank approximation: rank k, far below m or n. The model's claim is that taste has a few main directions, and both people and items can be located along them.

The training objective for explicit ratings fits only the observed entries, with L2 regularisation (ml3) to stop vectors growing to fit noise:

minimise   Σ over observed (u,i):  (r_ui − x_u · y_i)²   +   λ ( Σ_u |x_u|² + Σ_i |y_i|² )

Real models add bias terms: r̂ = μ + bu + bi + xu · yi, where μ is the global average rating, bu captures that some users rate everything high, and bi that some films are liked by everyone. Koren, Bell and Volinsky's 2009 article showed that biases alone explain a large share of rating variance, and the factors then model the interaction.

Why not use the singular value decomposition from linear algebra, which gives the best rank-k approximation of a matrix? Because SVD needs every entry, and here 99% are unknown. Filling them with zeros or averages biases the result badly. The Netflix Prize popularised the name "SVD" for the learned model above, but it is fitted to observed entries only, which needs an iterative algorithm. There are two standard ones.

4. Training with stochastic gradient descent

SGD loops over observed entries in random order. For each (u, i, r), compute the error e = r − xu · yi, then move both vectors a small step to reduce it, with η the learning rate:

x_u ← x_u + η ( e · y_i − λ · x_u )
y_i ← y_i + η ( e · x_u − λ · y_i )      (using the old x_u)

The intuition: if the prediction is too low (e > 0), move the user vector towards the item vector and the item vector towards the user vector, so their dot product grows; the λ terms shrink both slightly towards zero.

One update by hand, with k = 2, η = 0.05, λ = 0.1. User vector x = (1.0, 1.0), item vector y = (1.0, 0.2), observed rating r = 5. Prediction: 1.0 × 1.0 + 1.0 × 0.2 = 1.2. Error e = 3.8. New x = (1.0 + 0.05 × (3.8 × 1.0 − 0.1 × 1.0), 1.0 + 0.05 × (3.8 × 0.2 − 0.1 × 1.0)) = (1.185, 1.033). New y = (1.0 + 0.05 × (3.8 × 1.0 − 0.1 × 1.0), 0.2 + 0.05 × (3.8 × 1.0 − 0.1 × 0.2)) = (1.185, 0.389). New prediction: 1.185 × 1.185 + 1.033 × 0.389 = 1.404 + 0.402 = 1.81. The error shrank from 3.8 to 3.2 in one step; thousands of passes over millions of ratings do the rest.

SGD is simple, uses little memory, and accepts any differentiable loss, which is why it dominated the Netflix Prize and why variants with side features, time effects and pairwise losses are easy to write. Its drawbacks: a learning rate to tune, sequential updates that are awkward to parallelise (in practice, parallel workers update shared vectors without locks and tolerate occasional conflicts), and trouble with losses over all user–item pairs, which implicit data needs.

5. Training with alternating least squares

The objective is hard to minimise jointly, because the prediction multiplies two unknowns. But fix all item vectors, and the problem for each user becomes ordinary linear regression with an L2 penalty (ridge regression): the user's rated items' vectors are the inputs, the ratings are the targets, and xu is the weight vector. Ridge regression has an exact solution:

x_u = (Y_uᵀ Y_u + λI)⁻¹ Y_uᵀ r_u

where Yu stacks the vectors of the items user u rated and ru is the vector of those ratings. Solve that for every user (independently, so in parallel), then fix users and solve every item the same way, and repeat. Each half-step solves its subproblem exactly, so the loss never goes up. This is alternating least squares (ALS).

Solve user 1. User 1 rated items 1 and 2. Build A = y1y1ᵀ + y2y2ᵀ + λI. The outer products are y1y1ᵀ = [[1.00, 0.20], [0.20, 0.04]] and y2y2ᵀ = [[0.36, 0.24], [0.24, 0.16]]. Sum plus 0.1 on the diagonal: A = [[1.46, 0.44], [0.44, 0.30]]. The right-hand side b = 5 × y1 + 3 × y2 = (5.0, 1.0) + (1.8, 1.2) = (6.8, 2.2).

Solve A x = b. The determinant is 1.46 × 0.30 − 0.44 × 0.44 = 0.438 − 0.1936 = 0.2444. The inverse of a 2×2 matrix [[a, b], [c, d]] is (1/det) [[d, −b], [−c, a]], so x1 = (1/0.2444) × (0.30 × 6.8 − 0.44 × 2.2, −0.44 × 6.8 + 1.46 × 2.2) = (1/0.2444) × (2.040 − 0.968, −2.992 + 3.212) = (1.072, 0.220) / 0.2444 = (4.386, 0.900).

Check: x1 · y1 = 4.386 + 0.180 = 4.57 (target 5) and x1 · y2 = 2.632 + 0.360 = 2.99 (target 3). The regulariser keeps it from fitting exactly.

The other users, same procedure: user 2 (items 1 and 3) gets A = [[1.14, 0.40], [0.40, 1.14]], b = (4.2, 1.8), x2 = (3.570, 0.326). User 3 (items 2 and 3) gets A = [[0.50, 0.44], [0.44, 1.26]], b = (1.6, 5.4), x3 = (−0.825, 4.574). After this half-step the squared error on observed entries drops from 40.76 to 0.78 (the regularisation term rises to 5.71, because the vectors grew; the total objective falls from 41.62 to 6.49).

Then solve items with users fixed: y1 becomes (1.101, 0.173), y2 (0.613, 0.328), y3 (0.175, 1.120). The objective falls to 5.75, almost all of it regularisation; observed entries are now fitted to within 0.01. The predictions for the blanks: user 1, item 3: 1.77; user 2, item 2: 2.30; user 3, item 1: −0.12, which you would clip to the rating range. User 3 loves item 3 and dislikes item 2's neighbour item 1, and the factors have captured "users 1 and 2 like item 1's kind of thing; user 3 likes item 3's kind".

Cost. Building A for a user costs about k² operations per item they rated, and solving it costs about k³. With k = 64 that is 4,096 per interaction and 262,144 per user. For 50 million users with 80 interactions each, a user half-step is about 50 × 10⁶ × (80 × 4,096 + 262,144) ≈ 3 × 10¹³ operations: tens of seconds of compute on a large cluster, which is why ALS runs comfortably as a nightly batch job. Spark MLlib's ALS is a widely used implementation.

6. Implicit feedback: preference and confidence

With implicit data the explicit objective breaks. If you fit only observed entries, which are all positive, the model learns that everyone likes everything, a trivially perfect fit. You must include the blanks. But blanks are ambiguous, and observed counts mean "more evidence", not "likes more".

Hu, Koren and Volinsky's 2008 paper, written for a TV recommender built on viewing data from about 300,000 set-top boxes, separated two quantities. The preference pui is 1 if user u interacted with item i at all, else 0. The confidence cui is how much we trust that preference: cui = 1 + α · rui, where rui is the interaction strength (in their case, how much of a show was watched, summed over viewings). Unobserved pairs get preference 0 with confidence 1, a weak "probably not"; a show watched in full five times gets preference 1 with confidence 1 + 40 × 5 = 201, using their reported α = 40. The objective covers every user–item pair:

minimise   Σ over all (u,i):  c_ui (p_ui − x_u · y_i)²   +   λ ( Σ|x_u|² + Σ|y_i|² )

Every pair means m × n terms: 50 million × 5 million = 2.5 × 10¹⁴. Visiting them with SGD is impossible. ALS handles it with an algebraic trick. The user solve becomes xu = (YᵀCuY + λI)⁻¹ YᵀCupu, where Cu is a diagonal matrix of the user's confidences over all items. Write YᵀCuY = YᵀY + Yᵀ(Cu − I)Y. The first term, YᵀY, is the same for every user and is computed once per sweep. The second involves only items where Cu differs from 1, the user's observed items. So each user's solve costs about k² per observed item plus k³, linear in the data, despite fitting all pairs. This is why implicit ALS remains a workhorse.

The paper's results, on a test week following a four-week training window, used expected percentile rank: where, as a percentage of the ranked list, the shows people actually watched appeared (lower is better; random is 50%). Recommending by popularity gave 16.46%, an item-neighbourhood method 10.74%, and the factor model improved with more factors, reaching 8.35% at 200 factors. The paper also notes that predicting re-watching is much easier than predicting a first-time view, so it removed previously watched shows from the test, an evaluation choice that still matters today.

7. Implicit feedback with SGD: negative sampling and BPR

Many models cannot use the ALS trick: those with side features, nonlinear components or custom losses, trained by SGD. They need another way to include the blanks: negative sampling. For each observed (user, item) pair, a positive, sample a few unobserved items for the same user and treat them as negatives, label 0. Train with a binary cross-entropy loss on positives and sampled negatives.

Which unobserved items to sample matters a lot. Uniform sampling mostly picks obscure items the user never had a chance to see and that the model already scores low; the gradient from them is tiny and learning is slow. Sampling in proportion to popularity picks items the user plausibly encountered and passed over, which carry more information. A common compromise borrowed from word2vec samples in proportion to count raised to the 0.75 power, flattening the popularity curve. Hard negatives, items the current model scores high but the user did not engage with, teach the most but risk being false negatives: items the user would like but has not yet seen.

BPR (Bayesian personalised ranking, Rendle and colleagues, 2009) changes the question from "is this pair positive?" to "is this positive item ranked above this negative one?". For a triple (user u, observed item i, sampled unobserved item j), it maximises ln σ(sui − suj), where s is the model's score and σ the sigmoid. Only the difference matters, which suits ranking: we care about order, not calibrated probabilities. BPR with matrix factorisation scores is a standard strong baseline for implicit top-k recommendation. The same "positive versus sampled negatives" idea, scaled up to softmax over many negatives at once, is how two-tower retrieval models are trained in rec4.

8. Evaluating collaborative filtering

How you hold out data decides whether the evaluation means anything. A random split of interactions leaks the future: the model trains on a user's March purchases and is tested on their January ones. A temporal split, train on everything before a date and test on what happened after, matches deployment. Hu and colleagues trained on four weeks and tested on the following week. Within the test period, decide whether repeated interactions count: recommending a show someone already watches every week is easy and inflates scores, so for discovery surfaces, test only on new items per user.

Metrics should be ranking metrics on top-k lists, recall@k, nDCG@k, MRR (rec8), not RMSE on ratings. Compare against popularity: on many datasets, "recommend the most popular items" is a surprisingly strong baseline, and a personalised model that barely beats it is not earning its complexity. Report results by user activity bucket (users with 1-5 interactions versus 100+), because CF is strong for heavy users and weak for light ones, and an average hides that.

9. Serving collaborative filtering inside the funnel

In the multi-stage stack from rec1, collaborative filtering usually shows up in three places, and it is worth knowing which one an interviewer means.

As an item-to-item candidate source: a nightly (or hourly) job computes each item's top 100-200 neighbours and writes them to a key-value store. At request time, take the user's last 10-20 interactions, fetch each one's neighbour list (one batched read), merge the lists by summing similarities weighted by recency, drop items the user already has, and return the top few hundred. The cost is a handful of milliseconds and the source is very strong for "more like what you just did". The neighbour table for 5 million items × 200 neighbours × (8-byte ID + 4-byte score) is about 12 GB, comfortably held by a small key-value cluster or even in memory on each retrieval host.

As an embedding candidate source: item vectors from MF are loaded into an ANN index (lm10); the user vector is read from a store or folded in from recent interactions; the top few hundred items by inner product become candidates. This captures broad taste better than item-to-item lookups, which only explore around the user's recent items.

As ranking features: the MF dot product for this user and item, the item-to-item similarity between the candidate and the user's last item, the number of the user's recent items that list the candidate as a neighbour. These features are cheap and strong, and they let a ranker built on other signals benefit from collaborative structure.

Freshness differs by place. A neighbour table that is a day old is usually fine for established items. A user vector that is a day old misses everything the user did today, which is why folding in recent interactions at request time (the deep section shows how) or using the item-to-item source for the most recent behaviour is common.

10. What collaborative filtering cannot do

Three limits motivate much of the rest of this track. Cold start: an item nobody has touched has no column to learn from, and a user with no history has no row; CF alone cannot recommend them (rec11). Popularity bias: popular items have more data, more confident vectors and more co-occurrences, so they are recommended more and become more popular (rec10). No context and no content: plain MF knows nothing about time of day, device, the query, or what the item is about. Two-tower models (rec4) generalise MF by building user and item vectors from features, not just IDs, so new items can be placed from their content and context can shape the user vector.

Folding in new users without retraining

ALS gives a neat trick for users who arrived after the last training run. Hold item vectors fixed and solve the user's ridge regression from whatever interactions they have, x = (YuᵀYu + λI)⁻¹ Yuᵀ ru, at request time: with k = 64 and a dozen interactions this is microseconds. Some systems never store user vectors at all, computing them on the fly from the user's latest interactions, which also makes them perfectly fresh. The same idea, a user represented as a function of their items, is what the user tower of a two-tower model generalises.

Why the factors are not unique

If X and Y fit the data, so do XQ and YQ⁻ᵀ for any invertible k × k matrix Q, because XQ(YQ⁻ᵀ)ᵀ = XYᵀ. Regularisation narrows the choice but individual factors still have no fixed meaning, can rotate between training runs and can flip sign. Practical consequences: you cannot compare a factor across two training runs, embeddings from different runs live in different spaces (so a user vector from today's model must never be scored against item vectors from yesterday's), and "factor 3 is comedy" interpretations are fragile. Versioning user and item vectors together is a real production requirement.

Similarity measures beyond cosine

Cosine on binary columns is a reasonable default, but alternatives fix specific problems. Jaccard (shared users divided by users of either item) behaves similarly. Lift and pointwise mutual information compare co-occurrence with what independence would predict (count(A, B) × total ÷ (count(A) × count(B))), which removes popularity effects strongly but over-rewards rare pairs with tiny support. Conditional probability P(B | A) is asymmetric, useful for "people who bought A then bought B", where order matters (a phone then a case, rarely a case then a phone). Adding a shrinkage term, multiplying similarity by n/(n + β) where n is the number of shared users, pulls low-evidence similarities towards zero. Which works best is an empirical question per catalogue.

Matrix factorisation and neighbourhoods are closer than they look

An item-item model with learned similarity weights (rather than cosine) can be written as a factorisation in which a user's vector is the sum of the vectors of items they interacted with. Models such as SLIM and EASE learn a full item-item weight matrix with regularisation; EASE has a closed-form solution and, on several public benchmarks, is competitive with far more complex neural models. Conversely, the user tower of a two-tower model that averages the embeddings of recently watched items is a learned neighbourhood method. Knowing this lets you treat "neighbourhood or factorisation?" as a modelling choice along a spectrum rather than two different worlds.

Reproducibility and strong baselines

Recommender-systems research had a well-documented period in which many published neural models were compared against poorly tuned baselines. Dacrema, Cremonesi and Jannach's 2019 study tried to reproduce a set of neural recommendation papers from top conferences and found that most could not be reproduced with reasonable effort, and that of those that could, the majority were beaten by simple, well-tuned neighbourhood or factorisation methods. The lesson for practitioners: always run tuned item-kNN, implicit ALS or EASE, and popularity, on your own data, before believing a complex model is better.

Temporal dynamics

Taste and popularity move. Koren's work during the Netflix Prize added time-dependent biases (an item's popularity drifts; a user's rating scale shifts; a household's ratings on one day may come from a different person) and gained meaningful accuracy. In implicit settings the equivalent is weighting recent interactions more, either by decaying confidence with age or by training on a sliding window, which is what Hu and colleagues' choice of a four-week window did implicitly, since TV schedules change seasonally.

Scaling the item side

Serving MF for retrieval means finding, for a user vector, the items with the largest dot products: maximum inner product search. ANN indexes are usually built for cosine or Euclidean distance; inner product can be handled directly by some indexes, or converted by appending one extra coordinate to each item vector so that the largest inner product becomes the nearest neighbour. Vector norms matter here: in MF, popular items often get larger norms, so inner-product retrieval favours them, another route by which popularity bias enters. Normalising item vectors (cosine) reduces it at some cost in accuracy; it is a knob worth testing.

The public case: from the Netflix Prize to implicit feedback

Two public stories bracket the history of collaborative filtering in industry. The first is the Netflix Prize (2006-2009). Netflix released about 100 million anonymised star ratings and offered $1 million to the first team to beat its own system, Cinematch, by 10% on RMSE. Matrix factorisation with biases and temporal effects, trained by SGD, became the core technique of the leading teams, and the winning entry was a blend of over a hundred models. Netflix's 2012 engineering blog post "Netflix Recommendations: Beyond the 5 stars" described what happened next. Netflix took two components from an earlier progress-prize solution, matrix factorisation (0.8914 RMSE alone) and restricted Boltzmann machines (0.8990), blended them linearly to about 0.88, and put them into production. It evaluated the grand-prize methods offline but judged that "the additional accuracy gains that we measured did not seem to justify the engineering effort needed to bring them into a production environment". Meanwhile the business had moved to streaming, where what members play is a far richer signal than what they rate, and the objective moved from predicting stars to ranking titles.

The second story is the one this lesson's implicit section is built on. Hu, Koren and Volinsky's 2008 paper came from a TV recommender built on viewing data from about 300,000 set-top boxes, about 17,000 programmes over four weeks, with no ratings at all. Their preference-and-confidence model, with α = 40, and the ALS trick that makes fitting every pair affordable became the standard approach to implicit feedback and the "implicit" mode of Spark MLlib's ALS. The results (popularity 16.46%, neighbourhood 10.74%, 200-factor model 8.35% expected percentile rank) are a useful reminder of the ordering you should expect on your own data: popularity is a real baseline, neighbourhoods beat it, factor models beat them, and the gaps are modest.

How collaborative filtering fails in production

Popularity collapse. Recommendations drift towards the same few hundred items for everyone. Symptom: catalogue coverage (share of items ever recommended) and the number of distinct items shown per day falling. Causes: popularity-heavy negative sampling, inner-product retrieval with large popular-item norms, and the feedback loop of training on one's own recommendations. Fixes: normalise, re-rank for diversity, add exploration, and watch coverage as a guardrail metric.

Bot and fraud interactions. A seller pays for fake purchases that make their product co-occur with a bestseller; item-to-item tables now recommend it on the bestseller's page. CF is easy to poison because it trusts behaviour. Filter interactions from suspicious accounts, cap any single user's influence on a similarity, and monitor sudden jumps in an item's neighbour list.

Stale vectors. A nightly job fails silently and vectors are three days old; new items have none. Monitor model and index age, the share of shown items with no vector, and fall back to content-based or popularity sources for vector-less items.

Mismatched model versions. User vectors from one training run scored against item vectors from another produce nonsense, because factors are only defined up to rotation. Version user and item vectors together and refuse to serve mixed versions.

Accidental leakage in evaluation. Random splits, or test sets that include re-consumption, report excellent numbers that vanish online. Use temporal splits and test the product's actual task.

What to monitor

Training: objective by sweep, held-out recall@k and nDCG@k on a temporal split, by user-activity bucket. Outputs: catalogue coverage, Gini coefficient of recommendation counts across items (how concentrated they are), share of recommendations from the top 1% most popular items. Freshness: age of vectors and similarity tables, share of new items with vectors. Design: "customers also bought" for a grocery app

A grocery delivery app has 3 million weekly customers, 40,000 products and about 25 items per order. Product wants a "goes well with" row on every product page and a "you might have forgotten" row at checkout. The team proposes matrix factorisation on the customer × product purchase matrix. Critique the proposal and design something that will work, including how you handle milk and bananas, which are in 40% of all orders.

Answer

Critique. Customer-level MF learns long-term taste (vegetarian, family with toddlers, premium brands), which is good for a personalised home page, but "goes well with" is about items that go together in a basket, a session-level, item-to-item relation. The right unit is the order, not the customer.

Design. Build an item-to-item co-occurrence table from orders: for each pair of products, count the orders containing both, over the last 90 days with recent weeks weighted more. Compute a popularity-corrected similarity: raw co-occurrence would make milk and bananas the neighbours of everything, because they are in 40% of orders. Use lift, P(A and B) ÷ (P(A) × P(B)), with a minimum support (say 50 co-occurring orders) and shrinkage n/(n + 100) so rare pairs with tiny counts do not dominate. Keep the top 100 neighbours per product, recomputed nightly: 40,000 products × 100 entries is a small table, served from memory.

"Goes well with" on the page of pasta: look up its neighbours (pasta sauce, parmesan, basil), filter out-of-stock, re-rank lightly for variety (not five pasta sauces).

"You might have forgotten" at checkout: for each basket item, fetch neighbours; score each candidate by the sum of its similarities to the basket; add a personal signal, how often this customer buys it and how long since they last did (a repurchase model); drop items already in the basket. Here milk and bananas are legitimately good suggestions if this customer buys them weekly and they are missing; they should come from the personal repurchase signal, not from co-occurrence.

Where MF fits. Use implicit ALS on customer × product (confidence from purchase counts) as a separate candidate source for the personalised home page and for ranking features (customer–product affinity), alongside the co-occurrence table.

Evaluate. Offline: on held-out orders, hide one item per basket and measure how often it appears in the top 5 suggestions from the rest of the basket, versus a popularity baseline. Online: add-to-cart rate from the rows and basket size, with a guardrail on order cancellation or substitution rate.
