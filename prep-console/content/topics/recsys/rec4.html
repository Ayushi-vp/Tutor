A video platform has 20 million videos and a matrix-factorisation model from rec3. It works for established videos and regular users. Then three complaints arrive in the same week. Creators say their new uploads are invisible for days: the model has no vector for a video until the nightly retraining sees interactions, and it cannot get interactions without being recommended. The mobile team says recommendations ignore context: someone who watches cooking videos on the TV in the evening and short comedy clips on the phone on the train gets the same list on both. And the search team wants the same machinery for queries, but a matrix factorisation has no row for the text "easy vegan curry".

All three complaints have one root: matrix factorisation gives each user and item an ID vector, a free-floating set of numbers learned only from that ID's interactions. Anything without interaction history, a new video, a new query, a new context, has no vector. The fix is to compute vectors from features instead: a network reads the video's title, thumbnail, creator and category and outputs its vector; another network reads the user's recent history, device, time of day and (for search) the query text and outputs theirs. The score is still a dot product, so all the retrieval machinery, precomputed item vectors and an ANN index, still works. That is the two-tower model, and since the late 2010s it has been the standard learned retriever at YouTube, Pinterest, Meta, Spotify, Airbnb and most companies with a large catalogue.

Training it raises a problem that looks technical and turns out to be central. The model must learn to pick the right item out of millions, but computing a softmax over millions of items per training example is impossible. The cheap trick everyone uses, treating the other items in the training batch as negatives, quietly distorts what the model learns about popular items, and correcting that distortion was the subject of a well-known 2019 Google paper.

This lesson builds the two-tower model from MF, frames retrieval as classification, works the in-batch softmax loss for a batch of four with and without the correction, then covers temperature, harder negatives, serving with an ANN index and the unglamorous engineering of keeping millions of embeddings and an index fresh and consistent.

Think of a dating agency with millions of members that must suggest matches within a second. It cannot interview every pair. Instead, each member fills in a profile once, and the agency turns every profile into a point on a map, placed so that people who would get on well end up close together. To suggest matches, it places the new enquiry on the same map and looks at who is nearby. The expensive work, placing everyone on the map, is done in advance; the per-request work is one placement and one neighbourhood search.

A two-tower model is that agency. One network, the item tower, reads an item's features and places it on the map (outputs a vector). Another, the query tower or user tower, reads the user's features and context and places them on the same map. Closeness is measured by the dot product. The towers are trained together on past engagement, so that users land near the items they engaged with and away from the ones they did not. Because each item's position depends only on the item, all item positions are computed ahead of time and stored in a nearest-neighbour index.

Compare this with matrix factorisation from rec3. MF is a two-tower model whose towers are lookup tables: the user tower maps a user ID to a learned vector, the item tower maps an item ID to a learned vector, and nothing else goes in. Replace the lookup tables with networks that take features (still including the ID as one feature) and you gain three things: new items get vectors from their content, context such as time and device shapes the user vector, and a query's text can be a feature, so the same design serves search.

What does the learned map look like? Nobody tells the model what its 128 dimensions mean, but after training on billions of interactions, regions of the space correspond to recognisable tastes: beginner cooking videos cluster together, near but separate from professional chef content; a user who watches both lands between them. Because the user vector is computed from recent behaviour, it moves: after three football highlights in a row, the user's point drifts towards the football region and the next retrieval reflects it. Distances also carry popularity information unless you are careful, because items that many different users engage with are pulled towards many different user points; normalisation and the sampling correction below are partly about controlling that.

The analogy breaks at training. A dating agency could ask couples whether they got on; a recommender only sees which items users clicked from what they were shown, never what they would have thought of the millions of items they were not shown. So training must invent negatives: for each user–item pair that happened, pick some items that did not, and teach the model to put the real one first. How those negatives are picked turns out to shape the model as much as the architecture does, and that is the heart of this lesson.

1. The towers and what goes in them

Each tower is usually a multilayer perceptron (a stack of fully connected layers with nonlinearities between them) on top of feature encodings. Categorical features such as item ID, creator ID, category and country become embeddings, looked up in tables and learned during training. Variable-length features such as "the last 50 videos watched" become the average (or a learned weighted combination) of the embeddings of those videos; YouTube's 2016 paper did exactly this, inspired by bag-of-words language models. Text such as a title or a query can be encoded by averaging token embeddings or by a pre-trained text encoder. Continuous features such as item age or user tenure are normalised. All of these are concatenated and fed through the layers, ending in a vector of, say, 64-256 numbers.

What goes in which tower follows one rule: the item tower may only use what is known about the item regardless of who is asking; the query tower may use anything about the user and the moment. So "number of likes this video got in the last hour" can go in the item tower (it is about the item), though it changes so fast that putting it there forces frequent re-embedding (more on that in section 8). "Whether this user has watched this creator before" cannot go in either tower: it is about the pair. That is a cross feature, and it belongs to the ranker.

Embedding tables that appear on both sides, for example video-ID embeddings used for the candidate item and for the user's watch history, are usually shared, so that a video's ID embedding learned from being watched also helps represent users who watched it. Yi et al. describe sharing input embeddings between the towers wherever a feature is available to both.

2. Retrieval as classification over the corpus

What should the model optimise? YouTube's 2016 paper gave the framing that most retrieval models still use: treat "which video will this user watch next?" as a classification problem with millions of classes, one per item. Given the query vector u and item vectors v1...vN, the model's probability that the user picks item i is a softmax over the whole corpus:

P(i | user) = exp(u · v_i / τ)  /  Σ_j over all N items  exp(u · v_j / τ)

where τ is a temperature (section 5). Training maximises the log-probability of the item the user actually engaged with, that is, minimises the cross-entropy loss −ln P(positive | user) (ml5). The softmax has a useful property: to raise the positive's probability it must push down every other item in proportion to how much probability the model currently gives it, so the items the model wrongly likes are corrected hardest.

The problem is the denominator. With N = 20 million items, each training example needs 20 million item vectors and dot products, and the gradient touches all of them. With billions of training examples a day, that is out of the question. The solution is to estimate the denominator from a sample of items, the sampled softmax: compute the softmax over the positive plus a handful of negatives. YouTube's 2016 system sampled several thousand negatives per example from a background distribution and corrected for the sampling with importance weighting, which it reported as more than 100 times faster than the full softmax.

3. In-batch negatives

Sampling negatives from a separate distribution still means computing item-tower outputs for those extra items. A cheaper trick has become standard: in-batch negatives. A training batch contains B examples, each a (user, positive item) pair. Compute all B user vectors and all B item vectors, then form the B × B matrix of dot products. Row i holds user i's score for every item in the batch: the diagonal entry is their positive, the other B − 1 entries are other users' positives, used as negatives. Apply a softmax across each row and take the loss on the diagonal. No extra item computation, and large batches give many negatives: Yi et al. used batches of 8,192, so 8,191 negatives per example.

Worked example: a batch of four. Four users each clicked one item; item 1 is a very popular video, item 4 fairly popular, items 2 and 3 niche. Both towers output vectors normalised to length 1, so dot products are cosines between −1 and 1. Here is the score matrix (rows: users, columns: items; the diagonal is each user's positive):

item 1 (popular)item 2item 3item 4
user 1 (clicked item 1)0.800.300.100.50
user 2 (clicked item 2)0.400.700.200.60
user 3 (clicked item 3)0.100.200.900.30
user 4 (clicked item 4)0.600.500.200.55

Use temperature τ = 0.2, so logits are scores × 5. For user 2 the logits are 2.0, 3.5, 1.0, 3.0. Exponentiate: e2.0 = 7.39, e3.5 = 33.12, e1.0 = 2.72, e3.0 = 20.09; sum 63.32. Softmax: 0.117, 0.523, 0.043, 0.317. Loss for user 2: −ln 0.523 = 0.648. The same computation for the others gives losses 0.289 (user 1, softmax on item 1 = 0.749), 0.094 (user 3, 0.910) and 1.175 (user 4, whose positive item 4 at 0.309 is beaten by item 1 at 0.397). Mean batch loss: 0.551.

Now the gradient. For a softmax with cross-entropy, the gradient of the loss with respect to each logit is (probability − 1) for the positive and (probability) for each negative. So in row 2, training raises item 2's logit with weight 1 − 0.523 = 0.477 and lowers item 1's with weight 0.117, item 3's with 0.043, item 4's with 0.317. Across the whole batch, item 1 is pushed down as a negative in rows 2, 3 and 4 with weights 0.117 + 0.017 + 0.397 = 0.53. That is the in-batch mechanism working as intended for this batch. The problem is what happens across millions of batches.

4. Sampling bias and the logQ correction

Which items end up as in-batch negatives? Whatever items are positives in the batch, and items appear as positives in proportion to how often they are clicked. If item 1 is clicked 40 times as often as item 3, it appears in batches 40 times as often, so it serves as a negative 40 times as often. In the full softmax every item is a negative in every example; in the in-batch version, popular items are hugely over-represented among negatives. The result: the model is pushed to under-score popular items, more than their true relevance warrants. Retrieval quality suffers, and in a feed this shows up as the retriever avoiding items that are popular for good reason.

The fix comes from the theory of sampled softmax. If negatives are drawn with probability pj for item j, then subtracting ln pj from each item's logit before the softmax makes the sampled loss a (consistent) estimate of the full-softmax loss. Intuitively, an item that shows up very often as a negative has its logit reduced so that each appearance counts for less; a rare item's logit is raised, so its rare appearances count for more. This is the logQ correction (Q being the sampling distribution). Yi et al. apply it to every logit in the row, positives included:

corrected logit(i, j) = u_i · v_j / τ  −  ln p_j

Back to the batch of four, with estimated sampling probabilities p = 0.02 (item 1), 0.002 (item 2), 0.0005 (item 3), 0.01 (item 4). The corrections −ln pj are +3.91, +6.21, +7.60, +4.61: rare items get the bigger boost. User 2's corrected logits become 2.0 + 3.91 = 5.91, 3.5 + 6.21 = 9.71, 1.0 + 7.60 = 8.60, 3.0 + 4.61 = 7.61. Softmax: 0.015, 0.679, 0.223, 0.082. Item 1, the popular one, now receives a push-down weight of 0.015 instead of 0.117: almost none. Niche item 3 now receives 0.223 instead of 0.043. Across the batch, item 1's total push-down falls from 0.53 to 0.09 (0.015 + 0.000 + 0.071).

Notice the corrected loss for this batch is higher (0.96 versus 0.55): the corrected problem is harder, because popular positives no longer get an easy win. User 1, whose positive is the popular item 1, now has a loss of 1.25 instead of 0.29; the model must raise item 1's raw score for user 1 to win, which is precisely the under-scoring of popular items being undone. At serving time the correction is not applied: retrieval ranks by the raw dot product, which now estimates true preference rather than preference distorted by batch composition.

Estimating pj. With a fixed catalogue you could count item frequencies in the training data. YouTube's corpus changes constantly, so Yi et al. estimate frequencies from the training stream itself. Keep two arrays of size H, indexed by a hash of the item ID: A records the last training step at which the item appeared, and B a running estimate of the average number of steps between its appearances, updated each time the item appears as B ← (1 − α)B + α(t − A), then A ← t. The estimated sampling probability is 1/B. An item seen every 50 steps on average has p ≈ 0.02 per step. This adapts as popularity changes, needs no fixed vocabulary, and costs two array lookups per item; collisions from hashing are reduced with several hash functions, an idea borrowed from count-min sketches. They used H = 50 million with α = 0.01.

5. Normalisation and temperature

Two small choices have outsized effects. Normalising both tower outputs to length 1 makes the dot product a cosine. Yi et al. report it "improves model trainability and thus leads to better retrieval quality". It also removes one route for popularity bias at serving: without normalisation, popular items can acquire large vector norms that inflate their dot products with every user.

The temperature τ divides the logits. Cosines live between −1 and 1, a narrow range; dividing by τ = 0.05 stretches it to −20..20, so a cosine gap of 0.1 becomes a logit gap of 2, a factor of e² ≈ 7.4 in probability. A small τ makes the softmax concentrate almost all its mass, and therefore all the gradient, on the few negatives that score close to the positive: a form of automatic hard-negative mining. Too small and training becomes unstable and over-reacts to noisy labels; too large and all negatives are pushed equally and learning slows. It is tuned on retrieval recall; Yi et al. reported Wikipedia link-prediction results for τ from 0.05 to 0.14 and used 0.05 in their YouTube live test.

6. Harder and broader negatives

In-batch negatives have a blind spot beyond popularity: items that are rarely or never positives (the long tail, and items uploaded minutes ago) almost never appear in a batch, so they are almost never pushed down. A model can then score some irrelevant niche items highly and nothing in training corrects it. Mixed negative sampling (Yang et al., 2020) adds a set of items sampled uniformly from the whole corpus to each batch's negatives, so every item has some chance of being a negative. Their item-tower vectors must be computed, which costs extra, but a few thousand shared uniform negatives per batch are affordable.

Hard negatives are items the current model scores high that the user did not engage with. Good sources: items that were shown and skipped (an impression without a click), and top ANN results for the user that were not clicked. They teach fine distinctions ("this user likes beginner yoga videos, not advanced ones") faster than random items. The risk is false negatives: most "unclicked" items were simply never seen, and a high-scoring unseen item might be exactly what the user wants. Prefer shown-and-skipped impressions over merely unclicked items, mix hard with easy negatives, and watch for the model suppressing good content.

What counts as a positive matters as much. A click is a weak signal (clickbait is clicked); Yi et al.'s live system weighted each clicked example by a reward reflecting real engagement with the video, such as watch time, with unwatched clicks getting zero weight. Choosing the label for retrieval is a product decision: retrieve what people click, what they watch, or what they return for?

7. Serving: one user vector, one index search

At request time: fetch the user's features, run the query tower once (a few milliseconds on CPU for a moderate MLP), and search the ANN index for the top K items by inner product (lm10 explains HNSW and IVF; db15 covers running them inside a database). With normalised vectors, inner product equals cosine, which every index supports. K is typically 200-2,000, feeding the merge with other candidate sources (rec1).

Index sizing is simple arithmetic. 20 million items × 128 dimensions × 4 bytes = 10.2 GB in float32; 2.6 GB with 8-bit quantisation, plus graph or cluster overhead. That fits in one machine's memory, so the index is replicated, not sharded: each request goes to one replica. If one replica answers 2,000 searches per second and peak traffic is 10,000 QPS, you need 5 replicas, about 8 with headroom and a spare zone. Filters (region, language, age rating) are best applied inside the search, either with separate indexes per major partition or filtered search; post-filtering a top 500 that turns out to be 60% ineligible is a common way to end up with too few candidates.

The user vector deserves the freshest inputs. If the query tower reads the last 50 watches including ones from 30 seconds ago (from a nearline session store), every action shifts the vector and the next page reflects it. The item index can be hours old for established items with little harm; Yi et al. rebuilt their index of about 10 million videos every few hours. The user side is where freshness pays.

8. Keeping embeddings and the index fresh

Three kinds of change force work, and each has a different pipeline.

A new model version (retrained towers) changes the whole vector space. Every item must be re-embedded with the new item tower and a new index built; then the new query tower and new index must go live together. If the old index is queried with the new user vector, or the reverse, the dot products are meaningless, and nothing errors: recommendations just quietly get worse. Version every artefact (model, item vectors, index) and make serving refuse mismatches. Re-embedding cost is modest: 50 million items through a moderate item tower at 20,000 items per second per GPU is about 40 GPU-minutes.

New items need vectors within minutes, not at the next rebuild. A streaming job embeds each new item with the current item tower and inserts it into the live index (HNSW supports incremental inserts; IVF assigns the vector to its nearest existing cluster). Deleted or ineligible items are removed or masked at query time, and periodic full rebuilds clean up and rebalance.

Changing item features are subtler. If the item tower takes "views in the last 24 hours" as input, an item's vector is stale the moment its view count changes. Either re-embed frequently-changing items on a schedule, or, more commonly, keep fast-moving statistics out of the item tower entirely: retrieval vectors encode what the item is (content, creator, long-run audience), and the ranker, which runs per request, uses what is happening to it now.

Training itself is usually continuous: Yi et al. trained sequentially on day-partitioned data, catching up on 15 days and then consuming each new day as it arrived, with the frequency estimator running inside the same stream. Continuous training plus periodic index rebuilds is the standard shape.

9. A query tower for search

The same design serves search: the query tower reads the query text (plus, optionally, the user's context), and the item tower reads the document or product. Training pairs are (query, clicked or purchased result) from search logs, again with in-batch negatives. The model learns what your users mean by their words: on a marketplace, "cheap phone" lands near the listings people who typed that actually bought, and "apple" lands near phones on an electronics site and near fruit on a grocery site, without anyone writing a rule.

This differs from the general-purpose text embedding models used for RAG (lm9, lm10) in what the training signal teaches. A general embedding model is trained to place texts with similar meaning close together. A behaviourally trained query tower learns what satisfies the query, which may not be semantic similarity at all: people searching "gift for dad" buy whisky glasses and wallets, which share no words or meaning with the query. The behaviourally trained model usually wins on head and torso queries with plenty of click data; the general model wins on rare queries, new products with no clicks and new markets, and needs no training data. Many search systems start from a general embedding model and fine-tune it on their query–click pairs, getting some of both.

Two practical differences from recommendation. Search queries are short and ambiguous, so the query tower benefits from context features (the category the user is browsing, their recent searches). And lexical matching remains essential for exact identifiers, which embeddings blur; rec5 combines both.

10. Evaluating a retriever

The standard offline metric is recall@K: on held-out (user, engaged item) pairs, does the engaged item appear in the top K retrieved? Use a temporal split (train on days before, test on the day after), as Yi et al. did, averaging over a week to cover weekly patterns. Report it for several K matching the funnel (100, 500, 1,000), and by slice: new items, new users, popular versus long-tail items.

Two cautions. Offline labels come from what the old system showed, so recall rewards imitating it; a retriever that surfaces good items the old system never showed is under-credited. And retrieval is one source among several: what matters is its marginal contribution. The more faithful offline evaluation is funnel recall from rec1, measured against the ranker's preferences on a large candidate set, plus the share of final impressions and engagements that come only from this source. The final judge is an online test of the whole system with and without the new retriever, which is how Yi et al. reported their result.

Why logQ makes the sampled softmax unbiased

The full softmax's normaliser is a sum over all N items, Z = Σj exp(sj). If you sample negatives j from a distribution Q with probabilities qj, then exp(sj)/qj is an unbiased estimate of Z for a single sample (importance sampling: E[exp(sj)/qj] = Σ qj exp(sj)/qj = Z). Dividing by qj inside the exponential is the same as subtracting ln qj from the logit. So the corrected logits make the sampled normaliser an estimate of the true one, and the gradient approximately matches the full softmax's gradient. The estimate is not exactly unbiased for the log-loss (the log of an expectation is not the expectation of a log), but it is consistent as the number of negatives grows, which with thousands of in-batch negatives is close enough. Jean et al. (2015) gave this derivation for machine translation; Yi et al. applied it to in-batch sampling, where qj is the item's frequency in the stream.

Why the towers cannot learn feature crosses, precisely

The score u(x) · v(y) is a sum of k products, each a function of the user times a function of the item. Any interaction the model expresses must be captured by k such products. A rule like "this user likes this creator's cooking videos but not their travel videos" needs the user side to know about creator × topic combinations and the item side to encode them; with enough dimensions it is representable in principle, but the model cannot compute an explicit conjunction of a user feature and an item feature, which a ranker with crossed inputs does trivially. In practice two-tower models capture broad affinity well and specific conditional preferences poorly. That is the formal reason the ranker exists, and why some systems add a lightweight "late interaction" step on retrieved candidates.

Multiple user vectors

One vector per user forces a single point to represent many interests: cooking, football and jazz end up averaged into a point near none of them, and nearest-neighbour search returns items from whichever interest dominates. Multi-interest approaches compute several user vectors, for example by clustering the user's recent items and embedding each cluster, and run one ANN search per vector, merging results. Pinterest's PinnerSage described this idea publicly, representing each user by several embeddings from clustered pin interactions. The cost is several searches per request; the gain is coverage of minority interests, which a single vector systematically suppresses.

Hashing IDs and the long tail of embeddings

An ID embedding table for 100 million items at 64 dimensions is 25.6 GB in float32, and most rows belong to items with too few interactions to learn anything. Common remedies: hash IDs into a fixed number of buckets (collisions share a row), use a frequency threshold below which items share an "unknown" embedding and rely on content features, or use several hash functions and sum their rows to reduce collision damage. Yi et al. assign out-of-vocabulary IDs to a fixed set of hash buckets and learn embeddings for each, noting this helps the model "capture new" content. The design choice decides how quickly a new item moves from content-only to collaborative representation.

Retrieval objectives beyond the next click

"Predict the next clicked item" is a convenient label, but it inherits clickbait and short-term bias. YouTube's 2016 paper noted that predicting the next watch performed much better in live tests than predicting a randomly held-out watch, because consumption is sequential and asymmetric (episode 2 follows episode 1, not the reverse), and that it deliberately represented the user's searches as an unordered bag of tokens, so the model could not learn to simply replay the search that immediately preceded a watch (the authors note the classifier is then "no longer directly aware of the origin of the label"). Its "example age" feature, the age of the training example, let the model learn time-dependent popularity, and was set to zero at serving to reflect "now". These are reminders that retrieval quality depends as much on how the training task is framed as on the network.

Temperature as a knob on hardness and calibration

With small τ, the gradient from each example is dominated by the negatives within a small cosine margin of the positive; this is similar in effect to a margin-based loss focused on hard negatives. With large batches, small τ plus in-batch negatives already performs implicit hard-negative mining, which is why explicit hard negatives sometimes add less than expected. Temperature also changes what the raw scores mean: they are not calibrated probabilities, so thresholds such as "retrieve only items with cosine above 0.6" are fragile across model versions; use top-K, not score thresholds, in retrieval.

Distillation from the ranker

A retriever trained on clicks and a ranker trained on clicks with richer features disagree about which items are best, and the funnel loses whatever the retriever misses. Some teams add a distillation term: on a sample of requests, run the ranker on a large candidate set and train the retriever to put the ranker's top items near the top as well. This aligns the stages (rec1), at the cost of making the retriever inherit the ranker's biases and of an offline pipeline that runs the ranker on many more items than are served.

The public case: YouTube's sampling-bias-corrected retrieval

Yi and colleagues' 2019 RecSys paper, "Sampling-Bias-Corrected Neural Modeling for Large Corpus Item Recommendations", describes a two-tower retriever deployed at YouTube to retrieve personalised suggestions from a corpus of tens of millions of videos, conditioned on a seed video the user is watching (the "watch next" setting). Both towers were three-layer networks with hidden sizes 1,024, 512 and 128; input embeddings were shared between towers where possible; training used Adagrad with learning rate 0.2 and batches of 8,192 on daily-partitioned data streamed into the trainer, with billions of clicked videos per day. Item frequencies for the correction came from the streaming estimator with H = 50 million and α = 0.01. An index of about 10 million videos was rebuilt every few hours and typically covered more than 90% of training examples.

Offline, the bias-corrected softmax beat the plain in-batch softmax at every temperature tested, and both beat a squared-loss baseline. In a live A/B test where the new retriever's candidates were added to the production system's, the plain in-batch softmax (τ = 0.05) improved the engagement metric by 0.20%, and the corrected version by 0.37%. Numbers that small are normal and meaningful at that scale: retrieval is one source among several, and a third of a percent of engagement across YouTube is a large effect. The case shows the whole lesson in one system: features rather than IDs, retrieval as sampled classification, the bias from in-batch negatives and its correction, normalisation and temperature, continuous training and periodic index rebuilds.

How two-tower retrieval fails in production

Version skew. The query tower is deployed before the index built with the matching item tower finishes; for an hour, retrieval quality collapses with no errors. Defence: version tags on every vector and index, a serving check that refuses mismatches, and an alert on retrieval-sourced engagement.

Index lag. The streaming embed job stalls; new uploads stop appearing in retrieval; creators complain before dashboards do. Monitor the age of the newest item in the index and the share of last-hour uploads that are indexed.

Popularity drift. After removing the logQ correction "to simplify", or after a frequency-estimator bug, the retriever over- or under-retrieves popular items. Monitor the distribution of retrieved items by popularity decile against what engaged users actually consume.

Embedding collapse. A bug or bad hyperparameters makes many item vectors nearly identical; ANN search returns essentially random items with high similarity. Monitor the spread of item vectors (average pairwise cosine on a sample, norms) per build, and fail the build if they shift sharply.

Filtered-out results. Retrieval returns 500 candidates, eligibility filters remove 70% in a small market, and the ranker sees a thin pool. Partition the index by market or use filtered search; monitor candidates surviving filters per market.

What to monitor

Index: build time, age, item count, share of eligible items present, recall of the ANN against exact search on a nightly sample (to catch index degradation separately from model quality). Serving: query-tower and ANN latency percentiles, candidates returned after filtering, fallback rate. Quality: share of impressions and engagements sourced only from this retriever, distribution of retrieved items by popularity and age, new-item time-to-first-impression. Training: loss with and without correction on a held-out day, recall@K by slice, frequency-estimator sanity (estimated versus counted frequencies for a sample of items).

Design: two-tower retrieval for a music streaming home screen

A music service has 80 million monthly users, 100 million tracks (of which 8 million account for 99% of plays), 150,000 new tracks uploaded per day, and a home screen requested 3,000 times per second at peak. The current retrieval is item-to-item co-occurrence from the user's last 20 plays. New releases take a week to appear and users complain the home screen only reflects what they played this morning. Design a two-tower retriever: features, training, negatives, serving, refresh, and how you will know it works.

Answer

Towers. Item tower: track ID embedding (hashed, with tracks below 50 plays sharing buckets), artist ID, album, genre and mood tags, audio embedding from a pre-trained audio model, language, release age bucket. Query tower: user ID embedding, the average of embeddings of the last 50 plays and separately of tracks saved in the last 90 days (long-term taste), skips in the session, time of day, day of week, device, country. Both towers are MLPs to 128 dimensions, normalised.

Labels and negatives. Positive: a play longer than 30 seconds, weighted by completion and by whether the user saved the track. In-batch softmax with batches of 8,192 and the logQ correction using streaming frequency estimates, since a few hit tracks dominate plays; plus 2,000 uniform negatives per batch from the 8 million track active catalogue so the tail is pushed too (mixed negatives). Temperature tuned around 0.05. Shown-and-skipped tracks from the home screen as a modest share of hard negatives.

Serving. Index the 8 million active tracks plus all releases from the last 90 days: about 9 million × 128 × 1 byte (int8) ≈ 1.2 GB, replicated; at roughly 2,000 searches per second per replica, 3,000 QPS needs 2, run 4-6 across zones. Query tower on CPU per request from features in the session store (last 50 plays updated nearline). Retrieve 500, merged with co-occurrence and a "new releases from followed artists" source.

Refresh. Daily continuous training; weekly or daily full re-embed (9 million tracks, minutes on one GPU) with index rebuild and version-locked switch. Streaming embed of the 150,000 daily uploads within minutes, from content features alone. That fixes the week-long delay.

Evaluation. Offline: recall@500 on the next day's plays, sliced by new releases, long-tail tracks and light users; funnel recall against the ranker. Online: A/B test adding the retriever; primary metric home-screen listening minutes per user, guardrails on skip rate and diversity (distinct artists per user per week), and new-release share of plays.
