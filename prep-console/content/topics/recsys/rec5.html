A podcast app's search runs on a well-tuned keyword engine. Type "Joe Rogan 1169" and you get exactly that episode. Type a guest's name and you get every show they appeared on. Then a listener types electric cars climate impact and gets nothing at all. There are hundreds of relevant episodes; their titles and descriptions say things like "Are EVs really greener?" and "The carbon cost of batteries". Not one contains all of the query's words, and the ones that contain some of them are about other things. Spotify described exactly this query returning no results in its 2022 write-up about adding natural-language search to podcasts.

The obvious fix is to switch to embedding search, which matches meaning. It handles the climate query beautifully. Then a listener searches for an episode number, a guest's unusual surname or a show's exact title, and gets a list of vaguely related episodes about similar topics, with the exact match somewhere on page two. Embeddings are good at "about the same thing" and bad at "this exact string".

Real search engines therefore do both and merge the results, which is called hybrid search. That raises a deceptively hard question: how do you merge two ranked lists whose scores mean completely different things? And before either retriever runs, there is the query itself to deal with: misspelled ("climat"), abbreviated ("ev"), ambiguous ("apple"), or carrying structure the words hide ("red nike running shoes under 100" is a category, a brand, a colour and a price filter). Making sense of the query before retrieval is query understanding, and on most search products it moves quality as much as the retrieval models do.

This lesson covers both halves. It shows why lexical and dense retrieval fail on different queries, works reciprocal rank fusion by hand on two ranked lists and shows why simple score blending misleads, then goes through query normalisation, spelling correction with the noisy-channel model, expansion and intent detection. It ends with a comparison that matters for AI engineers: retrieval for an LLM's context window (lm9, ev6) is not the same problem as search for people, even when the components are identical.

Imagine a library with two librarians. The first has memorised the card catalogue: give her exact words and she finds every book containing them instantly, but if you describe a book in your own words she is stuck. The second has read every book and understands what each is about: describe what you want and she suggests books on that theme, but ask for the book with a specific title and she hands you several on the same subject, unsure which you meant. A sensible visitor asks both and combines their suggestions, trusting most the books both of them pick.

That is hybrid search. The first librarian is lexical retrieval (rec2), the second is dense retrieval (rec4, lm10). Each returns a ranked list. Combining them well is the fusion step. The simplest robust method, reciprocal rank fusion, ignores how confident each librarian sounds (their scores, which are on incomparable scales) and uses only the order in which they listed books: a book near the top of both lists wins; a book at the top of one list still makes it.

Why not just pick the better librarian? Because which one is better depends on the question, and you do not know in advance which kind of question you have. On a typical product search box, a large share of traffic is short and specific (a brand, a model, a product name), where the catalogue librarian shines, and a long tail is descriptive ("something to stop the door slamming"), where the well-read librarian shines. Average quality is maximised by consulting both on every question and letting agreement and a learned ranker sort it out, at the price of running two retrievers. In latency terms that price is small, because they run at the same time, not one after the other.

The analogy breaks in two places, and both matter. Real librarians can tell you when they are unsure; retrievers always return a full list, so a dense retriever will happily return 200 "similar" documents for a query that has exactly one right answer. And the visitor's request is not fixed: a good search engine rewrites the request first, fixing typos, adding synonyms, recognising that "ev" means electric vehicle in this context and that "under 100" is a price limit. That rewriting, query understanding, is the first box in the picture, and a weak one caps everything after it.

One more piece of vocabulary before the details. A retriever that finds the right documents but ranks them badly has good recall and poor precision at the top; one that ranks a few documents perfectly but misses many has the reverse. Fusion is mostly a recall tool (union the candidates, reward agreement); the ranker is the precision tool. Keeping those roles separate makes design discussions much clearer.

A useful way to hold the whole lesson: query understanding decides what is being asked, retrieval decides which documents could answer it, fusion decides how to merge different opinions, and the ranker decides the order people see. Problems in search usually live in exactly one of those four, and the debugging question is always which.

1. Two retrievers, two kinds of failure

It is worth being precise about why lexical and dense retrieval fail differently, because it decides when hybrid helps.

Lexical retrieval (BM25 over an inverted index) scores documents by shared terms, weighted by rarity. It fails on vocabulary mismatch: the relevant document uses different words ("EV" or "battery car" for "electric car", "carbon footprint" for "climate impact"). It also fails on conceptual queries with no distinctive words ("something relaxing to listen to while studying"). It succeeds on rare exact strings: identifiers, names, codes, quoted phrases, newly coined terms, where a match is strong evidence.

Dense retrieval (embeddings plus ANN) scores by learned similarity of meaning. It succeeds on paraphrase and concepts. It fails on exact-match needs: a model number becomes a few subword tokens whose embedding is dominated by "this is a product code", so all product codes look alike; a rare surname may be tokenised into fragments the model has no meaning for. It can fail on negation and precise constraints ("laptops without touchscreen" lands near touchscreen laptops). It also fails quietly: there is always a nearest neighbour, so it never returns "no results", only less relevant ones.

Because the failure sets overlap little, combining them helps most queries and hurts few, provided the fusion is sensible. BEIR (rec2) quantified the complementary strengths across 18 datasets; most production teams that publish on hybrid search report the same pattern on their own traffic.

2. Reciprocal rank fusion, worked

Suppose the query "electric car battery recycling" goes to both retrievers. BM25 returns, in order: D3, D1, D7, D2, D9, D5. The dense retriever returns: D1, D4, D3, D8, D2, D6. Their raw scores are useless for comparison: BM25's run from 18.2 down to 8.7, the dense cosines from 0.82 down to 0.77.

Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF), introduced by Cormack, Clarke and Büttcher in 2009, gives each document the sum, over the lists it appears in, of 1/(k + rank), with ranks starting at 1. Elasticsearch's RRF retriever defaults to k = 60 (the rank_constant), the value the original paper fixed in a pilot study and then validated. Compute each document:

Fused order: D1, D3, D2, D4, D7, D8, D9, D5/D6. Three observations. Documents both retrievers found (D1, D3, D2) take the top three, even D2, which neither ranked above fourth: agreement is strong evidence. Within the agreed set, ranks still matter, but only a little: D1 (2nd and 1st) edges D3 (1st and 3rd). And a document found by only one retriever at a high rank (D4, dense rank 2) still beats documents found only by one retriever lower down.

The role of k. With k = 60, the difference between rank 1 (1/61) and rank 5 (1/65) is small, about 6%; the scheme mostly rewards appearing in several lists. With k = 1, rank 1 is worth 1/2 and rank 5 only 1/6, so top positions dominate: on these lists, D1 scores 0.833, D3 0.750, D2 0.367 and D4 0.333, nearly tying D2 (found by both) with D4 (found by one). Large k is the conservative choice when you trust agreement more than either retriever's top pick. Elasticsearch also exposes rank_window_size, how deep into each list to look (default: the requested result size); fusing the top 100 of each list rather than the top 10 lets agreement deeper down count.

3. Why score blending misleads

The tempting alternative is to normalise each list's scores to 0-1 and take a weighted average, a convex combination. With min-max normalisation (subtract the list's minimum, divide by its range) and equal weights, on the same lists:

  • BM25 scores 18.2, 11.0, 10.4, 9.9, 9.1, 8.7 for D3, D1, D7, D2, D9, D5 become 1.00, 0.24, 0.18, 0.13, 0.04, 0.00. D3's unusually high score (perhaps it repeats a rare query term) squashes everything else towards zero.
  • Dense cosines 0.82, 0.80, 0.79, 0.78, 0.775, 0.77 for D1, D4, D3, D8, D2, D6 become 1.0, 0.6, 0.4, 0.2, 0.1, 0.0. A spread of 0.05 in cosine, which may be noise, is stretched across the whole range.

Averaging (missing documents count as 0): D3 0.70, D1 0.62, D4 0.30, D2 0.11, D8 0.10, D7 0.09. Now D4, found by one retriever, sits well above D2, found by both, and the order hinges on one outlier score. Change the depth of either list (fetch 50 instead of 6) and the minimums move, changing every normalised score. That fragility is why RRF is the robust default. Score blending can work, with per-query z-score normalisation, a tuned weight and a labelled set to tune on; OpenSearch's hybrid query offers min-max and L2 normalisation with weighted combination for teams that want it, and Elasticsearch supports both styles. But it must be tuned and monitored, because a change to either retriever's model shifts its score distribution and silently changes the blend.

4. Learned fusion: let the ranker decide

RRF is the right candidate-merging step, but the best final order usually comes from the learned ranker (rec6, rec7), which receives, for each candidate, the BM25 score per field, the dense cosine, each retriever's rank, whether the full query appears as a phrase, the attribute matches from query understanding, popularity, freshness and personalisation features. Trained on clicks or labels, it learns when to trust which signal: exact title matches dominate for navigational queries, the cosine matters more for long descriptive ones. Spotify's podcast search did exactly this: dense retrieval was added as an extra candidate source alongside Elasticsearch, and the query–episode cosine similarity was added as an input feature to the final re-ranking model.

The practical division of labour: the candidate stage unions both retrievers' top few hundred (RRF or plain union with quotas, rec1), and the ranker does the weighing. RRF on its own is the right final order when there is no learned ranker yet, as in many RAG systems and internal tools.

5. Query understanding: normalisation

Query understanding starts with the dull steps that prevent silly misses. Normalisation lowercases, folds accents (or not, per language), standardises Unicode, collapses whitespace, and handles units and numbers ("55in", "55 inch", "55\"" all become one form). Tokenisation of identifiers splits or joins consistently: "WH1000XM5", "WH-1000XM5" and "wh 1000 xm5" should all reach the same product. Language detection picks the analyser and the language-specific fields. These must mirror what the index does to documents (rec2's cardinal rule) or matches are lost.

6. Spelling correction with the noisy-channel model

Misspellings are common in typed search, especially on phones, and every one that goes uncorrected risks a null or poor result. The standard framing is the noisy channel: the user intended some query c, the act of typing corrupted it into the observed string t, and the corrector picks the c that best explains t:

best c = argmax over candidates c:   P(t | c) × P(c)

P(c), the language model or prior, says how plausible the intended query is: estimated from the query log and catalogue vocabulary, ideally with context ("running sheos" makes "shoes" more plausible than "sheds"). P(t | c), the error model, says how likely this particular typo is: adjacent-key substitutions and transpositions are common, dropped double letters are common, wholesale rewrites are rare. Candidates come from strings within a small edit distance (insertions, deletions, substitutions and, in the Damerau variant, transpositions of adjacent letters) of each query word.

Worked example: the user types sheos. Candidates within one edit include "shoes" (transpose e and o), "shes" (delete o), "sheds" (substitute o with d). From the query log, "shoes" was searched 200,000 times last month, "sheds" 2,000, "shes" 50. Suppose the error model gives a transposition probability of 0.002, and a substitution of a non-adjacent key 0.0005, and a deletion 0.001. Scores: shoes 0.002 × 200,000 = 400; sheds 0.0005 × 2,000 = 1; shes 0.001 × 50 = 0.05. "Shoes" wins by a factor of 400 over the runner-up. Also consider the original: "sheos" itself was searched 300 times (people do misspell it) but returns few results; with P(t | t) = 0.95 for "no typo", its score is 0.95 × 300 = 285, close to "shoes". This is why production systems compare the correction's score with the original's and the original's result count: correct automatically when the correction wins clearly and the original returns little; otherwise show results for the original with "Did you mean: shoes?".

The vocabulary is the trap. A dictionary-based corrector will "fix" brand names, product codes and new slang into common words. The candidates and the prior must come from your own query logs and catalogue, and any term that exists in the catalogue should be protected from correction. Error models are best learned from logs too: sequences where a user typed a query, got poor results and immediately retyped a close variant and clicked are free training pairs.

7. Expansion and rewriting

Query expansion adds terms to catch documents that do not use the query's words. Sources, from oldest to newest:

  • Curated synonyms: "tv" ↔ "television", "sofa" ↔ "couch". Precise, labour-intensive, domain-specific.
  • Behavioural mining: pairs of queries users reformulate into each other within a session, or queries that lead to clicks on the same items. "ev" and "electric car" share clicked results, so they are probably equivalent here.
  • Pseudo-relevance feedback: run the query, take the top 10 results, add terms that are frequent in them but not in the query, and run again. It helps when the first pass is mostly right and drifts badly when it is not.
  • Model-based expansion: an embedding model proposes related terms, or an LLM rewrites the query or generates a hypothetical answer to embed (rec14 discusses the cost at query rates).

Two rules keep expansion safe. Weight expanded terms below the original ones (in Elasticsearch, a bool query where the original is a must-match or boosted clause and expansions are optional should clauses), so documents matching the user's actual words still win. And make expansions context-aware: "apple" → "fruit" is right on a grocery site and wrong on an electronics site; "java" → "coffee" is right in a café chain's search and disastrous in a programming forum. Synonyms are often asymmetric: "iphone" implies "phone", but "phone" should not retrieve only iPhones.

Dense retrieval is, in a sense, implicit expansion: it matches documents that use related words without adding them to the query. This is one reason hybrid systems often need less hand-built expansion than pure lexical ones.

8. Intent: category, attributes and routing

Many queries hide structure. "red nike running shoes under 100" is not five words to match; it is a category (running shoes), a brand (Nike), a colour (red) and a price constraint (under 100). An attribute extractor, a sequence-labelling model or a rule set over a vocabulary of known brands and colours, tags each span, and the system turns tags into filters ("price < 100") or strong ranking features ("brand matches"). Without it, retrieval returns red socks and Nike headbands that happen to share words.

Category prediction classifies the query into the catalogue's taxonomy, using a model trained on which categories users clicked or bought from after each query. A confident prediction lets retrieval boost or filter to that category; a split prediction ("jaguar": cars 55%, animals 30%, guitars 15%) signals ambiguity, and the page should mix interpretations. Query-type classification distinguishes navigational queries (one known target: "nike air max 90", "netflix login"), where the exact item must be first and the rest barely matters, from exploratory ones ("comfortable shoes for standing all day"), where variety and dense retrieval matter more, and from questions, which might be routed to an answer system.

Intent outputs drive routing: identifier queries go lexical-first; long natural-language queries weight dense retrieval more; questions in a help centre may trigger a generated answer above the results. Routing is cheap and powerful, and its classifier needs the same care as any model: labelled data, per-class evaluation and monitoring.

9. Weighted fusion, more sources and personalisation

Real systems rarely fuse exactly two lists. A marketplace might merge BM25 on titles, BM25 on descriptions, a dense retriever, a "previously purchased by this user" source and a "popular for this category" source. RRF extends naturally: sum 1/(k + rank) over however many lists contain the document. Two refinements are common. Weighted RRF multiplies each list's contribution by a weight, ws/(k + rank), so a source known to be strong for this query class counts more; the weights can be set per query class from labelled data (for identifier queries, BM25 on titles might get weight 2 and dense 0.5). Per-source depth limits how far into each list fusion looks, which stops a weak source's long tail from diluting the pool.

Personalisation enters search in three places. Query understanding can use the user's context to resolve ambiguity: a user who has bought guitar strings is more likely to mean the guitar brand by "jaguar". Retrieval can add a personal source, such as items the user viewed or bought before that match the query (people re-find things constantly). And the ranker can take user features, as in recommendation. Search personalisation is usually lighter than feed personalisation, because the query is a strong, explicit statement of intent and users are surprised when two people get very different results for the same words; most teams personalise the order among relevant results rather than which results are relevant.

10. The latency budget of query understanding

Everything in sections 5-8 happens before retrieval starts, so it sits on the critical path of every search (rec1). A realistic budget for a search request of 150 ms at p99 might give query understanding 15-20 ms in total. That rules out a large model call per query: a hosted LLM rewrite takes hundreds of milliseconds. What fits: normalisation and identifier detection (microseconds), dictionary and query-log lookups for spelling (sub-millisecond with precomputed candidate tables), a small classifier for category and query type (a few milliseconds on CPU), an attribute tagger (a few milliseconds), and the query encoder for dense retrieval (5-20 ms on CPU for a small model, less on GPU).

Two tricks stretch the budget. Caching: the head of the query distribution repeats constantly, so the full understanding result (correction, category, attributes, query embedding) for the top million queries can be cached and served in a millisecond; Spotify cached query vectors for exactly this reason. Precomputation: for head queries, expensive rewrites, including LLM-generated expansions, can be produced offline in batch, reviewed, and stored as a lookup table, leaving live models only for the tail. The tail, where caching fails, is also where expensive understanding helps most, so some systems allow a slower path for rare, low-result queries only.

11. Evaluating hybrid search and query understanding

Evaluate each component and the whole, and always by query class. For retrieval and fusion, a labelled query set with graded relevance gives recall@100 (did the candidates contain the relevant items?) and nDCG@10 (are they in a good order?) for lexical alone, dense alone, RRF and the full ranker. The set must be stratified: if 70% of sampled traffic is head queries where BM25 already works, the average hides large changes on the tail. Report per class (navigational, identifier, descriptive, attribute-heavy, question-like) and per head, torso and tail.

For the speller, build pairs of (typed, intended) from logs and labelling, and measure correction precision (when it corrects, is it right?) and recall (of the misspellings, how many does it fix?), with special attention to false corrections of valid rare queries, which are far more damaging than missed corrections. For category and attribute models, standard classification metrics per class (ml7), plus the downstream effect: how often a wrong category filter removed the item the user eventually bought.

Online, the metrics are null-result rate, reformulation rate, click-through on the top results, and conversion per search, again per class. A healthy launch typically shows null results falling sharply for descriptive queries while navigational and identifier metrics stay flat; if navigational first-result click-through drops, fusion is overriding exact matches and needs routing or ranker features.

12. Semantic search for an LLM versus for people

AI engineers often build retrieval for RAG (lm9) and then for a product's search box, assuming the same pipeline serves both. The components overlap (BM25, embeddings, RRF, cross-encoder re-rankers), but the problems differ in ways that change the design.

Practical consequences. For RAG, fuse deep (top 50-100 from each retriever) and re-rank with a cross-encoder to choose the 5-20 chunks that fit the budget; measure recall@k and groundedness (ev6). For people, invest in query understanding, navigational precision and the ranker; measure nDCG@5 or @10, click-through on the first results, null-result rate and conversion. And for people, latency is a hard product constraint (rec1), while for RAG the LLM call dwarfs retrieval time, so a slower, more thorough retrieval is often affordable.

Why RRF works as well as it does

RRF has three properties that explain its robustness. It is scale-free: monotone changes to either retriever's scores (a new model version with a different cosine range) do not affect it. It is bounded: no single list can contribute more than 1/(k + 1), so one retriever's overconfident outlier cannot dominate. And with large k it approximates a vote: the fused order is mostly decided by how many lists a document appears in, with rank as a tiebreak, which is exactly the right behaviour when each retriever is noisy but their errors are independent. Its weakness is the flip side: it throws away calibrated confidence. If one retriever is far better than the other for a query class, RRF still treats them equally; weighted RRF (multiplying each list's contribution by a per-retriever or per-query-class weight) or a learned ranker fixes that.

Normalisation choices if you do blend scores

If you blend scores, normalise per query, not globally, because BM25's scale changes with query length and term rarity. Z-score normalisation (subtract the list's mean, divide by its standard deviation) is less sensitive to a single outlier than min-max. Even better is calibrating each retriever's score to a probability of relevance with a small model fitted on labelled data (ml15), after which a weighted sum has meaning. Fix the depth of each list used for normalisation, or the statistics drift with the number of results. And re-tune whenever either retriever changes, which in practice means score blending is a maintenance commitment that rank fusion avoids.

Late interaction: a middle ground

ColBERT-style models keep one embedding per token for documents and queries and score a pair by summing, over query tokens, the maximum similarity to any document token. This preserves some exact-match behaviour (a rare token in the query must match some token in the document) while capturing meaning, and in BEIR it averaged slightly above BM25 out of domain. The cost is storage (one vector per token instead of per document, mitigated by compression) and a more complex index. It is a reasonable choice for corpora where both exact terms and paraphrase matter, such as technical documentation.

Learned sparse retrieval and document expansion

Another way to get hybrid behaviour from one index is to make the lexical side smarter: document-expansion models add likely query terms to each document before indexing, and learned sparse models assign weights to vocabulary terms (including ones not in the text). Both run on an inverted index with BM25-like efficiency and close part of the vocabulary gap while keeping exact-match strength. They are attractive when operating a separate vector index is costly, and they combine well with dense retrieval too.

Query understanding as a model, not a rule set

Early search teams built query understanding as dozens of rules. Mature teams consolidate it into a few models trained on behavioural data: a query-to-category classifier trained on click and purchase categories, a sequence tagger for attributes trained on queries whose clicked items' attributes reveal the labels, and a rewrite model trained on reformulation pairs. Training labels come almost for free from logs, which also means they inherit the logs' biases: a category that the old system never showed for a query will never be learned as its intent. Exploration traffic (rec11) and periodic human labelling of samples keep the models honest.

Typeahead shapes the queries you get

Query understanding does not start with a submitted query. Typeahead (in11) suggests completions on every keystroke, and on many products a large share of submitted queries are accepted suggestions. That has two consequences. Suggestions normalise queries towards popular, well-spelled forms, so the long tail of raw typed text is smaller than it would be, and spelling correction partly moves into the typeahead index. And whatever the suggestion system favours becomes what users search for, which feeds back into the query log that trains the speller, the synonym miner and the category classifier. A suggestion bug, such as dropping a brand from the suggestion index, can therefore look like a drop in search demand for that brand. Analysing search quality without knowing which queries came from suggestions misses this.

Null results and the long tail

Queries follow a long-tailed distribution: a few thousand head queries account for a large share of volume, and millions of tail queries occur once or twice. Head queries can be hand-tuned and have abundant click data; tail queries are where spelling, expansion, dense retrieval and robust fusion earn their keep, and where null results concentrate. A useful habit: review the top null-result and low-click queries weekly, classify the failure (spelling, vocabulary mismatch, missing inventory, intent misread, tokenisation), and feed each class to the right fix. Missing inventory is a business signal, not a search bug, and is worth reporting.

The public case: Spotify's natural-language podcast search

In March 2022 Spotify's engineering blog described adding natural-language search for podcast episodes. The existing system was Elasticsearch-based term matching, and the post gives the motivating failure directly: the query "electric cars climate impact" returned no results even though relevant episodes existed, because none matched the exact words. Spotify added dense retrieval as an additional candidate source rather than a replacement. The model was a bi-encoder (one shared transformer encoder for queries and episodes, compared with cosine similarity) starting from the Universal Sentence Encoder CMLM model, chosen because it produces sentence embeddings directly and covers more than 100 languages, then fine-tuned on Spotify's own data.

The training data came from four places: successful historical podcast searches from Elasticsearch logs, query reformulations (sessions where an unsuccessful query was followed by a successful one), synthetic queries generated with a BART model fine-tuned on MS MARCO, and a small manually curated set of semantic queries used only for evaluation. Training used in-batch negatives, producing B positive pairs and B² − B negative pairs per batch, with hard-negative mining and a margin loss added in later iterations. Episode vectors were indexed in Vespa, a search engine with native approximate-nearest-neighbour support, serving tens of millions of episodes; query vectors were computed by GPU inference on Google Cloud Vertex AI with a cache so repeated queries are not re-encoded. The final-stage re-ranking model received the query–episode cosine similarity as a new input feature alongside its existing ones. A/B testing showed a significant increase in podcast engagement, and the feature had rolled out to most users by publication.

Every idea in this lesson appears: complementary failure of lexical search, dense retrieval as an extra source, behavioural and synthetic training data, in-batch negatives, the cosine fed to a learned ranker as fusion, and online evaluation as the final word.

How hybrid search fails in production

Navigational regressions. After adding dense retrieval, exact-title and identifier searches lose their first-place result to "similar" items. Symptom: first-result click-through drops for navigational queries while overall metrics look flat. Fix with routing, exact-match features in the ranker, or per-class fusion weights.

Over-eager spelling correction. A new brand launches and its name is "corrected" to a common word; the brand's searches return nothing relevant. Symptom: a spike of reformulations and null results for one term. Protect catalogue vocabulary and monitor corrections by term.

Expansion drift. A mined synonym ("pink" ↔ "rose", learned from a perfume-heavy season) leaks into clothing search. Symptom: precision complaints for one category. Keep expansions category-scoped and review newly mined synonyms before activation.

Score-blend drift. A team blending normalised scores updates the embedding model; its cosine range shifts from 0.7-0.85 to 0.3-0.6, and the blend silently tilts towards BM25. RRF would not have noticed; a blend must be re-tuned. Monitor the share of final results contributed by each retriever.

Latency from the second retriever. The embedding service adds 20-40 ms of query encoding plus ANN search. Run it in parallel with lexical retrieval with a deadline (rec1), cache query embeddings for frequent queries (as Spotify did), and fall back to lexical-only if it is late.

What to monitor

Null-result rate and low-result rate; reformulation rate (a second query within seconds); first-result and top-3 click-through; conversion per search; all sliced by query class (navigational, identifier, descriptive, question) and by head versus tail. Per-retriever contribution: share of clicked results found by lexical only, dense only, both. Speller: correction rate, share of corrections followed by a reformulation back to the original (a sign the correction was wrong). Latency per retriever and fallback rate.

Write edits1(word) generating all strings one deletion, transposition, substitution or insertion away (Norvig's approach). Score candidates by count × error probability, with transposition 0.002, deletion 0.001, insertion 0.001, substitution 0.0005, and the original word by its own count × 0.95.
  5. Test "sheos", "runing", "nikee" and a brand name from your list typed correctly. Add a rule: never correct a word that is in the dictionary with count ≥ 100.

What to look for. RRF orders should match the table, k = 1 should let single-list top results jump up, and k = 1,000 should make the order almost purely "how many lists". The min-max blend should change a lot when one outlier score changes, and RRF not at all, which is the robustness argument in one experiment. The speller should fix the typos, leave the correctly typed brand alone, and show how close "sheos" itself comes to its correction, which is why real systems use "did you mean" for borderline cases.

Diagnose and design: hybrid search for a hardware retailer

A DIY retailer's search (1.2 million products, 4,000 queries per second at peak) is BM25-only. Analysis of a week of logs shows: 9% null-result queries; most are descriptive ("thing to stop door slamming", "paint for bathroom ceiling mould"); 22% of queries contain a part or model number; navigational queries ("bosch psb 1800") convert well today. The team proposes switching entirely to an off-the-shelf embedding model. Respond with a design and an evaluation plan.

Answer

Do not switch; add. Pure dense retrieval would likely fix many descriptive null results but damage the 22% of queries with part numbers and the navigational ones, which convert well and blur under embeddings. Run both retrievers in parallel and fuse.

Query understanding first. Normalise identifiers (strip spaces and hyphens in codes; "psb1800", "psb 1800", "PSB-1800" all match) and detect them with patterns plus a catalogue lookup; route identifier queries lexical-first with exact-match boosts. Spelling correction from the query log and catalogue vocabulary, protecting brand and model names. Category and attribute extraction for things like sizes ("2.5 l", "50 mm"), colours and finishes, applied as boosts or filters.

Retrieval and fusion. BM25 over title, brand, description and specification fields (BM25F-style), top 200; dense retrieval with the embedding model fine-tuned on the retailer's query–purchase pairs (in-batch negatives), top 200, run in parallel with a 30 ms deadline and a query-embedding cache for the head queries. Merge with RRF (k = 60) as the candidate list and as the ranking until a learned ranker exists; then feed BM25 per field, cosine, both ranks, exact-code match and attribute matches into the ranker.

Capacity. 4,000 QPS of query encoding: with a cache hit rate of, say, 60% on head queries, 1,600 encodes per second; a small encoder on a few CPU nodes or one GPU handles that. The product index: 1.2 million × 384 dimensions × 1 byte ≈ 0.5 GB, replicated.

Evaluation. Offline: a labelled set stratified by query class (descriptive, identifier, navigational, attribute-heavy, null-result), measuring recall@100 and nDCG@10 for BM25, dense, RRF and the ranker; ship only if identifier and navigational classes do not regress. Online A/B: primary metric revenue or conversion per search; secondary null-result rate and reformulation rate; guardrails on navigational first-result click-through and p99 latency. Expect the null-result rate to fall substantially for descriptive queries, and confirm the identifier segment is flat.
