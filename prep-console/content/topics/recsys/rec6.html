The retrieval stages from rec2 to rec5 hand over a few hundred candidates. They are all plausible: matching the query, near the user's taste, popular with similar people. Now one decision decides what the user actually sees: which of these 300 go in the first five slots? The two-tower model that found them cannot answer that well, because by design it never lets the user and the item meet before a single dot product. It cannot know that this user finishes every video from this creator, or that they skip anything over ten minutes on their phone in the morning, or that this item's click rate collapsed in the last hour because its thumbnail is misleading.

The ranking model is where all of that comes together. It sees each (user, item, context) triple with every feature the company can compute, and predicts what will happen: will they click, how long will they watch, will they like it, will they hide it? Small improvements here move company-level metrics. Google's Wide & Deep paper reported a 3.9% increase in app installs from changing the ranking model on the Google Play store, a store with over a billion active users. DCN-v2's authors say that, for one of their production models, a 0.1% improvement in its offline loss metric is considered significant; they got 0.6%.

The ranking model is also where the hardest engineering lives. It has the largest compute budget per item, the largest memory footprint (embedding tables of tens of gigabytes), the most features to keep consistent between training and serving, and several outputs that must be combined into one order in a way the product team agrees with. And it is the model most interviewers will ask you to design.

This lesson builds the ranker up historically, because each step fixed a specific weakness of the one before: logistic regression with crossed features (with a click probability worked by hand), gradient-boosted trees, embeddings, Wide & Deep, factorisation machines, DCN (with one cross layer computed by hand) and DLRM, attention over user histories, multi-task heads and MMoE, and finally the value model and calibration that turn predictions into an order. The training objectives specific to ranking, pairwise and listwise losses, are rec7's subject; the metrics are rec8's.

Imagine a bookshop assistant who knows every regular customer. When someone walks in, the assistant considers the 30 books on the "new and notable" table and quickly sorts them for this person. The assistant uses everything: what the customer bought before, which authors they love, that they never buy hardbacks, that it is lunchtime so they want something light, that one of the books has been returned by three other customers this week. The assistant is not judging books in general or the customer in general; they are judging this book for this person right now. That judgement, made 30 times and then sorted, is ranking.

A ranking model does the same thing as a function. Its input is a long list of features about the user, the item, the context and, crucially, the combination of user and item. Its output is one or several predictions: the probability of a click, the expected minutes watched, the probability of a like or a "not interested". A small formula, the value model, combines the predictions into one score, and the candidates are sorted by score.

The assistant analogy breaks in two places worth noticing. The assistant learns from conversations; the model learns only from what users did with items that were shown, at the positions they were shown, which is biased in ways rec10 untangles. And the assistant judges books one at a time but presents a table; the model also scores items one at a time, and making the whole page good is a separate job (rec12). Within those limits, the ranker is the most accurate opinion the system has.

It also helps to know what a ranker is not asked to do. It does not search the catalogue; retrieval did that. It does not decide the page layout, diversity or business rules; re-ranking does (rec12). It does not decide what the company values; the value model's weights do, and those are a product decision. Its job is narrow and hard: given this user, this context and this candidate, predict each outcome as accurately and as well-calibrated as possible, a few hundred times per request, within tens of milliseconds. Keeping that narrow job in mind prevents a common interview mistake: trying to solve diversity or freshness inside the ranker's loss when a later stage is the right place.

The history of ranking models is mostly a history of one question: how do we let the model learn interactions between features? "Brazilian users like football" is an interaction between a user feature and an item feature. Logistic regression cannot learn it unless an engineer builds the crossed feature. Trees learn some interactions on their own. Deep models with embeddings and explicit cross layers learn many automatically. Every architecture in this lesson is an answer to that question with a different balance of accuracy, cost and engineering effort.

1. The prediction task and its data

A ranking model is trained on impressions: each row is one item shown to one user in one context, with labels for what happened (clicked, watched 37 seconds, liked, nothing). Billions of rows a day are normal at large companies; Wide & Deep was trained on over 500 billion examples. The label is typically very imbalanced: click rates of 1-5% are common, rarer actions (share, follow, purchase) far lower. Each row also records the features as they were at serving time (rec9 explains why logging them, rather than recomputing later, matters).

The model outputs a probability for binary actions and an expected value for continuous ones. The training loss for a binary head is log loss (cross-entropy): for an impression with label y (1 or 0) and prediction p, the loss is −[y ln p + (1 − y) ln(1 − p)]. It punishes confident wrong predictions heavily and rewards good calibration. Because the click rate itself varies by surface, a useful comparable version is normalised entropy (NE), introduced in Facebook's 2014 ads paper: the model's average log loss divided by the log loss you would get by always predicting the average click rate. NE below 1 means the model beats that constant; improvements are reported as relative NE reductions, and the paper notes that most feature-engineering experiments only decrease NE "by a fraction of a percentage".

2. Logistic regression and crossed features

The baseline that dominated ranking and ads for a decade is logistic regression (ml6) over millions of sparse binary features. Each categorical value becomes its own feature ("country = BR", "category = football", "device = mobile"), numeric features are bucketed or transformed, and the model learns one weight per feature. The prediction is σ(sum of weights of active features + bias), where σ(z) = 1/(1 + e−z).

Worked example. Predict whether a user in Brazil on a phone will click a football highlight. Active features and learned weights:

Without the cross, the logit is −3.2 + 1.08 + 0.1 + 0.2 − 0.2 = −2.02, and P(click) = 1/(1 + e2.02) = 11.7%. With it, the logit is −0.92 and P(click) = 1/(1 + e0.92) = 28.5%. The cross says "this combination is much more clickable than country and category separately suggest", something an additive model cannot otherwise express: the separate weights for BR (0.1) and football (0.2) are small because, averaged over all countries and categories, neither matters much.

That is the strength and the burden of the approach. Crossed features are precise, cheap and interpretable, and with millions of them logistic regression memorises a lot of useful structure; Wide & Deep calls this memorisation. But someone must decide which crosses to build, the number of possible crosses explodes (country × category × device × hour already has millions of combinations), and a cross never seen in training gets no weight, so the model cannot generalise to new combinations.

3. Gradient-boosted trees

Gradient-boosted decision trees (ml10) learn interactions automatically: a tree that splits on "item click rate > 0.04" and then on "user tenure < 30 days" has learned that the effect of click rate depends on tenure. GBDTs need no feature scaling, handle missing values, and with a few hundred dense features they are hard to beat with little tuning. LambdaMART, a GBDT trained with a ranking objective (rec7), was for years the state of the art in web search ranking.

Their weaknesses appear with scale and with IDs. A tree cannot use a feature with 100 million values (item ID) sensibly; it can only split it into "this side or that side". Trees do not share representations: what is learned about item A says nothing about similar item B unless dense features make them look alike. Multi-task outputs, sequences and incremental online training are awkward.

Facebook's 2014 paper on ads click prediction combined the two families neatly: train a GBDT, and use the index of the leaf each example lands in, in every tree, as a categorical input to logistic regression. Each leaf is effectively a learned cross of the conditions along its path. Their reported normalised entropy, relative to trees alone, was 99.43% for logistic regression alone and 96.58% for trees plus logistic regression: a 3.4% improvement, which they describe as significant given that most feature work moves NE by a fraction of a percent. The same paper found that retraining daily instead of weekly reduced NE by about 1%, an early measurement of how quickly click models go stale.

4. Numeric features: counts, rates and their transforms

Before the deep models, a word about the dense numeric features that every ranker uses, because how they are prepared matters as much as the architecture. The most predictive are usually counters and rates: the item's impressions and clicks over the last hour, day and week; its click-through rate; the creator's average completion rate; the user's click rate on this category; the time since the user last engaged with this creator. They have heavy-tailed distributions (a viral video has a million views in an hour, a typical one a few dozen), which hurts neural networks and linear models.

Three standard treatments. Log transforms (log(1 + count)) compress the tail. Smoothed rates avoid the trap of tiny denominators: an item with 2 clicks from 3 impressions has a raw click rate of 67%, which is noise; adding a prior, (clicks + α·global_rate) / (impressions + α) with α = 100 and a global rate of 3%, gives (2 + 3)/(3 + 100) = 4.9%, a sensible estimate that moves towards the item's own rate as evidence accumulates. Quantile normalisation maps each value to its position in the training distribution, a number in [0, 1]; Wide & Deep normalised continuous features this way, and YouTube's 2016 ranker additionally fed powers of the normalised value (its square and square root) so the network could form super- and sub-linear functions easily. Trees need none of this, which is one reason GBDTs are so convenient with engineered counters.

5. Embeddings: giving IDs meaning

The step that changed ranking was representing categorical values as learned dense vectors: embeddings. Instead of a one-hot "item = 12345" with a single weight, the item ID indexes a row of an embedding table, a matrix with one row of, say, 64 numbers per item. The rows are learned with the rest of the model. Items that attract similar users end up with similar rows, so what the model learns about one transfers to its neighbours. Multi-valued features, such as the last 50 items watched, become the average or an attention-weighted sum (section 8) of their rows.

Embedding tables are where the memory goes. 100 million item IDs × 64 dimensions × 4 bytes = 25.6 GB for one table; a user-ID table for 500 million users would be 128 GB. The fully connected layers on top are typically a few hundred megabytes. Standard ways to tame the tables: the hashing trick (hash IDs into, say, 10 million buckets and accept collisions: 2.56 GB), frequency thresholds (IDs seen fewer than N times share one "rare" row), smaller dimensions for low-value features, and 16- or 8-bit storage. Meta's DLRM paper is built around this fact: embeddings contribute the majority of parameters, "with several tables each requiring in excess of multiple GBs of memory".

6. Wide & Deep: memorise and generalise

Cheng and colleagues at Google (2016) framed the trade-off explicitly. A wide part, logistic regression over sparse and crossed features, memorises specific co-occurrences: their example cross is "user installed Netflix AND impression app is Pandora". A deep part, a feed-forward network over embeddings of the same categorical features plus dense features, generalises to combinations never seen in training, but can over-generalise for users with niche tastes. Wide & Deep sums the two parts' outputs before a single sigmoid and trains both jointly:

P(y = 1 | x) = σ( w_wideᵀ [x, crosses(x)]  +  w_deepᵀ a_final  +  b )

where afinal is the deep network's last hidden layer. Joint training matters: the wide part only needs to fix the deep part's specific mistakes, so it can be small.

On the Google Play store, the paper reports a live experiment over three weeks with 1% of users per arm. The control was a "highly-optimized wide-only logistic regression model with rich cross-product feature transformations". Offline AUC: wide 0.726, deep 0.722, Wide & Deep 0.728. Online app acquisitions relative to control: deep +2.9%, Wide & Deep +3.9%. Note the inversion: the deep-only model had a lower offline AUC but a sizeable online gain. Offline AUC was measured on data logged from the old system; the deep model's ability to generalise to apps the old system rarely showed is exactly what that data cannot reward. rec8 and rec10 return to this.

The paper also reports serving: to stay within about 10 ms per request, candidates were scored in parallel smaller batches; scoring 200 candidates in one batch on one thread took 31 ms, while batches of 50 on 4 threads took 14 ms. Latency engineering is part of ranking-model design.

7. Factorisation machines and DLRM: interactions as dot products

A factorisation machine (Rendle, 2010) adds to a linear model a term for every pair of features, the dot product of two learned latent vectors: ŷ = w0 + Σ wixi + Σi<j (vi · vj) xixj. Instead of learning a separate weight for "BR × football", it learns a vector for BR and a vector for football, and their dot product is the interaction weight. Two benefits: crosses never seen together still get a sensible weight (if BR's vector is close to Argentina's and Argentina × football was seen, BR × football inherits it), and all pairs are covered without hand engineering. Matrix factorisation (rec3) is a factorisation machine with only user ID and item ID as features.

DLRM (Naumov and colleagues at Meta, 2019) is the deep version of that idea and a widely used reference architecture. Categorical features go through embedding tables; dense features go through a "bottom" MLP to the same dimension; the interaction layer computes the dot product between every pair of these vectors (the second-order interactions, as in a factorisation machine) and concatenates them with the processed dense features; a "top" MLP turns that into the prediction. Its systems contribution is as important as the model: because embeddings are huge and MLPs small, DLRM trains with model parallelism for the embeddings (each device holds some tables) and data parallelism for the MLPs (each device holds a full copy and processes part of the batch), with an all-to-all exchange of looked-up embeddings between them each step.

8. Deep & Cross Network: explicit crosses of bounded degree

Plain feed-forward layers can represent interactions in principle, but they learn multiplicative crosses inefficiently: a ReLU network approximates x1·x2 only with many units. The Deep & Cross Network (DCN, and its improved version DCN-v2 from Wang and colleagues at Google, 2021) adds cross layers that create explicit crosses cheaply. With x0 the input vector (all embeddings and dense features concatenated) and xl the output of the previous cross layer, DCN-v2's layer is:

x_{l+1} = x_0 ⊙ (W_l x_l + b_l) + x_l

where ⊙ is element-wise multiplication. Each output element is the original input element times a learned linear combination of the current vector, plus the current vector (a residual connection, so the layer can learn to do nothing).

One cross layer by hand. Take a tiny input of three numbers, x0 = (1.0, 0.5, 2.0): say, a user's affinity for football, an item's freshness, and the item's football-ness. Let the learned weights be W = [[0, 1, 0], [0, 0, 0], [0.5, 0, 0]] and b = 0. For the first layer, xl = x0:

  • W x0 = (0 × 1.0 + 1 × 0.5 + 0 × 2.0, 0, 0.5 × 1.0 + 0 + 0) = (0.5, 0, 0.5)
  • x0 ⊙ (W x0) = (1.0 × 0.5, 0.5 × 0, 2.0 × 0.5) = (0.5, 0, 1.0)
  • x1 = (0.5, 0, 1.0) + (1.0, 0.5, 2.0) = (1.5, 0.5, 3.0)

Read it symbolically: the first element is x0,1 + W12 · x0,1 · x0,2, the original feature plus a learned weight times the product of features 1 and 2; the third element is x0,3 + W31 · x0,3 · x0,1, the product of the item's football-ness and the user's football affinity. One layer has created every pairwise product, each with its own learned weight in W; a second layer creates products of three features, and so on. The deep layers after (or beside) the cross network then use these crossed features.

The paper's production section at Google reports that, for one model trained on hundreds of billions of examples, DCN-v2 improved AUCLoss (1 − AUC) by 0.6% over the production MLP, where a gain of 0.1% "is considered a significant improvement", and that replacing the cross layers with same-sized ReLU layers lost most of the gain. Its practical lessons: insert the cross layers between the input and the hidden layers; gains plateau beyond 2 cross layers; and approximating W with a low-rank product of rank about input size / 4 preserved accuracy while cutting cost.

9. Attention over the user's history

A user's recent history is one of the strongest signals in any ranker, but averaging the embeddings of their last 100 items blurs it: a user who watches cooking and football gets a history vector halfway between, which matches neither a cooking candidate nor a football one well. Target attention, popularised by Alibaba's Deep Interest Network (2018), weights each past item by how relevant it is to the candidate being scored. For a football candidate, the football items in the history get high weights and dominate the summary; for a cooking candidate, the cooking items do. The history representation is therefore different for every candidate, which is only affordable in the ranker, where there are a few hundred candidates, not in retrieval.

Concretely, for candidate embedding c and history embeddings h1...hn, compute a relevance score ai for each (via a small network over [hi, c, hi ⊙ c] or a dot product), normalise, and take Σ aihi as the history feature. Newer work replaces this with transformer layers over the whole behaviour sequence, sometimes thousands of events long, which captures order and recency as well. Long sequences are expensive: the cost per candidate grows with sequence length, so systems truncate, summarise old history offline, or compute the user-side part once per request and share it across all candidates.

10. Multi-task heads and MMoE

A modern ranker rarely predicts one thing. A video feed might predict P(click), expected watch fraction, P(like), P(share), P(follow), P(comment) and P("not interested"). Each is a head: a small network on top of shared layers, with its own loss, and the training loss is a weighted sum of the heads' losses. Sharing helps rare tasks borrow representation from frequent ones (there are a hundred clicks for every share), and serving one model is cheaper than serving seven.

The simplest design, a shared bottom with separate heads, suffers when tasks conflict: what makes an item clickable (a striking thumbnail) is not what makes it satisfying (a complete, accurate video), and a single shared representation pulled both ways can hurt the less frequent task, called negative transfer. The multi-gate mixture of experts (MMoE) design addresses this: replace the shared bottom with several parallel "expert" networks, and give each task its own gate, a small softmax network that decides, per example, how much of each expert's output that task uses. Tasks that agree share experts; tasks that conflict learn to rely on different ones. Zhao and colleagues described a multi-task ranker with MMoE at YouTube in 2019, with engagement objectives (clicks, watch time) and satisfaction objectives (likes, ratings, dismissals) as separate tasks. Later variants separate task-specific experts from shared ones explicitly.

Loss weights across tasks are a real tuning problem: a head with a 0.1% positive rate produces small losses and can be ignored by the optimiser. Teams tune the weights, normalise per-task losses, or use schemes that adapt weights during training, and always monitor each head's own offline metric, not just the total loss.

11. The value model: from predictions to an order

The heads produce predictions; the product needs one order. A value model combines them, for example:

score = P(click) × E[watch seconds | click]  +  20 × P(like)  −  60 × P(hide)

Compare two candidates. Item A is clickbait: P(click) = 0.30, E[watch | click] = 20 s, P(like) = 0.01, P(hide) = 0.05. Score: 6.0 + 0.2 − 3.0 = 3.2. Item B is a solid video with a plainer thumbnail: P(click) = 0.15, E[watch | click] = 120 s, P(like) = 0.06, P(hide) = 0.005. Score: 18.0 + 1.2 − 0.3 = 18.9. Ranking by P(click) alone puts A first; the value model puts B first by a wide margin. The weights (20, 60) encode product priorities and must be tuned with online experiments against long-term metrics (rec13), because a weight that boosts short-term engagement can erode retention.

Two engineering consequences follow. Each head must be calibrated, because the formula adds and multiplies the predictions as if they were real probabilities and seconds; a P(hide) head that over-predicts by 2× effectively doubles its weight. And the formula should be versioned and logged with each request, because changing a weight changes the ranking as surely as changing the model.

12. Calibration and negative down-sampling

Training on every impression is expensive and mostly spent on negatives. A common shortcut is negative down-sampling: keep all positives and a fraction w of negatives. Facebook's ads paper tested rates from 0.1 to 0.0001 and found 0.025 best for its setting. Down-sampling makes positives look 1/w times more common, so the model's raw predictions p are inflated. The paper gives the correction:

q = p / ( p + (1 − p) / w )

With w = 0.025, a raw prediction of 0.5 becomes 0.5 / (0.5 + 0.5 × 40) = 0.5 / 20.5 = 0.024. The correction is monotone, so within one request the order by a single head does not change; but any use of the probability as a probability, value models, ad auctions, thresholds, comparisons across surfaces, needs q. After correction, check calibration empirically: bucket predictions (0-1%, 1-2%, ...) and compare each bucket's mean prediction with its observed rate (ml15). Facebook reports calibration as the ratio of average predicted to observed click rate, where 1 is perfect.

13. Serving the ranker

Per request, the ranker scores a few hundred candidates. The user-side work (user embeddings, history encoding) is shared and computed once; item-side and cross work happens per candidate. Candidates are scored in batches, on CPU for small models or on GPUs for large ones, where batching across requests raises throughput but adds queueing delay. Embedding lookups are memory-bound, not compute-bound: the hot rows of item tables are cached close to the model. The cost per item, multiplied by candidates per request and by queries per second, sets the fleet size (rec1): at 0.15 ms of CPU per item, 300 items and 10,000 QPS, 450 cores are busy all the time. Model size and candidate count are therefore always decided together, and model compression (smaller embeddings, quantised layers, distilled students) is routine.

14. Choosing an architecture

Interviewers often ask "which model would you use?" and want a reasoned answer, not the newest acronym. A useful decision path: with under tens of millions of training examples, mostly engineered dense features and a CPU serving budget, start with a GBDT (and LambdaMART if the objective is ranking, rec7). When ID features, content embeddings and user histories carry the signal and data runs to billions of impressions, use a deep model with embeddings; add explicit cross layers (DCN-v2) when interactions matter and you want them cheaply; add target attention when user history is rich; add multi-task heads with MMoE when several outcomes matter and conflict. Whatever the choice, the value model, calibration, logged features and an online evaluation plan come with it.

And always state the baseline you would beat first: the current production model, a GBDT on the same features, or logistic regression with sensible crosses. Many deep-model projects that "won" offline turned out, on closer inspection, to have beaten an under-tuned baseline, the same reproducibility lesson rec3 described for collaborative filtering.

Why AUC and log loss disagree with online results

Offline metrics are computed on impressions the old system chose to show. A new model that is better at items the old model rarely showed gets little credit, because those items barely appear in the evaluation data; Wide & Deep's deep-only model is a public example (lower AUC, higher online gain). AUC also pools all requests: it rewards separating a click in one user's feed from a non-click in another's, which never matters in production, where only the order within a request is used. Group AUC (average AUC per user or per request) and per-request ranking metrics (rec8) are closer to what matters, and counterfactual evaluation (rec10) is the principled fix.

Position as a feature: train with it, serve without it

An item shown in slot 1 is clicked more than the same item in slot 8, regardless of quality. A ranker trained on impressions learns that "items the old ranker put first are good", which entrenches the old ranker. The common fix, used in YouTube's multi-task ranker, is a separate shallow tower that sees position (and device) and whose output is added to the main logit during training; at serving, position is set to a fixed value or the tower is dropped, so the main model's prediction reflects relevance. This is the ranking-model side of the position-bias problem; rec10 covers the estimation side.

Calibrating a multi-task ranker

Each head can be miscalibrated differently, and calibration shifts with surface, country and time. Common practice: fit a per-head monotone calibration map (isotonic or Platt scaling, ml15) on a recent hold-out, optionally per major segment; monitor the ratio of predicted to observed rate per head daily; and re-fit automatically. A well-calibrated P(hide) of 0.005 multiplied by a weight of 60 contributes 0.3 to the score; if it drifts to 0.015 without a real change in hide rates, the value model silently down-ranks everything that head over-predicts.

Continuous training and freshness

Click models go stale fast: Facebook measured about 1% better NE from daily instead of weekly retraining in 2014, and modern feeds retrain continuously, consuming new impressions within minutes or hours. Embedding rows for new items must be created on the fly (hashing or dynamic tables), old rows evicted, and evaluation done on the next time slice. Continuous training introduces its own failure mode: a bug in the logging pipeline feeds bad labels straight into the live model. Teams guard with validation gates (the new checkpoint must not be worse than the previous one on a recent hold-out) and the ability to roll back a checkpoint.

Feature crosses are not free in serving either

Every cross feature that depends on the user and the item must be computed per candidate. "How many of this creator's videos did this user finish in the last 30 days?" needs a lookup keyed by (user, creator), for each of 300 candidates, within a few milliseconds. Systems precompute per-user maps of the creators, categories and items they interacted with, fetched once per request, so each cross becomes a local hash-map lookup. Deciding which crosses are worth that machinery is part of ranking design, and learned crosses (FM, DCN, attention) reduce how many need engineering, not whether the raw inputs must be fetched.

Scaling laws for recommenders

For years, recommendation quality improved with more data and bigger embeddings, but not obviously with bigger dense networks, unlike language models. Recent industrial work, such as Meta's generative recommender research that models user actions as sequences with transformer-style architectures, reports quality that keeps improving as compute grows, closer to language-model behaviour. Whether that holds across products is an open, actively researched question; the practical lesson for now is that sequence modelling of user behaviour is where much of the recent investment in ranking has gone, and that its serving cost (long sequences per candidate) is the main obstacle.

Distillation into cheaper rankers

The full ranker is usually too expensive for the pre-ranking stage (rec1). Distillation trains a small model to match the big one's scores on large candidate sets; the small model can then filter thousands of candidates at a fraction of the cost. The same idea compresses the ranker itself for serving: train the largest affordable model offline as a teacher and serve a smaller student. Distillation from a teacher that sees privileged features (available in training but not at serving) is another variant used in practice.

The public case: Wide & Deep on Google Play

Cheng and colleagues' 2016 paper is a compact case study of moving a production ranker from a linear model to a deep one. The setting was app recommendation on Google Play, which the paper describes as having over one billion active users and over one million apps. Retrieval returned a short list of candidate apps per query (the user's context and profile), and the ranker scored each by the probability that the user would install it. The production model at the time was a highly optimised logistic regression over sparse features with rich cross-product transformations.

The new model kept the same features. The wide part used cross-products such as user-installed apps × impression apps; the deep part embedded categorical features (32 dimensions each in their description), concatenated them with continuous features normalised by their quantiles into [0, 1], and passed them through three ReLU layers. The model was trained on over 500 billion examples; to avoid retraining from scratch every time new data arrived, each new model was warm-started from the previous one's embeddings and weights, and checked against the previous model before serving. In a three-week live experiment with 1% of users per group, Wide & Deep raised app acquisitions on the main landing page by 3.9% relative to the control (statistically significant), and the deep-only model by 2.9%. Offline AUC moved only from 0.726 to 0.728. To meet latency, scoring was multithreaded with smaller batches: 31 ms for one batch of 200 on one thread, 14 ms with batches of 50 on four threads.

Three lessons carry over to every ranker. The architecture's value came from generalisation, which offline AUC barely captured. Warm-starting and validation gates made continuous retraining safe. And serving latency was engineered alongside the model, not after it.

How rankers fail in production

Feature skew. A feature is computed by a batch job for training and by a different service at serving; a time-zone or default-value difference makes them disagree. The model performs worse online than offline with no error anywhere. Logging served features for training (rec9) and comparing distributions between logged and recomputed values catches it.

Calibration drift. A head's predicted rate drifts away from observed rates after a product change (a new button makes likes easier). The value model's balance shifts. Daily per-head calibration monitoring and automatic re-fitting prevent slow damage.

Embedding table blow-up. Without eviction, dynamic ID tables grow with every new item and user until training or serving runs out of memory. Frequency thresholds, time-based eviction and hashing keep them bounded.

Head starvation. A rare head (P(share)) quietly stops learning because its loss weight is tiny relative to the click head; its predictions flatten and the value model loses that signal. Per-head offline metrics in every training run expose it.

Latency regressions. A bigger model or a longer history sequence pushes p99 over budget at peak; the serving layer starts timing out and falling back to the pre-ranker order (rec1), so quality drops at exactly the busiest time. Load-test new models at peak QPS with realistic candidate counts before launch.

What to monitor

Per head: NE or log loss and AUC on the latest hold-out, predicted-versus-observed rate by bucket and by major segment. For the value model: the distribution of scores and of each term's contribution. For serving: p50 and p99 latency per batch, timeouts and fallback rate, GPU or CPU utilisation, embedding cache hit rate. For training: data volume per hour, label rates per head (a sudden drop in logged likes is usually a logging bug), checkpoint validation results. For features: missing-value rates and distribution shifts between logged and expected values.

Design: replace a GBDT ranker for a marketplace's home feed

A second-hand marketplace ranks 400 candidate listings per home-feed request with a GBDT over 180 engineered features (prices, distances, seller ratings, category click rates, the user's recent category views). It predicts P(click). Sellers complain that buyers message them less; product says clicks are up but messages and purchases per session are flat. The ML team proposes a deep model. Propose the architecture, objectives, value model and a migration and evaluation plan, and say what would make you keep the GBDT.

Answer

Diagnosis first. The objective is the main problem, not the model family: optimising P(click) rewards attractive photos and low prices that draw clicks without leading to messages. Whatever the architecture, add heads for the outcomes the business wants.

Objectives. Heads for P(click), P(message | impression), P(save), P(purchase within 7 days | impression) (sparse, delayed label), and P(hide or report). Value: score = P(click) × 1 + P(save) × 5 + P(message) × 20 + P(purchase) × 60 − P(hide) × 30, starting weights from the business value of each action, tuned online.

Architecture. Keep the 180 engineered features (they are valuable) as dense inputs, normalised. Add embeddings for listing ID (hashed), seller ID, category, brand and the user's last 50 viewed listings with target attention against the candidate. One or two DCN-v2 cross layers over the concatenated inputs, then an MMoE block with 4 experts and per-task gates, heads on top. Train on impressions with negatives down-sampled to 10% and re-calibrate each head; purchase labels joined with a 7-day window.

Cheaper first step. Before any deep model, train additional GBDT models for P(message) and P(purchase) and combine them with the click model in a value formula. If that captures most of the gain, the deep model's extra value is smaller than it seems.

Migration and evaluation. Offline: per-head NE and AUC, calibration by bucket, and per-request nDCG@10 using a graded label (purchase 3, message 2, save 1, click 0.5). Online A/B: primary metric messages per session (or purchases per buyer per week); guardrails on clicks, hides, seller-side metrics such as share of active sellers receiving a message, and p99 latency. Serving: 400 candidates per request needs a latency check; if the deep model is too slow, pre-rank to 200 with the GBDT and run the deep model on those.

Keep the GBDT if the multi-objective GBDT value model matches the deep model within noise online, because it is cheaper, easier to debug and to retrain; or if serving cost per request rises more than the measured value of the gain.
