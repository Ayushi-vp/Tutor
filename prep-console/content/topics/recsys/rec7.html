A search team has a click model from rec6: for every (query, document) pair it predicts the probability of a click, and they sort by it. It is a good model by its own measure, low log loss and good calibration. Yet the search quality raters keep flagging the same pattern: for many queries, the best document sits at rank 3 or 4, behind two decent but less useful ones. The team looks at the training loss and sees why. Most of the loss comes from the hundreds of obviously irrelevant documents per query, which the model has learned to score at exactly 0.2% click probability. Whether the best document scores 0.31 or 0.29 compared with its neighbours barely moves the loss, but it decides what the user sees first.

The model is being trained to answer the wrong question. Its loss asks "how accurately can you predict each document's label?". The product asks "for this query, is the order right, especially at the top?". Those are different questions, and optimising the first well does not guarantee the second. Learning to rank (LTR) is the family of methods that train directly on the second: models learn from lists of candidates for the same query, are penalised when they order a pair wrongly, and, in the best-known methods, are penalised most for mistakes near the top.

These methods have a remarkable track record. RankNet, a pairwise neural method from Microsoft Research (2005), and its successors LambdaRank and LambdaMART powered web-search ranking for years; an ensemble of LambdaMART rankers won the 2010 Yahoo! Learning to Rank Challenge, and LambdaMART remains a standard option in XGBoost and LightGBM today. At the end of the search funnel, a newer kind of model, the transformer cross-encoder, reads the query and each top document together; in 2019 a BERT cross-encoder more than doubled the MRR@10 of BM25 on the MS MARCO passage-ranking benchmark.

This lesson covers the three families of LTR loss (pointwise, pairwise, listwise) with RankNet's loss derived and its gradients, the "lambdas", computed by hand for four documents; shows how LambdaRank's nDCG weighting changes those forces; explains LambdaMART and how to train it in practice; and closes with cross-encoder re-rankers: why they are accurate, what they cost and how to fit them into a latency budget.

Think of judging a cooking competition. One way is to give every dish an absolute score out of ten. Another is to taste dishes two at a time and only say which is better. A third is to look at the whole table and arrange the dishes from best to worst. All three can produce a ranking, but they make different demands on the judge and make different mistakes. Absolute scores are hard to keep consistent (is this a 7 or an 8?), and a judge can be perfectly consistent about scores while getting the order of the top two wrong. Pairwise comparisons are easier and directly about order. Arranging the whole table focuses attention on what matters most: which dish wins.

Learning to rank has exactly these three approaches. Pointwise methods learn to predict each document's label on its own: a grade, a click probability. Pairwise methods learn from pairs of documents for the same query with different labels, and are penalised when the worse one scores higher. Listwise methods learn from the whole list for a query, with a loss tied to the quality of the entire ordering, usually emphasising the top.

The competition analogy breaks in one instructive way. Human judges get tired, so pairwise comparison of every pair is expensive for them; for a computer, comparing all pairs within a query is cheap as long as the list is short. What is expensive for the computer is that the quality measures we care about, such as nDCG, do not change smoothly as scores change: they jump only when two documents swap places. A model trained by gradient descent needs smooth signals. The clever part of RankNet and LambdaRank is how they manufacture a smooth push from a jumpy target.

A second way to see the difference is to ask what each approach is rewarded for. A pointwise model is rewarded for knowing how good each document is in absolute terms, which is more than ranking needs and harder to learn: it must agree with raters about whether a document is a 2 or a 3, even when its order relative to the other documents is already right. A pairwise model is rewarded only for relative judgements, which are easier and closer to the task, but treats a mistake at rank 50 like one at rank 1. A listwise model with top-weighted gradients is rewarded for exactly what users experience: the right document first, the next best second. Each step gives up something (calibrated scores, simplicity) to get closer to the real objective, and choosing among them is choosing which of those you can afford to give up.

Keep one more picture from rec1 in mind: learning to rank happens at the ranking and re-ranking stages of the funnel, on a few hundred candidates per query that retrieval already found. It cannot fix recall. Its job is to put the best of what was retrieved at the very top.

1. The data: query groups and graded labels

LTR training data is organised by query group: for each query (in recommendation, each request), a list of candidate documents with feature vectors and labels. Labels come in two kinds. Graded human judgements: trained raters label each (query, document) pair on a scale, for example 0 (irrelevant), 1 (related), 2 (relevant), 3 (highly relevant), 4 (perfect), following detailed guidelines; web search engines and the public LTR datasets (Microsoft's MSLR-WEB, Yahoo!'s challenge sets) use this. Behavioural labels: clicks, dwell time, purchases, derived from logs, plentiful but biased (rec10).

The features are the ranker's features (rec6), and for search they include many query-document matching signals: BM25 per field, the fraction of query terms matched, phrase and proximity matches, the dense cosine, plus document quality signals (popularity, freshness, authority) and, where used, personalisation. A typical public LTR dataset has between about 45 and 700 features per pair; MSLR-WEB30K, for instance, has 136.

The one structural rule: all comparisons happen within a group. A score for a "cheap flights" result and a score for a "pasta recipe" result are never compared, because no one ever chooses between them. Train/validation splits must also be by query, not by row, or the same query's documents leak across the split.

2. Pointwise: the baseline and its blind spot

The pointwise approach treats each (query, document) pair as an independent example: regress on the grade with squared error, or classify relevant versus not with log loss (exactly what rec6's click model does). Then sort by predicted score. It is simple, scales to billions of rows, and gives calibrated outputs, which matters when scores are combined in a value model or an auction.

The blind spot is what the loss rewards. Imagine a query with one perfect document, two good ones and 97 irrelevant ones. Squared error on 100 documents is dominated by the 97, so the model invests capacity in scoring irrelevant documents precisely, which changes nothing about the order users see. Worse, the loss treats errors at every position equally: predicting 2.6 instead of 3.0 for the perfect document costs the same as predicting 0.4 instead of 0.0 for an irrelevant one, even though only the first can change which document is on top. And scores across queries are forced onto one scale: a mediocre document for an easy query and an excellent one for a hard query may both be "relevance 2" in the model's eyes. Pairwise and listwise methods remove these mismatches.

3. RankNet: a probability that i should be above j

RankNet (Burges and colleagues, ICML 2005) trains a scoring function f (originally a neural network; any differentiable model works) on pairs. For documents i and j from the same query with scores si = f(xi) and sj = f(xj), it models the probability that i should be ranked above j as a sigmoid of the score difference:

P_ij = 1 / (1 + e^(−σ (s_i − s_j)))

where σ sets the sigmoid's steepness. If i is labelled more relevant than j, the desired probability is 1, and the cost is the cross-entropy between desired and modelled probabilities, which simplifies to:

C_ij = log(1 + e^(−σ (s_i − s_j)))

Read its behaviour from the formula, using σ = 1. If the scores are equal, C = log 2 = 0.693: even a tie is penalised, so the model is pushed to separate documents with different labels. If i is well ahead (si − sj = +1.5), C = log(1 + e−1.5) = 0.201, small. If i is behind (−1.0), C = log(1 + e1.0) = 1.313, and for large deficits the cost grows linearly with the gap. Pairs with equal labels contribute nothing.

The model learns from pairs, but the scoring function scores one document at a time, so at serving time RankNet is used exactly like a pointwise model: score each candidate, sort. Only training differs.

4. Lambdas: the forces on each document

Gradient descent needs the derivative of the cost with respect to the scores. For a pair where i should be above j:

∂C_ij / ∂s_i = −σ / (1 + e^(σ (s_i − s_j)))  ≡  λ_ij,      ∂C_ij / ∂s_j = −λ_ij

Burges calls λij the lambda and gives it a physical reading: each pair exerts a force, pushing the more relevant document up and the less relevant one down by the same amount, and the force is larger the more wrongly the pair is ordered. A document's total lambda is the sum of the forces from all pairs it is in. Because the model's parameters affect scores, and scores receive these forces, training can be organised as "compute each document's lambda, then backpropagate", which is much faster than handling each pair separately.

Worked example. One query, four documents with labels A = 3, B = 2, D = 1, C = 0, and current model scores A 1.0, B 2.0, C 0.5, D 1.5. The current ranking is B, D, A, C, which puts the best document third. There are six pairs with different labels. With σ = 1:

A wrong pair at ranks 99 and 100 is as important as one at ranks 1 and 2. Users disagree.

5. LambdaRank: weight each force by what it does to nDCG

The fix, from Burges and colleagues' LambdaRank work, is strikingly simple: multiply each pair's lambda by |ΔnDCG|, the absolute change in nDCG that swapping the two documents' positions would cause, leaving all other documents in place. Burges's overview says experiments showed this "gives very good results". Pairs whose swap would change the top of the list get large forces; pairs deep in the list get tiny ones.

Compute it for the example, using the common gain 2grade − 1 (so grades 3, 2, 1, 0 give gains 7, 3, 1, 0) and discount 1/log2(rank + 1) (1, 0.631, 0.5, 0.431 for ranks 1-4; ev6 and rec8 explain the formula). Current order B, D, A, C: DCG = 3 × 1 + 1 × 0.631 + 7 × 0.5 + 0 × 0.431 = 7.131. Ideal order A, B, D, C: IDCG = 7 + 3 × 0.631 + 1 × 0.5 + 0 = 9.393. nDCG = 0.759.

Swapping two documents at ranks p and q with gains gi and gj changes DCG by (gi − gj) × (discount(p) − discount(q)); divide by IDCG for ΔnDCG. For A (rank 3, gain 7) and B (rank 1, gain 3): (7 − 3) × (1 − 0.5) / 9.393 = 0.213. For D (rank 2, gain 1) and C (rank 4, gain 0): (1 − 0) × (0.631 − 0.431) / 9.393 = 0.021.

Training moves A towards the top far more decisively than RankNet would, and spends little effort on the correctly placed bottom of the list.

Two refinements are standard. Truncation: compute ΔnDCG@k for a chosen k (say 10), so pairs entirely below rank k contribute nothing, which also saves computation for long lists. And the metric is swappable: Burges notes that |ΔnDCG| can be replaced by the change in any IR metric, such as MAP or MRR, giving variants tuned to other goals.

6. LambdaMART: boosted trees on lambdas

Gradient boosting (ml10) builds an ensemble of regression trees, each fitted to the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the current predictions. Friedman's version with regression trees is called MART (Multiple Additive Regression Trees). LambdaMART is MART where the "gradient" for each document is its LambdaRank lambda. Each boosting round: score all documents with the current ensemble, compute every document's lambda within its query group (and the second derivatives used for Newton-style leaf values), fit a tree to the lambdas, add it with a learning rate, repeat.

LambdaMART combines the strengths of trees, excellent with a few hundred engineered dense features, no normalisation needed, robust and fast to train, with an objective aimed at the top of the ranking. That combination dominated web search learning to rank: an ensemble of LambdaMART rankers won Track 1 of the 2010 Yahoo! Learning to Rank Challenge, and boosted-tree rankers took most top places there. It remains the default first model for search ranking with engineered features.

Training it in practice. In XGBoost, set objective="rank:ndcg" (or rank:pairwise, rank:map) and pass query groups; LightGBM's equivalent is objective="lambdarank" with group sizes, a label_gain list mapping grades to gains, and a truncation level. Typical settings: a few hundred to a few thousand trees, depth 6-8 or 31-255 leaves, learning rate 0.05-0.1, early stopping on validation nDCG@10. Split train, validation and test by query. Evaluate nDCG@k per query and average, and slice by query class.

7. Listwise losses for neural rankers

Neural rankers usually use an explicitly listwise loss. The most common is the softmax cross-entropy over the list: turn the model's scores for a query's candidates into a probability distribution with a softmax, turn the labels into a target distribution (for example proportional to 2grade − 1, or all mass on the clicked item), and minimise the cross-entropy between them. For the example, with all target mass on A, the loss is −log(softmax(scores)A) = −log(e1.0 / (e2.0 + e1.5 + e1.0 + e0.5)), which pushes A up and every other document down in proportion to its current probability, the same mechanism as the in-batch softmax in rec4, but over realistic candidates from the same query rather than random batch items.

Research has produced many more listwise losses (ListNet, ListMLE, approximate-nDCG losses, the LambdaLoss framework that grounds lambdas in a proper probabilistic objective). In practice the softmax loss is a strong, simple default for neural rankers, often combined with a pointwise loss on the same scores when calibrated outputs are also needed (rec6's value model).

8. Cross-encoder re-rankers

All the models so far score a document from a feature vector that was computed separately for the query and the document (or from a few hand-built matching features). A cross-encoder instead feeds the query and the document text together into a transformer, "[CLS] query [SEP] document [SEP]", so self-attention (lm2) lets every query token look at every document token, and a small head on the output produces a relevance score. It can check whether the document actually answers the question, respect negations and constraints ("without touchscreen"), and match entities exactly, all things a bi-encoder's single vector blurs.

Nogueira and Cho's 2019 monoBERT made this concrete. They fine-tuned BERT as a binary classifier of (query, passage) relevance with cross-entropy, using passages from BM25's top 1,000 as candidates, truncating the query to 64 tokens and the whole input to 512. On the MS MARCO passage-ranking dev set, BM25 alone scored MRR@10 of 16.7; re-ranking its top 1,000 with BERT Base gave 34.7 and with BERT Large 36.5, which they report as 27% better (relative) than the previous state of the art. Cross-encoder re-ranking of a cheap first stage became the standard architecture for text search and for RAG (lm9), and BEIR (rec2) found BM25 plus a cross-encoder the strongest zero-shot combination.

The cost. Nothing can be precomputed per document, because the document's representation depends on the query. Every query needs one transformer pass per candidate. Re-ranking 1,000 candidates with BERT Large per query, as in the paper, is far too slow for most products; in production the re-ranker sees the top 20-200, uses a smaller model (tens to a few hundred million parameters), truncates documents to their most relevant passage, and runs batched on GPUs. A rough budget: a small cross-encoder scoring 50 pairs of a few hundred tokens takes tens of milliseconds on a modern GPU; at 1,000 QPS that is 50,000 pairs per second, which sizes the GPU fleet.

9. Query-document features for search LTR

Feature-based rankers in search depend on features that describe how the query and the document match, computed per candidate at serving time. The standard families: lexical matching (BM25 per field, as in rec2; the number and fraction of query terms matched in title and body; whether the full query appears as a phrase; the minimum window containing all query terms); semantic matching (the bi-encoder cosine from rec4 and rec5, and later the cross-encoder score); document quality (popularity, authority, freshness, length, spam scores); query features (length, predicted category, how often it is searched), which let the model behave differently for different kinds of query; and behavioural features (historical click-through for this query-document pair, smoothed as in rec6, and for the document across all queries).

Behavioural features are the strongest and the most dangerous: a document's past click rate for a query largely reflects where the old ranker put it. Using them without position correction (rec10) bakes the old ranking into the new one, which is a feedback loop dressed up as a feature. Many teams compute them only from traffic with randomised or interleaved positions, or apply the same propensity corrections used for training labels.

10. Choosing the target metric and depth

LambdaMART and listwise losses optimise a metric at a depth, so the choice is a design decision. If users mostly click the first result (navigational search), MRR or nDCG@1-3 is the right target. If they scan a page of ten (product search), nDCG@10. If they consume the whole list (a playlist), a deeper metric or a pairwise objective. The gain function (linear or exponential in the grade) says whether the difference between "perfect" and "good" matters more than the difference between "related" and "irrelevant". And queries can be weighted: by traffic (head queries dominate) or equally (every query matters, which favours the tail). Each choice changes what the model learns, so write it down and evaluate with the same definitions you trained with.

11. Fitting re-rankers into the funnel

A common search stack therefore looks like: hybrid retrieval (rec5) to a few hundred candidates; a feature-based ranker (LambdaMART or a neural ranker trained with a listwise loss) to order them using engineered and behavioural features; a cross-encoder on the top 20-50 for the final order of the first page. Each stage is trained for its role. The cross-encoder's score can become one more feature for a final light model that also sees freshness, popularity and personalisation, because the cross-encoder judges text relevance only.

Three tactics control cost. Adaptive depth: re-rank fewer candidates when the earlier ranker is confident (a large gap between its first and second scores) or for navigational queries. Caching: head queries repeat, so cache the re-ranked list for minutes. Distillation: train a smaller cross-encoder, or a better bi-encoder, to imitate the large cross-encoder's scores on many (query, document) pairs; the large model becomes a teacher that never serves traffic. Distillation from cross-encoders into bi-encoders is one of the main ways modern dense retrievers are trained.

Why the lambda trick works

LambdaRank defines the gradient, not the loss. That looks suspicious: is there any function whose gradient those lambdas are? Burges's overview discusses this, and later work (the LambdaLoss framework from Google researchers, 2018) showed that LambdaRank's gradients correspond to optimising a well-defined bound on an nDCG-based loss within a probabilistic framework. Practically, the empirical record is what matters: lambdas weighted by metric changes reliably improve the target metric, and the "define the gradient you want" idea, push hardest on what matters most to the metric, is a useful design pattern beyond ranking.

Pairwise methods and the position of errors

Plain pairwise losses minimise the number of discordant pairs, which is closely related to AUC per query. That is a fine objective when every position matters equally, as when the whole list will be shown or consumed (a playlist that will be played through). It is the wrong objective when attention is concentrated at the top, as in search. Choosing between pairwise and top-weighted listwise objectives is therefore a product question: how does the user consume the list? This connects to the metric choice in rec8: optimise what you measure, and measure what users experience.

Label gain choices change the model

The gain function mapping grades to gains (2grade − 1 versus the grade itself) changes how much the model cares about the difference between "perfect" and "good" versus between "related" and "irrelevant". With exponential gains, grades 4 and 3 differ by 8 (15 versus 7) while 1 and 0 differ by 1: the model concentrates on getting the very best documents to the top. With linear gains, all steps are equal. LightGBM's label_gain parameter exposes this directly. Choose it to match what the product values, and report the metric with the same gains you trained with, or comparisons mislead.

Clicks as labels: the bias problem in one paragraph

Training pairwise models on clicks ("clicked document beats unclicked documents shown above it") was an early idea from Joachims's work in the early 2000s, which observed that users scan from the top and that a click on result 3 with no click on result 2 suggests 3 is preferred to 2 ("click > skip above"). Naive "clicked beats all unclicked" pairs are badly biased by position: lower documents were simply less likely to be seen. Unbiased learning to rank, covered in rec10, weights each click by the inverse of the probability that its position was examined, making click-trained LambdaMART or neural rankers consistent estimators of the ranker you would learn from true relevance.

Late interaction and multi-stage re-rankers

Between bi-encoders and cross-encoders sit late-interaction models such as ColBERT (rec5): per-token document embeddings precomputed, token-level matching at query time. They cost more storage than bi-encoders and much less query-time compute than cross-encoders. Pipelines sometimes use a cascade: a cheap cross-encoder on the top 100, an expensive one on the top 10. Each stage should be trained on candidates from the stage before it (hard negatives from the actual pipeline), or it learns to distinguish relevant documents from easy negatives it will never see in production.

Multi-objective learning to rank

Search rankers rarely have one label. A product search may want relevance (from raters), conversion (from purchases) and margin; a news search wants relevance and freshness. One approach trains on a combined label (for example a grade adjusted by purchase outcome); another trains separate models and blends their scores, as rec6's value model does; LambdaMART variants can also scale each pair's lambda by changes in several metrics at once. The blend weights become product decisions validated by online experiments, and the offline evaluation must report each objective separately, because a combined metric can hide a large regression in one of them.

Cross-encoders for recommendation

The cross-encoder idea, letting the two sides interact fully before scoring, is exactly what the ranker in rec6 does with feature crosses and target attention over the user's history. The difference is the input: text pairs for search, feature vectors and behaviour sequences for recommendation. Recent recommendation models that run transformers over the user's history together with the candidate are, in this sense, cross-encoders for recommendation, and inherit the same cost structure: affordable only on a few hundred candidates.

The public case: monoBERT on MS MARCO

Nogueira and Cho's 2019 paper, "Passage Re-ranking with BERT", is short and influential. MS MARCO's passage-ranking task has about 8.8 million passages and real Bing queries, with on average about one passage labelled relevant per query. The authors used BM25 (Lucene, untuned) to retrieve the top 1,000 passages per query, then re-ranked them with BERT fine-tuned as a binary relevance classifier: the query (truncated to 64 tokens) and passage concatenated into one input of up to 512 tokens, the [CLS] output fed to a single-layer classifier, trained with cross-entropy on relevant passages versus non-relevant ones from BM25's top 1,000. Passages were then sorted by the predicted probability, which is a pointwise loss on a cross-encoder.

Results on the dev set, MRR@10 (the mean of 1/rank of the first relevant passage within the top 10): BM25 16.7; the best earlier neural models around 27-29; BERT Base 34.7; BERT Large 36.5 (35.8 on the evaluation set), a 27% relative improvement over the previous state of the art. On the TREC-CAR dataset, BERT Large reached MAP 33.5 versus 15.3 for tuned BM25. The cost was equally clear: one BERT Large pass per (query, passage) pair, a thousand per query, run on TPUs for an offline benchmark. The paper set the template for "cheap first stage, cross-encoder second stage" that most text search and RAG systems now follow, with smaller and distilled re-rankers to make it affordable.

How LTR systems fail in production

Label-distribution mismatch. Human-judged training queries over-represent the head or the tail relative to traffic, so the model is tuned for the wrong mix. Weight training queries by traffic share or evaluate per slice.

Feature leakage across the query split. Splitting by row instead of by query puts the same query's documents in train and test, inflating offline nDCG. Always split by query (and by time for behavioural labels).

Re-ranker latency spikes. Long documents or traffic peaks push cross-encoder latency past the deadline; the system falls back to the earlier ranker's order without anyone noticing. Monitor re-ranker timeouts and fallback rate, cap input length, and batch adaptively.

Re-ranker trained on easy negatives. A cross-encoder fine-tuned on random negatives looks excellent offline and barely changes the order of real top candidates, which are all plausible. Fine-tune on negatives from the actual first stage.

Stale click labels. Behavioural labels collected under an old ranker reflect what it showed; a model trained on them inherits its preferences. Mix in randomised or interleaved traffic (rec10, rec13).

Where labels come from, and what they cost

Graded judgements are the backbone of search LTR and they are not cheap. A rater needs the guidelines, the query (sometimes with the user's likely intent spelled out), and time to read the document: tens of seconds to minutes per pair for long documents. Forty thousand pairs, the size of the drill's dataset, is weeks of rater work, and each pair is usually judged by two or three raters to measure agreement (ev5 covers kappa and adjudication). That cost shapes practice: labels are spent on queries where the ranker is uncertain or where online metrics are poor, refreshed as content and intent change ("covid" meant something different in 2019), and supplemented with behavioural labels from clicks after bias correction (rec10). LLM-generated relevance labels, checked against a sample of human labels, are increasingly used to scale judgements further; rec14 discusses when they can be trusted. Whatever the source, keep a fixed, human-labelled evaluation set that never feeds training, so model changes are judged against a stable yardstick.

What to monitor

Offline per release: nDCG@1, @5, @10 and MRR per query class on held-out labelled sets, plus the share of queries whose top result changed versus production (large changes deserve a look). Online: click-through and success rate at positions 1-3, reformulation rate, time to first click. Re-ranker: p50 and p99 latency, candidates re-ranked per query, timeouts, GPU utilisation. Data: label volumes and grade distributions per week, rater agreement for human labels (ev5).

Design: re-ranking for a legal-research search engine

A legal-research product searches 12 million court opinions and statutes. Queries are long natural-language questions or citations. Today: BM25 top 100, then a LambdaMART ranker with 60 features trained on 40,000 human-graded (query, document) pairs. Lawyers say the right case is "usually on the first page but rarely first". Traffic is 150 QPS at peak with a 1.5 s budget. Propose how to improve the top positions, with costs and an evaluation plan.

Answer

Diagnosis. "On the first page but rarely first" means recall@10 is fine and precision at rank 1-3 is the problem: a re-ranking problem, not retrieval. LambdaMART over engineered features cannot read whether a case actually addresses the legal question.

Plan. (1) Check LambdaMART's training: is it truncated at nDCG@10 with exponential gains so the top is emphasised? Are queries split properly? Cheap wins first. (2) Add a cross-encoder re-ranker on the top 30 from LambdaMART. Documents are long, so split each into passages of about 300 tokens, score the query against each passage (or the top few passages chosen by BM25 within the document), and use the maximum passage score as the document score. Fine-tune from a legal-domain pre-trained encoder on the 40,000 graded pairs plus hard negatives from the current top 30, with a listwise softmax loss over each query's candidates. (3) Combine: final score = the cross-encoder score blended with LambdaMART's (for authority, recency and citation-count signals), tuned on validation nDCG@3. (4) Route citation queries ("Smith v. Jones 2019") to exact citation lookup first.

Cost. 30 documents × about 3 passages = 90 pairs per query; at 150 QPS, 13,500 pairs per second. A base-size cross-encoder on a modern GPU handles perhaps 1,000-2,000 short pairs per second, so plan around 8-14 GPUs with headroom, or distil to a smaller model. Latency: about 100-200 ms batched, within the 1.5 s budget.

Evaluation. Offline: nDCG@1, @3 and MRR on a held-out set of graded queries split by query, sliced by query type (question, citation, statute); require no regression on citation queries. Online: interleaving (rec13) of old versus new rankings to measure preference quickly with modest traffic, then an A/B test on first-result click-through, time to first relevant click, and document saves; guardrail on p99 latency.
