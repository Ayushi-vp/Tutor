Two teams at a streaming service each claim a better home-page ranker. Team A reports "+6% nDCG@10". Team B reports "+11% hit rate@10 against 100 sampled negatives". Leadership asks which to ship. Nobody can answer, because the two numbers measure different things in different ways, and, as it turns out, either could be positive while the true effect on members is zero or negative. Team A's labels came from what the old ranker showed. Team B's protocol, ranking each held-out title against a hundred random titles instead of the whole catalogue, is one that a 2020 paper by Google researchers showed can reverse which of two models looks better.

Ranking metrics look simple, a few formulas with logarithms in them, and they are where an enormous amount of recommender and search work quietly goes wrong. The metric decides what the model is tuned towards (rec7's LambdaRank literally optimises it), what the team believes about its progress, and which experiments get run. A metric that rewards the wrong thing or an evaluation protocol that leaks information produces months of "progress" that never shows up for users.

Senior interviews probe this hard. "How would you evaluate this recommender?" is a standard question, and the strong answer is not a list of acronyms but a protocol: which metric for which surface and why, at what depth, computed how, against what baseline, sliced how, with what confidence, alongside which beyond-accuracy measures, and with what expectations about the online result.

The stakes are concrete. A ranking team ships perhaps ten to twenty model changes a year, and each needs weeks of A/B testing to measure. Offline evaluation decides which candidates get those scarce test slots. If the offline metric is biased towards imitating production, the team spends its year testing models that cannot win; if it is measured with a protocol that reverses comparisons, it ships worse models with confidence. Good offline evaluation is therefore a throughput multiplier for the whole team, not a formality before launch.

This lesson builds the toolkit. ev6 introduced recall@k, MRR and nDCG for RAG retrieval; here we compute the full set, precision@k, recall@k, MRR, MAP and nDCG with both gain conventions, by hand for one recommendation list, then cover how to aggregate them, the beyond-accuracy metrics (coverage, diversity, novelty, calibration), the protocol choices that quietly decide the answer, and the main reasons offline gains so often vanish online. Experiment design itself is rec13's subject.

Picture a friend who recommends five films. How would you grade them? You might count how many of the five you enjoyed (that is precision). You might ask how many of the films you would have enjoyed they managed to mention (recall). If you only wanted one film for tonight, you would care how far down their list the first good one was (reciprocal rank). If some films were wonderful and others merely fine, and you would mostly watch the first one or two, you would want the wonderful ones first: that is what nDCG measures, giving each film credit for how good it is, discounted by how far down the list it sits.

And then there are things none of those capture. If all five films are sequels in the same franchise, the list is accurate and dull (diversity). If all five are the year's biggest hits that you already know about, it adds nothing (novelty). If you love comedies and documentaries in equal measure but the list is all comedies, it misrepresents you (calibration). A real evaluation looks at both kinds of measures, because the second kind is where accuracy-optimised systems drift.

Notice that the metrics in the box disagree about how good the list is: reciprocal rank says perfect (1.0), precision says 60%, nDCG with exponential gains says under 50%. None is wrong. Reciprocal rank asks "was the first thing good?", and it was, a little. Precision asks "how much of the list was good?". nDCG asks "were the best things first?", and the best title was third while another excellent one was missing. Choosing a metric is choosing which of these questions your product cares about, which is why the first step of any evaluation is describing how people use the list: do they take the first acceptable result and leave, scan a short row, or scroll through dozens of items?

A second habit worth building early: always compute a metric for a trivial baseline next to your model. "Recommend the most popular titles" or "rank by BM25" gives a floor. If your model's nDCG@10 is 0.31 and popularity's is 0.29, the model is adding little, however sophisticated it is. If popularity scores 0.05, the problem is genuinely personal and the model is doing real work. Absolute metric values mean little on their own; the gap to sensible baselines and to production is what carries information.

The analogy breaks in an important way. Your friend's list can be graded by asking you about every film. A recommender's list is graded using what users actually did, and users only act on what they were shown. A film never recommended has no "enjoyed" or "ignored" label at all, so the grades we compute are always partial and shaped by the system that produced the data. Much of the "offline gains vanish online" story at the end of this lesson follows from that one fact.

1. The setting: per-user lists, labels and depth

Offline evaluation of a recommender or search ranker needs three things. A set of evaluation units: users (for recommendations) or queries (for search). For each unit, the model's ranked list of items. And relevance labels: which items are relevant for that unit, ideally with grades. In recommendation, labels usually come from held-out behaviour (what the user watched, bought or rated after the training cut-off); in search, from human judgements or behaviour (rec7).

Every metric is computed per unit on the top k of the list, the cut-off, and then averaged over units. Choose k to match the product: the number of items visible without scrolling, the number of slots in a carousel, the number of chunks sent to an LLM (ev6). A metric at k = 100 says nothing about a carousel of 6.

The worked example used throughout: one user, a top-5 list from a streaming service, graded by what the user did in the next week: 2 = finished the title, 1 = started it, 0 = did not watch. The list's grades by rank are 1, 0, 2, 0, 1. The user also finished one title that the list did not include (grade 2, not retrieved). So there are four relevant titles in total: two with grade 2 (one at rank 3, one missing) and two with grade 1 (ranks 1 and 5).

2. Precision@k and recall@k

Precision@k is the share of the top k that is relevant. Counting any grade above 0 as relevant, the top 5 contains 3 relevant titles: precision@5 = 3/5 = 0.60. Recall@k is the share of all relevant items that appear in the top k: 3 of 4, recall@5 = 0.75.

They pull in different directions. Lengthening the list can only raise recall and usually lowers precision. Precision suits surfaces where every slot costs attention (a short carousel); recall suits stages whose job is not to miss things, like retrieval (rec1, rec4). Hit rate@k is a third variant: 1 if any relevant item is in the top k, else 0; it suits "the user needs just one good option" and is common in next-item recommendation papers. None of the three care about order within the top k: the list 0, 0, 1, 2, 1 has the same precision@5 and recall@5 as ours.

3. Reciprocal rank and MRR

The reciprocal rank (RR) is 1 divided by the rank of the first relevant item, or 0 if none is in the list. Our first relevant title is at rank 1, so RR = 1.0. If we count only "finished" (grade 2) as relevant, the first one is at rank 3 and RR = 0.33. MRR (mean reciprocal rank) averages RR over users or queries.

MRR is the right metric when there is one answer and the user stops when they find it: navigational search, a known-item lookup, question answering with one correct passage (MS MARCO's official metric is MRR@10, rec7). Its blind spot is everything after the first hit: moving our grade-2 title from rank 3 to rank 2 leaves MRR unchanged. Its scale is also very non-linear: dropping from rank 1 to rank 2 halves the score, while dropping from rank 9 to 10 barely moves it, which matches how people use search results.

4. Average precision and MAP

Average precision (AP) rewards placing all relevant items early. Walk down the list; at each rank where a relevant item appears, compute precision at that rank; sum those values and divide by the total number of relevant items (including ones not retrieved). For our list: rank 1 is relevant, precision@1 = 1/1 = 1.0; rank 3 is relevant, precision@3 = 2/3 = 0.667; rank 5 is relevant, precision@5 = 3/5 = 0.6. AP = (1.0 + 0.667 + 0.6) / 4 = 0.567. The missing fourth relevant title contributes 0, which is how AP penalises incomplete recall. (Some libraries divide by the number of relevant items retrieved instead, here 3, giving 0.756; check which definition a tool uses.) MAP averages AP over users or queries.

AP is binary: it does not distinguish "finished" from "started". It was the standard metric of the TREC evaluations for years and remains common for tasks with binary relevance.

5. nDCG, worked with both gain conventions

Discounted cumulative gain (Järvelin and Kekäläinen, 2002) gives each item a gain from its grade and divides it by a discount that grows with rank, log2(rank + 1):

DCG@k = Σ over ranks r = 1..k:  gain(grade_r) / log2(r + 1)
nDCG@k = DCG@k / IDCG@k

where IDCG@k is the DCG of the best possible ordering of all known relevant items. The discounts for ranks 1-5 are 1.000, 0.631, 0.500, 0.431, 0.387: rank 2 counts 63% as much as rank 1, rank 5 under 40%.

Linear gains (gain = grade), as in ev6. DCG@5 = 1 × 1.000 + 0 + 2 × 0.500 + 0 + 1 × 0.387 = 2.387. The ideal list orders all four relevant titles by grade: 2, 2, 1, 1. IDCG@5 = 2 × 1.000 + 2 × 0.631 + 1 × 0.500 + 1 × 0.431 = 2 + 1.262 + 0.5 + 0.431 = 4.193. nDCG@5 = 2.387 / 4.193 = 0.569.

Exponential gains (gain = 2grade − 1, common in web search and in LambdaMART, rec7): grades 0, 1, 2 give gains 0, 1, 3. DCG@5 = 1 + 0 + 3 × 0.5 + 0 + 1 × 0.387 = 2.887. IDCG@5 = 3 + 3 × 0.631 + 1 × 0.5 + 1 × 0.431 = 5.824. nDCG@5 = 0.496.

Same list, two different nDCGs. The exponential version makes a "finished" title worth three "started" ones, so the list is punished more for having its best item at rank 3 and for missing the other grade-2 title entirely. Neither is wrong; they encode different beliefs about how much better "finished" is than "started". The point is to choose deliberately, use the same definition for training (rec7) and evaluation, and state it when reporting.

Two more definitional traps. If the IDCG is computed only from relevant items that the model retrieved, rather than all known relevant items, the missing documentary no longer counts against the list, and nDCG rises (here to 2.387 / (2 + 0.631 + 0.5) = 0.762 with linear gains). And users with no relevant items in the test period have IDCG = 0; they must be excluded or assigned a fixed value, and the choice changes averages. Libraries differ on both.

6. Aggregating over users, and confidence

Metrics are computed per user (or query) and then averaged. Macro-averaging (each user counts once) is standard and protects light users from being drowned out by heavy ones. Weighting by activity answers a different question (how good is the average impression?). Report which.

Averages hide distributions. Slice by user activity (cold, light, heavy), by country, device, and content type; a model can raise the average by improving heavy users while hurting new ones. And attach uncertainty: per-user metric values are noisy, so compute a confidence interval by bootstrapping users (resample users with replacement, recompute the mean, take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles), and compare two models on the same users with a paired bootstrap of the per-user differences (ev7 covers both). A 0.5% nDCG gain with an interval of ±0.8% is not a gain.

7. Coverage and concentration

Accuracy metrics are computed per user, so they cannot see what the system does to the catalogue as a whole. Catalogue coverage is the share of items recommended to at least one user in a period: a system that recommends the same 2,000 titles to everyone may have decent accuracy and 4% coverage of a 50,000-title catalogue. Coverage is a minimum; the distribution of exposure matters more. The Gini coefficient of impressions across items summarises inequality (0 if all items get equal exposure, near 1 if a few items get almost all), and simpler versions are "share of impressions going to the top 1% of items". These matter for catalogue economics (licensed content nobody sees is wasted money) and for marketplaces and creator platforms, where exposure is income (rec12 covers fairness of exposure).

8. Diversity, novelty and serendipity

Intra-list diversity measures how different the items in one list are from each other: the average over pairs of items of (1 − similarity), where similarity might be the cosine of item embeddings or simply "same category". A list of five Marvel films has low diversity; one comedy, one documentary, one thriller has high diversity. Averaging it over users gives a system-level number.

Novelty measures how unfamiliar the recommended items are likely to be. A common definition is the average self-information of recommended items, −log2(pi), where pi is the share of users who have interacted with item i. An item 20% of users have watched contributes −log2(0.2) = 2.3 bits; one only 0.1% have watched contributes 10.0 bits. A list of blockbusters has low novelty. Serendipity tries to capture "relevant and surprising": items the user engaged with that a simple baseline (popularity, or "more of the same genre") would not have recommended. It is harder to measure and usually estimated against a baseline model.

These metrics are not goals in themselves: a list of random obscure items is maximally novel and diverse and useless. They are guardrails and trade-off dials, reported next to accuracy so that a model which gains 1% nDCG by collapsing diversity is visible before launch.

9. Calibration of the list

Steck's 2018 paper at Netflix pointed out a specific accuracy failure. A user who has watched 70% romance and 30% action titles often gets a list that is nearly all romance, because romance titles have slightly higher predicted scores for them, and the top-10 of a ranked list amplifies small score differences into a winner-takes-all mix. The minority interest disappears, and with it part of what the user wanted.

He defined calibration as how well the distribution of genres in the recommended list, q(g), matches the distribution in the user's history, p(g), measured with the KL divergence, Σg p(g) log(p(g) / q(g)), where q is smoothed slightly towards p so that a genre missing from the list does not give an infinite value. For the 70/30 user, a list of 9 romance and 1 action titles has q = (0.9, 0.1): KL = 0.7 ln(0.7/0.9) + 0.3 ln(0.3/0.1) = 0.7 × (−0.251) + 0.3 × 1.099 = −0.176 + 0.330 = 0.154. A 7/3 list has KL = 0. Calibrated re-ranking (rec12) trades a little accuracy for a much lower KL. The word "calibration" here is distinct from probability calibration (ml15, rec6): this is about the mix of the list, not the meaning of a predicted probability.

10. Choosing metrics per surface

Putting the pieces together, a reasonable default per surface:

  • Retrieval stage (rec1, rec4): recall@K at the depth passed to ranking (K = 200-2,000), and funnel recall against the ranker's preferences. Precision and order do not matter here.
  • Navigational or question search: MRR@10 and success@1 (was the first result right?), because users stop at the first good answer.
  • Result pages and product search: nDCG@10 with graded labels, plus click-through and conversion online.
  • Home-feed carousels and rows: nDCG or recall at the number of visible slots, plus diversity, novelty and calibration, because the row is consumed as a set.
  • Playlists and autoplay: metrics over the whole sequence (completion, skips per hour), because every item is consumed in order.
  • RAG retrieval (ev6): recall@k of the passages needed, at the number of chunks sent to the model, and downstream groundedness.

Whatever the surface, pair one primary accuracy metric with two or three beyond-accuracy guardrails and the slice view, so that a trade-off is always visible rather than discovered after launch.

11. Protocols that quietly decide the answer

The same model and metric can produce very different numbers depending on how the evaluation is set up. Four choices dominate.

Temporal versus random split. Random splits let the model train on a user's later behaviour and test on earlier behaviour, and let it learn item popularity from the future. A temporal split (train before a date, test after) matches deployment. Numbers drop, honestly.

Re-consumption. On many services people re-watch, re-listen and re-buy. Predicting "the user will play the same podcast they play every morning" is trivially easy and inflates every metric. Decide whether the product goal is discovery (exclude already-consumed items from both list and labels) or re-engagement (include them), and evaluate that goal.

Full ranking versus sampled negatives. Ranking each held-out item against the entire catalogue is expensive for large catalogues, so many papers rank it against a small random sample, often 100 items, and report hit rate or nDCG on that. Krichene and Rendle (KDD 2020) showed that these sampled metrics are "inconsistent with their exact version, in the sense that they do not persist relative statements, e.g., recommender A is better than B, not even in expectation". The intuition: against 100 random items, a held-out item only needs to beat mostly irrelevant, often unpopular items, so the metric mostly measures whether the model scores relevant items above random ones, which is AUC; indeed they show that as the sample shrinks, all top-k metrics collapse towards AUC. Top-of-list quality against the real competition, other plausible items, is lost.

Candidate set for ranking models. A ranker evaluated on logged impressions only sees items the old funnel produced. If the new ranker will serve on a different candidate set (because retrieval also changed), its offline metrics are for the wrong inputs.

12. Why offline gains vanish online

Every team that ships rankers learns that a meaningful offline gain often becomes a neutral online test, and occasionally a negative one. The reasons are structural, not bad luck.

Logged-data bias. Offline labels exist only for items the old system showed, at the positions it showed them. A new model that would show different items is evaluated mostly on the old model's choices; its genuinely new picks have no labels and count as non-relevant. This systematically favours models that imitate production and penalises exploratory ones (Wide & Deep's deep-only model, with lower offline AUC and higher online gain, is a public example, rec6). Counterfactual evaluation with logged propensities (rec10) is the principled fix.

Position and presentation bias. Clicked items were often clicked because they were on top. Labels encode the old ranking, and a model that reproduces it scores well offline without being better.

Proxy mismatch. nDCG on next-week watches is a proxy for what the business wants: satisfied members who stay subscribed. A model can move the proxy without moving the goal, for example by recommending titles people start but do not enjoy.

User adaptation and novelty effects. Users react to change: a new ranker may get a burst of curiosity clicks that fade, or an initial dip while users adjust (rec13). Offline metrics assume behaviour is fixed.

Funnel and system effects. The ranker's offline gain is measured on a fixed candidate set; online, retrieval, re-ranking, business rules and latency fallbacks all interact with it. A gain at positions the re-ranker overrides does not reach users.

Statistical power. Offline gains are measured on millions of past events with tiny error bars; online effects are often an order of magnitude smaller in relative terms and need large samples to detect. A flat test can be an underpowered test.

The practical response is to treat offline metrics as a filter, not a forecast: they remove clearly bad models and rank candidates for online testing. Track, over many launches, the ratio between offline and online gains for your system; it calibrates expectations and exposes metrics that do not predict anything. Use fast online methods such as interleaving (rec13) to check direction cheaply before full A/B tests.

Why the log2 discount?

Järvelin and Kekäläinen chose a logarithmic discount because it decays smoothly, models a patient user who may look several results deep, and is easy to compute; the base controls how fast it decays, and log2(rank + 1) has become the convention. Other user models give other metrics. Rank-biased precision assumes the user moves from each result to the next with a fixed persistence probability p and stops otherwise, so position r is reached with probability pr−1; expected reciprocal rank assumes the user stops at the first satisfying result, with satisfaction probability rising with grade, so a highly relevant item early lowers the value of everything after it. These "user model" metrics make assumptions explicit and can be fitted to click logs, which is an argument for preferring them when you can.

The sampled-metric problem in a little more depth

Krichene and Rendle's analysis shows that for a sampled metric the expected value depends on the rank of the held-out item among all items through a smooth function determined by the sample size. With 100 samples out of a million items, an item ranked 50th and one ranked 5,000th in the full catalogue both very likely land at rank 1 among the 100 sampled competitors, so the metric barely distinguishes them; differences in the true top-10 are washed out. They propose corrected estimators that recover the exact metric's expectation from sampled ranks, but recommend avoiding sampled evaluation where possible. For production systems, full-catalogue ranking is usually affordable offline with an ANN index or brute force on GPUs; the cost argument for sampling is weaker than it looks.

Graded labels from implicit feedback

Converting behaviour into grades is a design choice with consequences. Watch fraction thresholds (finished = 2, started = 1), purchase over add-to-cart over click, explicit likes and saves as the top grade: each mapping encodes what the business values, and changing it can reorder models. Two cautions: dwell-time thresholds depend on item length (a 30-second watch is a full song and a fraction of a film), so normalise per item type; and negative signals ("not interested", quick skips) are informative and are lost if every non-positive becomes grade 0. Some teams use negative gains for explicit negative feedback, which nDCG can handle if the ideal ordering is computed accordingly.

Metrics for slates and sessions

Per-item metrics assume each item's value is independent of the others on the page. Real users experience slates: a page with two near-duplicates is worse than nDCG says. Diversity-aware variants such as α-nDCG discount an item's gain when it covers subtopics already covered higher up. For feeds, session-level metrics (time spent, number of satisfied interactions per session, return the next day) capture what per-list metrics cannot, at the cost of being measurable only online or with simulators.

Metrics on a funnel, not a model

Each stage of the funnel (rec1) needs its own metric, and the stages interact. Retrieval is judged by recall at its output depth, the ranker by nDCG at the visible depth on the candidates it actually receives, the re-ranker by diversity and calibration on the final page. A frequent mistake is evaluating a new ranker on the old retrieval's candidates and a new retriever with the old ranker, then shipping both together: the combination was never measured. Another is evaluating the ranker on a fixed candidate set that includes the held-out relevant items by construction, which ignores the chance that retrieval would have missed them; in production, the ranker's ceiling is retrieval's recall. End-to-end offline evaluation, in which the full pipeline produces the list from scratch for each test user and the list is scored, is slower but is the only offline number that corresponds to what users would see. Teams usually run per-stage metrics on every change and the end-to-end evaluation before any A/B test.

Simulators and replay

Between static offline metrics and live experiments sit two intermediate tools. Replay (or rejection-sampling) evaluation uses traffic where the old system chose items at random, for example an exploration slot (rec11): for each logged event, ask whether the new policy would have chosen the same item; if yes, count the outcome, if not, discard the event. Because the logged choice was random, the kept events are an unbiased sample of what the new policy would have experienced, at the cost of discarding most data. User simulators model how users respond to slates and run candidate policies against them; they make long-horizon effects (fatigue, return visits) explorable offline, but are only as good as their behaviour models and are easily over-fitted. Both are used to pre-screen policies, never as a substitute for an online test.

Offline metrics that predict online outcomes

The most useful offline metric is the one whose changes correlate with online changes on your system. Teams with a long history of experiments compute, across past launches, the correlation between each offline metric's delta and the online metric's delta, and retire metrics that never predict anything. This is the same idea as validating a proxy in causal inference: a surrogate metric is only as good as its demonstrated relationship with the outcome.

The public case: sampled metrics reverse conclusions

In the late 2010s it became common in recommender research, especially in papers on neural sequential recommendation, to evaluate by ranking each user's held-out item against 100 (sometimes 50 or 1,000) randomly sampled items the user had not interacted with, and reporting hit rate@10 and nDCG@10 on that small list. It made evaluation cheap on large catalogues. Krichene and Rendle's KDD 2020 paper "On Sampled Metrics for Item Recommendation" examined the practice formally. Their central finding, in the abstract's words: sampled metrics "are inconsistent with their exact version, in the sense that they do not persist relative statements, e.g., recommender A is better than B, not even in expectation". They also showed that "the smaller the sampling size, the less difference there is between metrics, and for very small sampling size, all metrics collapse to the AUC metric".

The consequence was concrete: published comparisons between recommenders made with sampled metrics could not be trusted to reflect which model was better at the top of a real, full-catalogue list. The paper proposed corrections that estimate the exact metric from sampled results, and recommended avoiding sampled metrics where possible. Follow-up replication work in the community re-evaluated several popular sequential recommenders with full ranking and found some reported gains shrank or disappeared. For a practitioner the lesson is general: the evaluation protocol is part of the metric, and a cheap protocol can produce confident, wrong conclusions.

How evaluation fails in production teams

Metric drift by definition. Two teams report nDCG@10 with different gains, IDCG definitions or treatment of empty users; a dashboard averages them. Pin definitions in a shared library and version them.

Leaky splits. Features computed over the whole dataset (an item's total popularity, including the test period) leak the future into training. Compute features as of the training cut-off.

Average hides harm. A model improves heavy users and degrades new users; the average rises. Slice by user activity and other key segments and set per-slice guardrails.

No uncertainty. Small evaluation sets produce differences within noise that are reported as wins. Bootstrap intervals per metric and paired comparisons per model pair.

Offline-online ratio ignored. Teams keep promising online gains equal to offline gains and keep missing. Track the historical ratio and set expectations from it.

Building the evaluation harness

Mature teams turn all of this into a shared harness rather than per-project notebooks. It owns the splits (fixed, dated, reproducible), the label definitions and gain tables, the metric implementations (with unit tests against hand-worked examples like this lesson's), the baselines (popularity, item-kNN, production), the slices and the bootstrap intervals, and it writes every run to a results store keyed by model version and data snapshot. Two benefits matter most: results from different teams are comparable because they share definitions, and every launch adds a data point to the offline-versus-online history that tells you which metrics predict anything. The harness also runs in CI for model changes, the recommendation-system counterpart of the eval gates in ev12.

What a good offline report contains

The surface and its depth k; label definition and gains; split dates; whether re-consumption is excluded; full-catalogue or sampled ranking (and why); per-user macro averages with bootstrap 95% intervals for recall@k, nDCG@k, MRR or MAP as appropriate; beyond-accuracy metrics (coverage, exposure Gini, intra-list diversity, novelty, calibration KL); slices by user activity, region, device, item age; comparison against production and against simple baselines (popularity, item-kNN) on the same users; and a stated expectation for the online test based on past offline-online ratios.

Evaluate: two candidate rankers for a podcast app's home screen

The home screen shows a carousel of 8 episodes. Model X (new) and Model Y (production) are compared offline on four weeks of data. Model X: nDCG@50 +7%, hit rate@10 against 100 sampled negatives +9%, catalogue coverage −30%, intra-list diversity −18%. Model Y is unchanged. The team trained on a random 80/20 split of all plays. Leadership wants to ship X next week. What do you say, and what evaluation do you run before an A/B test?

Answer

Problems with the evidence. (1) Depth: the carousel shows 8, but the metrics are at 50 and at 10; neither measures what users see. Use nDCG@8 and recall@8. (2) Sampled hit rate against 100 negatives can reverse model comparisons (Krichene and Rendle); rank against the full episode catalogue. (3) A random split of plays leaks the future and, on a podcast app where people replay the same shows daily, rewards predicting re-listening; use a temporal split and decide whether the carousel's goal is discovery (exclude shows the user already follows) or continuation. (4) Coverage −30% and diversity −18% suggest X gains accuracy by concentrating on popular episodes; that may hurt discovery, creators and long-term engagement. (5) No confidence intervals or slices.

What to run. Rebuild the evaluation: temporal split (train to day 21, test days 22-28); labels graded (listened over 75% = 2, 25-75% = 1, under 25% or skipped = 0); separate evaluations for "continue listening" and "new shows" if those are distinct goals; full-catalogue ranking; nDCG@8, recall@8, MRR; per-user bootstrap intervals and a paired bootstrap for X − Y; slices by user tenure, listening frequency and language. Beyond accuracy: coverage, exposure Gini, share of impressions to the top 1% of shows, novelty, calibration KL across podcast categories, new-show share.

Decision rule. If X's gain survives the corrected protocol at depth 8 with intervals excluding zero, and coverage and diversity losses are moderate or fixable by re-ranking (rec12), run an A/B test with listening minutes per user as primary metric and new-show discovery, diversity and 28-day retention as guardrails, long enough to see past novelty effects (rec13). If the gain disappears under the corrected protocol, it was an artefact of evaluation, not a better model.
