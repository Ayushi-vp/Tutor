A team adds a feature to its video ranker: "how many times was this video clicked in the last hour?". Offline, it is the best feature they have added all year: AUC up half a point. Online, the A/B test is flat. Two weeks of investigation later, the cause is embarrassingly simple. In the training data, the feature had been computed by a batch job the next morning, with complete data, so for each impression it included clicks that arrived after the impression, including, for clicked impressions, the click itself. Online, the same feature came from a streaming counter that lagged by a few minutes and missed late events. The model had learned to read the answer off a feature that would never contain it in production.

Versions of this story are the most common way ranking projects fail, and they rarely involve the model. They involve data: which features exist, how fresh they are, how they are computed, how labels are attached to the right impressions, and whether the numbers the model trained on are the numbers it will see. ml14 introduced training/serving skew and hd8 designed a feature store; this lesson is the ranking-specific deep dive: the families of features rankers use, the freshness tiers and where each is computed, why mature systems log features at serving time and wait for labels rather than recomputing them, how the joiner that builds training data works and what it costs, how delayed outcomes like purchases are labelled, and the particular shapes skew takes in ranking.

The stakes are large. A ranker's quality is bounded by its features, and its offline evaluation is only meaningful if training data matches serving. Several of the "offline gains vanish online" causes from rec8 are, underneath, data problems. And at scale the data path is the expensive part of the system: rec1 and hd3 noted that impression logs dominate the data platform, at tens of terabytes per day.

Interviewers probe this area because it separates people who have run rankers in production from people who have trained models in notebooks. Questions like "how do you make sure the feature the model trained on is the feature it sees?", "how do you attach a purchase that happens four days later to the right impression?" and "what happens to your training data during an outage?" have concrete, experience-based answers, and this lesson aims to give you those answers with the numbers behind them.

The lesson ends with a public case, ByteDance's Monolith system, which takes freshness to its limit: training continuously on a stream and pushing updated embeddings to serving every few minutes, with measurements of what that buys.

Think of a weather forecaster who predicts tomorrow's rain from today's observations: temperature, pressure, humidity, wind. Imagine two ways of building their training data. In the first, every evening they record the observations exactly as they saw them on their instruments that day, and the next day they note whether it rained. In the second, they skip the recording and, months later, reconstruct "what the observations were" from a weather archive. The archive has been cleaned, corrected and filled in with readings that arrived late. The second dataset looks better, and models trained on it look more accurate, but it describes a world the forecaster never sees at forecast time, with complete, corrected, slightly-later data. Their real forecasts, made from raw instruments, disappoint.

Ranking has the same choice. The model's inputs at serving time are whatever the feature systems returned at that millisecond: possibly stale, possibly missing, possibly computed by a different code path from the training pipeline. Log-and-wait is the first method: record the inputs the model actually saw with each impression, wait for the outcome, and train on that. Recompute-and-join is the second: rebuild features later from the warehouse. Both are used; the first is safer, the second more flexible, and the difference between them is a source of bugs that never raise an error.

The analogy breaks in one useful way. A forecaster's observations do not change because of the forecast. A recommender's data does: what it shows decides what gets clicked, which decides tomorrow's counters and training labels. That feedback loop is rec10's subject; here it matters only because it means the training data is always data the system itself produced, which is one more reason to record exactly what the system did and saw.

It helps to picture the volumes involved, because they drive every design choice that follows. A feed serving 10,000 requests per second, with 20 items per page, produces 200,000 impressions every second. Each impression is a small record: who, what, where on the page, which model, and the few hundred numbers the model looked at. Clicks, watches and purchases trickle in afterwards, seconds to days later, each needing to find its impression among the billions logged that day. The training pipeline is therefore a large, continuous matching problem, and the feature systems are a set of caches and counters that must answer within a few milliseconds for every one of those impressions. Neither is glamorous, and both decide whether the model works.

A final framing before the details: every feature has three versions that should be identical and often are not. There is the feature as defined (a sentence in a design doc, "clicks on this item in the last hour"), the feature as served (whatever the streaming counter returned at that millisecond), and the feature as trained on (whatever ended up in the training table). Most data bugs in ranking are a gap between two of those three, and most of the practices in this lesson exist to close the gaps.

With that picture, the rest of the lesson is about four questions: what features to have, how fresh each must be, how labels get attached to the right impression, and how to make sure training and serving agree.

1. Four families of features

Ranking features fall into four families, and knowing which family a feature belongs to tells you where it can be computed and what it costs.

User features describe the person regardless of candidate: account age, country, language, subscription tier, long-term affinities (share of watch time per category over 90 days), recent history (the last 50 items with watch fractions), and learned user embeddings. They are fetched once per request, usually as one row from a key-value store.

Item features describe the candidate regardless of user: category, creator, duration, upload age, content embeddings from text, image and audio models, and above all engagement statistics: impressions, clicks, completion rate, likes and hides over several time windows. They are the same for every user, so hot items' features can be cached in the ranker's memory.

Context features describe the request: time of day, day of week, device, operating system, network quality, location, the surface (home feed, search, item page), and, in training only, the position the item was shown at (rec10 explains why position is handled specially).

User-item cross features depend on both: how many of this creator's videos the user finished in the last 30 days; whether the user follows the creator; time since the user last engaged with this category; the similarity between the candidate's embedding and the user's recent items. These are often the most predictive features a ranker has, because they directly encode "this person and this kind of thing", and they are the most expensive to serve, because they must be computed per candidate per request. The standard trick: fetch per-user maps once per request (creator → engagement stats, category → stats), then each candidate's cross features are local hash-map lookups.

Many features exist in several windows at once (item clicks over 1 hour, 1 day and 7 days), letting the model combine a noisy fresh signal with a stable slow one.

3. Counters and rates, computed correctly

Engagement counters are the backbone of ranking features, and they are full of traps.

Event time versus processing time. A click that happened at 10:00:05 may reach the stream processor at 10:00:40 or, from a phone that was offline, at 14:30. Counting by processing time puts events in the wrong window. Stream processors such as Flink and Kafka Streams support windows by event time, with watermarks that decide how long to wait for late events before closing a window.

As of the impression. In training data, each impression's counters must reflect only events before the impression time. Worked example: an item had 4,000 impressions and 120 clicks in the 24 hours before an impression at 09:00, a click rate of 3.0%. A training pipeline that computes "clicks in the last 24 hours" at midnight, after the fact, includes the next 15 hours: say 500 more impressions and 30 more clicks, giving 150 / 4,500 = 3.3%. Two things went wrong. The feature is systematically fresher than it can ever be at serving time. And, more insidiously, for impressions that were clicked, the counter includes their own click, so clicked impressions have slightly higher counter values than unclicked ones: label leakage. For a popular item the effect per impression is tiny; for a new item with ten impressions, one click moves the rate from 10% to 20%, and the model learns to cheat. Point-in-time correctness (hd8) is the cure for recomputed features; logging is the cure for served ones.

Smoothing. Rates from small counts are noise. A smoothed rate, (clicks + α × prior) / (impressions + α), with a prior such as the category's average rate, keeps new items near the prior until evidence accumulates (rec6 worked one). Feeding both the smoothed rate and the raw impression count lets the model decide how much to trust it.

Exact versus approximate. Counting distinct users per item per window exactly needs memory per user; at scale, approximate structures such as HyperLogLog (distinct counts) and count-min sketches (frequencies) are used. Their error is small and predictable, which is usually fine for features, but must be the same in training and serving.

4. Two ways to build training data

A training example for the ranker is (features as of the impression, labels observed afterwards). There are two ways to get the features.

Recompute-and-join. Store raw events in the warehouse; when building training data, recompute every feature for every impression as of its timestamp with point-in-time logic, and join the labels. Its strength is flexibility: a new feature can be backfilled over months of history and trained on immediately. Its weakness is fidelity: the recomputation is a different code path from serving, run with complete data and today's code, so any difference in logic, defaults, lag or late data becomes skew.

Log-and-wait. At serving time, write the full feature vector the model used, plus the request ID, the item, the position and the model version, to an impression log. Join labels as they arrive. Train on the logged features. Its strength is fidelity: training sees exactly what serving saw, including stale values, missing lookups and defaults, so the model learns to cope with real serving conditions. Its weakness is the wait: a new feature must be logged for days or weeks before there is enough data to train on, and logging every feature for every impression is expensive (next section). Google's Rules of Machine Learning recommends this directly: save the features used at serving time and pipe them into training.

Most mature systems use log-and-wait as the default and allow recomputation for backfilling new features, carefully checked against logged values on the overlap period. hd8's deep dive called this the log-and-train versus compute-and-join trade-off; for rankers, with their fast-moving counters and cross features, logging usually wins.

5. The joiner, sized

The joiner is the job that matches engagement events to the impressions that caused them. Both carry the request ID (and item ID); the joiner holds impressions until their attribution window closes, attaches any events that arrive in time, and emits training examples: positives with their labels, and negatives when the window closes without an event.

Size it for a feed at 10,000 requests per second with 20 impressions per request: 200,000 impressions per second. If each logged feature vector is 1.5 KB compressed, the impression stream is 300 MB/s, about 26 TB per day. To attach clicks within a 30-minute window, the joiner must hold 30 minutes of impressions: 200,000 × 1,800 s × 1.5 KB ≈ 540 GB, spread across the joiner's workers. That is feasible but expensive; a 7-day purchase window would need 7 × 48 = 336 times as much, about 180 TB, which no one holds in memory. Hence the standard design, described in Monolith's paper: keep recent impressions in an in-memory cache, spill older ones to an on-disk key-value store, and when an engagement event arrives, look in the cache first and the disk store second.

Three more details matter. Events arrive out of order, so joins are by ID, not by arrival time. Duplicates happen (retries, double-logged clicks), so the joiner de-duplicates by event ID. And the joiner is a natural place to down-sample negatives (rec6), recording the sampling rate so the model can be re-calibrated, and to drop impressions from bots or internal test accounts.

Storage can be cut further: log full features only for a sample of negatives, or log compact references (feature-store version and keys) for slow-moving features and full values only for fast-moving ones, at the cost of a small recomputation step for the referenced values.

6. Labels with delay

Clicks arrive within seconds; watch completion when the session ends; purchases, subscriptions and churn days or weeks later. Delayed labels force a trade-off between accuracy and freshness. Waiting a full 7-day window before emitting an example gives correct labels for week-old impressions, so the model is always a week behind. Emitting after one day mislabels late conversions as negatives, biasing the model against items whose buyers deliberate longer (expensive items, for example). Chapelle's 2014 work on display advertising modelled this explicitly: learn, alongside the conversion probability, a distribution of the delay between click and conversion, so an impression without a conversion after one day is treated as "probably negative, with some probability of converting later" instead of a firm negative.

A common practical structure: train the fast labels (click, short watch) on fresh data continuously; train slow labels (purchase, 7-day return) on older, complete data; combine heads in the value model (rec6). Each head can have its own window and its own training cadence.

7. The shapes of skew in ranking

Training/serving skew (ml14) is any difference between what the model trained on and what it sees online. In rankers it takes recognisable forms.

Two code paths. A Spark job computes "hours since the user's last visit" in UTC for training; the serving service computes it in the user's local time zone. Every value is off by the zone offset. Logging served values, or sharing one library between batch and serving, removes this class.

Missing values and defaults. Online, a feature-store lookup that times out returns 0; in training data the same feature was never missing, or was missing for different reasons (new users). The model has never seen "0 because of a timeout" and treats it as "user has zero affinity". Log missingness explicitly (a separate "was missing" flag), use the same default everywhere, and monitor missing rates online.

Freshness mismatch. As in the opening story, recomputed counters are complete and current; served counters lag. The model over-trusts fresh counters. Train on logged values, or reproduce serving lag in recomputation.

Self-inclusion. The counter used for an impression includes that impression (or its click), in training but not at serving. Compute "as of just before" the impression.

Embedding versions. Content or user embeddings used as features were refreshed with a new encoder model; the ranker was trained on the old ones. Values are in a different space. Version embeddings and retrain or keep the old encoder until the ranker is retrained.

Population skew. Training data over-represents some traffic (heavy users, one country, desktop), or excludes traffic served by fallbacks (rec1). Not a code bug, but the same symptom: offline metrics that do not hold online.

8. Detecting and preventing skew

Three checks catch most skew. Logged versus recomputed: for a sample of impressions, compare the logged serving features with the training pipeline's values; any feature whose distributions differ systematically is a bug or a lag effect. Distribution monitoring: each feature's online distribution and missing rate versus the latest training data, with a drift statistic such as PSI (ml14) and alerts on sudden changes. Prediction parity: re-score a sample of logged requests offline with the exact served model version and logged features; predictions should match the logged predictions to numerical precision. A mismatch means the offline scoring path (feature transforms, model loading) differs from serving.

Prevention is structural: one feature definition shared by batch and serving (a feature store, hd8), shared transformation code (the same library for normalisation and bucketing), logging served features, explicit missing flags, and versioned embeddings.

9. New features: the cost of log-and-wait

Log-and-wait has one real cost: a brand-new feature has no history in the logs. Teams handle it three ways. Start logging the new feature immediately in production (even before the model uses it) and wait one or two weeks before training on it; slow but safe. Backfill it by recomputation over months of history to experiment quickly, then validate the backfill against the logged values on the overlap and, if they differ, train the production model only on logged data. Or "shadow log": compute the feature in serving, log it, but do not feed it to the model, so the data accumulates while experiments run. A feature-request process that starts logging early saves weeks on every model iteration.

10. Sequence features and how to log them

The strongest modern ranking signal is the user's recent behaviour sequence: the last 50 to several thousand items they interacted with, each with an action type, a timestamp and sometimes a duration (rec6's target attention and sequence models consume it). Sequences raise their own data questions. Logging cost: writing a 1,000-event history with every impression would multiply log volume many times. The usual answer is to log a reference (user ID and a sequence version or timestamp) and store the sequences once per user in a time-versioned store from which training reconstructs "the sequence as of the impression". This is recomputation, so it must be checked against what serving fetched. Freshness: the serving sequence comes from a nearline store that may lag by seconds; training must reproduce that lag or the model learns to rely on the very latest event, which serving sometimes lacks. Leakage: the sequence as of the impression must exclude the impression's own outcome; an off-by-one in a timestamp comparison silently includes it.

11. Choosing, pruning and paying for features

Features are not free. Each one costs storage in the logs, a lookup or computation at serving time, a pipeline to keep it fresh and an on-call burden when it breaks. Mature teams periodically prune: measure each feature's contribution (by ablation, retraining without it, or with importance scores from the model, which GBDTs give directly and which permutation tests give for neural models), and remove features whose contribution is within noise. Facebook's 2014 ads paper reported that a small fraction of features carried most of the model's accuracy, a common pattern. Also prune for robustness: a feature with marginal accuracy value and a history of outages is a net negative.

When adding a feature, the questions to answer up front are: which lane computes it (rec1), how fresh it must be, what its value is when the lookup fails, whether it is logged at serving, how it is computed point-in-time for any backfill, and who owns it. A feature that cannot answer those questions should not reach production, however good it looks in a notebook.

Why logged features make models robust to serving failures

Logged data contains the system's real imperfections: features that were missing because a store timed out, counters that lagged during an incident, defaults for new users. A model trained on it learns how much to trust each feature under those conditions. A model trained on clean recomputed data has never seen a timeout default and can misbehave badly during the next incident, exactly when you would want it to degrade gracefully. This is one reason to mark degraded requests explicitly (rec1): you want the model to learn from realistic data, but not to treat an outage day as normal behaviour.

Point-in-time joins at scale

Recomputing features as of millions of impression timestamps is an "as-of join": for each impression at time t, find the latest feature value with timestamp ≤ t (and, for availability-time correctness, the latest value that was actually available to serving by t, hd8). Done naively it is quadratic; done well it sorts both sides by entity and time and merges them in one pass. Feature-store tools (Feast, Tecton, the platforms at large companies) implement this. The subtle part is the timestamp to use: the time the underlying event happened, the time the feature value was computed, or the time it reached the online store. Only the last reproduces what serving could have seen.

Negative sampling and its bookkeeping

Down-sampling negatives in the joiner (rec6) saves storage and training time but changes the label rate, so the sampling rate must travel with each example for re-calibration and for any weighted metric. If different surfaces or time periods use different rates, store the rate per example, not as a global constant. A subtle bias appears if sampling depends on anything correlated with features (for example, keeping all negatives for new users and 5% for others): the model learns a distorted base rate per segment unless each example is weighted by the inverse of its sampling probability.

Expiring and filtering embeddings

Online-trained models face an ever-growing set of IDs. Monolith's approach: a collisionless hash table (cuckoo hashing) for embeddings, so different IDs never share a row; frequency filtering, so an ID gets an embedding only after it has appeared a minimum number of times (rare IDs would not learn anything useful and would waste memory); and expiry, so an ID inactive for a configured period is evicted. These are the online counterparts of the hashing and thresholds in rec6, and they turn "memory grows forever" into a tunable steady state.

Fresh features versus fresh models

There are two ways to react quickly to change: fresh features (the model is a day old, but its inputs include the last hour's counters and the user's last five minutes) and fresh parameters (the model itself updates every few minutes). Fresh features capture changes the model already knows how to interpret ("this video is suddenly popular"); fresh parameters capture things it has never seen (a brand-new item's ID embedding, a new trend's pattern). Most systems get most of the value from fresh features and daily or hourly retraining; continuous training pays off where new IDs dominate, such as short video and ads.

Label quality: what counts as an engagement

Labels look objective ("clicked: yes") and are full of judgement calls. Accidental taps on mobile, especially near scroll gestures, produce clicks followed by an immediate back; many teams drop or down-weight clicks with dwell under a couple of seconds. Bots and scrapers generate impressions and clicks with no human behind them and must be filtered before joining, or they teach the model their patterns. Autoplay makes "watched" ambiguous: a video that played in the background for 30 seconds while the user scrolled past was not chosen. Repeated impressions of the same item to the same user (the user scrolled past it three times) need a policy: one example per impression, or de-duplicated per session. Each of these choices changes the label distribution and therefore the model, so they should be written down, versioned with the training data and changed only with an experiment, exactly like model changes.

Data volume as a design variable

At 26 TB of impression logs per day, retention is a cost decision. Thirty days of full logs is nearly 800 TB. Common choices: keep full feature logs for a few weeks, keep down-sampled negatives longer, aggregate older data into per-user and per-item statistics, and keep a small uniformly sampled slice indefinitely for long-term studies and reproducing old experiments. The training window itself is a modelling choice: more history helps rare items and slow trends, while recent data reflects current behaviour; many rankers weight recent days more.

The public case: Monolith, ByteDance's real-time training system

Monolith, described by ByteDance engineers at the ORSUM workshop of RecSys 2022 and used in the BytePlus Recommend product, is a public example of pushing feature and model freshness to their limits. The authors' motivation: recommendation data is mostly sparse IDs whose population changes constantly, and user interests shift fast, so a model that is hours old is already behind.

The data path is the one this lesson describes, made real-time. User actions (clicks, likes, conversions) go to one Kafka queue and the features used at serving go to another; a Flink streaming job acts as the online joiner, pairing actions with features by a unique request key, because the two streams arrive without time-order guarantees. Because some actions arrive days later ("a user may take a few days before they decide to buy an item they were presented days ago"), the joiner keeps recent features in an in-memory cache and spills older ones to an on-disk key-value store, looking in the cache first and the disk store on a miss. Joined examples go through negative sampling into a training-example queue that feeds both batch training (to bootstrap or change architecture) and online training.

On the model side, embeddings live in a collisionless cuckoo hash table with frequency filtering and expiry. During online training, the system tracks which IDs were updated since the last sync and pushes only those sparse parameters from the training parameter servers to the serving ones at a minute-level interval; dense parameters, which change slowly, are synced less often. Parameter servers are snapshotted daily; the authors argue that losing up to a day of updates on one failed server among many is a negligible loss.

Their measurements make the freshness argument concrete. On the public Criteo display-ads dataset, simulating online training, average online-serving AUC rose as the parameter sync interval shortened: 79.66 at 5 hours, 79.78 at 1 hour and 79.80 at 30 minutes, against about 79.4 for batch training. They also report a live A/B experiment on an ads model in which online training outperformed batch training on every day of the test. The case shows both what freshness buys and what it costs: streaming joins, late-event storage, parameter synchronisation and fault tolerance are all needed before a model can learn from what happened ten minutes ago.

How the data path fails in production

Silent logging loss. A client release stops sending one engagement event type (say, likes on one platform). The like head's label rate drops by a third; the model learns that likes are rarer and down-weights likeable content. Monitor label rates per head, per platform and per app version, with alerts on step changes.

Joiner backlog. The joiner falls behind during a traffic spike; impressions age out of the in-memory window before their clicks arrive and are emitted as negatives. The training data's click rate dips for exactly the busiest hours. Monitor joiner lag and the share of positives arriving after window close.

Feature outage baked into training. A feature service returns defaults for six hours; logged data faithfully records them; the next model learns that the default value predicts low engagement. Mark degraded requests and exclude or down-weight them.

Backfill mismatch. A new feature is backfilled by recomputation and trained on; online, the served values differ (lag, different source). The offline gain does not appear. Validate backfills against logged values before training a production model on them.

Clock problems. Device clocks are wrong; event-time windows put events in the future or far past. Use server receipt time as a sanity bound and clamp outliers.

What to monitor

Per feature: online missing rate, distribution versus training (quantiles or PSI), freshness (age of the newest data behind the value). Logging: impressions logged versus served (they should match), bytes per impression, schema changes. Joiner: lag, cache hit rate, examples emitted per minute, label rates per head, share of late events. Training data: example count per hour, positive rate per head, sampling rates recorded. Parity: daily logged-versus-recomputed comparison for a sample, and offline-versus-online prediction match for re-scored requests.

Goal: build a toy event stream, compute counters correctly and incorrectly, join labels with a window, and watch leakage inflate offline performance. Python 3.12, standard library only.

  1. Create rec9_lab.py. Simulate one day of a feed: 50 items with hidden true click probabilities between 1% and 10%, and 100,000 impressions at random times across 24 hours, each assigned to an item in proportion to its popularity. Each impression is clicked with its item's probability; the click arrives 0-300 seconds later (uniform), and 5% of clicks arrive 1-3 hours late.
  2. For each impression, compute three versions of "item click rate in the previous 6 hours": correct (events with event time strictly before the impression), lagged (correct but ignoring events in the last 5 minutes, as a stream with lag would), and leaky (all events in the 6 hours before the impression plus the 6 hours after, including its own click).
  3. Join labels with a 30-minute attribution window by impression ID; count how many true clicks become negatives because they arrived late.
  4. Train a one-feature logistic regression (by SGD) on the first 18 hours with each counter version, and evaluate log loss on the last 6 hours using the lagged counter (what serving would have).
  5. Print the training log loss and the "serving" log loss for each version.
  6. Repeat with items that have only 20-50 impressions per day (new items) and compare the size of the leakage effect.

What to look for. The leaky version should look best on training data and do worst when evaluated with serving-time features, and the gap should be much larger for low-traffic items, where one click moves the rate a lot. The lagged version trained and evaluated consistently should be close to the correct version. The 30-minute window should turn the late-arriving 5% of clicks into negatives, a small, measurable label bias.

Diagnose: a conversion model that gets worse every Monday

An e-commerce ranker predicts P(purchase within 7 days | impression) and is retrained daily on impressions from 8 to 1 days ago, joined with purchases. Features are recomputed from the warehouse at training time. The team notices the model's calibration is poor on Mondays (it under-predicts purchases by about 15%), and that items with long consideration times (furniture, electronics) are consistently under-ranked. A new feature, "item add-to-cart rate in the last hour", gave +0.4% AUC offline but nothing online. Explain the likely causes and redesign the data path.

Answer

Causes. (1) Truncated attribution: training on impressions from 8 to 1 days ago with a 7-day purchase label means impressions from 1-6 days ago have incomplete windows; recent impressions' late purchases are labelled negative. Long-consideration categories are hit hardest, so they are under-predicted and under-ranked. (2) The Monday effect: weekend browsing converts later (people buy on Monday or Tuesday), so the most recent days in Monday's training run, the weekend impressions, are the most truncated; the model trained on Monday learns an artificially low rate. (3) The add-to-cart feature: recomputed from the warehouse with complete data, including events after the impression and possibly the user's own add-to-cart, while the serving value comes from a lagging stream. That is leakage plus freshness skew, so the offline gain was not real.

Redesign. Log served features with each impression (request ID, item, position, model version, full feature vector) and train on logged values; backfill new features only for experiments, validated against logged values. Labels: train the purchase head only on impressions whose 7-day window has closed (8-14 days old), and add a fast head for add-to-cart on fresh data; combine both in the value model. Alternatively, use a delayed-feedback model that learns the conversion-delay distribution so recent impressions count as partial negatives. Compute counters as of the impression, with the same lag as serving. Monitor calibration per category and per weekday, label completeness per day, and logged-versus-recomputed differences for every feature.

Check it worked. Calibration ratio (predicted / observed) near 1 for every weekday and category on a held-out week; long-consideration categories' share of impressions back to their historical level; any new feature's offline gain measured on logged values before an A/B test.
