Give a 2022-era language model this question: "A shop sells pens in packs of 12. Ana buys 7 packs, gives 15 pens to her class and splits the rest equally between herself and two friends. How many pens does each person get?" Ask for the answer immediately and a large model often produces a confident wrong number, say 23. Ask it to write out its working first ("7 packs of 12 is 84 pens; 84 minus 15 is 69; 69 split between 3 people is 23 each") and it gets 23 right far more often. In this example the direct answer happens to be right too, but across a benchmark of such problems the gap is enormous: Google's 540-billion-parameter PaLM model went from solving 17.9% of grade-school maths problems to 56.9% just by being shown eight examples with the working written out.

That observation, published in January 2022 as chain-of-thought prompting, started a line of work that changed how frontier models are built. If writing steps helps, can a model be trained to write better steps? Can it be trained to check its own steps, notice a mistake and try another approach? Can you get a better answer simply by letting the model think for longer, or by asking it the same question many times and taking the most common answer? By late 2024 the answer to all of these was yes. OpenAI's o1 and then DeepSeek-R1 were trained with reinforcement learning to produce long internal reasoning before answering, and their accuracy on competition mathematics and programming jumped far beyond what model size alone had delivered.

For an AI engineer this matters in very practical ways. Reasoning models are now a standard option on every major API, with a parameter that controls how long they think. They are slower and more expensive per request, sometimes by a factor of ten or more, and they are better only on some kinds of problems. Deciding when to pay for one, how to budget its latency and tokens, and how to explain why it works are questions you will meet both in product design and in interviews.

This lesson builds the idea from the ground up. It starts with why a transformer that answers immediately is limited, using the forward pass you met in the LLM track, and why written-out steps lift that limit. It then shows how errors compound along a chain, what test-time compute is and the two ways of spending it, how chain-of-thought prompting turned into trained reasoning (o1, DeepSeek-R1-Zero and R1), what reasoning traces look like, where reasoning helps and where it wastes money, and the arithmetic of its cost and latency. The public case is the original chain-of-thought paper by Wei et al., with its numbers and its ablations. The later topics of this track then open each piece: sampling and search (rs2), the reinforcement-learning algorithms (rs3, rs4), rewards (rs5), distillation (rs6), product use (rs7) and evaluation (rs8).

The mental model: a calculator that may only write the final digit

Imagine being asked to multiply 47 by 86 with one rule: you must say the answer immediately, without writing anything down. Most people would fail, not because they do not know how to multiply but because the method needs intermediate results held somewhere: 47 × 80 = 3,760, 47 × 6 = 282, then 3,760 + 282 = 4,042. Give them a piece of scrap paper and almost everyone succeeds. The scrap paper does two things. It gives more time, because the work is spread over several steps, and it gives memory, because each intermediate result is written down where the next step can read it.

A language model is in the first situation whenever it answers directly. As the LLM track showed (lm3, lm8), the model produces text one token at a time, and each token comes from exactly one forward pass: one trip of the whole context through a fixed stack of layers, ending in a probability for every possible next token. The number of layers is fixed (32 in a typical 7-billion-parameter model, 80 in Llama 2 70B, 126 in Llama 3.1 405B), so the amount of step-after-step computation the model can do before committing to a token is fixed too. If the first token of the answer must already contain the result, all the reasoning has to fit inside those layers.

A chain of thought (CoT) is the scrap paper. When the model writes "47 × 80 = 3760" before the answer, each of those tokens took its own forward pass, and because generated tokens are appended to the input, every later pass can read them. The intermediate result now lives in the text, not in the hidden activations of one pass. The model can do arbitrarily many small steps, each well within what one pass can manage, and string them together.

From a trick to a training target

At first this was a prompting trick: show the model worked examples, or add "Let's think step by step", and it writes its working. It worked well for large models and barely at all for small ones. The next step was to ask what would happen if models were trained to reason this way, rewarded not for imitating tidy human working but simply for getting the final answer right. The result, from 2024 onwards, is the reasoning model: an ordinary transformer, post-trained mostly with reinforcement learning (RL, learning from rewards; rs3) on problems whose answers a program can check, which writes long chains of thought (often thousands of tokens) full of attempts, checks ("wait, that can't be right") and corrections before giving a short final answer.

The new knob: test-time compute

Until about 2024 the way to a better model was to spend more train-time compute: more parameters, more data, more training. Reasoning models made a second knob practical: test-time compute, the computation spent while answering a particular question. You can spend it serially, by letting the model write a longer chain, or in parallel, by sampling many answers and combining them with a vote or a checker. Either way you pay per question, in latency and money, and get accuracy back. That trade is the core of the subject, and the rest of this track is about spending test-time compute well and training models that use it well.

Where the analogy breaks

Scrap paper is a faithful record of how a person computed. A model's chain of thought is generated text: it influences the answer because the answer is conditioned on it, but the model can also reach conclusions for reasons it never writes down, and it can write plausible-looking steps that are not what actually drove the result. And unlike a person, a model gains from writing only if its training made the written steps reliable; small models write fluent nonsense and do worse with a chain than without one.

1. What "reasoning" means here

In this track, a reasoning problem is one whose answer depends on intermediate results the question does not state: a word problem that needs three arithmetic steps, a program that has to handle several cases, a puzzle where one deduction enables the next. The contrast is a lookup, where the answer is a single fact the model either knows or does not ("What is the capital of Peru?"). The distinction matters because the techniques in this track help reasoning problems a great deal and lookups hardly at all: no amount of thinking recovers a fact the model never learned.

Reasoning problems also tend to have checkable answers: a number that matches a reference, code that passes tests, a proof a proof-checker accepts. Checkability is the second ingredient of the modern recipe, because it lets a program, rather than a person, say whether an answer was right, which makes it cheap to generate millions of training signals (rs4) and to verify answers at inference (rs2).

2. The forward pass has a fixed depth

Recall from lm3 what happens when a model produces one token. The context (prompt plus everything generated so far) is embedded, flows through L transformer blocks (attention then MLP, each adding to the residual stream), and the final vector is turned into a probability for each token in the vocabulary. The number of blocks L is fixed by the architecture. The total work per token is roughly 2N FLOPs (floating-point operations) for a model with N parameters, plus attention's cost over the context, but the important number for reasoning is not the total; it is the serial depth, the length of the longest chain of operations that must happen one after another. Within one pass, that is about L layers.

Some computations are inherently serial. To multiply 47 by 86 you must compute the partial products before you can add them; to trace a program you must know the value of a variable after line 3 before you can evaluate line 4; to follow a chain of family relationships ("Ana's mother's brother's daughter") you must resolve each link before the next. A network of fixed depth can do a fixed number of such dependent steps in one pass, and theoretical work (deep section) shows there are simple problems, such as tracking the state of a finite-state machine over a long input, that a transformer answering immediately provably cannot solve at all input lengths. In practice the symptom is familiar: models answer one-step questions well and multi-step ones badly when forced to answer at once.

3. Writing steps down: the scratchpad effect

Generation is a loop: run a pass, sample a token, append it to the context, run the next pass. Whatever the model writes becomes input to every later pass. That makes written text a form of external memory that persists across passes, and it turns T generated tokens into roughly T × L layers of serial computation, because each pass can build on the results of the previous ones. A chain of thought therefore buys two things at once: more serial steps and a place to store intermediate results.

It also buys a third, less mechanical thing: decomposition. "Ana buys 7 packs of 12, gives away 15, splits the rest three ways" is an unusual problem, but "7 × 12", "84 − 15" and "69 ÷ 3" are each things the model has seen countless times in pretraining. Breaking the problem into familiar steps turns one hard prediction into several easy ones.

The steps must carry information. In the public case below, Wei et al. tried replacing the chain of thought with a row of dots of the same length as the equation the model would need, so the model got the extra forward passes without the content. Accuracy stayed at the level of direct answering. More passes are only useful if what they write can be read and used by later passes.

4. Why errors compound along a chain

A chain is only as good as all of its steps. If a solution needs n steps and the model gets each one right independently with probability q, the probability that every step is right is

P(chain correct) = q^n

By hand: with q = 0.95 and n = 10 steps, 0.95^10 = 0.599, so the model solves only 60% of such problems even though each step is "95% reliable". With q = 0.99 it is 0.99^10 = 0.904; with q = 0.90 it is 0.90^10 = 0.349. Small per-step improvements produce large end-to-end gains, which is one reason decomposition helps: easy steps have q close to 1.

Independence is an approximation (a model that misreads the question gets many steps wrong together), but the arithmetic explains two behaviours of reasoning models. First, they re-check. If the model verifies each step and catches a fraction c of its errors, the effective per-step error falls from 1 − q to (1 − q)(1 − c). Catching 80% of errors at q = 0.95 turns a per-step error of 0.05 into 0.01, so q becomes 0.99 and the 10-step chain goes from 0.60 to 0.90. The price is the tokens spent on checking. Second, they backtrack: abandoning a path that has gone wrong and starting another resets the compounding. Both behaviours emerged on their own when models were trained with reinforcement learning, exactly because they raise the probability of a correct final answer.

5. Chain-of-thought prompting: few-shot and zero-shot

The first way to get a chain of thought was to ask for one. Few-shot chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al., 2022) puts a handful of worked examples in the prompt, each a question, a short written reasoning and an answer, and then the new question. The model imitates the format and writes its own reasoning. Wei et al. used eight hand-written examples for maths problems, the same eight for every test question.

Zero-shot chain of thought (Kojima et al., 2022) needs no examples: append "Let's think step by step" after the question, let the model write, then extract the answer with a second prompt. On the instruction-tuned GPT-3 model text-davinci-002 it raised accuracy on the MultiArith arithmetic benchmark from 17.7% to 78.7% and on GSM8K from 10.4% to 40.7%. GSM8K, which recurs throughout this track, is a set of grade-school maths word problems that need two to eight arithmetic steps; its test split has 1,319 problems.

Prompting had a clear limit: it only elicits a habit the model already has. Small models wrote chains that read well but contained wrong steps, and their accuracy fell. Large models improved a lot but still made errors they did not catch, because nothing in their training had ever rewarded checking.

6. One answer as many possible chains

A useful way to think about chain-of-thought generation is probabilistic. For a question x, the model can produce many different chains z, each with its own probability, and each chain ends in some answer. The probability that the model's final answer is a particular a is the sum over all chains that end in a:

P(answer = a | x) = Σ_z P(z | x) · P(a | x, z)

Sampling one chain and reading off its answer is a random draw from this distribution. If the correct answer is the single most likely answer but holds only, say, 60% of the probability, then one sample is right 60% of the time, but the most common answer among many samples is right far more often. That is the idea behind self-consistency, majority voting over sampled chains, which rs2 develops in full.

By hand, for the simplest case where every sample is either right or wrong and wrong samples all agree with each other (the worst case for voting): with per-sample accuracy p = 0.6 and three samples, the majority is right when at least two are right: p³ + 3p²(1 − p) = 0.216 + 3 × 0.36 × 0.4 = 0.216 + 0.432 = 0.648. Three samples turned 60% into 65%. In practice wrong answers scatter over many different values, so the correct answer wins with a plurality even more often; the lab shows 60% per sample becoming about 92% with five samples when errors scatter over five wrong values.

7. Test-time compute: serial and parallel

Both the longer chain and the vote spend extra computation at answer time, but in different shapes, and the difference matters in production.

Serial test-time compute is a longer single chain. Its strength is that later steps can use earlier ones: a check can catch an error in the line above, a failed approach can inform the next. Its cost is latency, because tokens are generated one after another: 6,000 thinking tokens at 60 tokens per second is 100 seconds before the answer begins.

Parallel test-time compute is many independent attempts combined afterwards, by majority vote, by a learned verifier that scores each attempt, or by running tests on generated code (rs2). Its strength is that the attempts run concurrently, so wall-clock time is set by the slowest single attempt, and serving systems batch them efficiently (lm13). Its weakness is that attempts cannot learn from each other, and the combination rule needs answers that can be compared or checked.

The compute itself is easy to estimate. A forward pass costs about 2N FLOPs per token, so generating T tokens costs about 2NT. By hand: a 7-billion-parameter model writing 8,000 thinking tokens spends 2 × 7×10⁹ × 8,000 = 1.12×10¹⁴ FLOPs; a 70-billion-parameter model answering directly in 400 tokens spends 2 × 70×10⁹ × 400 = 5.6×10¹³. The small model thinking hard spends twice the compute of the large model answering briefly. Whether that is a good trade depends on the problem, which is what the research in the deep section measures.

8. From prompting to training: o1

In September 2024 OpenAI released o1-preview, describing o1 as a model trained with large-scale reinforcement learning to "think before it answers" in a long chain of thought. The announcement reported two scaling results: performance improved smoothly with more reinforcement-learning compute during training and with more thinking time at test time. On AIME 2024, the American Invitational Mathematics Examination (15 hard problems, each with an integer answer from 0 to 999), o1 averaged 74% with one sample per problem against 12% for GPT-4o, and reached 83% with a consensus of 64 samples and 93% when re-ranking 1,000 samples with a learned scoring function. OpenAI did not publish the training method in detail and chose to hide the raw chain of thought from users, showing a summary instead.

9. DeepSeek-R1-Zero: reasoning from rewards alone

In January 2025 DeepSeek published the first detailed open recipe. DeepSeek-R1-Zero started from DeepSeek-V3-Base, a pretrained model with no instruction tuning, and applied reinforcement learning with GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization, the subject of rs4) on maths and code problems. The reward was purely rule-based: an accuracy reward (does the boxed final answer match the reference, do the generated programs pass the test cases?) and a format reward (is the reasoning enclosed in <think> and </think> tags?), weighted equally. The prompt template only asked the model to reason and then answer; it said nothing about how to reason. DeepSeek deliberately avoided neural reward models for reasoning because they found them prone to reward hacking, the policy finding outputs a learned scorer over-rates (rs5).

The results, in the current version of the paper: AIME 2024 pass@1 (the average accuracy of a single sample) rose from 15.6% at the start of training to 77.9%, and to 86.7% with a 16-sample majority vote, above the average human competitor. (The first version of the paper, from January 2025, reported 71.0% pass@1 for its checkpoint; the later version reports a longer-trained model.) Just as striking, the average response length on training problems grew steadily from a few hundred tokens to many thousands without anyone asking for longer answers, and behaviours such as re-checking earlier steps and trying alternative approaches appeared, including a much-quoted moment where an intermediate checkpoint writes "Wait, wait. Wait. That's an aha moment I can flag here" and re-evaluates. Nothing in the reward mentioned length or checking; they were learned because they made correct answers more likely.

10. DeepSeek-R1: making it usable

R1-Zero's chains were effective but hard to read, and they mixed English and Chinese. The released DeepSeek-R1 used a multi-stage pipeline. First a cold start: supervised fine-tuning on thousands of curated long-reasoning examples in a readable, conversational style. Then reasoning-focused RL with an extra reward for language consistency. Then rejection sampling: generate many answers, keep the correct and readable ones, and fine-tune on them together with non-reasoning data (writing, factual questions) so the model stays a good general assistant. Finally a second RL stage with both rule-based rewards and preference rewards for helpfulness and harmlessness. DeepSeek also distilled R1's reasoning into smaller Qwen and Llama models by plain supervised fine-tuning on its outputs, which rs6 covers.

11. What a reasoning trace looks like

A reasoning model's chain is not a tidy textbook solution. A typical trace on a competition problem runs to thousands or tens of thousands of tokens. It restates the problem, proposes an approach, computes, notices something odd ("wait, that gives a negative length"), goes back, tries a different approach, verifies the final answer by substituting it back, and sometimes verifies again. It repeats itself and wanders. This messiness is a feature of how it was trained: RL rewarded only the final answer, so the model kept whatever habits raised the chance of getting there, including redundant checks.

APIs present the trace in different ways. Some return it in full (DeepSeek-R1, Claude's extended thinking returns summarised thinking blocks on recent models), some hide it and show only a summary or nothing (OpenAI's o-series). In every case the reasoning tokens are generated and billed as output tokens, and APIs report them separately so you can see how many were used. Most APIs also expose a control: an effort level (low, medium, high) or a thinking budget in tokens, which rs7 covers.

12. Where reasoning helps and where it does not

Reasoning helps most on problems that are multi-step, where intermediate results must be combined, and checkable, where the training could reward correctness: mathematics, competitive programming, debugging, multi-step planning, questions that require combining several facts, and agentic tasks where the model must check whether an action worked. It helps little on lookups and classification, where there is nothing to compute, and on short creative tasks, where there is no right answer to converge on.

It can also hurt. Overthinking is the tendency of reasoning models to spend far more tokens than a problem needs. Chen et al. found o1-like models used on average 1,953% more tokens than conventional models on the question "what is 2 plus 3?", and in one example in their paper an o1-style model wrote 901 tokens containing 13 separate solutions, although its first solution, 39 tokens in, was already correct. On easy inputs this is pure cost and latency, and long chains occasionally argue themselves out of a correct early answer. The practical lesson is to match thinking to difficulty, either with an effort parameter or by routing.

13. The cost and latency arithmetic

Because thinking tokens are output tokens, the arithmetic is simple and unforgiving. By hand: a reasoning model that averages 6,000 thinking tokens and 400 answer tokens generates 6,400 output tokens. At 60 tokens per second that takes 6,400 / 60 ≈ 107 seconds; at $10 per million output tokens it costs 6,400 × 10 / 10⁶ = $0.064. A fast model answering in 400 tokens at 80 tokens per second takes 5 seconds and, at $2 per million, costs $0.0008, eighty times less. Across 100,000 queries a day that is $6,400 against $80. If the reasoning model solves 82% of your tasks and the fast one 55%, you are paying about $6,320 a day for 27,000 extra correct answers, about 23 cents each. Whether that is worth it is a business question, and the common answer is to route: the fast model for easy requests, the reasoning model where accuracy matters and someone can wait (rs7).

Why fixed-depth transformers are provably limited

Theoretical work has asked what a transformer can compute if it must answer right after reading its input. With realistic assumptions (fixed depth, limited numerical precision), such transformers fall inside a complexity class of very shallow, highly parallel circuits, and under standard conjectures that class cannot solve some simple sequential problems at all input lengths: tracking the state of a finite-state machine across a long string, deciding whether two nodes of a graph are connected, composing a long sequence of permutations. The intuition is the one from the body: the computation needs as many dependent steps as the input is long, and a fixed stack of layers has a fixed number of steps. Merrill and Sabharwal (ICLR 2024) then asked what intermediate tokens add. Their answer depends on how many: a number of steps logarithmic in the input length helps only slightly; a linear number lets a transformer decoder recognise every regular language (so it can simulate any finite-state machine); and a polynomial number gives exactly the class of problems solvable in polynomial time. The chain of thought acts as the recurrent state a pure transformer lacks.

Two cautions keep this from being over-read. The results are about what parameters exist, not what training finds; and they say nothing about how long a chain a particular real problem needs. They do explain why the gains from reasoning are largest on long, sequential problems and why the needed chain length grows with problem size.

Chain of thought as a latent variable

The sum in the body, P(a | x) = Σ_z P(z | x) P(a | x, z), treats the chain as a hidden (latent) variable that is marginalised out. This view connects the techniques of the track. Self-consistency estimates the most probable answer by sampling chains and voting, which approximates the marginal. Training on correct chains (STaR, rejection sampling; rs6) is a form of expectation–maximisation: sample chains, keep those that lead to the right answer, and raise their probability. Reinforcement learning with a correctness reward directly maximises the expected accuracy E_z[1(a(z) = a*)], the probability mass on chains ending in the right answer. Seen this way, a reasoning model is a model whose distribution over chains has been reshaped so that most of its mass sits on chains that end correctly.

Why RL instead of imitating human working

One could collect human step-by-step solutions and fine-tune on them. It helps (that is how many maths models were trained before 2024) but has three limits. Human solutions show the final, clean path, not the exploration, false starts and verifications that make long reasoning robust, so the model learns to sound confident rather than to check. They are expensive to write at scale, especially for hard problems. And the model is capped at imitating its demonstrators. Reinforcement learning rewards outcomes, not form, so the model may discover any strategy that works, including verbose re-checking that no human would write down, and it scales to any problem with an automatic checker. The price is that RL needs the model to succeed sometimes (a problem it never solves gives no signal), and it needs a reward that cannot be gamed, which is why checkable domains came first.

Does RL create ability or elicit it?

An important open question is whether RL teaches new reasoning or makes the model reliably do what it could already occasionally do. Yue et al. (2025) measured pass@k, the probability that at least one of k samples is right, for base models and their RL-trained versions across several model families. RL models win clearly at k = 1, but as k grows to hundreds the base models catch up and overtake, and the RL models' correct solutions were already within the base model's sampling distribution. RL sharpens the distribution: it moves probability onto paths that work, which raises single-sample accuracy enormously, and narrows the long tail of rarer correct approaches. In the same study, distillation from a stronger teacher did expand the set of problems solvable at large k. This does not make RL unimportant (pass@1 is what users experience, and a 15.6% to 77.9% jump is transformative), but it frames RL as mostly elicitation, at least at the scales studied.

When test-time compute beats a bigger model

Snell et al. (2024) compared spending extra inference compute against using a larger model, with total FLOPs held equal. They found that the best strategy depends on difficulty, and that choosing the strategy per prompt ("compute-optimal" scaling) was more than four times as efficient as plain best-of-N sampling. On problems where a smaller model already had a somewhat non-trivial success rate, test-time compute let it outperform a model 14 times larger. On the hardest problems, where the small model almost never succeeded, extra inference was a poor buy and pretraining a bigger model was better. The rule of thumb that follows is useful in interviews: test-time compute converts occasional success into reliable success; it does not create competence from nothing.

Two axes of training compute

OpenAI's o1 announcement made a claim that shaped the field: accuracy rose smoothly with the amount of RL compute in training as well as with thinking time at test time. That means reasoning models have three compute budgets: pretraining (knowledge and raw ability), RL post-training (how well the model uses thinking), and inference (how much it thinks on this question). They interact: a model trained with more RL uses a given thinking budget better, and a stronger base model gives RL more to work with. DeepSeek-R1-Zero's steadily growing response length is the visible sign of the second budget turning into the third: as RL progressed, the policy learned that spending more tokens per problem raised its reward.

Serial versus parallel, more carefully

The two ways of spending test-time compute have different scaling behaviour. Parallel sampling with majority voting saturates: once the most common answer is stable, more samples change nothing, and if the model's most common answer is wrong, voting confidently returns the wrong answer. Parallel sampling with a perfect checker (unit tests, a proof checker) keeps improving as long as any sample is right, which is why coverage, the fraction of problems solved by at least one of k samples, grows steadily with k on code tasks (rs2). Serial thinking can in principle go further than either because each step conditions on the previous ones, but in practice a single chain also saturates and can drift, so frontier systems combine them: a trained reasoner, several parallel samples, and a vote or verifier on top, as in o1's 1,000-sample re-ranking result.

The emergence-with-scale caveat

Wei et al. reported chain-of-thought prompting as an emergent ability, near useless below about 100 billion parameters and strongly positive above. Later small models do benefit from chains of thought, because they were trained on much more reasoning-style data and, more recently, distilled from reasoning models (rs6): a 7-billion-parameter distilled model can outperform the 2022 PaLM 540B by a wide margin on the same benchmarks. The lesson is that the ability depends on what the model was trained on, not on size alone; size was the proxy in 2022 because larger models had absorbed more of the patterns needed for reliable steps.

The public case: chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al., Google, 2022)

The paper that named the technique, "Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models" by Jason Wei and colleagues at Google Research (NeurIPS 2022), is short and worth reading in full. The method is exactly the few-shot version described in the body: eight hand-written exemplars, each a maths word problem, a few sentences of reasoning and the answer, prepended to every test question. The comparison is standard few-shot prompting with the same eight questions and answers but no reasoning.

The headline result is on GSM8K. PaLM 540B with standard prompting solved 17.9% of the test problems; with chain-of-thought prompting it solved 56.9%, and 58.6% when a calculator was used afterwards to fix arithmetic slips in the written equations. The previous best, 55%, came from a fine-tuned GPT-3 175B combined with a trained verifier (Cobbe et al., 2021), so a prompt with eight examples beat a purpose-built fine-tuned system. Other models showed the same direction: Codex (code-davinci-002) went from 19.7% to 63.1%, and GPT-3 175B (text-davinci-002) from 15.6% to 46.9%.

Three findings in the paper are directly useful. First, the gains appeared only with scale: LaMDA models up to 8 billion parameters did worse with chains of thought (LaMDA 8B fell from 3.2% to 1.6% on GSM8K), and the authors found small models "produced fluent but illogical chains of thought". Second, gains were largest on the hardest tasks: performance more than doubled on GSM8K, the dataset with the lowest baseline, while on one-step problems (the SingleOp subset of MAWPS, a collection of simple arithmetic word problems) improvements were negative or very small. Third, the ablations tested three alternative explanations. Asking for an equation only, without words, did not help much on GSM8K. Asking for a row of dots as long as the needed equation ("variable compute only") performed about the same as the baseline, so extra tokens alone were not the reason. Asking for the reasoning after the answer also performed about the same as the baseline, so the chain helps because the answer is conditioned on it, not because the prompt merely activates relevant knowledge.

The authors also read model outputs by hand. Of 50 GSM8K problems LaMDA 137B answered correctly, all but two chains were logically and mathematically correct; of 50 it got wrong, 46% of chains were almost right with a minor error (a calculator mistake, a symbol-mapping error, one step missing) and 54% had major errors of understanding or coherence. That breakdown anticipates the later recipe: minor errors are what self-checking and voting fix, while major errors need a better base model.

What this means in production today

Few-shot chain-of-thought prompting is rarely the right tool with modern models: instruction-tuned models write reasoning when asked, and reasoning models do it whether asked or not. Prompting a reasoning model to "think step by step", or giving it long worked examples, often adds little and sometimes hurts, because it has its own trained way of thinking (rs7). What carries over is the engineering logic: decompose, make intermediate results explicit, and check them.

Characteristic failure modes

Latency surprises. A feature tested on short inputs ships, and on real inputs the model thinks for a minute. The symptom is a long tail in response time and timeouts at the client or load balancer. Watch percentiles of reasoning tokens per request, not averages, and set both an effort level and a maximum output cap.

Truncated thinking. If the maximum output tokens is smaller than what the model wants to think, the response can end mid-thought with no answer. APIs signal this with a stop reason such as a length limit; treat it as a failure, retry with a larger budget or fall back, and never parse a truncated chain as an answer.

Cost blow-ups. Reasoning tokens are billed as output, and hidden reasoning is still billed. A team that budgets by visible response length can underestimate cost by an order of magnitude. Log the reported reasoning-token count per request and alert on its distribution.

Overthinking easy traffic. Sending simple requests (classification, extraction, short answers) to a reasoning model at high effort wastes money and time for no accuracy. Route by difficulty or use the lowest effort setting that holds accuracy on an evaluation set.

Leaking the chain. Chains of thought can contain material you would not show users: restated system prompts, half-formed wrong ideas, sensitive data from context. Decide deliberately whether to display, summarise or hide the chain, and do not assume it is safe to show just because it is the model's own text.

What to measure

For any reasoning feature, keep four numbers per release on your own evaluation set: accuracy, mean and p95 reasoning tokens, mean and p95 latency, and cost per correct answer. Plot accuracy against thinking budget at least once; most tasks have a knee beyond which extra budget buys nothing, and that knee is where you set the default.

It makes three of the lesson's arguments concrete: errors compound along a chain, writing steps down helps because each step becomes easier, and parallel sampling plus a vote converts a mediocre per-sample accuracy into a much better one. It ends with the cost arithmetic from section 13.

  1. Save the script as rs1_lab.py and run python rs1_lab.py (tested with Python 3.12 and NumPy).
  2. Read part 1 against section 4: 0.95^10 should print 0.599. Notice how fast q = 0.90 collapses (0.122 at 20 steps).
  3. Part 2 is a toy model of direct answering versus a chain of thought: the same problem, but per-step accuracy 0.80 when the steps are done "in the head" and 0.97 when they are written down. At 10 steps the direct answer is right about 11% of the time and the chain about 73%. The gap grows with the number of steps, which is Wei et al.'s finding that chain of thought helps most on the hardest problems.
  4. Part 3 simulates majority voting with wrong answers spread over five values. With p = 0.6, five samples give about 0.92. With p = 0.3 voting still helps (about 0.53 at five samples) because the correct answer only needs a plurality when errors scatter. Change n_wrong to 1 to make all wrong answers agree: p = 0.3 then gets worse with more samples. (Ties go to the correct answer here, which flatters small even n slightly.)
  5. Part 4 reproduces the latency and cost numbers: about 107 seconds and $0.064 for the reasoning model against 5 seconds and $0.0008.
# rs1 lab: why steps help, and what test-time compute buys. Pure NumPy.
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 1) Error compounding: a chain of n steps, each right with prob q.
for q in (0.99, 0.95, 0.90):
    print(f"q={q}:", "  ".join(f"n={n}: {q**n:.3f}" for n in (1, 5, 10, 20)))

# 2) Toy "model": answers a k-step sum problem.
#    Direct answer: must do all k steps "in its head" -> per-step accuracy 0.80.
#    Chain of thought: writes each partial sum down -> per-step accuracy 0.97.
def solve(k, per_step, trials=20000):
    ok = rng.random((trials, k)) < per_step     # True where the step was right
    return ok.all(axis=1).mean()                # right only if every step was right

for k in (1, 3, 6, 10):
    print(f"k={k:2d} steps  direct={solve(k, 0.80):.3f}  cot={solve(k, 0.97):.3f}")

# 3) Parallel test-time compute: majority vote over n samples.
#    Each sample is right with prob p; wrong samples scatter over 5 wrong answers.
def majority(p, n, trials=20000, n_wrong=5):
    right = rng.random((trials, n)) < p
    wrong_ans = rng.integers(1, n_wrong + 1, size=(trials, n))
    ans = np.where(right, 0, wrong_ans)          # answer 0 is the correct one
    counts = np.stack([(ans == a).sum(1) for a in range(n_wrong + 1)], 1)
    winner = counts.argmax(1)                    # ties go to the lowest id (answer 0)
    return (winner == 0).mean()

for p in (0.3, 0.6):
    print(f"p={p}:", "  ".join(f"n={n}: {majority(p, n):.3f}" for n in (1, 5, 15, 45)))

# 4) Cost of thinking: output tokens, seconds and dollars per query.
for name, think, answer, tps, usd_per_m in [("fast", 0, 400, 80, 2.0),
                                            ("reasoning", 6000, 400, 60, 10.0)]:
    tok = think + answer
    print(f"{name:9s} {tok:5d} tokens  {tok/tps:6.1f} s  ${tok*usd_per_m/1e6:.4f}")

Expected output (first lines): q=0.95: n=1: 0.950 n=5: 0.774 n=10: 0.599 n=20: 0.358 and k=10 steps direct=0.108 cot=0.733.

Work it out

A reasoning model averages 6,000 thinking tokens and 400 answer tokens per query at 60 output tokens/s; output costs $10 per million tokens. A non-reasoning model answers in 400 tokens at 80 tokens/s for $2 per million. The reasoning model solves 82% of your task set, the fast model 55%. For 100,000 queries a day, compare latency and daily cost, and say when each is right.

Answer

Reasoning: 6,400 tokens / 60 ≈ 107 s per answer; 100,000 × 6,400 = 640M tokens × $10/M = $6,400/day; 82,000 solved → ≈ $0.078 per solved query.

Fast: 400 / 80 = 5 s; 40M tokens × $2/M = $80/day; 55,000 solved → ≈ $0.0015 per solved query.

The reasoning model costs 80× more per day for 27 more points of accuracy: $6,320 for 27,000 extra correct answers, about 23 cents each. It is right when a wrong answer is expensive and nobody is waiting (code review, analysis, batch jobs); wrong for interactive chat. The usual answer is a router: send easy queries to the fast model and hard ones (detected by a classifier, the fast model's disagreement across two samples, or a failed check) to the reasoning model. If 60% of traffic is easy and the fast model handles it at the reasoning model's accuracy, routing cuts the daily bill to about 0.6 × $80 + 0.4 × $6,400 ≈ $2,610 with little loss.

Estimate

Your model gets each step of a 12-step derivation right with probability 0.96. Estimate the success rate, then estimate it again if the model re-checks each step and catches 75% of its errors.

Answer

Without checking: 0.96^12 = e^(12 ln 0.96) = e^(12 × −0.0408) = e^(−0.490) ≈ 0.61. With checking, the per-step error falls from 0.04 to 0.04 × 0.25 = 0.01, so per-step accuracy is 0.99 and 0.99^12 ≈ e^(−0.121) ≈ 0.89. Checking costs tokens (perhaps 30-50% more), but turns a 61% solver into an 89% one: the same trade that reinforcement learning discovered on its own in R1-Zero.
