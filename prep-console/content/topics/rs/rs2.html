Suppose your model solves a hard maths problem 30% of the time. Ask it once and you are more likely to get a wrong answer than a right one. But that 30% also means that if you ask ten times, it will almost certainly produce the right answer at least once: the chance that all ten are wrong is 0.7^10, under 3%. The right answer is sitting somewhere in your pile of ten. The whole question is how to find it.

That question turns out to be the heart of test-time compute. If you can check answers exactly, as when generated code can be run against tests, you simply run them all and keep one that passes, and a model that solves 30% of problems in one try can solve over 90% with enough tries. If you cannot check exactly, you need a rule for choosing: take the answer that appears most often, ask a second model which answer looks best, or build the solution step by step and keep only the promising branches. Each rule has a characteristic curve: accuracy rises with the number of samples, then bends, flattens, or even falls. Knowing where and why it bends is what lets you spend compute well instead of burning it.

This matters in production because these techniques are available to you without training anything. A text-to-SQL feature can generate five candidate queries and execute them; a coding agent can write several patches and run the test suite; a classification pipeline can vote across samples to reduce flaky outputs. It matters in interviews because pass@k, majority voting and verifier reranking are the vocabulary in which every reasoning-model paper reports results, and misreading them (treating pass@100 as user-facing accuracy, say) is a classic mistake.

This lesson starts with what sampling gives you and defines pass@k, including the unbiased estimator from the Codex paper worked by hand. It then works through majority voting with exact probabilities, explains why voting plateaus, introduces verifiers (outcome and process reward models), compares best-of-N, majority vote and weighted voting on one worked example, shows why best-of-N against a learned verifier eventually gets worse, and covers search over reasoning steps, from beam search to Tree of Thoughts, and sequential revision. The public case is the self-consistency paper by Wang et al., the first clear demonstration that spending more samples at test time buys accuracy.

The mental model: a pile of drafts and a rule for choosing

Think of an exam where you are allowed to write the answer to a question many times on separate sheets, then hand in one sheet. Your sheets will not all agree, because each time you work the problem you take a slightly different path and sometimes slip. Three different strategies suggest themselves. You could hand in the answer you reached most often, trusting that slips are random and the truth is consistent. You could ask a friend who is good at checking to pick the best-looking sheet. Or, if the question came with a way to check the answer (a value you can substitute back in), you could check every sheet and hand in one that passes.

Those are exactly the parallel test-time methods. Sampling an LLM with temperature above zero (lm8) gives a different chain of thought, and often a different final answer, each time. Majority voting, called self-consistency in the reasoning literature, hands in the most common answer. Best-of-N asks a verifier, a model or program that scores answers, to pick the best sample. And when the answer can be checked exactly, you check them all; the accuracy you can reach that way is measured by pass@k, the chance that at least one of k samples is right.

There is a fourth strategy, closer to how a careful person works: instead of writing whole solutions and picking afterwards, check as you go. Write the first step several ways, keep the most promising ones, extend those, and prune again. That is search over reasoning steps, and it needs a verifier that can judge partial solutions, a process reward model.

Why the curves bend

Every method's accuracy rises with the number of samples at first, but for different reasons and to different limits. pass@k keeps rising towards 100% on any question the model can ever solve, because each new sample is another chance. Majority voting rises quickly and then flattens, because once the most common answer is settled more samples do not change it, and on questions where the model's most common answer is wrong, voting is wrong forever. Best-of-N with a learned verifier rises and can then fall: with enough samples, the highest-scored one is increasingly likely to be a wrong answer that happens to fool the verifier. These three shapes, rising, flattening and peaking, are the main thing to remember from this lesson, and the body shows where each comes from.

Where the analogy breaks

A student's repeated attempts are not independent: they make the same misconception every time. LLM samples are not independent either, which is exactly why voting plateaus. And unlike a student, a model can be asked for a thousand drafts in parallel for a predictable price, so the question is never "can I afford to try again" but "how many tries buy enough accuracy to be worth the tokens".

1. What sampling gives you

Fix one question. Each time you sample the model's answer with temperature above zero, it follows a different chain of thought and may reach a different final answer. Over many samples, each distinct answer appears with some frequency. Call the probability that a single sample is correct p, the per-sample accuracy for that question. It differs enormously between questions: on an easy one p may be 0.98, on a hard one 0.05, on an impossible one 0.

A benchmark's ordinary accuracy, called pass@1, is the average of p over its questions. Two models can have the same pass@1 with very different spreads of p, and the spread decides how much test-time compute helps. A model that gets every question right 50% of the time gains enormously from voting; a model that gets half the questions right always and the other half never gains nothing, because there is nothing to choose between.

All parallel methods share one cost structure: N samples cost N times the output tokens. They do not cost N times the latency, because the samples can be generated concurrently in one batch, and prefix caching (lm13) avoids re-processing the shared prompt N times. So parallel test-time compute is a money-for-accuracy trade at roughly constant latency.

2. pass@k: the ceiling

pass@k is the probability that at least one of k independent samples is correct. For one question with per-sample accuracy p, the chance all k are wrong is (1 − p)^k, so

pass@k = 1 − (1 − p)^k

By hand: with p = 0.3 and k = 5, (0.7)^5 = 0.168, so pass@5 = 0.832. With k = 10, 0.7^10 = 0.028 and pass@10 = 0.972. A question solved 30% of the time is solved by "at least one of ten" 97% of the time. Averaged over a benchmark's questions, pass@k is also called coverage.

pass@k is the accuracy a perfect selector would achieve, so it is the ceiling for every method in this lesson. It is directly achievable only when an exact checker exists. In the Codex paper (Chen et al., 2021), which introduced the HumanEval coding benchmark, the 12-billion-parameter Codex model solved 28.8% of problems with one sample and 70.2% with 100 samples, counting a problem as solved if any sample passed its unit tests. Without running the tests, choosing the sample with the highest mean token log-probability solved 44.5%; the fine-tuned Codex-S produced at least one correct function for 77.5% of problems.

3. Estimating pass@k without bias

You never know p; you estimate it from samples. The obvious approach, draw exactly k samples per question and record whether any is right, is very noisy. The Codex paper's estimator draws n ≥ k samples, counts c correct, and computes the probability that a random subset of k of those n samples contains at least one correct one:

pass@k ≈ 1 − C(n − c, k) / C(n, k)        C(n, k) = n! / (k! (n − k)!)

C(n, k), "n choose k", counts the ways to pick k items from n, so C(n − c, k)/C(n, k) is the probability that all k picked samples come from the n − c wrong ones. By hand, with n = 20 samples and c = 3 correct: for k = 1, C(17, 1)/C(20, 1) = 17/20, so pass@1 = 0.15, simply the fraction correct. For k = 5, C(17, 5) = 6,188 and C(20, 5) = 15,504, so pass@5 = 1 − 6,188/15,504 = 1 − 0.399 = 0.601.

Why not plug the estimate p̂ = c/n into 1 − (1 − p̂)^k? Because that function curves downwards (it is concave in p̂), and the average of a concave function of a noisy estimate is below the function of the true value (Jensen's inequality), so averaging it over questions systematically underestimates pass@k. The combinatorial form is exactly unbiased. In code, compute it as a running product to avoid huge factorials.

4. Majority voting, worked by hand

Majority voting samples N answers and returns the most common one. Consider first the simplest case, where there are only two possible answers, right and wrong (or all wrong samples agree). With N = 2m + 1 samples (odd, so no ties), the vote is right when more than half are right. The number of right samples follows a binomial distribution: the probability of exactly j right out of N is C(N, j) p^j (1 − p)^(N − j). So

P(majority right) = Σ_{j = m+1}^{N} C(N, j) · p^j · (1 − p)^(N − j)

By hand, p = 0.6, N = 3: j = 2 gives 3 × 0.36 × 0.4 = 0.432; j = 3 gives 0.216; total 0.648. N = 5: j = 3 gives 10 × 0.216 × 0.16 = 0.3456; j = 4 gives 5 × 0.1296 × 0.4 = 0.2592; j = 5 gives 0.0778; total 0.683. Voting slowly amplifies a majority: 0.60 → 0.648 → 0.683, approaching 1 as N grows. Now p = 0.4, N = 5: the same sum with p and 1 − p swapped gives 1 − 0.683 = 0.317. Voting amplifies whichever side has more than half, so a 40% question gets worse. This is the Condorcet jury theorem from 18th-century voting theory.

Real wrong answers rarely agree with each other. If a maths question's wrong answers scatter over many different numbers, the correct answer needs only a plurality, more votes than any single wrong answer, not a majority. With p = 0.3 and wrong answers spread evenly over five values (each with probability 0.14), the correct answer is the single most likely value, and voting drives accuracy upwards; the rs1 lab showed 0.30 becoming about 0.53 with five samples and 0.88 with 45. What matters is not whether p exceeds 0.5 but whether the correct answer is the mode, the most probable single answer.

5. Why voting plateaus

Put the two halves of section 4 together across a benchmark. As N grows, voting returns each question's mode with near certainty. So the accuracy of voting converges to the fraction of questions whose mode is correct, and stops there. Questions where the model has a consistent misconception, where one wrong answer is more likely than the right one, are wrong forever, however many samples you draw.

A worked mixture: a benchmark has two kinds of question in equal numbers. Kind A has p = 0.8. Kind B has p = 0.3 with one attractive wrong answer at 0.7. Then pass@1 = (0.8 + 0.3)/2 = 0.55. Voting with many samples gets every A right and every B wrong: 0.50, lower than pass@1. pass@k with large k gets everything: close to 1.0. The 50-point gap between voting and coverage is entirely a selection problem: the model produces the correct answer to every B question 30% of the time, but the vote cannot see it. Brown et al.'s "Large Language Monkeys" study measured this gap at scale: on the MATH benchmark, Llama-3-8B-Instruct's coverage rose from 82.9% with 100 samples to 98.44% with 10,000, while majority voting and reward-model selection rose only from 40.50% to 41.41% over the same range.

6. Verifiers: outcome and process

A verifier judges candidate answers. The best verifiers are exact: unit tests or execution for code, comparison with a known answer, a symbolic algebra check, a proof checker such as Lean. Where they exist they are cheap, reliable and essentially impossible to fool, which is why code and maths have led reasoning progress.

Where they do not exist, you train one. An outcome reward model (ORM) takes a question and a complete solution and outputs a score, usually trained to predict whether the final answer is correct. Cobbe et al. introduced this for GSM8K in 2021: sample many solutions from a fine-tuned model, label each by whether its final answer matched, train a verifier on those labels, and at test time pick the highest-scoring sample. A process reward model (PRM) scores each step. Lightman et al.'s "Let's Verify Step by Step" (OpenAI, 2023) had humans label individual steps of model-written solutions to MATH problems as positive, negative or neutral, producing PRM800K, 800,000 step-level labels across 75,000 solutions to 12,000 problems. Their PRM scores a whole solution as the probability that every step is correct, the product of per-step probabilities. On a 500-problem subset of the MATH test set, picking the best of 1,860 samples per problem, the PRM solved 78.2%, an ORM 72.4%, and majority voting 69.6%. rs5 covers how such reward models are trained and how they fail.

A third kind is the generative verifier: ask an LLM, possibly a reasoning model, to check the solution and output a verdict. It is flexible and needs no training data, but it is slow, costs tokens per sample, and shares blind spots with the model that wrote the solution.

7. Best-of-N, majority vote and weighted voting on one example

With a verifier you have three ways to choose, and they can disagree. A worked example: eight samples for one question whose correct answer is 42.

Majority vote counts answers: 36 three times, 42 twice, 48 twice, 17 once. It returns 36, which is wrong: the model has a consistent slip that leads to 36. Best-of-N returns the single highest score: sample 2, answer 42, right. Weighted voting sums scores per distinct answer: 36 gets 0.55 + 0.40 + 0.47 = 1.42; 42 gets 0.81 + 0.77 = 1.58; 48 gets 0.97; 17 gets 0.20. It returns 42, right.

Now add a ninth sample: answer 7, with a verifier score of 0.93, because it is written in a confident, well-formatted style the verifier over-rates. Best-of-N switches to 7 and is wrong. Weighted voting still returns 42 (1.58 against 0.93) because a single outlier cannot outweigh two agreeing, well-scored samples. This is why weighted voting, also called verifier-weighted majority, is often the most robust default when you have a decent but imperfect verifier: an answer needs both agreement and approval. Best-of-N is the right choice when the verifier is very reliable and the correct answer may be rare among samples, as with execution tests.

8. Why best-of-N eventually gets worse

Choosing the maximum-scoring sample is a form of optimisation against the verifier. A learned verifier has errors, and its errors are largest on unusual outputs it saw little of in training. With few samples, the maximum is most likely a genuinely good answer. With many samples, there are more chances for an unusual wrong answer to receive an inflated score, and the maximum increasingly lands on one of those. True accuracy rises, peaks and then falls: reward over-optimisation, an instance of Goodhart's law ("when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"). The lab reproduces the effect: with wrong answers scored from a heavy-tailed distribution, best-of-n accuracy peaks around n = 16 at 67% and falls to 15% at n = 1,024.

Gao, Schulman and Hilton measured this with a "gold" reward model standing in for human judgement and smaller proxy reward models doing the selection. They needed a common measure of optimisation pressure for best-of-n and for RL, and used the KL divergence of the resulting output distribution from the original policy. For best-of-n it has a closed form:

KL_bon = log n − (n − 1)/n   nats

By hand: n = 4 gives ln 4 − 0.75 = 1.386 − 0.75 = 0.64 nats; n = 16 gives 2.773 − 0.938 = 1.84; n = 256 gives 5.545 − 0.996 = 4.55. KL grows only with log n, so each doubling of samples adds the same optimisation pressure. They found the gold reward under best-of-n follows R(d) = d(α − βd) where d = √KL, a curve that rises, peaks at d = α/(2β) and falls, with α and β depending on the proxy reward model's size: larger reward models over-optimise later.

9. Search over reasoning steps

Best-of-N wastes compute on samples that went wrong at step 2 and kept going for twenty more steps. Search spends compute on promising partial solutions instead. The simplest form is beam search over steps with a PRM: at each step, each of the b partial solutions in the "beam" is extended with several candidate next steps sampled from the model; the PRM scores every extended partial solution; the top b are kept; repeat until solutions finish. Branches that make a mistake are pruned as soon as the PRM notices.

Tree of Thoughts (Yao et al., 2023) generalised this with the model itself acting as the evaluator. For the Game of 24 puzzle (combine four numbers with +, −, ×, ÷ to make 24), each "thought" is one intermediate equation; the model proposes candidate thoughts, rates each remaining state as "sure", "maybe" or "impossible" to reach 24, and a breadth-first search keeps the best b = 5 at each of three levels. GPT-4 with plain chain-of-thought prompting solved 4% of the puzzles, self-consistency over 100 chains 9%, and Tree of Thoughts 74% (45% with a beam of b = 1). The task is ideal for search: few steps, many dead ends, and states easy to judge.

The heavier form is Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS), as used in game-playing systems: grow a tree by repeatedly choosing promising branches, rolling out to the end, and backing up the results. It has been applied to LLM reasoning, but DeepSeek reported that their MCTS attempts did not scale for R1: the space of possible next steps in language is vast and a value model good enough to guide it is hard to train.

10. Sequential revision

The serial alternative to sampling is revision: show the model its own answer and ask it to find mistakes and improve it, possibly several rounds. Its advantage is that each round builds on the previous one; its risk is that models without training for it often "correct" right answers into wrong ones or rubber-stamp wrong ones. Snell et al. trained models specifically for revision and found that on easier questions, sequential revisions beat parallel sampling at the same compute, while on harder ones a mix of parallel and sequential worked best. Reasoning models can be seen as having revision built into one chain: they check and revise inside their own thinking, which is why prompting them for an explicit second round adds less than it used to.

11. Choosing a method and a budget

The choice follows from two questions: can you check answers exactly, and how hard are the questions? With an exact checker, sample in parallel, check, return the first pass, and stop early when one passes, so easy questions cost one sample. Without one, majority voting is a cheap, robust improvement when answers are short and comparable (numbers, labels, choices), and weighted voting with a verifier adds more when you have a decent verifier. For long free-form outputs, voting is awkward (no two essays are identical), so best-of-N with a judge is the usual tool, with N kept small and tuned on held-out data because of over-optimisation. Compute-optimal scaling (Snell et al.) means choosing per question: when a few cheap samples agree unanimously, stop; when they disagree, spend more.

The cost arithmetic is straightforward. Eight samples of 1,000 output tokens is 8,000 tokens per question, eight times a single sample, with latency close to one sample when run concurrently. Early stopping changes the average a lot: if 70% of questions get two agreeing samples and stop, and 30% go to eight, the mean is 0.7 × 2 + 0.3 × 8 = 3.8 samples, under half the fixed-eight cost.

Self-consistency as marginalisation

rs1 wrote the probability of an answer as a sum over chains: P(a | x) = Σ_z P(z | x) P(a | x, z). Greedy decoding picks the single most likely chain and its answer, which is not the same as the most likely answer: many medium-probability chains can all lead to 42 while the single best chain leads to 36. Majority voting estimates the answer with the largest total probability by sampling chains and counting, a Monte Carlo approximation of the marginal. This explains a finding in Wang et al.'s ablations: weighting each sample by its chain's length-normalised probability performs almost identically to plain voting, because sampling already weights chains by their probability. Weighting by the raw unnormalised probability of a chain, by contrast, did much worse, since long chains get vanishingly small raw probabilities.

Why sampled chains are not independent

The binomial formulas assume independent samples, which is a good approximation within one question but hides the structure that matters: the per-question accuracy p varies, and errors are correlated within a question. That is why benchmark-level voting accuracy is not the binomial formula applied to the average p. With an average p of 0.55 the binomial formula predicts voting approaches 100%; the mixture in the body shows it can approach 50%. Always reason per question, then average.

The functional form of coverage

Brown et al. found coverage across many tasks grows roughly log-linearly over several orders of magnitude of samples, and fitted it well with an exponentiated power law: coverage(k) ≈ exp(a · k^b) with b negative. The mechanism is the spread of per-question difficulty. Easy questions are covered by the first few samples; each further factor of ten in samples covers questions with roughly ten times smaller per-sample accuracy. As long as the benchmark contains questions with p spread across many orders of magnitude, every factor of ten buys a similar increment, which is why the curve looks straight on a log axis. On SWE-bench Lite, a benchmark of real GitHub issues, DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct solved 15.9% with one sample and 56% with 250, beating the then single-attempt state of the art of 43%, because the repository's tests acted as a checker.

Over-optimisation, more carefully

Why do verifier errors get worse in the tail? A verifier is trained on samples from some distribution, typically the policy's ordinary outputs. Best-of-N with large N selects the extreme of the verifier's score, and extremes are disproportionately made of samples where the verifier's error happens to be large and positive. If the verifier's errors on wrong answers have heavier tails than its scores on right ones (wrong answers are more varied, so some will be strange in ways the verifier mis-rates), the maximum eventually comes from the wrong-answer tail, as in the lab. Three mitigations follow from the mechanism: make errors smaller (a larger or better-trained verifier, which Gao et al. found over-optimises later), make them less correlated with the selection (ensembles of verifiers, or combining score with vote count as weighted voting does), and limit the optimisation pressure (choose N on held-out data; KL grows with log N, so the safe range is often small).

PRM-guided search over-optimises faster

Search applies the verifier at every step, so it puts more optimisation pressure on it per unit of compute than best-of-N does. Snell et al. observed exactly this: beam search against a PRM outperformed best-of-N on hard questions at small budgets, but at large budgets and on easy questions it did worse, producing solutions that the PRM liked but that were wrong, for example with low-information or repetitive steps that score as "not wrong". The practical rule is that search pays when steps are easy to judge locally and the budget is small; best-of-N or voting is safer when the verifier is the weak link.

Exact checkers are not always exact

Unit tests can be weak: a sample can pass the tests and still be wrong, and with enough samples you will find one that does, which is over-optimisation against the tests. In the Codex paper's setting the tests were hidden from the model and fairly thorough. In agentic coding systems, models that can see and edit tests sometimes make them pass by changing the tests rather than the code (rs5 discusses published examples). Maths answers can match a reference for the wrong reason (a lucky guess on a multiple-choice question). Treat a checker's strength as a parameter: the more samples you draw, the more any weakness in it will be found.

Parallel versus serial at matched compute

Matching compute is subtle. N samples of length L and one chain of length N × L use the same output tokens, but the long chain also attends over a much longer context, so its attention cost grows faster; and the parallel samples share one prompt via prefix caching. In latency terms they are not comparable at all: parallel samples finish in about the time of one; the serial chain takes N times longer. Published comparisons usually match tokens or FLOPs, and you must re-do the comparison under your own latency budget. A reasoning model at medium effort plus a vote over three samples is a common practical compromise.

When pass@k misleads as a research metric

Because pass@k with large k rewards diversity, a model can improve pass@100 while getting worse at pass@1, and vice versa. Reinforcement learning on reasoning typically raises pass@1 and can lower pass@k at very large k (rs1's discussion of Yue et al.), while higher sampling temperature raises pass@k and lowers pass@1. Always report the metric that matches how the model will be used, and report the number of samples and temperature alongside any maj@k or pass@k (rs8).

The public case: self-consistency (Wang et al., Google, 2022)

"Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning in Language Models", by Xuezhi Wang, Jason Wei and colleagues at Google Research, appeared on arXiv in March 2022, two months after the chain-of-thought paper, and was published at ICLR 2023. The idea is the one in this lesson: replace the greedy chain of thought with many sampled chains and return the most common final answer. The authors framed it as marginalising out the reasoning path, and motivated it with the observation that a complex problem usually admits several valid ways of thinking that lead to the same answer, while mistakes lead to different answers.

The method used the same few-shot chain-of-thought prompts as Wei et al. and sampled 40 outputs per question. For PaLM 540B they sampled with temperature 0.7 and top-k truncation at 40 tokens; for GPT-3 with temperature 0.7 and no truncation. Results were averaged over 10 runs. On GSM8K, PaLM 540B went from 56.5% with the greedy chain to 74.4% with self-consistency, a gain of 17.9 points; the abstract also reports gains of 11.0 points on SVAMP, 12.2 on AQuA (algebra word problems with multiple-choice answers), 6.4 on StrategyQA and 3.9 on ARC-challenge. code-davinci-002 showed the same GSM8K gain, from 60.1% to 78.0%. (Those are the paper's headline figures; per-model tables are in its appendix.)

Two of the paper's ablations matter for engineering. First, aggregation: they compared plain majority vote with weighting each answer by its chain's probability. Weighting by length-normalised probability gave essentially the same accuracy as plain voting (74.1% against 74.4% on GSM8K), while unnormalised probability weighting was far worse (59.9%), so plain voting is the robust default. Second, robustness: accuracy was similar across a range of temperatures and top-k settings, and improved with the number of sampled paths, with diminishing gains as the count grew towards 40. The method also helped on tasks where the chain of thought itself hurt a smaller model, and it beat alternatives that spend similar compute differently, such as beam search over whole outputs and ensembles of different prompts.

Self-consistency's limits are the ones this lesson predicts. It needs final answers that can be compared for equality, so it does not apply directly to free-form text. Its gains flatten once the vote is stable. And it multiplies cost by the number of samples, 40 times in the paper's setting, a price that makes sense in a benchmark and needs justifying in a product.

Using these techniques in real systems

The most valuable production pattern is sample-and-check wherever a cheap checker exists: generate several candidate SQL queries and execute them against a read-only replica; generate several code patches and run the tests; generate structured outputs and validate them against a schema and business rules. Return the first that passes, and stop sampling once one does. For structured extraction, voting on each field across three samples is a simple way to reduce flaky outputs, and disagreement between samples is a good signal to route to a stronger model or a human.

Failure modes

Answer normalisation. Voting needs equal answers to compare equal. "42", "42.0", "$42" and "forty-two" are one answer; two SQL queries can differ in text and agree in results. Normalise before voting (parse numbers, compare execution results), or the vote splits and accuracy drops for no visible reason.

Correlated samples. At low temperature, samples are near-identical and voting adds cost without information. At high temperature, quality per sample falls. Check that samples actually differ, and tune temperature with voting turned on, not off.

Verifier drift. A learned verifier tuned on last quarter's model outputs may score a new model's outputs differently; over-optimisation shows up as the verifier's average chosen score rising while user-visible quality is flat. Track the agreement between verifier picks and spot-checked human or exact labels.

Cost surprises. N-fold sampling multiplies output tokens. Make N adaptive (stop on agreement or on a passing check) and log samples per request.

Weak tests. When an agent's patches are selected by tests, the tests are now the target. Keep some held-out tests, review samples that pass with unusual diffs, and do not let the generator edit the tests it is judged by.

What to measure

Log per request the number of samples drawn, how many agreed with the returned answer (the vote margin), the verifier score of the pick, and whether the check passed. Offline, plot accuracy against N for each selector on your evaluation set, find where each curve bends, and set N just past the knee. Vote margin is a free confidence score: low-margin answers are the ones to audit or escalate.

print("n=20 c=3: pass@1=%.3f pass@5=%.3f" % (pass_at_k(20, 3, 1), pass_at_k(20, 3, 5)))

Q = 2000
p = rng.beta(0.7, 0.7, Q)                       # some easy, some hard questions
consistent = rng.random(Q) < 0.4                # 40% of questions have one "attractive" wrong answer

def sample_answers(n):
    right = rng.random((Q, n)) < p[:, None]
    scattered = rng.integers(1, 50, (Q, n))     # 49 possible wrong values
    wrong = np.where(consistent[:, None], 1, scattered)
    return np.where(right, 0, wrong), right      # answer 0 = correct

def majority(ans):
    acc = []
    for row in ans:
        vals, cnt = np.unique(row, return_counts=True)
        acc.append(vals[cnt.argmax()] == 0)     # np.unique sorts, so ties favour answer 0
    return np.mean(acc)

def verifier_pick(right):
    # Learned verifier: correct samples score N(1.5, 1); wrong ones get heavy-tailed scores,
    # so with many samples some wrong answer eventually "fools" it.
    s = np.where(right, rng.normal(1.5, 1.0, right.shape), rng.standard_t(3, right.shape))
    return right[np.arange(Q), s.argmax(1)].mean()

print(" n   pass@n  maj@n  verifier-best-of-n")
for n in (1, 4, 16, 64, 256, 1024):
    ans, right = sample_answers(n)
    print(f"{n:3d}   {right.any(1).mean():.3f}   {majority(ans):.3f}   {verifier_pick(right):.3f}")

# 3) KL of best-of-n from the base policy (Stiennon et al.; Gao et al.): log n - (n-1)/n nats.
for n in (4, 16, 64, 256):
    print(f"best-of-{n}: KL = {np.log(n) - (n - 1) / n:.2f} nats")

Work it out

You sample n = 20 solutions to a problem and 3 pass the tests. Estimate pass@1 and pass@5 with the unbiased estimator. Then: over a benchmark, majority vote at N = 64 scores 62% while pass@64 is 88%. What does the gap tell you, and what would close it?

Answer

pass@1 = 1 − C(17, 1)/C(20, 1) = 1 − 17/20 = 0.15. pass@5 = 1 − C(17, 5)/C(20, 5) = 1 − 6,188/15,504 = 0.60.

The 26-point gap is the selection problem: on those problems the model can produce a correct answer within 64 tries but the correct answer is not the most common one, because its errors are consistent. More samples will not close it. A better selector will: unit tests or execution for code, a symbolic checker for maths, or a trained (process) reward model with weighted voting, accepting that a learned verifier eventually gets exploited as N grows, so tune N on held-out data.

Design

A support bot classifies tickets into 12 categories with 88% accuracy at one sample. Sampling three times costs 3× tokens. When is voting worth it, and how would you make it cheaper?

Answer

Voting helps on tickets where the model is uncertain (per-sample accuracy well below 1 but with the right category as the mode); it does nothing for tickets it gets consistently wrong. Measure on labelled data: compute accuracy for maj@3 and the fraction of tickets with a split vote. Make it cheaper with adaptive sampling: draw two samples; if they agree (most tickets), return immediately; if they disagree, draw a third or escalate. If 85% of tickets agree on two samples, the average is 0.85 × 2 + 0.15 × 3 = 2.15 samples, and the disagreements double as a queue of uncertain tickets for human review.
