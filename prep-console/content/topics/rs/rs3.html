Suppose you have a model that solves 40% of a set of maths problems, and a program that can check any answer. You would like the model to solve more. Supervised fine-tuning needs correct worked solutions to imitate, and you do not have them for most problems; you only have the checker. What you can do is let the model attempt each problem many times, see which attempts the checker accepts, and somehow make the model more likely to produce attempts like those. That "somehow" is reinforcement learning, and making it work on a model with billions of parameters, generating thousands of tokens per attempt, is one of the central engineering problems behind modern LLMs.

The same machinery runs RLHF, where the "checker" is a reward model trained on human preferences (lm6), and it is what turned GPT-3 into InstructGPT and ChatGPT. It runs the reasoning training of o1 and DeepSeek-R1 (rs1), where the reward is answer correctness. The algorithm that dominated this work from 2017 to 2024 is Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), and its successors for reasoning (GRPO, next topic) are small modifications of it. You cannot understand those modifications, or read an RL paper's loss function, without the foundations: what a policy gradient is, why it is noisy, what baselines, advantages and value functions are for, and what PPO's clipping and the KL penalty actually do.

These are also among the most asked interview topics for AI engineers working near post-training. Interviewers like to ask you to derive the policy gradient, to explain why a baseline does not bias it, to compute PPO's clipped objective for a given ratio and advantage, and to explain what the four models in an RLHF loop are for and what they cost. Each of those is a short calculation once the ideas are in place, and an embarrassing silence if they are not.

This lesson builds the ideas in order. It maps the RL vocabulary onto text generation, states the objective and shows why you cannot simply backpropagate through it, derives the policy-gradient estimator, and computes one REINFORCE update by hand with and without a baseline. It then introduces per-token credit assignment with value functions and generalised advantage estimation (GAE), the importance ratio that lets you reuse a batch, PPO's clipped objective worked token by token, and the KL penalty that keeps the policy near its reference. It ends with the full RLHF loop, its memory and time budget, and the original PPO paper as the public case. The lab trains a toy policy with and without a baseline and reproduces every hand calculation.

The mental model: practice with a scorekeeper

Imagine learning darts with your eyes closed. After each throw, someone tells you only your score. You cannot see where the dart went or why; you only know that this throw scored 40 and that one 5. A sensible strategy is: whatever you did on throws that scored better than your usual, do a little more of; whatever you did on throws that scored worse than usual, do a little less of. Repeat for thousands of throws and your aim improves, although no one ever showed you the correct technique.

Three features of that strategy are exactly the ideas of this lesson. First, you adjust towards actions that scored well, in proportion to how well: that is the policy gradient. Second, "better than your usual" matters, not raw score: if every throw scores between 38 and 42, getting 40 tells you nothing, and treating it as good would make you reinforce everything you did. Comparing against your usual is the baseline, and the difference is the advantage. Third, you should adjust "a little": a single lucky throw should not overhaul your whole technique. Limiting how far one round of feedback moves you is what PPO's clipping does, and staying close to the technique you started with, so you do not develop some bizarre throw that happens to fool the scorekeeper, is the KL penalty.

The vocabulary, translated

Reinforcement learning (RL) describes an agent that observes a state, chooses an action, and receives a reward; a sequence of states and actions from start to finish is an episode or trajectory. The agent's decision rule, a probability distribution over actions for each state, is its policy, written π. For an LLM the translation is direct. The policy is the model itself, π_θ, with parameters θ. The state is the prompt plus the tokens generated so far. The action is the next token. The episode is one complete response. The environment is almost trivial: appending the chosen token gives the next state, with no randomness. And the reward is almost always a single number at the end of the response: a reward model's score in RLHF, or 1 if the checker accepts the final answer and 0 otherwise in reasoning training.

The plan of attack

The goal is to raise the average reward of the model's responses. The obstacle is that the reward is a black box applied to sampled text, so ordinary backpropagation does not reach it. The workaround is to raise the log-probability of each sampled response in proportion to how much better than expected it scored. Everything else in this lesson, baselines, value functions, importance ratios, clipping and KL penalties, exists to make that simple idea less noisy, more sample-efficient and safer.

Where the analogy breaks

A dart thrower adjusts a handful of muscles; an LLM adjusts billions of parameters shared across every token and every prompt, so making one response more likely also changes the probabilities of countless others. That sharing is why RL on LLMs generalises from training problems to new ones, and also why it can produce surprising side effects, such as a model that learns to write longer answers everywhere because longer answers scored better on some prompts.

1. The objective, and why you cannot backpropagate through it

For a prompt x drawn from a training set, the policy samples a response y ~ π_θ(· | x) and receives reward R(x, y). The objective is the expected reward:

J(θ) = E_{x ~ D, y ~ π_θ(·|x)} [ R(x, y) ]

We want ∇_θ J, the direction in parameter space that increases it. In supervised learning the loss is a differentiable function of the model's output, so backpropagation computes the gradient. Here two things block it. The response is produced by sampling discrete tokens, and "which token was drawn" has no derivative with respect to θ. And the reward often comes from something that is not differentiable at all: a unit test, an exact-match checker, or a separate network applied to discrete text. You cannot pass gradients through either.

2. The log-derivative trick and the policy gradient

The way around is to move the gradient off the reward and onto the probabilities, which the model can differentiate. Write the expectation as a sum over responses (for one prompt): J = Σ_y π_θ(y) R(y). The reward does not depend on θ; only the probabilities do. So

∇J = Σ_y ∇π_θ(y) · R(y)
   = Σ_y π_θ(y) · ∇log π_θ(y) · R(y)        using ∇π = π · ∇log π
   = E_{y ~ π_θ} [ R(y) · ∇log π_θ(y) ]

The middle line uses the log-derivative trick: the derivative of log π is ∇π / π, so ∇π = π∇log π. The result is an expectation under the policy itself, which means it can be estimated by sampling: draw responses, compute the gradient of each one's log-probability (exactly what backpropagation gives you, as in supervised training), and weight it by the reward. Responses with high reward get their log-probability pushed up harder. This is the policy gradient, and its simplest sample-based estimate is the REINFORCE algorithm (Williams, 1992).

For a response of T tokens, the log-probability is a sum, log π(y | x) = Σ_t log π(a_t | s_t), so its gradient is the sum of the per-token gradients. In code, the REINFORCE "loss" is −R · Σ_t log π(a_t | s_t), averaged over samples: it looks like cross-entropy on the sampled tokens, scaled by the reward. Minimising it with an optimiser performs gradient ascent on J.

3. One REINFORCE step by hand

Strip the problem to its bones: a policy that chooses one of four responses, with a softmax over four logits z (lm8). For a softmax, the gradient of log π(a) with respect to the logits is the one-hot vector of a minus the probability vector: onehot(a) − p. (This is the same "probabilities minus target" gradient as cross-entropy, ml5, with the sign flipped.)

Start with z = [0, 0, 0, 0], so p = [0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25]. Sample four responses: 2, 0, 2, 1, and a checker gives rewards 1, 0, 1, 0. The per-sample gradient of log π for response 2 is [−0.25, −0.25, +0.75, −0.25]; for response 0 it is [+0.75, −0.25, −0.25, −0.25]; for response 1 it is [−0.25, +0.75, −0.25, −0.25]. Weighting by reward (only the two samples of response 2 count) and averaging over four samples: 2 × [−0.25, −0.25, 0.75, −0.25] / 4 = [−0.125, −0.125, +0.375, −0.125]. With a learning rate of 1, the new logits are those numbers, and the new probabilities are proportional to e^z = [0.882, 0.882, 1.455, 0.882], giving p ≈ [0.21, 0.21, 0.36, 0.21]. Response 2, the one that was rewarded, became more likely.

4. Why the raw estimate is noisy, and baselines

Now change only one thing: suppose the rewards come from a reward model whose scores sit around 5, so the four samples score 6, 5, 6, 5 instead of 1, 0, 1, 0. Which response is better has not changed. But the estimate becomes Σ R_i (onehot(a_i) − p) / 4 = [−0.125, −0.125, +1.625, −1.375]. Response 3, which was never sampled, is pushed down hard, simply because every sampled response received a positive reward and the softmax had to take probability from somewhere. In expectation these effects cancel; in any one batch they are pure noise, and the noise grows with the size of the offset.

The fix is to subtract a baseline b from the reward:

∇J = E[ (R(y) − b) · ∇log π_θ(y) ]

This is still exactly right in expectation, as long as b does not depend on which response was sampled, because the expected gradient of the log-probability is zero: Σ_y π ∇log π = Σ_y ∇π = ∇ Σ_y π = ∇1 = 0. So subtracting b × (something whose average is zero) changes nothing on average, but removes the shared part of the reward from every sample. The quantity R − b is called the advantage: how much better this response did than expected.

By hand, with a baseline: use the mean reward of the four samples, b = 0.5 (or 5.5 with the offset; it does not matter). The advantages are +0.5, −0.5, +0.5, −0.5. Now every sample contributes: response 2 (twice) gets +0.5 × [−0.25, −0.25, 0.75, −0.25]; response 0 gets −0.5 × [0.75, −0.25, −0.25, −0.25]; response 1 gets −0.5 × [−0.25, 0.75, −0.25, −0.25]. Summing and dividing by 4 gives [−0.125, −0.125, +0.25, 0]. Response 2 goes up, the two responses that failed go down, and the unsampled response 3 is left alone, whatever the offset. In the lab, with rewards offset by 5, the variance of the gradient estimate is about 2.7 without a baseline and 0.03 with one, and a policy trained for 300 steps reaches 0.84 probability on the best response with a baseline against 0.56 on average without, with some runs collapsing onto a bad response.

A baseline built from the other samples of the same prompt is especially good for LLMs, because prompts differ enormously in difficulty: on an easy prompt every sample scores high, on a hard one every sample scores low, and only the comparison within a prompt says which response was better. RLOO (REINFORCE leave-one-out) uses, for each sample, the mean reward of the other k − 1 samples of the same prompt; GRPO (rs4) uses the group mean and also divides by the group's standard deviation.

5. Credit assignment: one reward, many tokens

REINFORCE gives every token in a response the same advantage. If a 500-token solution is correct, every token gets pushed up, including the ones that were irrelevant or slightly wrong; if it fails, every token gets pushed down, including a perfect first 400 tokens. Over many samples this averages out, because tokens that genuinely help appear more often in rewarded responses, but it makes learning slow and noisy. This is the credit-assignment problem.

The classical RL answer is a value function V(s), a learned prediction of the expected final reward from a state. For an LLM it is usually a copy of the model (or of the reward model) with a scalar output head, predicting from each prefix how good the eventual response will be. With it you can ask, token by token, whether a choice made the outlook better or worse. The temporal-difference (TD) error measures exactly that:

δ_t = r_t + γ · V(s_{t+1}) − V(s_t)

where r_t is the reward received at step t (zero except at the end, apart from KL terms), and γ is the discount factor, which down-weights distant rewards; LLM training almost always uses γ = 1. A positive δ_t means the token moved the predicted outcome up.

6. Generalised advantage estimation, worked

One TD error is low-variance but trusts the value function completely; the full reward-minus-value is unbiased but noisy. Generalised Advantage Estimation (GAE; Schulman et al., 2015) blends them with a parameter λ between 0 and 1:

A_t = δ_t + (γλ) δ_{t+1} + (γλ)² δ_{t+2} + …     computed backwards: A_t = δ_t + γλ A_{t+1}

By hand: a three-token response, γ = 1, value predictions V = [0.5, 0.6, 0.8] for the three prefixes, value 0 after the end, and reward 1 at the last token. The TD errors are δ_0 = 0 + 0.6 − 0.5 = 0.1, δ_1 = 0 + 0.8 − 0.6 = 0.2, δ_2 = 1 + 0 − 0.8 = 0.2. With λ = 1, A = [0.5, 0.4, 0.2]: each is simply the final reward minus that prefix's value. With λ = 0, A = [0.1, 0.2, 0.2]: each token is credited only with the jump in prediction it caused. With λ = 0.95, working backwards: A_2 = 0.2, A_1 = 0.2 + 0.95 × 0.2 = 0.39, A_0 = 0.1 + 0.95 × 0.39 = 0.4705. Small λ trusts the critic and lowers variance at the cost of bias when the critic is wrong; λ near 1 does the opposite. PPO for LLMs typically uses λ = 0.95 or 1.

The value model is trained alongside the policy by regression: minimise (V(s_t) − target_t)², where the target is the advantage plus the old value estimate (the "return"). It is a whole second LLM, which is the main cost that later methods remove.

7. Reusing a batch: the importance ratio

Sampling responses is by far the slowest part of RL on LLMs, so it is wasteful to take one gradient step and throw the batch away. But the policy-gradient formula is an expectation under the current policy. After one step the policy has changed, and the batch was sampled by the old one, π_old. The standard correction is importance sampling: reweight each sample by how much more or less likely the current policy makes it. For a token, the importance ratio is

r_t(θ) = π_θ(a_t | s_t) / π_old(a_t | s_t) = exp( log π_θ(a_t | s_t) − log π_old(a_t | s_t) )

and the "surrogate" objective L = E[r_t(θ) · A_t] has the same gradient as the policy gradient at θ = θ_old, while remaining usable for a few steps afterwards. By hand: if the sampler gave a token log-probability −1.50 and after some updates the current policy gives −1.20, the ratio is e^0.30 = 1.35: the token is now 35% more likely than when it was sampled. The trouble is that nothing stops r from becoming enormous; maximising r·A with a positive advantage drives the token's probability up without limit, and the old samples quickly stop being representative. Without a limit, multiple epochs on one batch produce destructively large updates.

8. PPO's clipped objective, token by token

TRPO (Trust Region Policy Optimization), PPO's predecessor, enforced a hard limit on the average KL divergence between π_old and π_θ, using second-order optimisation that is complicated and expensive. PPO replaces the constraint with a simple modification of the objective:

L^CLIP(θ) = E_t[ min( r_t(θ) · A_t ,  clip(r_t(θ), 1 − ε, 1 + ε) · A_t ) ]       ε ≈ 0.2

Read it one token at a time. The second term is the surrogate with the ratio forced into [1 − ε, 1 + ε]. Taking the minimum of the two makes the objective pessimistic: it gives the policy credit for a change only up to the clip boundary, and gives it full blame for changes in the wrong direction. Four cases with ε = 0.2:

AdvantageRatior·Aclip(r)·AminGradient
+21.102.202.202.20flows: keep raising this good token
+21.352.702.402.40zero: already raised 35%, stop
+20.701.401.601.40flows: moved the wrong way, correct it

When the minimum picks the clipped term, that term is a constant in θ (the ratio was replaced by the fixed number 1.2 or 0.8), so its gradient is zero and the token stops contributing. The effect is a soft trust region: within one batch, each token can be pushed at most about ε in the direction its advantage favours, and the optimiser's effort moves to tokens that have not yet moved. The fraction of tokens that end up clipped, the clip fraction, is a standard health metric; if it is large, steps are too big.

9. The KL leash to a reference model

The clip limits how far one batch moves the policy from π_old. RLHF adds a second, different limit: how far the whole training run moves the policy from a frozen reference model π_ref, normally the SFT model it started from. The reward model was trained on responses near the SFT model's distribution; far from it, its scores are unreliable, and an unconstrained policy finds outputs it over-rates (reward hacking, lm6 and rs5). The standard implementation subtracts a per-token penalty from the reward:

r_t = −β · ( log π_θ(a_t | s_t) − log π_ref(a_t | s_t) )      plus the reward model's score at the final token

Summed over a response, Σ_t (log π − log π_ref) is a single-sample estimate of the KL divergence between the policy and the reference over responses. By hand: a token the policy now gives probability 0.5 and the reference 0.4 has log-ratio ln(0.5/0.4) = 0.223; with InstructGPT's β = 0.02 the token's penalty is 0.0045. Small per token, but a 500-token response whose tokens average a log-ratio of 0.05 accumulates a KL of 25 nats and a penalty of 0.5, comparable to the reward-model score differences being optimised. β sets the exchange rate between reward and drift.

Two practical variants are worth knowing. The KL can be put into the loss rather than the reward (GRPO does this, rs4), and the per-token estimator can be changed: log π − log π_ref is unbiased but can be negative and noisy, so some implementations use Schulman's "k3" estimator, (ρ − 1) − log ρ with ρ = π_ref/π, which is always non-negative and has lower variance. For the token above, ρ = 0.8, giving −0.2 + 0.223 = 0.023.

10. The full PPO loop for RLHF

Putting the pieces together, one PPO iteration on an LLM is:

  1. Rollout. Take a batch of prompts and sample responses from the current policy, recording each token's log-probability under it (these are π_old) and under the frozen reference.
  2. Score. Run the reward model on each (prompt, response) to get one score. Build per-token rewards: the KL penalty at every token, plus the score at the final token.
  3. Advantages. Run the value model on every prefix, compute TD errors and GAE advantages and the value targets. Normalise advantages across the batch (subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation), which keeps step sizes stable.
  4. Update. For a few epochs, shuffle the batch into mini-batches; recompute log π_θ for the sampled tokens, form ratios, compute the clipped policy loss and the value loss (and optionally an entropy bonus that rewards keeping the distribution spread out), and take optimiser steps on both models.
  5. Discard the batch and start again with fresh rollouts from the updated policy.

11. What it costs

Memory, by hand: training a model with Adam in mixed precision needs about 16 bytes per parameter: 2 for the bf16 weights, 2 for the bf16 gradients, 4 for an fp32 master copy of the weights and 8 for Adam's two fp32 moment estimates (dl5, lm12). Frozen models need only their bf16 weights, 2 bytes per parameter. For a 7-billion-parameter policy and value model with a 7B reference and a 7B reward model: 7×10⁹ × 16 = 112 GB for each trained model, 7×10⁹ × 2 = 14 GB for each frozen one, 252 GB in total before activations and the KV cache for generation. That is several 80 GB GPUs for a "small" model, which is why production RLHF shards the models across many GPUs and why dropping the value model (saving 112 GB here) is so attractive.

Time: generation is autoregressive, one forward pass per token, so a batch of 512 responses of 1,000 tokens needs 1,000 sequential steps, while the training update processes all tokens in parallel. For reasoning RL, with responses of 10,000 tokens or more, rollouts can take most of the wall-clock time. Systems therefore run rollouts on a fast inference engine (vLLM or SGLang, lm13) and updates on a training framework, copying weights between them after each update.

12. Simpler alternatives that keep PPO's core

Many of PPO's parts were designed for robotics and games, where episodes are long, rewards arrive at every step and states vary wildly. LLM training is different: the environment is deterministic, the reward is one number per response, and you can cheaply sample several responses per prompt. Ahmadian et al. (2024) argued that most of PPO is unnecessary in this setting and showed that REINFORCE with a leave-one-out baseline (RLOO), with no value model and no clipping, outperformed PPO on their RLHF benchmarks. GRPO, used for DeepSeekMath and DeepSeek-R1, keeps PPO's clipped ratio but replaces the value model with group-normalised rewards. rs4 works through it. And DPO (lm6) skips sampling altogether by optimising the same KL-regularised objective in closed form on fixed preference pairs, trading the ability to learn from the policy's own new samples for simplicity and stability.

Why the baseline leaves the gradient unbiased, and which baseline is best

The identity E_{a~π}[∇log π(a)] = 0 holds for any distribution, and it also holds conditional on the state, so the baseline may depend on the state (the prompt, the prefix) but not on the action being scored. If it depended on the action, the subtraction would shift probability towards actions with low baselines, biasing learning. Among state-dependent baselines, the one that minimises variance is roughly the expected reward from that state, which is exactly what a value function estimates; that is why actor-critic methods use V(s) as their baseline. A group mean over k samples of the same prompt is a cheap Monte Carlo estimate of the same quantity. One subtlety: if a sample is included in its own baseline (the plain group mean), its advantage is slightly shrunk towards zero by a factor (k − 1)/k, which is why RLOO leaves each sample out of its own baseline.

Reward-to-go and why it matters less for LLMs

In general RL, a refinement of REINFORCE weights each action's gradient not by the episode's total reward but by the reward collected after that action (the reward-to-go), because an action cannot influence rewards that came before it. In LLM RL with only a final reward, the reward-to-go of every token is the same final reward, so the refinement changes nothing for the task reward. It does matter for the per-token KL penalty: a token's reward-to-go includes the penalties of all later tokens, which is how PPO with GAE credits a token for steering the response into territory the reference finds unlikely.

The clip is a heuristic trust region, and it leaks

TRPO's theory says that if you keep the KL between consecutive policies small, a surrogate objective lower-bounds true improvement, guaranteeing monotonic progress. PPO's clip is an inexpensive approximation of that idea, but it constrains each token's ratio only for the tokens in the batch, only in the favoured direction, and only until the objective goes flat; nothing prevents the parameters from moving far in directions the batch does not measure, and with several epochs the actual KL between π_old and π_θ can exceed what ε suggests. Engineering studies of PPO (Engstrom et al., 2020) found that much of its performance on standard benchmarks came from code-level details (advantage normalisation, value clipping, learning-rate annealing, gradient clipping) rather than the clip itself. In LLM practice teams monitor the approximate KL between π_old and π_θ per update and stop the epochs early if it exceeds a target, a direct trust-region check layered on top of the clip.

Dual clipping and asymmetric clipping

The PPO objective has a hole for negative advantages: if a bad token's ratio becomes very large (the policy made it much more likely), r·A is a huge negative number and its gradient is unclipped, which can produce a giant update from one token. "Dual-clip" PPO adds a lower bound, max(L^CLIP, c·A) with c around 3, for negative advantages. Conversely, reasoning-RL work has found the upper bound 1 + ε too tight for low-probability tokens: a token with probability 0.01 can only rise to 0.012 per batch, which stifles exploration of rare but useful moves. DAPO raises the upper bound (ε_high = 0.28) while keeping the lower one at 0.2 (rs4). These are small edits with large effects on entropy and stability.

On-policy drift in practice

The ratio formula assumes the log-probabilities recorded during rollout are exactly what the training code would compute for the same weights. In real systems they come from an inference engine with different kernels, precision and sometimes quantisation, so the "old" probabilities are slightly wrong and ratios start away from 1 even before any update. Dropout has the same effect, which is why RLHF implementations disable it (Huang et al.'s reproduction lists this explicitly). Two common responses are to recompute π_old with the training engine before the update, or to add a correction for the mismatch. Asynchronous pipelines, which keep generating with slightly stale weights while training proceeds, make the data deliberately off-policy by one or more versions and rely on the ratio and clip to absorb it.

Value models are hard for long reasoning

A value model must predict, from a partial chain of thought, the probability that the eventual answer will be correct. For a 10,000-token reasoning trace this is a hard prediction problem in its own right: early prefixes carry little signal, the target is a noisy 0/1 outcome, and the value model must keep up with a policy whose behaviour changes during training. Errors in V feed straight into GAE advantages and bias learning, which is one reason DeepSeekMath's authors replaced it with group statistics. The trade is clear: a critic gives per-token credit and lower variance when it is accurate; group baselines give no per-token credit but are unbiased and cost nothing to train.

Entropy and exploration

Policy-gradient training sharpens the distribution: probability flows towards rewarded responses, and entropy falls. Some fall is the point, but if entropy collapses early, the policy samples nearly the same response every time, all samples of a prompt get the same reward, advantages become zero and learning stalls. PPO's optional entropy bonus adds c·H(π) to the objective to resist this; reasoning RL more often relies on sampling temperature near 1, asymmetric clipping and filtering out prompts whose samples are all right or all wrong (rs4). Watching entropy is one of the most informative signals in any run.

Reward and advantage normalisation

Reward models' scores have no fixed scale (only differences matter in Bradley–Terry training), and their scale can drift across prompts. Implementations therefore normalise: reward models are often shifted so that SFT responses average zero, and advantages are whitened (zero mean, unit variance) across each batch before the PPO loss. Whitening makes the effective learning rate independent of the reward's scale, but it also means a batch where every response is equally mediocre still produces full-size updates, a subtle behaviour that rs4's discussion of GRPO's standard-deviation division revisits.

The public case: "Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms" (Schulman et al., OpenAI, 2017)

The PPO paper by John Schulman, Filip Wolski, Prafulla Dhariwal, Alec Radford and Oleg Klimov is eight pages plus appendices, and its framing is practical. Vanilla policy gradients were data-inefficient and unstable if you took more than one step per batch; TRPO was reliable but complicated, needing conjugate-gradient optimisation, and incompatible with architectures that share parameters between policy and value function or use dropout. The goal was TRPO's reliability with first-order optimisation only. The paper proposes the clipped surrogate objective, describes an alternative with an adaptive KL penalty (raise the penalty coefficient when the measured KL between old and new policy exceeds 1.5 times a target, lower it when below the target divided by 1.5), and combines the policy loss with a value-function loss and an optional entropy bonus into one objective. The algorithm alternates between collecting T steps of data from N parallel actors and running K epochs of mini-batch Adam updates on it.

The ablation is the part interviewers draw on. On seven simulated robotics tasks from OpenAI Gym (MuJoCo physics), each trained for one million timesteps with three random seeds, scores were normalised so a random policy scored 0 and the best result 1, then averaged over all 21 runs. With no clipping or penalty, the score was −0.39, negative because one task (HalfCheetah) ended worse than random: the destructive large updates the paper warned about. Clipping scored 0.76 with ε = 0.1, 0.82 with ε = 0.2 and 0.70 with ε = 0.3. The adaptive KL penalty scored between 0.68 and 0.74 depending on its target, and fixed KL penalties between 0.62 and 0.72. That is the origin of the ubiquitous ε = 0.2. The MuJoCo hyperparameters were a horizon of T = 2,048 steps, Adam with step size 3×10⁻⁴, 10 epochs, mini-batches of 64, discount γ = 0.99 and GAE λ = 0.95. A figure in the paper shows that after one PPO iteration on the Hopper task the new policy had a KL of about 0.02 from the old one, the point where the clipped objective peaked.

Against other algorithms, PPO beat TRPO, the cross-entropy method, vanilla policy gradients and A2C on almost all of the continuous-control tasks. On 49 Atari games, compared with well-tuned A2C and ACER, PPO "won" 30 games by average reward over the whole of training (ACER 18, A2C 1), a measure that favours fast learning, while ACER won 28 to PPO's 19 by average reward over the last 100 episodes, which favours final performance. The conclusion the authors drew, and that the field adopted, was not that PPO is always best but that it strikes a good balance of sample efficiency, simplicity and wall-clock time.

That balance is why PPO became the RL algorithm of RLHF. OpenAI's summarisation work in 2020 and InstructGPT in 2022 used it with the additions of this lesson: a reward model's score at the end of the response, a per-token KL penalty to the SFT model (InstructGPT used β = 0.02 and trained its RL models for 256,000 episodes), and a value model initialised from the reward model. The algorithm designed for simulated robots and Atari became, with those changes, the method that aligned the first widely used chat models.

Failure modes in production runs

Reward hacking. Training reward climbs while held-out quality, judged by people or a stronger evaluator, stalls or falls. Typical exploits are length (longer answers score higher), confident tone, repetition of phrases the reward model likes, or for checkers, formats that trick the parser. Keep a gold evaluation separate from the training reward and compare them throughout (rs5).

KL blow-up. KL to the reference rises steadily and quality collapses into repetitive or odd text. Raise β, lower the learning rate, or reduce epochs per batch.

Entropy collapse. Entropy drops early, samples of a prompt become identical, advantages vanish and reward plateaus. Raise temperature, adjust clipping, or remove prompts that are too easy.

Truncation. Responses that hit the maximum length have no proper ending; scoring them as if complete teaches the model that rambling is fine. Implementations either give truncated responses a fixed low score (the "EOS trick", EOS being the end-of-sequence token, in Huang et al.'s reproduction of OpenAI's setup) or mask them out of the loss.

Mismatched log-probabilities. If the inference engine's log-probabilities differ from the trainer's, ratios start away from 1 and the clip fires on tokens that never changed. Check the mean and spread of the ratio on the first mini-batch of each update: it should be very close to 1.

What to watch on the dashboard

Mean training reward and a held-out gold metric; KL to the reference; policy entropy; mean and maximum response length; clip fraction and approximate KL between π_old and π_θ per update; value loss and explained variance (for PPO); the fraction of truncated responses; and for reasoning RL, the fraction of prompts whose samples are all correct or all wrong, which contribute no learning signal under group baselines.

The lab reproduces every hand calculation in this lesson on a toy four-response "policy" and shows the effect of a baseline. It needs only NumPy and runs in about a second.

  2. Part 2 trains the policy for 300 steps, 20 times each, with and without a baseline, when every reward carries an offset of 5. Compare the gradient variance (about 2.7 against 0.03) and the probability the policy ends up putting on the best response (about 0.56 on average without a baseline, with some runs collapsing to near 0, against about 0.84 with one). Then set offset=0.0: the gap shrinks a lot, because 0/1 rewards already have a natural baseline near the mean.
  3. Part 3 computes GAE for the three-token example of section 6. Check that λ = 1 gives [0.5, 0.4, 0.2] and λ = 0.95 gives [0.4705, 0.39, 0.2].
  4. Part 4 evaluates PPO's clipped objective for the five rows of the table in section 8 and reports whether each token's gradient flows or is zero. Try ε = 0.1 and see which rows change.
# rs3 lab: policy gradients on a toy "pick a response" bandit, then PPO's clip by hand. Pure NumPy.
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
true_reward = np.array([0.2, 0.5, 0.8, 0.3])     # chance each of 4 responses is judged correct

def softmax(z):
    e = np.exp(z - z.max()); return e / e.sum()

# 1) One REINFORCE gradient by hand: grad of log softmax(z)[a] is onehot(a) - probs.
z = np.zeros(4); p = softmax(z)
samples, rewards = [2, 0, 2, 1], [1.0, 0.0, 1.0, 0.0]
b = np.mean(rewards)                              # baseline = mean reward of the group
g = sum((r - b) * (np.eye(4)[a] - p) for a, r in zip(samples, rewards)) / len(samples)
print("probs", p, "baseline", b, "\ngradient", g.round(4))

# 2) Train with and without a baseline. Rewards carry an offset of 5 (like a reward model whose
#    scores are all around 5): it changes nothing about which response is best, only the noise.
def train(use_baseline, steps=300, k=4, lr=0.1, offset=5.0):
    z = np.zeros(4); grads = []
    for _ in range(steps):
        p = softmax(z)
        a = rng.choice(4, size=k, p=p)
        r = (rng.random(k) < true_reward[a]).astype(float) + offset
        adv = r - r.mean() if use_baseline else r
        g = sum(adv[i] * (np.eye(4)[a[i]] - p) for i in range(k)) / k
        grads.append(g); z += lr * g
    return softmax(z)[2], np.mean(np.var(grads, axis=0).sum())

for ub in (False, True):
    runs = np.array([train(ub) for _ in range(20)])
    print(f"baseline={ub!s:5}  P(best) after 300 steps = {runs[:,0].mean():.3f} "
          f"(worst run {runs[:,0].min():.3f})  gradient variance = {runs[:,1].mean():.3f}")

# 3) GAE for a 3-token response, gamma = 1, reward 1 only at the end.
V = np.array([0.5, 0.6, 0.8]); r = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 1.0]); V_next = np.append(V[1:], 0.0)
delta = r + V_next - V
for lam in (1.0, 0.95, 0.0):
    A = np.zeros(3); run = 0.0
    for t in reversed(range(3)):
        run = delta[t] + lam * run; A[t] = run
    print(f"lambda={lam}: deltas={delta.round(3)}  advantages={A.round(4)}")

# 4) PPO clipped objective for one token, epsilon = 0.2.
def clip_obj(ratio, A, eps=0.2):
    unclipped, clipped = ratio * A, np.clip(ratio, 1 - eps, 1 + eps) * A
    return min(unclipped, clipped), clipped < unclipped   # True -> clipped branch, zero gradient
for ratio, A in [(1.1, 2.0), (1.35, 2.0), (0.7, 2.0), (0.75, -1.5), (1.4, -1.5)]:
    val, flat = clip_obj(ratio, A)
    print(f"ratio={ratio:4}  A={A:+.1f}  objective={val:+.3f}  gradient={'zero' if flat else 'flows'}")

What it proves: the baseline does not change which response is best, only how reliably you get there; and the clip's zero-gradient cases are exactly the ones where a token has already moved past 1 ± ε in the direction its advantage wanted.

Work it out

For PPO with ε = 0.2, compute the per-token objective value and whether its gradient is zero in four cases: (a) A = +1, r = 1.1; (b) A = +1, r = 1.3; (c) A = −1, r = 0.9; (d) A = −1, r = 0.7. What behaviour does this produce?

Answer
  • (a) min(1.1, clip(1.1) = 1.1) = 1.1: unclipped, gradient flows. Keep increasing this good token's probability.
  • (b) min(1.3, 1.2) = 1.2: the clipped term wins and is constant, so the gradient is zero. The token already moved 30% up; stop pushing.
  • (c) min(−0.9, −0.9) = −0.9: unclipped. Keep decreasing this bad token.
  • (d) min(−0.7, clip(0.7) × −1 = −0.8) = −0.8: clipped and constant, so the gradient is zero. Already decreased by 30%; stop.

The clip only removes the incentive to move further in the direction the advantage favours; moves in the wrong direction (for example A = +1 with r = 0.7) are unclipped and fully corrected, because the min picks the pessimistic term. The net effect is a soft trust region: several epochs on one batch without the policy running away.

Estimate

You plan PPO-based RLHF for a 13B model, with a 13B reward model, using Adam in mixed precision. Estimate the memory for model states, and how much a critic-free method would save.

Answer

Trained models need about 16 bytes per parameter: 13×10⁹ × 16 = 208 GB each for the policy and the value model, 416 GB together. Frozen models need 2 bytes per parameter: 26 GB each for the reference and the reward model, 52 GB together. Total about 468 GB of model state before activations and the KV cache for generation, so at least six 80 GB GPUs with sharding, in practice more. A critic-free method (RLOO, GRPO) drops the value model and saves 208 GB, about 44%, and a reasoning setup with a rule-based checker also drops the reward model, saving another 26 GB.
