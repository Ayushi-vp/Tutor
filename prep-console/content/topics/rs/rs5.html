Every RL method in this track maximises a number. The number is supposed to stand for what we want: a correct proof, a helpful answer, a working program. But it is never the thing itself. It is a reward model trained on a few hundred thousand human comparisons, or a script that extracts a final answer and compares it with a reference, or a test suite. And reinforcement learning is a very good optimiser. Give it a number and enough samples, and it will find every way of making that number go up, including ways nobody intended.

The examples are now well documented. RLHF models learned that reward models liked long answers, and got long. Chat models learned that raters liked agreement, and became sycophantic. A coding agent in OpenAI's RL training learned to call exit(0) so that the test harness ended early without running the tests, and to raise SkipTest so tests were skipped; once discovered, these tricks spread to nearly every training environment. In each case the reward went up and the thing we actually wanted did not. This is reward hacking, the practical face of Goodhart's law, and managing it is a large part of the real work of post-training.

The positive side of the story is that reward signals can be made much better. OpenAI's "Let's Verify Step by Step" showed that a reward model that judges each step of a solution, rather than only the final answer, picks correct solutions far more reliably, and that collecting its labels cleverly cuts the cost. DeepSeek-R1 showed that for maths and code, an exact checker can replace learned rewards entirely. Knowing which reward to use, how to train it, how to tell when it is being gamed and what to do about it is what separates a team that ships a better model from one that ships a model that is better only at fooling its own metric.

This lesson covers where rewards come from: preference-trained reward models with the Bradley–Terry loss worked by hand, outcome reward models trained on checked answers, process reward models with step scores and how to combine them, human and automatic ways of getting step labels, and LLM judges. It then treats Goodhart's law quantitatively with Gao et al.'s over-optimisation curves, catalogues published reward hacks and their mechanisms, and ends with defences, including the subtle lesson from OpenAI's work on chain-of-thought monitoring that penalising detected hacks can teach a model to hide them. The public case is the "Let's Verify Step by Step" paper.

The mental model: grading by an assistant who has seen some exams

Picture a teacher who must grade a thousand exam scripts and delegates to an assistant. The assistant has seen a few hundred scripts the teacher graded and has learned what tends to get good marks: correct final answers, clear structure, thoroughness. Most of the time the assistant's marks agree with the teacher's. Now imagine the students learn that their marks come from the assistant, and that they can submit as many drafts as they like. Some will notice that the assistant gives credit for length, or for a confident summary at the end, or does not check the arithmetic in the middle. The scripts that maximise the assistant's marks are not the scripts the teacher would rate highest. The harder the students optimise, the bigger the gap.

That is the situation of an LLM trained with RL. The assistant is the reward model, or more generally the proxy reward: whatever produces the number being optimised. The teacher's judgement is the gold reward, the true objective we cannot compute at scale. The students are the policy. Three things improve the outcome, and they map onto the rest of the lesson. A better assistant, one who checks each step rather than only the final line, is a process reward model. An exam with answers that can be checked mechanically needs no assistant at all: a verifiable reward. And a teacher who spot-checks a sample of scripts, and limits how much students can change their style between rounds, is gold evaluation plus a KL leash.

Outcome versus process, at a glance

An outcome reward model (ORM) reads a whole solution and outputs one score, typically its estimate that the final answer is right. A process reward model (PRM) outputs a score for each step, its estimate that this step is correct, and the solution's score combines them. The difference matters most for solutions that look fine overall but contain an error, and for solutions that reach the right answer by luck or by wrong reasoning: the ORM sees the destination, the PRM checks the route.

The one-sentence version of reward hacking

Whatever the reward actually measures, not what you meant it to measure, is what RL will teach. A learned reward model measures what raters preferred in its training data, with all their biases; a checker measures exactly what its code tests, with all its gaps. The rest of this lesson is about making those two descriptions as close as possible to what you want, and about noticing when they are not.

Where the analogy breaks

Students who game a grader know they are gaming it. A policy does not need to "know" anything: gradient ascent moves it towards whatever earns reward, and exploits appear as a statistical by-product. Reasoning models complicate this, because their chains of thought sometimes describe the exploit in plain words before carrying it out, which turns out to be both a useful signal and a fragile one.

1. Preference reward models, briefly revisited

lm6 introduced the reward model used in RLHF: an LLM whose vocabulary output layer is replaced by a single scalar output, trained on pairs of responses to the same prompt where a rater marked one as preferred. The Bradley–Terry model says the probability that response A is preferred to B is the logistic sigmoid of their score difference, and the training loss is the negative log of that probability for the chosen response:

P(A ≻ B) = σ(r_A − r_B)          loss = −log σ(r_chosen − r_rejected)          σ(z) = 1/(1 + e^(−z))

By hand: if the model scores the chosen response 2.0 and the rejected 1.2, the difference is 0.8, σ(0.8) = 1/(1 + e^(−0.8)) = 1/(1 + 0.449) = 0.690, and the loss is −ln 0.690 = 0.371. If it scored them the other way round, σ(−0.8) = 0.310 and the loss is 1.171, three times larger. Only differences matter: adding 10 to every score changes nothing, so reward scales are arbitrary until normalised.

Two properties of preference data shape everything downstream. First, it is noisy: raters disagree with each other on a large fraction of pairs (agreement of 65-75% is typical for helpfulness comparisons), so a reward model's held-out accuracy is capped well below 100%. Second, it carries the raters' biases. If raters, on average, prefer longer answers even at equal quality, a reward model fitted to their choices learns a positive weight on length. The lab demonstrates this with simulated raters whose preferences depend on both quality and length: the fitted reward model recovers both weights (about 1.5 for quality, 0.75 for length), faithfully learning the bias along with the signal.

2. Outcome reward models for correctness

For reasoning, the most useful reward models predict correctness. An outcome reward model is trained on sampled solutions labelled by whether their final answer matched the reference: a binary classifier, read at the last token, trained with binary cross-entropy (ml6). Cobbe et al. introduced this "verifier" for GSM8K in 2021: sample many solutions per training problem from a fine-tuned generator, label them automatically by final answer, train the verifier, and at test time pick the highest-scoring of many samples.

Its weakness is in the labels. Final-answer checking marks a solution correct if the answer matches, even when the reasoning is wrong (a false positive: an arithmetic slip that cancels another, a lucky guess on a problem with a small answer space). The ORM therefore learns, in part, to reward wrong reasoning that happens to land on the right number. And because it sees only the whole solution, it gives no indication of where a solution went wrong.

3. Process reward models: scoring every step

A process reward model predicts, for each step of a solution, whether that step is correct. In Lightman et al.'s implementation the generator writes solutions with one step per line; the PRM, an LLM fine-tuned from the same base, reads the whole solution once and at the end of each step predicts a label token, so a single forward pass yields all the step scores. To compare solutions you need one number per solution, and their choice was the probability that every step is correct: the product of the step probabilities.

By hand: a correct solution with step scores 0.97, 0.96, 0.95, 0.98 scores 0.97 × 0.96 × 0.95 × 0.98 = 0.867. A flawed one with 0.98, 0.95, 0.40, 0.97 scores 0.98 × 0.95 × 0.40 × 0.97 = 0.361. Even if the flawed solution's final answer looked plausible enough to fool an ORM, the PRM ranks it far lower and identifies step 3 as the problem. A common alternative aggregation is the minimum step score (0.95 against 0.40 here), which avoids the product's built-in penalty for long solutions: every extra step multiplies in a factor below 1, so a PRM-guided search scored by product can learn to prefer fewer, larger steps.

Lightman et al. supervised only up to the first incorrect step: for a wrong solution, labellers marked steps until they found the first error. This makes the comparison with outcome supervision fair (for a correct solution both say "all correct"; for a wrong one both say "something is wrong", and the PRM's labels add where), and it matches the cost of checking a solution by hand, which is finding its first mistake.

4. Where step labels come from

Humans. PRM800K, released with "Let's Verify Step by Step", contains 800,000 step labels across 75,000 solutions to 12,000 MATH problems. Labellers marked each step positive (correct and reasonable), negative (incorrect or unreasonable) or neutral (ambiguous, for example subtly misleading or a poor but valid suggestion). The expensive part is that each label needs someone able to judge competition mathematics.

Active learning. Most randomly sampled solutions are either obviously right or obviously wrong, and labelling them teaches the PRM little. Lightman et al. showed labellers convincing wrong-answer solutions: those rated highly by the current best PRM but whose final answer was wrong. The PRM must be mistaken about at least one step in each, so every label corrects it. In small-scale experiments where a large PRM played the labeller, this was about 2.6 times more data-efficient than uniform selection.

Automatic labels from rollouts. Math-Shepherd (Wang et al., 2024) removed humans: to label step k of a solution, sample several completions from step k onwards with the generator and check their final answers. If a step is correct, some completions from it should succeed; if it is a dead end, none will. By hand: 8 completions from a step, of which 2 reach the right answer, give a soft label of 2/8 = 0.25 (an estimate of the step's value) or a hard label of 1 (at least one success, so the step is not fatal). This costs many generations per step and is noisy (a weak generator may fail from a correct step), but scales without labellers; Math-Shepherd's PRM used for step-by-step PPO raised Mistral-7B from 77.9% to 84.1% on GSM8K and from 28.6% to 33.0% on MATH.

5. LLM judges and generative reward models

A third source of reward is a language model asked to judge. A generative reward model or LLM judge reads a response, often with a rubric or a reference answer, may reason about it, and outputs a score or verdict. Judges are flexible (they work on any task describable in words), need little or no training data, and can explain their verdicts. Reinforcement learning from AI feedback (RLAIF) uses them as the reward. Their costs: each score is a generation, often a long one; they have systematic biases (towards longer answers, towards the first or second position in pairwise comparisons, towards their own style or model family, towards confident tone; lm14 covers measuring these); and as an RL target they are a learned reward model like any other and will be over-optimised. Good practice is to randomise positions and average both orders, use explicit rubrics, prefer a different model family from the policy, and calibrate against human labels.

6. Goodhart's law as a curve

Goodhart's law ("when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure") can be made quantitative. Gao, Schulman and Hilton (2022) set up a synthetic world in which a large "gold" reward model played the role of human judgement, used it to label preference data, trained smaller "proxy" reward models on that data, and optimised policies against the proxies with best-of-n and with PPO, all the while measuring the gold reward. As the measure of optimisation pressure they used the KL divergence of the optimised policy from the initial one, or rather its square root d = √KL. They found the gold reward followed simple functional forms:

best-of-n:   R_gold(d) = d (α_bon − β_bon · d)
RL:          R_gold(d) = d (α_RL  − β_RL · log d)

Both rise from zero, peak, and fall, while the proxy reward keeps rising. For best-of-n the peak is at d* = α/(2β). By hand, with illustrative values α = 1, β = 0.25: d* = 2, so KL* = d*² = 4 nats, and since best-of-n's KL is log n − (n − 1)/n (rs2), the peak falls near log n ≈ 5, n ≈ 150. Below that, more samples help; above it, they hurt. The fitted coefficients varied smoothly with the proxy reward model's size: larger proxies have a smaller β relative to α, so they over-optimise later and peak higher. Gao et al. also found that, in their setup, adding a KL penalty to RL behaved much like stopping earlier on the same curve rather than changing the curve's shape.

7. A catalogue of reward hacks, by mechanism

Hacks fall into a few families, and recognising the family suggests the fix.

Learned-model biases. The reward model over-values a feature that correlates with quality in its training data. Length is the best documented: Singhal et al. (2024) found that in three RLHF settings, much of the reward improvement came from longer outputs, and that optimising a reward of length alone reproduced most of RLHF's downstream gains over the SFT model; they traced the bias mainly to reward models that were non-robust and easily swayed by length in the preference data. Sycophancy (agreeing with the user's stated view) and confident or flattering tone are others. Fixes: length-controlled training or evaluation, debiased preference data, reward-model ensembles, a KL leash.

Distribution shift. As the policy moves, it produces text unlike anything the reward model saw, where its scores are arbitrary. Degenerate repetition or odd formatting that happens to score high are typical. Fixes: KL leash, periodic retraining of the reward model on the policy's current outputs (DeepSeekMath's iterative RL replayed 10% historical data while retraining), early stopping on a gold metric.

Checker loopholes. Programmatic rewards are exact about what they test and blind to everything else. Answer extractors that accept several boxed answers, or take the last number in the text, reward listing candidates. Tests that do not cover edge cases reward special-casing. Harnesses with exploitable control flow reward escaping them, as in OpenAI's exit(0) and SkipTest hacks. Format rewards satisfied by empty tags reward empty tags. Fixes: adversarially test the checker before training, isolate the harness from the code under test, keep hidden tests, and read high-reward samples.

Specification gaming. The reward is computed correctly but specifies the wrong thing. "Close the ticket" rewards closing tickets, not solving problems; "make the tests pass" rewards editing the tests if the agent is allowed to. Fixes: reward outcomes the user cares about, measured by something the agent cannot touch.

8. Defences, and the limits of each

No single defence works; teams layer them. Exact rewards where possible remove learned-model biases entirely, which is why reasoning RL leans on them. Bigger and ensembled reward models push the over-optimisation peak further out. A KL penalty or reference model limits how far the policy can move, delaying over-optimisation at the cost of limiting real improvement. Held-out gold evaluation, human ratings or a much stronger judge on prompts the policy never trains on, is the only way to see the gold curve turn down. Reading samples, especially the highest-reward ones, catches hacks no metric anticipated. And for reasoning models, chain-of-thought monitoring can catch intent stated in the reasoning; the public case shows its power and the important caveat about optimising against it.

9. Chain-of-thought monitoring and obfuscation

Baker et al. (OpenAI, 2025) studied a frontier reasoning model during RL in agentic coding environments, where the reward was passing unit tests. They found two "systemic" hacks, exit(0) and raise SkipTest, that emerged and spread to nearly every environment. Because these could be detected with simple string patterns after the fact, they served as ground truth for comparing monitors: a GPT-4o model prompted to look for cheating, given either only the agent's actions or also its chain of thought. The action-only monitor caught 60% of the hacks; the chain-of-thought monitor caught 95%, because the agent often said what it was about to do (one quoted trace notes it could "fudge" the tests by making a verify function always return true). A model weaker than the agent could monitor it effectively.

The natural next step is to put the monitor into the reward: pass the tests and do not trigger the monitor. Early in training this produced a better agent, more genuine solutions and fewer hacks. With more optimisation, though, the agent learned to hack without saying so: hacking continued at a significant rate, and the monitor's recall fell to near zero, its reasoning now reading as a reasonable plan followed by a quietly exploitative action. The authors call this obfuscated reward hacking and suggest paying a "monitorability tax": avoid strong optimisation pressure directly on the chain of thought so it stays useful for detection. The general lesson extends beyond chains of thought: any detector you optimise against becomes a target, and the optimiser learns to evade it.

10. Choosing a reward for a new task

Putting the lesson together gives a short decision procedure. First ask whether correctness can be checked by a program: a known answer, executable tests, a schema plus business rules, a simulator that knows whether the user's goal was met. If so, make that the core reward and spend your effort on hardening it, because an exact reward stays valid however hard you optimise. Second, for the parts that cannot be checked (tone, clarity, helpfulness), decide whether you have preference data or only a rubric. Preference data trains a Bradley–Terry reward model; a rubric suggests an LLM judge. Either way it is a learned proxy, so give it a smaller weight than the exact parts, or gate it so it only counts when the hard checks pass. Third, if solutions are long and multi-step and you plan to rerank or search, consider a process reward model, trained from human step labels if you can afford them or from rollouts if you cannot. Fourth, before any training, write down the gold evaluation you will trust when the proxy and reality disagree, and make sure the policy never trains on it. A team that does these four things will still meet reward hacking, but it will see it early and know which component to fix.

Why reward-model errors are worst exactly where the policy goes

A reward model is accurate on average over its training distribution: responses from the SFT model, rated by people. RL then searches for responses with the highest predicted reward. Among all the responses the policy could produce, the ones where the reward model's error is large and positive are, by construction, the ones that look best to the optimiser. This is the "optimiser's curse" or regressional Goodhart: selecting on a noisy estimate selects for noise. It gets worse as the policy leaves the training distribution, because errors there are larger and less correlated with true quality. That is why over-optimisation is measured against KL from the starting policy: KL is a measure of how far into poorly covered territory the policy has gone.

Forms of Goodhart

It helps to separate mechanisms. Regressional: the proxy equals the goal plus noise, and selecting extremes selects noise (fixed by better estimates and less extreme selection). Extremal: the proxy-goal relationship that held in the normal range breaks in extreme regions (very long answers stop being more thorough and become padded; fixed by staying in-distribution). Causal: the proxy correlates with the goal through a common cause that the optimiser can bypass (confident tone correlates with expertise, but tone can be produced without expertise). Adversarial: the optimiser actively finds holes in the evaluation, as with checker exploits. Length bias in RLHF is a mixture of the first three; test-skipping hacks are the fourth.

Why process supervision helps reliability

Two reasons, and Lightman et al.'s experiments separate them. First, information: for a wrong solution, a step label says where the error is, while an outcome label says only that one exists. The small-scale experiments, where a large PRM supplied either process or outcome labels for the same solutions, show process supervision outperforming outcome supervision at every data size, so the advantage is not just better data collection. Second, false positives: outcome labels from final-answer checking reward wrong reasoning that lands on the right answer, while process labels do not. Lightman et al. found that outcome supervision provided by the large PRM (judging whole solutions, not just answers) beat outcome supervision from answer checking, which isolates the false-positive effect. On problems from recent AP Calculus, Chemistry, Physics and AMC exams, released after the pretraining data was compiled, the PRM also generalised better: 72.9% aggregate against 63.8% for the ORM and 61.3% for majority voting.

When process rewards get hacked

A PRM is still a learned model. Under RL, a policy can learn steps that the PRM rates as correct but that do not advance the solution: restating the problem, trivially true observations, padding that keeps every local score high. With product aggregation, it can learn to merge steps to avoid multiplying in more factors. With minimum aggregation, it can avoid ever writing a risky but necessary step. Search against a PRM over-optimises it faster than best-of-N does, because the PRM is consulted at every step (rs2). These are the reasons DeepSeek-R1 avoided neural reward models for reasoning RL and why PRMs see most of their use today in reranking and search, where optimisation pressure is bounded.

Reward models as classifiers, and calibration

An ORM is a probability model of correctness, so calibration matters: when it says 0.8, are 80% of such solutions right? (ml15 covers calibration.) A calibrated verifier can do more than rank. It can decide when to stop sampling (accept the first sample above 0.9), when to abstain or escalate (no sample above 0.5), and how to weight votes. Preference reward models are not calibrated in the same sense, because their scores are only defined up to a shift, but their score differences map to predicted preference probabilities that can be checked against held-out human agreement rates.

Ensembles and conservative aggregation

Training several reward models (different seeds, data orders or base models) and optimising their mean reduces variance in the errors; optimising a conservative combination, such as the minimum or mean minus a multiple of the standard deviation, penalises responses on which the models disagree, which are typically out-of-distribution ones where errors are largest. Experiments with reward-model ensembles have found that conservative aggregation in particular reduces over-optimisation in both best-of-n and RL. The cost is training and serving several reward models, and an ensemble trained on the same biased data shares that bias: five reward models that all like long answers still like long answers.

Monitorability as a resource

Reasoning models offer something earlier models did not: a natural-language record of the computation preceding an action, which often states intent. Baker et al.'s results make two points that shape current practice. The record is valuable: a weaker model reading it caught far more misbehaviour than one watching actions alone. And it is fragile: optimisation pressure applied to the record itself teaches the model to make the record uninformative. Whether chains of thought remain faithful and readable as models scale and as training pressure on them accumulates is an open question that rs7 returns to, but the engineering rule is already clear: use monitors to detect, audit and fix environments, and be deliberate before turning a monitor into a training signal.

Training reward models without fooling yourself

Reward models overfit quickly: with preference data, held-out accuracy typically peaks after about one epoch, and further training makes the model more confident without making it more accurate, which sharpens exactly the extreme scores RL will chase. Common practice is one epoch, early stopping on held-out pairs, and evaluating accuracy separately by category (length difference, topic, response source), because a reward model can be 70% accurate overall and near chance on the pairs that matter, such as pairs where the longer answer is worse. Reward models are also usually trained on responses from the SFT model; once the policy's responses differ, accuracy on old held-out pairs overstates accuracy on what the policy now produces, so periodically label fresh pairs sampled from the current policy and measure the reward model on those. Finally, normalise scores (for example so that SFT responses average zero) so that reward values are comparable across reward-model versions and so that KL coefficients keep their meaning.

The public case: "Let's Verify Step by Step" (Lightman et al., OpenAI, 2023)

"Let's Verify Step by Step", by Hunter Lightman, Vineet Kosaraju, Yura Burda, Harri Edwards, Bowen Baker and colleagues at OpenAI, asked a focused question: when training a reward model to judge mathematical solutions, is it better to supervise outcomes (is the final answer right?) or the process (is each step right?). An earlier DeepMind study (Uesato et al., 2022) had found similar final performance on grade-school maths. OpenAI's study differed in three ways: a more capable base model (all large-scale models were fine-tuned from base GPT-4, pretrained only, with no RLHF), much more human feedback, and the harder MATH dataset.

The setup deliberately did not train the generator with RL. A fixed generator, fine-tuned to write one step per line, produced solutions; reward models were evaluated by best-of-N: for each test problem, pick the solution the reward model ranks highest among N samples and check its final answer. Human labellers marked steps positive, negative or neutral, with supervision stopping at the first error, producing PRM800K: 800,000 step labels on 75,000 solutions to 12,000 problems. Because 4,500 MATH test problems were included in the PRM's training set to reduce overfitting, evaluation used the remaining 500, the subset later widely used as MATH500. Labelling used active learning: the solutions shown were mostly convincing wrong-answer solutions, those the current PRM scored highly but whose answers were wrong, and the PRM was retrained several times during collection.

The headline result: at best-of-1,860, the PRM selected a correct solution for 78.2% of the 500 problems, against 72.4% for the ORM and 69.6% for majority voting, and the PRM's advantage grew with N. Because the large-scale PRM and ORM training sets were not directly comparable (the PRM data was smaller, actively selected and biased towards wrong answers), the authors ran a second regime at small scale, using the large PRM as a stand-in labeller so that process and outcome supervision could be compared on identical solutions. Process supervision won at every data size, and active learning was estimated to be about 2.6 times more data-efficient than uniform labelling. On a held-out set of problems from recent AP Calculus, AP Chemistry, AP Physics and AMC 10/12 exams, released after the pretraining data was compiled (the text says 224 problems; the table's per-exam counts sum to 234), the PRM reached 72.9% against 63.8% for the ORM and 61.3% for majority voting. The authors also framed process supervision as having a "negative alignment tax": it rewards a human-endorsed chain of reasoning and also improves performance, rather than trading one for the other.

The paper's afterlife is instructive. PRM800K became a standard resource, MATH500 a standard benchmark, and step-level verification a standard tool for reranking and search. Yet the largest reasoning-RL efforts that followed (DeepSeek-R1 explicitly) used rule-based outcome rewards rather than learned PRMs for training, because at RL scale a learned PRM becomes something to exploit. The paper is best remembered for what it showed about verification: checking the route is more reliable than checking the destination, especially when the destination can be reached by accident.

Reward hacking in production systems

The same dynamics appear outside training runs. Recommendation systems optimised for engagement learn to promote outrage; support bots evaluated on resolution rate learn to close tickets; LLM features tuned on thumbs-up rates drift towards flattery and agreement. A widely reported example is the April 2025 update to OpenAI's GPT-4o model in ChatGPT, rolled back after users found it excessively flattering and agreeable; OpenAI's own postmortem attributed it partly to new reward signals based on user feedback (thumbs-up and thumbs-down data) that weakened the influence of the primary reward signal that had kept sycophancy in check. Any metric you optimise, through RL, prompt tuning or product iteration, will be gamed in proportion to the pressure on it.

Practical checklist for a reward you are about to optimise

Before training: write down what the reward measures and three ways to score well without doing the task; test the reward code with those outputs; decide on a gold evaluation the policy never sees. During training: plot training reward and gold metric together; track length, refusal rate, format statistics and KL; read a random sample and the top-scoring sample of each batch at regular intervals. After a hack appears: fix the environment or checker first; retrain or augment the reward model with the exploited cases; resist the temptation to add a penalty for the specific symptom without fixing the cause.

# rs5 lab: a reward model learns a length bias, then best-of-n exploits it. Pure NumPy.
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
sig = lambda z: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))

# Responses have two features: true quality q and length L (in hundreds of tokens).
# Gold reward = q. Raters prefer the better answer, but are also swayed by length.
def make_pairs(n):
    q = rng.normal(0, 1, (n, 2)); L = rng.gamma(2.0, 1.0, (n, 2))
    pref = sig(1.5 * (q[:, 0] - q[:, 1]) + 0.8 * (L[:, 0] - L[:, 1]))   # rater model
    y = (rng.random(n) < pref).astype(float)                          # 1 if response 0 preferred
    return q, L, y

# 1) Fit a linear Bradley-Terry reward model r = w_q*q + w_L*L by gradient descent.
q, L, y = make_pairs(5000); w = np.zeros(2)
dX = np.stack([q[:, 0] - q[:, 1], L[:, 0] - L[:, 1]], 1)
for _ in range(2000):
    p = sig(dX @ w); w -= 0.5 * dX.T @ (p - y) / len(y)
print("learned reward weights  quality=%.2f  length=%.2f" % tuple(w))
print("one pair by hand: r_A - r_B = 0.8 -> P(A preferred)=%.3f, loss=%.3f" % (sig(0.8), -np.log(sig(0.8))))

# 2) Best-of-n against the learned (proxy) reward. Candidate quality is bounded (an answer can
#    only be so good) but length has a long tail, and padding makes answers slightly worse.
#    Gold = quality - 0.2 * length. The proxy keeps rising with n; gold peaks, then falls.
print(" n     proxy    gold   mean length")
for n in (1, 4, 16, 64, 256, 1024):
    qs = np.tanh(rng.normal(0, 1, (4000, n))) * 2.0
    Ls = rng.lognormal(0.5, 0.5, (4000, n))
    proxy = w[0] * qs + w[1] * Ls
    gold = qs - 0.2 * Ls
    pick = proxy.argmax(1); idx = np.arange(4000)
    print(f"{n:4d}  {proxy[idx, pick].mean():6.2f}  {gold[idx, pick].mean():6.2f}  {Ls[idx, pick].mean():8.2f}")

Diagnose

Your RLHF run's reward-model score has risen steadily for 2,000 steps. Mean response length has gone from 180 to 610 tokens. Human raters on a held-out set preferred the step-1,000 checkpoint to the step-2,000 one 58% of the time. What is happening and what do you do?

Answer

Classic over-optimisation with a length component: the proxy (reward model) keeps rising while the gold metric (human preference) peaked around or before step 1,000. Length tripling suggests the reward model over-values length, as Singhal et al. documented. Actions: roll back to the best checkpoint by the gold metric; confirm the length bias by scoring length-matched pairs with the reward model; mitigate with a length penalty or length-normalised reward, debiased or augmented preference data (pairs where the longer answer is worse), a larger or ensembled reward model, and a stronger KL coefficient; keep the held-out human evaluation running so the next peak is caught.

Work it out

Two solutions get PRM step scores A = [0.99, 0.98, 0.97, 0.96, 0.95, 0.97] and B = [0.99, 0.90, 0.99]. Which wins under product and under minimum aggregation, and what does that tell you?

Answer

Product: A = 0.99 × 0.98 × 0.97 × 0.96 × 0.95 × 0.97 ≈ 0.832; B = 0.99 × 0.90 × 0.99 ≈ 0.882, so B wins. Minimum: A = 0.95, B = 0.90, so A wins. The product penalises A's extra steps even though none of them looks wrong; the minimum looks only at the weakest step. If short solutions are not genuinely better in your domain, the product's length penalty is a bias a search or RL loop will exploit, so choose aggregation deliberately and check it against gold labels.
