DeepSeek-R1 has 671 billion parameters (37 billion active per token, since it is a mixture-of-experts model, lm17). It reasons very well and is expensive to serve: it needs a cluster of GPUs, and its long chains of thought multiply the cost of every request. Most teams that want reasoning in a product cannot run it, and even teams that can would rather not pay for it on every query. What they want is a model small enough to run on one GPU, or on a laptop, that reasons almost as well on their problems.

Reinforcement learning, which produced R1, turns out to be a poor way to get there. DeepSeek tried it: they trained a 32-billion-parameter base model with the same kind of large-scale RL for more than 10,000 steps and reached 47% on the AIME 2024 maths competition. Then they took the same base model and simply fine-tuned it on R1's own worked solutions, about 800,000 of them. That model scored 72.6%. Copying the teacher's reasoning beat discovering it from scratch by more than 25 points, at a fraction of the compute. A 1.5-billion-parameter model distilled the same way outscored GPT-4o on that benchmark.

This is distillation: training a small student model to imitate a large teacher. For reasoning it has become the main way capabilities spread from frontier models to the small open models that people actually deploy, and it is a practical tool for any team: if a large model can solve your domain's problems, you can usually build a smaller, cheaper model that solves most of them, provided you generate, filter and select the training data well. That last condition is where most of the engineering lives, and where interviews probe: how do you build the dataset, what does it cost, how much data do you need, and what goes wrong?

This lesson starts with classic knowledge distillation and the soft-target loss worked by hand, then sequence-level distillation as used for reasoning. It covers rejection sampling and its cost and yield, the STaR loop in which a model bootstraps from its own correct rationales, why distillation beats RL on small models, and the s1 result that 1,000 carefully chosen examples can be enough, together with its budget-forcing trick for controlling thinking length. It ends with the failure modes of distilled reasoners and the case of DeepSeek's distilled models in detail. The lab computes the losses, the yield of a rejection-sampling run and a toy version of STaR.

The mental model: apprenticeship with worked examples

Think of a master craftsperson and an apprentice. The apprentice could learn by trial and error alone, attempting jobs and being told only whether the client was satisfied. That is reinforcement learning, and it works, slowly, provided the apprentice sometimes succeeds. Or the apprentice could watch the master do hundreds of jobs from start to finish, including the moments where the master stops, frowns, checks a measurement and redoes a joint, and then practise copying that process. That is distillation, and for a beginner it is far faster: the apprentice learns techniques they would rarely have stumbled on by themselves.

Three refinements make the picture match practice. First, the apprentice should copy only jobs that turned out well: if the master occasionally botches one, those should be thrown away. That is rejection sampling, keeping only teacher outputs that pass a check. Second, which jobs to watch matters more than how many: a thousand varied, difficult, well-executed jobs teach more than fifty thousand easy, repetitive ones. That is the lesson of the s1 study. Third, an apprentice with no master can still improve by attempting jobs, keeping a record of the ones that worked and studying their own successes, and, for jobs they failed, being shown the finished result and working out how one gets there. That is STaR, the Self-Taught Reasoner.

Why imitation works so well here

A reasoning trace is an unusually rich training example. It does not just say "the answer is 42"; it shows how to set up the problem, which approaches to try, how to notice an error and how to recover. A student fine-tuned on hundreds of thousands of such traces learns not only answers but a procedure, the habit of writing long, self-checking chains that rs1 showed to be the source of reasoning models' accuracy. RL has to discover that habit from a one-bit reward; distillation shows it directly.

What gets transferred, in numbers

It helps to see the scale of the trade. DeepSeek's dataset of 800,000 samples, at reasoning traces of a few thousand tokens each, is on the order of a few billion tokens: tiny next to the trillions of tokens of pretraining, and small next to the RL compute that produced R1. Fine-tuning a 7B student on it takes days on a modest cluster, not months on a large one. In exchange the student gets most of the teacher's benchmark performance in its strongest domains. The asymmetry is the whole point: the teacher's expensive discovery process (pretraining a huge model, then thousands of RL steps) is paid once, and its result is copied into as many cheaper students as you like. What does not transfer is anything the traces do not show: knowledge outside the problems you chose, behaviour in domains you did not sample, and the teacher's robustness on inputs unlike the training set.

Where the analogy breaks

A human apprentice understands why the master checks a joint. A student model learns the statistical pattern of checking, which usually transfers the benefit but can also produce empty imitation: chains full of "wait, let me verify" that verify nothing. And an apprentice who watched only the master's hands, never their own, struggles when their own work goes wrong in ways the master's never did. Models have the same problem, called exposure bias, and the deep section shows how on-policy distillation and RL address it.

1. Knowledge distillation: soft targets

The original idea (Hinton, Vinyals and Dean, 2015) was for classifiers. A large teacher's output is a probability distribution over classes, and that distribution contains more than the right answer: an image of a 7 that gets probability 0.01 for "1" and 0.0001 for "3" tells you something about which classes are similar. Training a student to match the teacher's whole distribution, the soft targets, transfers that structure. The loss is the KL divergence from the teacher's distribution p to the student's q (ml5):

KL(p ‖ q) = Σ_v p(v) · log( p(v) / q(v) )        = cross-entropy(p, q) − entropy(p)

For language models the same applies at every token position: the teacher gives a distribution over the vocabulary for the next token, and the student is trained to match it. This is logit distillation (or token-level distillation).

By hand, with a three-word vocabulary: the teacher predicts p = [0.7, 0.2, 0.1] and the student q = [0.5, 0.3, 0.2]. KL(p ‖ q) = 0.7 ln(0.7/0.5) + 0.2 ln(0.2/0.3) + 0.1 ln(0.1/0.2) = 0.7 × 0.336 + 0.2 × (−0.405) + 0.1 × (−0.693) = 0.236 − 0.081 − 0.069 = 0.085 nats. Compare training on a single sampled token: if the teacher's output at this position was word 0, the ordinary cross-entropy loss is −ln 0.5 = 0.693, and its gradient pushes up word 0 while saying nothing about whether word 1 is more plausible than word 2. The soft target carries that information in every position, which makes logit distillation more sample-efficient.

Hinton et al. also divided the logits by a temperature T > 1 before the softmax for both teacher and student, flattening the distributions so that small probabilities, where the "dark knowledge" about similarities lives, contribute more to the loss, and multiplied the loss by T² to keep gradient magnitudes comparable.

2. Sequence-level distillation: training on the teacher's text

Logit distillation needs the teacher's full probability vectors at every position, a shared vocabulary between teacher and student, and running the teacher alongside training. For reasoning distillation those conditions often fail: teachers are accessed through APIs that return text, students use different tokenizers, and traces are tens of thousands of tokens long. Sequence-level distillation (Kim and Rush, 2016) sidesteps all of it: generate complete outputs with the teacher and fine-tune the student on them with ordinary supervised fine-tuning (lm6), next-token cross-entropy on the generated tokens.

It may look like a crude approximation, but it has a strong advantage for reasoning: whole outputs can be checked. A logit target at a position cannot be marked right or wrong; a complete solution can. So sequence-level distillation combines naturally with filtering, and the dataset can contain only solutions that reached the correct answer, written in the style you want. DeepSeek-R1's distilled models were made this way, and so are most open reasoning models.

3. Rejection sampling: yield and cost

Building the dataset means generating several candidate traces per problem and keeping the good ones, rejection sampling. If the teacher solves a problem with probability p per sample and you draw k samples, the chance of getting at least one correct trace is 1 − (1 − p)^k, the same formula as pass@k (rs2). By hand: with p = 0.7 and k = 4, 1 − 0.3^4 = 1 − 0.0081 = 0.992. With p = 0.2, 1 − 0.8^4 = 0.590. With p = 0 the problem yields nothing, however many samples you draw: distillation can only transfer what the teacher can do.

Cost is dominated by generation. By hand: 100,000 problems × 4 samples × 6,000 tokens per trace = 2.4 × 10⁹ generated tokens. At a hypothetical $2 per million output tokens that is $4,800; on your own GPUs it is the throughput arithmetic of lm13. The lab simulates such a run with per-problem teacher accuracy drawn from a skewed distribution (mean about 0.67): about 93% of problems yield at least one correct trace, giving about 267,000 correct traces in total, and the generation spent per problem kept is about 25,700 tokens. Two refinements cut the cost a lot: stop sampling a problem as soon as one trace passes, and spend more samples only on problems where the first attempts failed (hard problems are the most valuable to keep).

Correctness is not the only filter. DeepSeek also removed traces with mixed languages, very long paragraphs and code blocks, because R1's raw reasoning was sometimes chaotic and hard to read, and for problems whose answers could not be checked by rules they used DeepSeek-V3 as a judge comparing the prediction with the reference (a generative reward model, rs5). Further filters common in practice: deduplication, decontamination against evaluation benchmarks, length limits, and removal of traces that reach the right answer with visibly wrong reasoning.

4. What the student learns from a trace

Fine-tuning on a trace trains the student to predict each of its tokens given the problem and everything before it. That covers the final answer, but most of the tokens, and therefore most of the gradient, belong to the reasoning: the setup, the attempts, the "wait, that's not right" and the corrections. This is why distillation transfers a procedure and not just answers. It is also why trace quality matters so much. A trace that wanders for 20,000 tokens before stumbling on the answer teaches wandering; a trace with a wrong intermediate step that happens to reach the right answer teaches that step. The student learns the distribution of traces it is shown, including their flaws.

5. STaR: bootstrapping from your own correct rationales

What if there is no stronger teacher? The Self-Taught Reasoner (STaR; Zelikman, Wu, Mu and Goodman, 2022) showed that a model can be its own teacher if you have a way to check answers. The loop:

  1. Prompt the model with a few examples of rationales and have it generate a rationale and an answer for every training problem.
  2. Keep the rationales whose answers are correct.
  3. Rationalization: for each problem it got wrong, give the model the correct answer as a hint and ask it to generate a rationale that reaches it. Keep those that do, and store them without the hint, as if the model had produced them unaided.
  4. Fine-tune the original base model on all kept rationales.
  5. Repeat with the fine-tuned model generating.

The filter in step 2 is the learning signal: the model's own correct outputs are, on average, better than its typical outputs, so training on them raises the typical quality, and the next round's correct outputs are better still. Rationalization addresses the obvious limit, that a model cannot learn from problems it never solves, by letting it learn explanations for answers it could not find but can justify. Using GPT-J, a 6-billion-parameter model, STaR reached 72.5% on the CommonsenseQA benchmark, comparable to a fine-tuned GPT-3 model 30 times larger (73.0%), and improved substantially on arithmetic. The lab's toy version shows the shape: accuracy rises over rounds, and faster with rationalization (from 0.44 to 0.64 over six rounds, against 0.37 to 0.43 without it).

STaR is a precursor of today's methods. Its filter-and-fine-tune step is rejection-sampling fine-tuning; with the filter replaced by a reward and the fine-tuning replaced by a policy-gradient step, it becomes RL with verifiable rewards (rs4). DeepSeekMath's "unified paradigm" makes the same point: these methods differ mainly in where the samples come from and how each sample's gradient is weighted.

6. Why distillation beats RL on small models

DeepSeek-R1's paper ran the comparison directly. Qwen2.5-32B-Base trained with large-scale RL on maths, code and STEM data for over 10,000 steps (they call the result Qwen2.5-32B-Zero) reached 47.0% on AIME 2024, on par with Alibaba's QwQ-32B-Preview (50.0%). The same base fine-tuned on R1's outputs, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B, reached 72.6%, and beat the RL model on every reasoning benchmark in the table: 94.3% against 91.6% on MATH-500, 62.1% against 55.0% on GPQA Diamond, 57.2% against 40.2% on LiveCodeBench.

The explanation follows from rs4. RL only reinforces behaviours the policy already produces sometimes; it sharpens the distribution. Discovering long, self-correcting reasoning from scratch requires a capable base and very large amounts of RL compute, and a smaller base has fewer such behaviours to sharpen. Distillation does not wait for the student to discover anything: it shows the patterns directly, thousands of times. Yue et al. reached the same conclusion from pass@k measurements: RL-trained models' solutions were already within the base model's sampling distribution, while distillation genuinely expanded the set of problems the student could solve. The R1 authors' two conclusions are worth quoting in substance: distilling a more powerful model into a smaller one gives excellent results economically, while advancing beyond the current frontier still needs stronger base models and larger-scale RL.

7. How much data: the s1 result

DeepSeek used 800,000 samples. How many are needed? The s1 study (Muennighoff et al., 2025) pushed in the opposite direction. From 59,029 candidate questions drawn from 16 sources, with reasoning traces generated by Google's Gemini Flash Thinking API, they selected 1,000 by three criteria: quality (drop samples with formatting problems), difficulty (keep questions that two Qwen2.5 models of 7B and 32B both failed, preferring longer traces) and diversity (spread across subject areas). They fine-tuned Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct on these 1,000 examples in 26 minutes on 16 H100 GPUs.

The ablations are the instructive part. On AIME 2024 (with a cap of about 30,000 thinking tokens), the curated 1,000 scored 50.0%; 1,000 random questions from the pool scored 36.7%; 1,000 chosen only for diversity 26.7%; the 1,000 with the longest traces 33.3%; and training on all 59,000 scored 53.3%, with a confidence interval for the difference from s1K of −13% to +20%, at 394 H100 GPU hours against 7. No single criterion worked alone; combined, a thousand well-chosen traces captured most of the gain. The reason echoes LIMA (lm6): the base model already holds most of the necessary knowledge, and the examples teach a behaviour, writing long, self-checking chains, which can be learned from a modest number of high-quality demonstrations. Scale matters more when the student must also learn new knowledge or many domains.

8. Budget forcing: controlling thinking length

s1 also introduced a simple test-time control. Reasoning models delimit their thinking with special tokens (for example, <think> and </think>). Budget forcing uses them: to cap thinking, insert the end-of-thinking token once a token budget is reached, forcing an answer; to extend thinking, suppress the end-of-thinking token when the model tries to emit it and append "Wait" instead, which tends to make it re-examine its work. On AIME 2024, extending s1-32B's thinking this way raised its score from 50% to 57%, though the gains flatten and too many forced continuations can loop. Budget forcing is the open-weight cousin of the effort and thinking-budget parameters that commercial APIs expose (rs7).

9. A practical recipe

For a team distilling reasoning into a small model for its own domain, the evidence supports a short recipe. Collect real, decontaminated problems with checkable answers. Generate several traces per problem from a strong open teacher whose licence permits it. Keep correct, readable traces of reasonable length, and favour hard and diverse problems over volume. Fine-tune a student (full fine-tuning or LoRA, lm11) for a few epochs; DeepSeek used 2-3 epochs with a cosine learning-rate schedule and a 32,768-token context. Mix in some general data so the student does not forget other skills. Evaluate at several thinking budgets on held-out problems, then consider a short RL stage with the same checker to sharpen and to shorten traces.

10. Distill first, then RL

Distillation and RL are best seen as two stages with different jobs. Distillation moves the student into the right region quickly: after fine-tuning on teacher traces it writes long, self-checking chains and solves a large fraction of problems. That is exactly the precondition RL needs (rs4): a policy whose pass rate on training prompts sits in the middle, so groups of samples disagree and carry signal. A short RL stage with the same checker then trains the student on its own outputs, which fixes exposure bias, sharpens accuracy, and can be combined with a length penalty to cut the overthinking inherited from the teacher. This is why so many open reasoning models released after R1 are "R1-distilled base plus GRPO". The ordering matters: RL on an undistilled small base wastes most of its samples on groups that are all wrong, while distillation without RL leaves the student imitating a teacher it cannot fully match.

11. Choosing the student

Three properties of the student base matter more than its raw size. Domain pretraining: DeepSeek built its two smallest students on Qwen2.5-Math, which had already seen a great deal of mathematics, and those students did far better than general bases of the same size would be expected to. Context length: traces of 10,000 to 30,000 tokens need a base that handles long contexts in training and at inference, or traces must be shortened. Tokenizer and chat template: sequence-level distillation does not need the teacher's tokenizer, but the student's template must represent the thinking delimiters cleanly. Size still sets a ceiling, as the bar chart shows, so start from the smallest student that meets your accuracy target at your latency budget, measured on your own problems.

Forward and reverse KL: what the student is asked to do

Training on teacher samples (sequence-level distillation) or minimising KL(teacher ‖ student) token by token are both "forward KL": the student is penalised wherever the teacher puts probability and the student does not. Forward KL is mode-covering: a student without enough capacity to represent everything the teacher does spreads its probability over all of it, producing outputs that average incompatible behaviours, which for text can mean incoherent blends. Reverse KL, KL(student ‖ teacher), penalises the student for putting probability where the teacher does not; it is mode-seeking, so a limited student concentrates on a subset of the teacher's behaviours and does them well. Reverse KL has to be estimated on the student's own samples, which leads to on-policy distillation.

Exposure bias and on-policy distillation

A student trained only on teacher text has only ever seen the teacher's prefixes. At inference it conditions on its own prefixes, and once it makes an error it is in a state no training example covered, where it may compound the error rather than recover from it. This exposure bias is worse for long outputs, which reasoning traces are. On-policy distillation (for example generalized knowledge distillation, Agarwal et al., 2024) fixes it by letting the student generate and having the teacher score the student's tokens (its log-probabilities at each position), training the student to move towards the teacher on the student's own trajectories. It needs an open teacher that can be run during training, with a compatible tokenizer, and it costs teacher forward passes on every student sample; in return each token gets dense, per-position feedback instead of RL's single bit. An RL stage after distillation addresses exposure bias more cheaply, with a sparser signal.

Distillation as importance sampling of the teacher's ability

One way to see why the R1 distillation result is so large: a 32B base model sampled at temperature 1 might produce an R1-quality chain on a hard problem with probability 10⁻⁶; RL must sample enough to find such chains and then reinforce them, which is prohibitively slow if they are that rare. The teacher produces such chains with probability near one. Distillation is therefore a way of sampling from the region of chain space the student would almost never visit, and teaching the student to go there. That is also why distillation, unlike RL, can expand pass@k at large k: it moves probability onto chains outside the student's previous effective support.

The capacity gap

Students much smaller than their teacher sometimes learn less from it than from a medium-sized teacher. A plausible mechanism is the mode-covering behaviour above: the largest teacher's distribution is too complex for the small student, while an intermediate teacher's outputs are simpler and more imitable. In reasoning distillation the gap appears as small students producing long chains that mimic the teacher's checking without its reliability. Practical responses include shorter or simplified traces for small students, distilling in stages (large to medium to small), choosing a student base with strong domain pretraining (DeepSeek's 1.5B and 7B students were based on Qwen2.5-Math, specialised maths models), and an RL stage after distillation to make the student's own behaviour reliable.

What data selection is really selecting

s1's three criteria map onto mechanisms. Quality removes examples that teach the wrong format or garbled reasoning, which a student would imitate faithfully. Difficulty ensures examples need long reasoning, so the student learns the long, self-checking behaviour rather than short answers; easy examples teach the student that problems end quickly. Diversity spreads the behaviour across domains so it generalises rather than attaching to one problem type. Length is a cheap proxy for difficulty, but a poor one on its own: in s1's ablation, selecting only the 1,000 longest traces scored 33.3% against 50.0% for the combined criteria, because length also selects for wandering traces and ignores quality and coverage. Selection matters most when data is small; at DeepSeek's 800,000 samples the law of large numbers smooths over individual bad examples, but systematic flaws in the teacher (overthinking, a particular error pattern) still transfer.

Contamination through the teacher

A teacher may have memorised benchmark problems, and its traces on related training problems can leak those problems' solutions, or the benchmark problems themselves may appear in a scraped problem pool. A student distilled on such data scores well on the benchmark for the wrong reason. Decontaminate the problem pool against every evaluation set you will report (by n-gram overlap and by embedding similarity), and evaluate on problems released after both the teacher's and the student's training data were collected (rs8).

Licences and hidden chains

Distillation has a non-technical dimension. Many commercial terms of service restrict using model outputs to develop competing models, and several reasoning APIs do not return the raw chain of thought, only a summary or nothing, which limits what can be distilled from them. Open-weight teachers vary in licence; DeepSeek released R1 under the MIT licence and explicitly permitted distillation. For a company, the source and licence of every trace in a distillation set is a compliance question, not just an engineering one.

The public case: DeepSeek-R1's distilled models (DeepSeek-AI, 2025)

Alongside R1, DeepSeek released six distilled models built on other companies' open bases: Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Qwen2.5-14B, Qwen2.5-32B, Llama-3.1-8B and Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct. All were produced by supervised fine-tuning only, with no RL stage, on the same dataset of about 800,000 samples generated with R1. That dataset was R1's own SFT data: roughly 600,000 reasoning samples from rejection sampling on the first-stage RL checkpoint (for each prompt, several responses sampled and only correct ones kept, with chains containing mixed languages, long paragraphs or code blocks filtered out, and some answers judged by DeepSeek-V3 against references where rules could not check them) and roughly 200,000 non-reasoning samples (writing, factual question answering, self-cognition, translation and software engineering) partly reused from DeepSeek-V3's SFT data. Each student was fine-tuned for 2-3 epochs with a cosine learning-rate schedule decaying to one-tenth of its starting value, starting rates from 1×10⁻⁴ (1.5B) down to 2×10⁻⁵ (70B), a maximum context of 32,768 tokens and a batch size of 64.

The results (the paper's Table 15), as AIME 2024 pass@1 / MATH-500 pass@1: Distill-Qwen-1.5B 28.9 / 83.9; Distill-Qwen-7B 55.5 / 92.8; Distill-Qwen-14B 69.7 / 93.9; Distill-Qwen-32B 72.6 / 94.3; Distill-Llama-8B 50.4 / 89.1; Distill-Llama-70B 70.0 / 94.5. For comparison, GPT-4o (May 2024 version) scored 9.3 / 74.6 and Claude 3.5 Sonnet (October 2024) 16.0 / 78.3, and R1 itself 79.8% on AIME 2024. The paper notes that the 1.5B model surpasses these non-reasoning baselines on the maths benchmarks, and that performance improves with student size. With a 64-sample majority vote, the 32B student reached 83.3% on AIME 2024.

The comparison that made the case was Table 16: the same Qwen2.5-32B base trained with large-scale RL alone for over 10,000 steps reached 47.0% on AIME 2024 against the distilled model's 72.6%, and lost on every benchmark listed. DeepSeek also reported that they deliberately skipped an RL stage on the distilled models "even though incorporating RL could substantially boost model performance", leaving it to the community, which promptly did it: many later small open reasoning models start from an R1-distilled checkpoint and add GRPO.

The release changed the practical landscape. Within weeks, distilled R1 models were the default way to run open reasoning locally, quantised versions ran on consumer hardware, and the recipe (generate with a strong reasoner, filter, fine-tune a small base) became the standard way to build domain reasoners. It also showed the limits: users found the small distilled models prone to very long, looping chains on easy questions and weaker outside maths and code, exactly the failure modes the deep section predicts for a small student imitating a large teacher's style.

The serving arithmetic that justifies it

The business case is usually made per correct answer. Suppose a teacher served through an API answers your domain problems at 85% accuracy with traces averaging 5,000 output tokens at $2 per million, and a distilled 14B student you host answers at 80% with traces averaging 4,000 tokens, at an effective $0.20 per million on your own GPUs. Per query, the teacher costs 5,000 × 2 / 10⁶ = $0.010 and the student 4,000 × 0.2 / 10⁶ = $0.0008. Per correct answer, that is $0.0118 against $0.0010, about twelve times cheaper, for five points of accuracy. At 1 million queries a month the saving is around $9,000, against a one-off distillation cost of a few thousand dollars of generation plus fine-tuning. The five-point gap is the decision: if those errors are costly, route the student's low-confidence cases (for example, disagreement between two samples) to the teacher. The numbers here are illustrative; the method of comparing cost per correct answer at matched latency is the point.

Failure modes in practice

Inherited overthinking. The student copies the teacher's thinking length, including on easy inputs. Mitigate with length-filtered traces, examples of short reasoning for easy problems, budgets at inference, or a short RL stage with a length penalty.

Surface imitation. Chains that say "let me verify" and verify nothing, or that re-derive the same wrong step. Detect by evaluating on problems harder than the training set and reading failures; mitigate with harder, more diverse traces and an RL stage.

Forgetting. A student fine-tuned only on maths traces gets worse at chat, instruction following or other languages. Mix in general data (DeepSeek's 200,000 non-reasoning samples did this) and keep a regression suite.

Format drift. The student's output format (think tags, answer boxing) differs from what the application parses. Make the format part of the filter and the evaluation.

Benchmark contamination. Scores look better than real-world performance. Decontaminate and evaluate on fresh problems.

What to measure

For the dataset: yield per problem, distribution of trace lengths, fraction filtered for each reason, and overlap with evaluation sets. For the student: held-out accuracy at several thinking budgets against the teacher, mean and p95 reasoning tokens, accuracy on easy inputs at low budgets (overthinking), and general-capability regressions. The decision metric is usually cost per correct answer at your latency limit, student against teacher.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 1) Token-level distillation on a 3-word vocabulary.
p = np.array([0.7, 0.2, 0.1])            # teacher's next-token distribution
q = np.array([0.5, 0.3, 0.2])            # student's
kl = np.sum(p * np.log(p / q)); H = -np.sum(p * np.log(p))
print(f"KL(teacher||student) = {kl:.4f}   soft cross-entropy = H(p)+KL = {H + kl:.4f}")
print(f"hard-label cross-entropy on the teacher's sampled token (word 0) = {-np.log(q[0]):.4f}")

# 2) Rejection sampling: k samples per problem, keep correct ones, at most one per problem.
N, k, L = 100_000, 4, 6_000              # problems, samples each, tokens per trace
acc = rng.beta(2.0, 1.0, N)              # teacher accuracy per problem (mean about 0.67)
correct = rng.random((N, k)) < acc[:, None]
kept_problems = correct.any(1).sum()
print(f"generated {N*k*L/1e9:.1f}B tokens; problems with >=1 correct trace: {kept_problems:,} "
      f"({kept_problems/N:.1%}); all correct traces: {correct.sum():,}")
print(f"tokens generated per kept problem: {N*k*L/kept_problems:,.0f}")

# 3) Toy STaR: a "model" with skill s solves a problem of difficulty d with prob sigmoid(s - d).
#    Each round it trains on its own correct rationales; skill grows with the hardest problems
#    it solved. Rationalization (hint = answer) lets it also learn from problems it failed.
def star(rounds=6, rationalize=False):
    s, d = 0.0, rng.uniform(-2, 4, 5000); hist = []
    for _ in range(rounds):
        solved = rng.random(d.size) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(s - d)))
        learn = d[solved]
        if rationalize:                  # with the answer as a hint, it can explain harder ones
            hinted = (~solved) & (rng.random(d.size) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(s + 1.5 - d))))
            learn = np.concatenate([learn, d[hinted]])
        s = 0.5 * s + 0.5 * np.percentile(learn, 75)   # move towards what it can now explain
        hist.append((1 / (1 + np.exp(-(s - d)))).mean())
    return np.round(hist, 3)
print("STaR accuracy by round           :", star())
print("STaR + rationalization by round  :", star(rationalize=True))
Estimate

You want to distil a reasoning teacher into a 7B model for insurance-claims calculations. You have 40,000 problems with verified answers. The teacher solves 60% of them per sample on average and writes 5,000-token traces. You sample up to 4 times per problem, stopping at the first correct trace. Estimate the generated tokens and the number of problems that yield a trace (assume every problem has p = 0.6).

Answer

Yield: 1 − 0.4^4 = 1 − 0.0256 = 97.4%, about 38,980 problems. Expected samples per problem with early stopping: 1 + 0.4 + 0.4² + 0.4³ = 1 + 0.4 + 0.16 + 0.064 = 1.624. Tokens: 40,000 × 1.624 × 5,000 ≈ 325 million, against 800 million if you always drew 4. In reality p varies by problem: easy ones stop after one sample, and the remaining budget concentrates on hard ones, which are the most valuable to keep. Before fine-tuning, filter for readability and length, decontaminate against your evaluation set, and consider keeping only the hardest and most diverse few thousand if the s1 result applies to your domain.

Diagnose

Your distilled 7B model matches the teacher on your test set's easy and medium problems but scores 25 points lower on the hard ones, and its failed chains are often longer than its successful ones, full of "wait, let me reconsider". What is happening and what do you try?

Answer

Surface imitation plus exposure bias: on hard problems the student makes an early error the teacher would not, then reproduces the teacher's style of re-checking without the competence to find the error, looping. Try: more hard, diverse traces (and fewer easy ones) in the distillation set; a short GRPO stage with your checker so the student learns from its own mistakes; on-policy distillation if you can run the teacher; a larger student or a stronger maths-pretrained base; and a thinking budget at inference so loops end with a best guess rather than a timeout.
