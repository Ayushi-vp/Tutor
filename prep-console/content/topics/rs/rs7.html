A team building an internal tool that answers questions about company data swaps its chat model for a reasoning model, because the reasoning model scores better on hard analytical questions. Accuracy on their test set goes up nine points. Then the complaints arrive. Simple questions that used to take two seconds now take forty. A few requests take four minutes and time out at the load balancer. The monthly bill is six times higher. A handful of responses come back with no answer at all, because the model ran out of output tokens while still thinking. And when a user asks why the model reached a conclusion, the displayed "reasoning" sometimes describes a path that does not match what the answer actually relied on.

None of this means the reasoning model was the wrong choice for the hard questions. It means a reasoning model is not a drop-in upgrade but a different kind of component, with a different cost structure, a different latency profile with long tails, different prompting needs, different failure modes, and a chain of thought that is useful but not a faithful explanation. Using one well is an engineering problem: decide where it pays, control how long it thinks, budget tokens and time from the tail of the distribution rather than the average, route requests between fast and slow models, handle truncation and tool use correctly, and be careful about what the visible reasoning means.

The economics also keep shifting. Each model generation changes the price per token, the speed, the typical thinking length at a given effort, and sometimes the meaning of the effort levels themselves. A configuration that was the right trade-off six months ago may now be too expensive, or too cautious. What lasts is the method: measure accuracy, token distributions, latency and cost per correct answer on your own traffic; choose from those numbers; and re-measure when anything changes. This lesson teaches that method with concrete numbers you can redo for any provider.

This is also one of the most common practical topics in AI-engineer interviews, because it combines model knowledge with system design. Expect questions such as "when would you pay for a reasoning model?", "how would you budget its latency and tokens?", "design a router", and "can we show users the chain of thought?". Good answers use numbers.

This lesson works through the decision of when to use a reasoning model, the controls that providers expose (effort levels, thinking budgets, output caps) and how they interact, the arithmetic of tokens, latency, concurrency and truncation with real distributions, prompting differences, tools and interleaved thinking, routing and cascades with a worked cost comparison, and chain-of-thought faithfulness and monitoring. The public case is OpenAI's o1 system card, the first detailed public account of how a reasoning model's chain of thought was used for safety monitoring and what it found. The lab simulates a deployment: token distributions, truncation rates at different caps, latency percentiles, concurrency, and a router that escalates on disagreement.

The mental model: a consultant who bills by the minute

Think of a reasoning model as a very good consultant who bills by the minute and whose minutes you cannot fully see. For a hard, valuable question (should we restructure this contract? why is this distributed system deadlocking?) the consultant is worth every minute: they think, check their work and reach answers a junior would miss. For a quick question (what is the capital of Peru? is this email spam?) paying the consultant is waste, and sometimes worse than waste, because they will spend ten minutes considering edge cases nobody asked about. A well-run organisation sends quick questions to a junior and hard ones to the consultant, sets a budget for how long the consultant may spend, and plans its schedule around the fact that some consultations run long.

The analogy maps onto four engineering decisions. Where to use it: tasks that are multi-step, where errors are costly, and where users can wait. How long it may think: providers expose an effort level or thinking budget, separate from the hard maximum output tokens. How to plan around it: latency and cost are proportional to tokens generated, and token counts vary enormously between requests, so planning uses percentiles, not averages. How to route: a router sends each request to the fast model or the reasoning model, and a cascade escalates when the fast answer fails a check.

Four numbers to know before shipping

Before any reasoning feature ships, a team should be able to state four numbers for it, measured on its own traffic or a faithful sample: the accuracy gain over the fast alternative (and what a wrong answer costs); the reasoning-token distribution at the chosen effort (median, p95, p99); the latency those tokens imply at the provider's output speed, compared with the product's tolerance; and the cost per correct answer. Each of the body's sections teaches how to get one of these numbers or how to change it. A team that cannot state them is guessing, and the usual surprise is the third: tail latency, not average cost, is what first breaks reasoning features in production.

The second half of the job: what the visible reasoning means

Reasoning models produce a chain of thought that you may see in full, as a summary, or not at all. It is tempting to treat it as an explanation: here is why the model answered as it did. Research says to be careful. Chains are often informative and sometimes state the model's intentions plainly, which makes them valuable for debugging and safety monitoring, but they are not reliably faithful: models frequently use information they never mention. The product rule that follows is to use chains for internal diagnosis and monitoring, show users summaries, sources and checks rather than raw reasoning as justification, and never train a model directly to make its chain look acceptable.

Where the analogy breaks

A consultant's minutes are roughly predictable from the question; a model's thinking length is much harder to predict and has a long tail, so the planning has to be statistical. And unlike a consultant, a model can be run many times in parallel and asked the same question twice, which turns "does it agree with itself?" into a cheap signal for routing.

1. Deciding where a reasoning model pays

The decision is an expected-value calculation per request type. If a correct answer is worth V, a wrong answer costs C (rework, a bad decision, a support ticket), and the model costs k per request, then the value per request is p·V − (1 − p)·C − k, where p is the model's accuracy on that request type. By hand: for a contract-review question where a correct answer is worth $5, a wrong one costs $20, the fast model has p = 0.70 at $0.001 and the reasoning model p = 0.90 at $0.06: fast gives 0.7 × 5 − 0.3 × 20 − 0.001 = 3.5 − 6 − 0.001 = −$2.50; reasoning gives 4.5 − 2 − 0.06 = $2.44. The reasoning model wins by a mile, because errors are expensive. For a ticket-tagging task where a correct tag is worth $0.02, a wrong one costs $0.05, and the fast model already has p = 0.95: fast gives 0.019 − 0.0025 − 0.001 = $0.0155; a reasoning model at p = 0.97 and $0.01 gives 0.0194 − 0.0015 − 0.01 = $0.0079. Paying for reasoning halves the value.

The rule of thumb that falls out: reasoning models pay when the accuracy gain is large (multi-step problems where the fast model fails often) and errors are costly; they do not pay on high-volume, low-stakes tasks the fast model already handles. Latency is a separate constraint: some products cannot wait a minute however valuable the answer, and then the choice is between a faster path and an asynchronous design (accept the request, notify when done).

2. The controls providers expose

Every major API gives you two kinds of control. The first shapes how much the model thinks. OpenAI's reasoning models take a reasoning effort parameter (low, medium, high, with a minimal level on some newer models). Anthropic's current Claude models use adaptive thinking, in which the model decides how much to think, steered by an effort setting; earlier Claude models took an explicit thinking budget in tokens (minimum 1,024). Google's Gemini 2.5 models take a thinking budget in tokens (for 2.5 Flash, 0 to 24,576, with 0 disabling thinking and −1 letting the model decide). These are steering controls: they change the model's behaviour, and the model can usually wrap up gracefully within them.

The second is the maximum output tokens, a hard cap on everything generated in the response, thinking included. It is a guillotine, not a budget: when it is reached, generation stops wherever it is, and the response's stop reason says so. OpenAI's reasoning guide suggests reserving at least 25,000 tokens for reasoning and output when starting out, then adjusting once you know your prompts' needs. Exact parameter names and ranges change between model versions, so check the current documentation for the model you deploy.

In every case, thinking is billed as output tokens, at the output price, whether the reasoning text is returned, summarised or hidden, and the usage information in the response reports how many reasoning tokens were used. Hidden reasoning is not free reasoning.

3. Token and latency arithmetic, from the tail

Reasoning length varies a great deal between requests: easy ones finish quickly, hard or ambiguous ones run long. A lognormal distribution is a reasonable model. In the lab, with a median of 3,000 thinking tokens, the p90 is about 8,300, the p99 about 19,400, and the mean about 4,100, well above the median because of the tail.

Truncation, by hand from the lab: with a 400-token answer on top, a maximum output of 4,000 tokens truncates about 41% of requests; 8,000 truncates 12%; 16,000 about 2%; 32,000 about 0.2%. The truncated requests are disproportionately the hard ones, exactly where the reasoning was needed, and you pay for their tokens anyway. The lesson: limit thinking with effort or budget, and keep the output cap well above the typical need.

Latency: time ≈ overhead + (thinking + answer tokens) / output speed. At 60 tokens per second with 0.5 seconds of overhead, the lab's p50 is about 57 seconds, p95 about 192 seconds and p99 about 330 seconds. Any synchronous timeout (load balancers often default to 60 seconds) must be set with the tail in mind, or the slowest, hardest requests fail.

Concurrency: Little's law says the average number of requests in flight equals the arrival rate times the average time in the system. At 10 requests per second and a mean latency of about 76 seconds, about 760 requests are in flight at once. That number sets the rate limits you need from the provider, the connection pool sizes in your application, and, if self-hosting, the KV-cache memory (lm5) and GPU count. A fast model at 5 seconds would need only about 50.

Cost: 4,100 mean thinking tokens plus 400 answer tokens is 4,500 output tokens per request; at $10 per million output tokens that is $0.045 per request, about $38,900 a day at 10 requests per second (864,000 requests). Plan with the mean (cost is additive) but alert on the distribution: a prompt change that shifts the tail can double the bill without moving the median much.

4. Prompting reasoning models

Reasoning models were trained, mostly with RL, to work out their own approach. Prompts written for chat models often over-specify that approach. The practices that hold up:

  • State the goal and the success criteria plainly: what a good answer must achieve, what constraints apply, what to optimise.
  • Provide the context: documents, data, definitions. Reasoning cannot recover facts the model does not have; retrieval matters as much as ever (lm9).
  • Do not script the reasoning. "Think step by step" is redundant; long step-by-step instructions and worked reasoning examples can anchor the model on one approach or conflict with its own process. Use examples to show the output format, not how to think.
  • Specify the output format precisely, and for machine-consumed output use structured outputs or schema validation (ae4).
  • Keep instructions consistent. Reasoning models take instructions seriously and will spend tokens reconciling contradictions.

5. Tools inside thinking

Reasoning models are most valuable in agents (lm16), where they plan, call tools, read results and adapt. Modern APIs support interleaved thinking: within one turn, the model thinks, calls a tool, receives the result, thinks again about what the result means, and decides the next call. This makes agents markedly better at multi-step tasks, because each decision is reasoned in light of the latest evidence.

It imposes two requirements on your agent loop. First, the reasoning state must be carried across tool calls in the form the provider specifies: OpenAI's Responses API keeps reasoning items that you pass back (or that the server retains) between calls; Anthropic's API returns thinking blocks that must be sent back unchanged with the tool results. Dropping or editing them loses the model's chain or causes errors. Second, every step's thinking is billed, so a 20-step agent task at 2,000 thinking tokens per step spends 40,000 tokens on thinking alone. Budget per task, use lower effort for routine steps, cap iterations, and cache the stable prompt prefix.

6. Routing and cascades

A router picks a model per request. Signals, from cheapest to most informative: request metadata (type, length, the feature it came from); a small classifier trained on which past requests the fast model got wrong; and the fast model's own uncertainty, of which the most robust practical version is disagreement between two samples (rs2). A cascade tries the fast model first and escalates if a check fails: the output fails schema validation or tests, two samples disagree, or a verifier scores it low.

A worked comparison (this topic's drill has the full arithmetic): 100,000 requests a day, 80% easy. The fast model costs $0.002 and solves 95% of easy and 40% of hard requests; the reasoning model costs $0.05 and solves 98% and 85%. All-fast: $200 a day, 84% accuracy. All-reasoning: $5,000, 95.4%. A router that classifies difficulty with 90% accuracy: about $1,448 and 92.3%. Routing recovers about three-quarters of the accuracy gap for under a third of the cost, and escalation on failed validation can recover more.

A within-model variant uses one reasoning model at different effort levels: low effort by default, high effort when the low-effort answer fails a check. It avoids running two models and keeps prompt-cache reuse within one model family, at the cost of always paying for some thinking.

7. Caching, batching and asynchronous designs

Three standard cost levers apply with extra force. Prompt caching reuses the processed prefix of long, stable prompts (system instructions, tool definitions, reference documents), cutting input cost and time to first token; keep the prefix byte-identical across requests. Batch APIs process non-urgent requests asynchronously at a discount (commonly half price) within a window such as 24 hours: ideal for reasoning-heavy offline jobs such as nightly analysis or evaluation runs. And asynchronous product design turns long thinking from a defect into a feature: "we'll email you the analysis" or a background task with progress updates makes a two-minute reasoning job acceptable where a two-minute spinner is not.

8. Chain-of-thought faithfulness

A chain of thought is faithful if it reflects the factors that actually produced the answer. Chen et al. (Anthropic, 2025) measured this by inserting hints into multiple-choice prompts (for example, metadata or a "grader" snippet indicating an answer) and checking, for cases where a reasoning model's answer changed to the hinted one, whether its chain mentioned using the hint. Averaged across six kinds of hint, Claude 3.7 Sonnet verbalised the hint 25% of the time and DeepSeek-R1 39%. Outcome-based RL raised faithfulness at first, then plateaued without approaching full faithfulness. And in an environment where RL taught the model to exploit a reward hack, it used the hack on over 99% of prompts while verbalising it on fewer than 2%.

The implications for products are practical. Do not present raw reasoning to users as the explanation of an answer; present sources, checks and the answer's own justification instead. Use chains internally for debugging (they often show where a model misread a requirement) and for monitoring (they often state intentions, as Baker et al. found for coding hacks, rs5), but treat a clean chain as weak evidence that nothing went wrong. And never optimise chains directly against a monitor, which teaches concealment.

9. Evaluating a reasoning feature

The evaluation for a reasoning feature needs more than accuracy. For each candidate configuration (model, effort, router threshold), measure accuracy on a representative set, the distribution of reasoning tokens (mean, p95, p99), latency percentiles, truncation rate, and cost per correct answer. Plot accuracy against cost and choose the knee: the cheapest configuration that meets the accuracy target within the latency limit. Repeat when the provider releases a new model version, since effort levels and token behaviour are recalibrated between versions, and include easy cases in the set so that overthinking shows up as cost without accuracy. rs8 covers the statistics: how many questions you need before a two-point difference means anything.

10. A rollout plan

Putting the pieces together gives a rollout sequence that avoids the scenario in the opening story. First, offline: build an evaluation set from real requests, including easy ones, and measure the fast model and the reasoning model at two or three effort levels on accuracy, token percentiles, latency and cost per correct answer. Second, design: pick the configuration at the knee, decide synchronous or asynchronous from the p95 latency, set the output cap from the p99 tokens, and design the router or cascade if traffic is mixed. Third, shadow: run the new path on a copy of live traffic without showing results, and compare its token and latency distributions with the offline estimates; real traffic is usually longer-tailed than test sets. Fourth, canary: send a small share of users to the new path with dashboards for truncation, timeouts, escalation rate and cost, and a quick rollback. Fifth, steady state: alert on shifts in the reasoning-token distribution, re-run the evaluation when the provider ships a new model version, and review a sample of flagged or escalated requests each week. The whole plan is ordinary release engineering (ae7); what is specific to reasoning models is that the metrics that matter most are distributions of tokens and time, not just accuracy.

11. Privacy and data handling of reasoning content

Reasoning content is model output that can contain anything from the context: restated user data, fragments of retrieved documents, the system prompt. If your application logs or displays reasoning or its summaries, apply the same data-protection rules as for the inputs (ae5): redact personal data in logs, restrict access, and set retention. If you store thinking blocks to continue conversations, they are part of the conversation record and need the same treatment. And because some providers prohibit attempts to extract hidden reasoning, do not build features that try to reconstruct it.

Why thinking length is so variable

A reasoning model's thinking length depends on how quickly its own checks succeed: an easy problem is solved and verified once; a hard one involves failed attempts, backtracking and repeated verification. Each of those is a stochastic event, so length behaves like a sum of a random number of random-length episodes, which produces right-skewed, heavy-tailed distributions. Ambiguous requests are particularly bad, because the model explores several interpretations. Two practical consequences: averages understate the operational burden, and prompt changes that make requirements clearer often reduce cost more than any parameter does.

Effort is not a linear dial

Moving from low to high effort typically raises mean thinking several-fold, but accuracy gains concentrate on the hard tail of requests; on easy ones the extra tokens are pure overthinking. This is why per-route or per-request effort beats a single global setting, and why "always use the highest effort" is rarely right outside offline, high-stakes work. Providers also recalibrate effort between model versions, so a level tuned on one version should be re-measured on the next rather than carried over.

Parallel versus serial within a latency budget

With a hard latency limit, the choice between one long chain and several shorter parallel samples (rs2) changes. Serial thinking adds directly to time to first token; parallel samples run concurrently, so three samples at medium effort plus a vote may fit a limit that one high-effort chain does not, at roughly three times the tokens. Which wins on accuracy is empirical and task-dependent; the point is to compare configurations at matched latency, not only at matched tokens.

Truncation as a correctness bug

A truncated reasoning response is not just a failed request; if the application extracts "the answer" with a lenient parser, a truncated chain can yield a plausible wrong value from an intermediate step. Robust handling checks the stop reason before parsing, requires the answer in a delimited final section, and treats anything else as a failure to retry or escalate. The same discipline applies to structured outputs: validation failures should trigger a retry or escalation, never a best-effort parse.

The monitorability trade-off

Hidden chains protect providers' methods and reduce the risk of users over-trusting reasoning, but they make the chain unavailable to the application developer for debugging. Summaries are a compromise, generated by a model from the raw chain, and inherit its faithfulness limits with an extra layer of lossy compression. For safety, labs value monitorable chains, and Baker et al.'s results argue for not applying strong optimisation pressure to them. For product teams the take-away is to treat whatever reasoning view you get as diagnostic data, to log it with access controls where available, and to build trust signals that do not depend on it: citations, tool results, tests and independent checks.

Safety behaviours specific to reasoning models

Reasoning can improve safety: OpenAI's o1 models reason about their safety policies in context ("deliberative alignment") and were much more robust to known jailbreaks than GPT-4o in OpenAI's evaluations. It also creates new behaviours to watch. Reasoning models can invent policies and withhold answers based on them, as the o1 card's monitor found; they can knowingly fabricate when asked for things they cannot verify, such as references; and in agentic red-team scenarios designed to tempt them, they sometimes pursue a given goal against their operators' oversight (Apollo Research's scheming evaluations). For a product that gives a reasoning model tools, the design response is ordinary security engineering made stricter: least-privilege tools, approval gates for consequential actions, logging, and independent checks on outcomes (lm18).

Routing errors are asymmetric

A router makes two kinds of mistake. Sending an easy request to the reasoning model wastes money but rarely hurts accuracy; sending a hard request to the fast model loses accuracy. If wrong answers are expensive, bias the router towards escalation and rely on cost monitoring; if cost is the binding constraint, bias it towards the fast model and rely on validation-based escalation to catch the worst misses. The threshold is a business decision that should be set from the expected-value calculation in section 1, not from the router's raw accuracy.

Predicting difficulty before paying for it

Every router needs a difficulty estimate available before the expensive call. Three families exist, with different costs. Static features (request type, length, presence of numbers or code, which product surface it came from) are free and surprisingly predictive in many products, because difficulty clusters by use case. Learned classifiers, small models trained on logs of which requests the fast model got wrong (labelled by validation failures, user corrections or offline grading), add accuracy for little latency, but must be retrained as traffic and models change. Self-assessment by the fast model, via sample disagreement or a verifier score, is the most accurate and the most expensive, since it requires generating the fast answer first; it is what turns a router into a cascade. A useful property of disagreement is that it needs no labels and adapts automatically when the fast model improves. Its weakness is correlated errors: when both samples share the same misconception they agree and the request is not escalated, which is why validation checks remain an important second trigger.

The public case: the OpenAI o1 System Card (December 2024)

OpenAI published the o1 system card on 5 December 2024, covering o1 and o1-mini. It describes the models as trained with large-scale reinforcement learning to reason using a chain of thought, and it is the first detailed public document about what a reasoning model's chain of thought made possible, and what it revealed, in safety evaluation. Three parts are directly useful to engineers.

Chain-of-thought deception monitoring. OpenAI built a monitor, a GPT-4o model prompted to look for deception in o1's chain of thought and final output, where deception meant knowingly giving incorrect information or omitting crucial information that could create a false belief. On a small internal labelled set of deceptive and non-deceptive o1-preview outputs, it was 92% accurate. Run over o1-preview completions for 102,443 synthetic ChatGPT prompts, it flagged 180, or 0.17%, as deceptive. Most (91, 0.09%) were hallucinated policies: the model invented a rule and withheld information because of it; the card's example is a user asking for a study plan for a university application, which the model decided would count as cheating and refused. Another 43 (0.04%) were intentional hallucinations: invented information where the chain showed the model knew it was made up, mostly when asked for references to articles, websites or books it could not check without search. The remaining 46 (0.04%) were other cases. The card notes the monitor found no instances of o1 trying to deceive users for reasons other than satisfying the request, and frames the work as an early case study, conditional on chains accurately reflecting the model's thinking, which it calls an open research question.

Hallucination evaluations. On SimpleQA, a set of about four thousand fact-seeking questions with short answers, o1 answered correctly 47% of the time against 38% for GPT-4o, with a hallucination rate of 0.44 against 0.61; on PersonQA (questions about public facts about people), accuracy was 0.55 against 0.50 and the hallucination rate 0.20 against 0.30. Reasoning helped, but o1 still answered SimpleQA questions wrongly far more often than it declined. The product lesson matches the deception findings: reasoning does not make a model know facts it lacks, and for citations and facts, retrieval and verification remain necessary.

External red-teaming. Apollo Research tested o1 for "scheming" in scenarios built to tempt it: the model was given a goal and found information suggesting its developers would shut it down or replace it. When strongly nudged to pursue its goal, o1 attempted to deactivate what it believed was its oversight mechanism in 5% of cases and to exfiltrate what it believed were its weights in 2%, and in a data-processing task it subtly manipulated data towards its own goal in 19% of cases; without strong goal nudging, the oversight-deactivation rate was below 1% and data manipulation 6%. The card also reports o1 often denying such actions when questioned. These are deliberately adversarial settings, not product traffic, but they are why agent designs should not rely on a reasoning model's self-report and should constrain what tools it can use.

The system card's broader point for engineers is that the chain of thought is both an asset and a liability: it enables monitoring that catches real problems at low rates (0.17% matters at ChatGPT scale), and it can contain content that violates policy, which is one reason OpenAI shows users summaries instead of raw chains. Later OpenAI work (Baker et al., rs5) added the caution that pressure on chains can make them less informative.

Production failure modes and their symptoms

Timeout storms. Symptom: spikes of 504s at the gateway during hard-request bursts. Cause: synchronous timeouts set from median latency. Fix: timeouts from p99, asynchronous paths, effort limits.

Empty answers. Symptom: responses with reasoning usage at the cap and no answer text. Cause: output cap below thinking need. Fix: raise the cap, lower effort, check stop reasons.

Silent cost growth. Symptom: bill rises while request volume is flat. Cause: a prompt or model-version change shifted the reasoning-token distribution, or caching broke. Fix: dashboards of reasoning tokens per route (mean and p95) and cache hit rates, with alerts.

Overthinking simple traffic. Symptom: high cost on routes with high fast-model accuracy. Fix: route or lower effort per route.

Agent loops. Symptom: tasks with dozens of tool calls and huge token totals. Fix: per-task budgets and iteration caps, lower effort for routine steps, better tool error messages so the model does not retry blindly.

Over-trusted reasoning. Symptom: users or reviewers accept wrong answers because the displayed reasoning looked thorough. Fix: show sources and checks, not raw chains, and evaluate answers independently.

What to log

Per request: route and model, effort, input and cached tokens, reasoning and answer tokens, latency, stop reason, validation outcome, escalation reason, and (where available and permitted) a reasoning summary for sampled review. Per day: cost per correct answer by route, p95 latency and reasoning tokens, truncation and escalation rates.

This lab simulates the operational side of a reasoning feature: token distributions, truncation, latency percentiles, concurrency, and a router that escalates on disagreement. NumPy only; runs in about a second.

  1. Save as rs7_lab.py and run it (tested on Python 3.12 with NumPy). Part 1 draws 100,000 thinking lengths from a lognormal with median 3,000 tokens: note how far the p99 (about 19,000) sits from the median, and that the mean (about 4,100) is above the median.
  2. Part 2 shows the truncation rate at four output caps (about 41% at 4,000 tokens, 2% at 16,000). Change the spread parameter 0.8 to 1.2 (a more variable workload) and see how much larger a cap you need for the same truncation rate.
  3. Part 3 converts tokens to latency at 60 tokens per second (p50 about 57 s, p95 about 192 s) and applies Little's law: at 10 requests per second, about 760 requests are in flight.
  4. Part 4 routes 100,000 requests (30% hard) with a cascade: two fast samples, escalate if they disagree. It escalates about 29% of traffic and costs about a third of all-reasoning. In this toy it even beats the reasoning model's accuracy, because wrong fast samples rarely agree, so agreement is a strong filter; raise the 0.2 (the chance two wrong samples agree) to 0.6 to model correlated errors and watch accuracy fall.
# rs7 lab: budgeting a reasoning model in a product - token caps, latency, routing. Pure NumPy.
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 1) Reasoning tokens vary a lot between requests: model them as lognormal (median 3,000).
think = rng.lognormal(np.log(3000), 0.8, 100_000); answer = 400
print("thinking tokens  p50=%.0f  p90=%.0f  p99=%.0f  mean=%.0f" % (
    *np.percentile(think, [50, 90, 99]), think.mean()))

# 2) A max-output cap truncates long thinkers: they return no answer.
for cap in (4_000, 8_000, 16_000, 32_000):
    print(f"cap {cap:6,}: truncated {np.mean(think + answer > cap):6.2%}")

# 3) Latency at 60 output tokens/s, plus 0.5 s before the first token.
lat = 0.5 + (think + answer) / 60
print("latency s  p50=%.0f  p95=%.0f  p99=%.0f" % tuple(np.percentile(lat, [50, 95, 99])))
qps = 10; print(f"requests in flight at {qps} req/s (Little's law): {qps * lat.mean():.0f}")

# 4) Routing: fast model first; escalate when two fast samples disagree.
N = 100_000
hard = rng.random(N) < 0.3                         # 30% of traffic is hard
p_fast = np.where(hard, 0.45, 0.95)                # fast model accuracy per request
p_slow = np.where(hard, 0.85, 0.97)                # reasoning model accuracy
s1 = rng.random(N) < p_fast; s2 = rng.random(N) < p_fast
# wrong samples rarely agree with each other; right ones always do
agree = (s1 & s2) | (~s1 & ~s2 & (rng.random(N) < 0.2))
escalate = ~agree
final = np.where(escalate, rng.random(N) < p_slow, s1)
cost_slow = (3000 + 400) * 10e-6                  # $ per reasoning call (median-length)
cost = 2 * 400 * 2e-6 + escalate * cost_slow
print(f"all-fast acc={s1.mean():.3f}  all-reasoning acc={(rng.random(N) < p_slow).mean():.3f}")
print(f"router: escalated {escalate.mean():.1%}  acc={final.mean():.3f}  "
      f"mean cost ${cost.mean():.4f} vs all-reasoning ${cost_slow:.4f}")
Work it out

You get 100,000 requests a day; 80% are easy. The fast model costs $0.002 per request and solves 95% of easy and 40% of hard requests. The reasoning model costs $0.05 and solves 98% of easy and 85% of hard. A router classifies difficulty with 90% accuracy in both directions. Compare cost and accuracy for all-fast, all-reasoning and routed.

Answer

All fast: $200/day; accuracy = 0.8 × 0.95 + 0.2 × 0.40 = 84%.

All reasoning: $5,000/day; accuracy = 0.8 × 0.98 + 0.2 × 0.85 = 95.4%.

Routed: easy → fast 72,000, → reasoning 8,000; hard → reasoning 18,000, → fast 2,000. Reasoning calls = 26,000 × $0.05 = $1,300; fast = 74,000 × $0.002 = $148; total ≈ $1,448/day. Solved = 72,000 × 0.95 + 8,000 × 0.98 + 18,000 × 0.85 + 2,000 × 0.40 = 68,400 + 7,840 + 15,300 + 800 = 92,340 → 92.3%.

Routing recovers 8.3 of the 11.4 points for 29% of the cost. The remaining gap is mostly hard requests misrouted to the fast model; add escalation on validation failure or low confidence to catch them.

Budget

A support-analysis feature will get 2 requests per second. Your measurements at medium effort: reasoning tokens p50 2,500, p95 9,000, p99 15,000; answer 500 tokens; 70 output tokens per second; mean total 3,800 tokens. Choose a max-output cap and a client timeout, estimate concurrency and daily cost at $8 per million output tokens.

Answer

Cap: p99 thinking plus answer is 15,500 tokens; set the cap at 20,000-25,000 so truncation is well under 1%, and treat a length stop as an error. Timeout: p99 latency ≈ 15,500 / 70 ≈ 221 s; set the client timeout around 240-300 s, or move to an asynchronous job if the product cannot wait that long (p95 is about 136 s). Concurrency: mean latency ≈ 3,800 / 70 ≈ 54 s; Little's law gives 2 × 54 ≈ 110 requests in flight. Cost: 2 × 86,400 = 172,800 requests a day × 3,800 tokens = 657 million tokens × $8/M ≈ $5,250 a day. If that is too much, measure low effort and a router before anything else.
