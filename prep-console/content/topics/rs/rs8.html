A new open reasoning model is announced with a table of benchmark scores, and it beats the previous best on AIME by four points. Is it better? The table does not say that AIME has 30 questions, so four points is a little over one question. It does not say whether the score is a single sample per question or an average over sixteen, or whether it is a majority vote over sixty-four samples, a different and much more expensive system. It does not say how long the model was allowed to think. And it does not say whether any of the questions, or their published solutions, were in the training data. Without those details the four points could be real, noise, compute, or memorisation.

Evaluation is where reasoning-model claims are made and where they most often mislead. Reasoning models are evaluated on small, hard benchmarks, sampled at high temperature, with results that vary run to run, and the most popular benchmarks are exactly the ones most likely to have leaked into training data. Apple researchers showed in 2024 that simply changing the names and numbers in grade-school maths problems lowered most models' scores, and that adding one irrelevant sentence lowered them dramatically, which raised hard questions about what benchmark numbers had been measuring.

For an AI engineer the consequences are practical. You will choose models from benchmark tables, so you need to read them critically. You will build evaluation sets for your own product, so you need to know how many questions it takes to detect the differences you care about, how to compare two models properly, and how to keep your set uncontaminated. And in interviews, "how would you know if model B is really better than model A?" is a standard question with a quantitative answer.

This lesson covers which reasoning benchmarks still discriminate and why others stopped, the metrics (pass@1, avg@k, maj@k, pass@k) and what each measures, the standard error of a benchmark score worked for 500 questions and for 30, paired comparisons, the effect of repeated sampling, sample-size planning, clustering, and contamination: how it happens, how it inflates scores, and how to detect it. The public case is GSM-Symbolic. The lab computes confidence intervals by formula and bootstrap, compares paired and unpaired tests, shows what repeated sampling can and cannot fix, and simulates contamination.

The mental model: a benchmark score is a measurement with an error bar

Think of a benchmark as a thermometer reading. A reading of 80% accuracy is a measurement of something you cannot see directly: how often the model would be right on the whole population of problems like these. Like any measurement it has error, from two sources. The first is which questions you happened to include: a different 500 questions of the same kind would give a slightly different score. The second is the model's own randomness: reasoning models are sampled at temperature, so asking the same question again can give a different answer. Both shrink with more data (more questions, more samples per question), and a sensible report states the reading together with its error bar.

Two further problems are not random noise and do not shrink with more data. A thermometer can be miscalibrated: if the model has seen the test questions during training, its score measures memory as well as reasoning, and contamination inflates it by a fixed, unknown amount. And a thermometer can be saturated: a scale that tops out at 100 cannot tell a hot day from a very hot one, and a benchmark on which every strong model scores 97% cannot tell strong models apart.

What the metrics measure

Reasoning results come in several metrics that are easy to confuse. pass@1 is the probability that one sampled answer is correct; because one sample per question is noisy, it is usually estimated by sampling each question k times and averaging, sometimes written avg@k. maj@k (also cons@k, for consensus) is the accuracy of the most common answer among k samples, a different system that spends k times the compute (rs2). pass@k is the probability that at least one of k samples is correct, the ceiling achievable with a perfect checker. For the same model, pass@1 ≤ maj@k ≤ pass@k in typical cases, sometimes by tens of points: DeepSeek-R1 scored 79.8% pass@1 and 86.7% cons@64 on AIME 2024.

Reading a results table: five questions

Whenever you meet a table of reasoning-benchmark scores, five questions turn it from marketing into evidence. Which metric is each number (pass@1, avg@k, maj@k, pass@k), and are the compared numbers the same metric? How many questions does each benchmark have, and therefore how wide is the interval around each score? What settings were used: temperature, number of samples, thinking budget, maximum output length? Could the benchmark be in the training data, and are there results on items released after the model's cutoff? And is the benchmark close to the problems you actually care about? A difference that survives all five is worth acting on; most headline differences do not survive the second.

Where the analogy breaks

A thermometer measures a well-defined quantity. A benchmark measures performance on a particular population of questions, and the population you care about (your users' problems) may differ from the benchmark's. A perfectly precise, uncontaminated score can still answer the wrong question. That is why every serious team keeps its own evaluation set built from its own tasks, alongside public benchmarks.

1. The benchmark landscape for reasoning

Reasoning benchmarks have a short useful life. GSM8K (grade-school word problems, 1,319 test questions) was hard for 2022 models and is now near-saturated for any frontier model. MATH (12,500 competition problems) and its 500-question subset MATH-500 followed: DeepSeek-R1 scored 97.3% on MATH-500. When top models cluster in the high 90s, the remaining errors are dominated by ambiguous questions, wrong reference answers and answer-format mismatches, so differences stop reflecting ability.

The benchmarks that still discriminate between strong reasoning models share two features, difficulty and freshness. AIME (American Invitational Mathematics Examination) offers 15 problems per paper, two papers a year, 30 questions per year, each with an integer answer from 0 to 999, so guessing is useless and grading is exact; but its tiny size means huge error bars, and each year's problems and solutions are published, so last year's exam is next year's training data. GPQA Diamond has 198 graduate-level science multiple-choice questions written to be hard to answer even with web search; it discriminates well but is small and has a 25% guessing floor. LiveCodeBench continually adds new competitive-programming problems and lets you evaluate only on problems released after a model's training cutoff. SWE-bench Verified, 500 real GitHub issues checked by human annotators, measures agentic coding with the repository's tests as the checker. Very hard sets such as Humanity's Last Exam (2,500 expert-written questions across many subjects) were built to leave headroom. None is sufficient alone; a credible evaluation uses several and adds a private set from the target domain.

2. Metrics: say exactly what was measured

For each question i, define p_i as the probability that one sampled answer is correct. The benchmark's pass@1 is the average of p_i over questions: what a user gets from one call. It is estimated by sampling each question k times and averaging all k × n outcomes (avg@k); the estimate is the same quantity as one sample per question, only less noisy. maj@k is the accuracy of the majority answer of k samples; it measures a voting system and depends on the distribution of wrong answers, not only on p_i (rs2). pass@k is the average of 1 − (1 − p_i)^k, the coverage a perfect checker would achieve, estimated without bias from n ≥ k samples as in rs2.

Results also depend on settings that must be reported with them: sampling temperature and top-p, the number of samples, the thinking budget or effort level, the maximum output length (a reasoning model truncated at 8,000 tokens scores lower than at 32,000), the prompt, and the answer-extraction rule. DeepSeek-R1's paper, for instance, reported AIME pass@1 averaged over multiple samples at a stated temperature and maximum length, and cons@64 separately. Comparing one model's maj@64 with another's pass@1, or scores at different thinking budgets, compares different things.

3. The standard error of a score

If a benchmark's n questions are a random sample from a population of similar questions, and the model's accuracy on that population is p, the observed accuracy has a standard error (SE), the standard deviation it would have across repeated draws of n questions, of

SE = √( p (1 − p) / n )          95% confidence interval ≈ p̂ ± 1.96 · SE

By hand, 500 questions at 80%: p(1 − p) = 0.8 × 0.2 = 0.16; divided by 500 gives 0.00032; the square root is 0.0179. The 95% interval is 0.80 ± 1.96 × 0.0179 = 0.80 ± 0.035, from 76.5% to 83.5%. One AIME year at 50%: 0.25/30 = 0.00833, square root 0.0913, interval ± 0.179: from 32% to 68%. One AIME score cannot distinguish 45% from 60%. The uncertainty is largest at 50% accuracy and shrinks towards 0% and 100%, and it falls only with the square root of n: halving the interval needs four times the questions.

The bootstrap gives the same answer without a formula: resample the n per-question results with replacement many times, recompute the score each time, and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. The lab does both on a simulated 500-question run and they agree (an interval about ±3.7 points wide around 76.6%). The bootstrap generalises to metrics without simple formulas, such as the difference in maj@k between two models.

4. Comparing two models: pair the questions

The natural question is whether model B is better than model A. Treating their scores as independent measurements, the SE of the difference is √(SE_A² + SE_B²). By hand: A = 80.0%, B = 83.0% on 500 questions: SE_A² = 0.8 × 0.2/500 = 0.000320; SE_B² = 0.83 × 0.17/500 = 0.000282; SE of difference = √0.000602 = 0.0245, so the 95% interval for the 3-point difference is ±4.8 points. Not significant.

But the two models answered the same questions, and questions differ hugely in difficulty: both models get the easy ones right and the hard ones wrong. Those shared outcomes carry no information about which model is better; only the questions where they disagree do. A paired comparison analyses the per-question differences d_i = (B right) − (A right), each −1, 0 or +1. By hand: suppose A alone is right on b = 40 questions and B alone on c = 55, with the rest agreeing. The mean difference is (c − b)/n = 15/500 = 0.03. The variance of d is (b + c)/n − (mean)², so its SE is √((b + c) − (c − b)²/n)/n = √(95 − 0.45)/500 = 9.72/500 = 0.0195, about 1.95 points. The interval is now ±3.8 points and z = 3.0/1.95 = 1.54: still short of significance at 95%, but closer. This is McNemar's test in another form. The fewer the disagreements, the more precise the paired comparison, and in practice paired standard errors are often much smaller than unpaired ones.

5. Repeated sampling: what it fixes and what it does not

Reasoning models are sampled at temperature (DeepSeek-R1 was evaluated at 0.6), so one sample per question adds noise from the model's own randomness. The variance of a score averaged over k samples per question, on n questions, splits into two parts:

Var(score) ≈ [ Var_between(p_i) + mean_i( p_i (1 − p_i) ) / k ] / n

The first term is question-sampling variance: which questions are in the benchmark. The second is within-question sampling variance: the model's randomness on each question, which shrinks as 1/k. The lab shows the split for 30 AIME-like questions with per-question solve rates spread between 0 and 1: re-running the same 30 questions with one sample each moves the score by about 7 points (standard deviation); with 4 samples, 3.5; with 16, 1.7; with 64, 0.9. But the uncertainty about the population, which 30 questions were drawn, stays near 6 points however many samples you take. Repeated sampling makes the measurement of these questions precise; only more questions make it generalise. That is why a reasoning model's AIME score is typically averaged over many samples per question and why it should still carry a wide interval.

6. Planning: how many questions do you need?

Before building an evaluation set, decide the smallest difference you need to detect (δ) and compute the sample size. For an unpaired comparison at 95% confidence and 80% power (an 80% chance of detecting a real difference of size δ):

n ≈ (z_0.975 + z_0.8)² · 2 p (1 − p) / δ²  =  (1.96 + 0.84)² · 2 p (1 − p) / δ²

By hand, δ = 3 points at p ≈ 0.8: (2.8)² = 7.84; 2 × 0.16 = 0.32; δ² = 0.0009; n ≈ 7.84 × 0.32/0.0009 ≈ 2,800 questions per model. For δ = 10 points, about 250. A paired design replaces 2p(1 − p) by the variance of the per-question differences, roughly the fraction of questions on which the models disagree; at 15% disagreement, n ≈ 7.84 × 0.15/0.0009 ≈ 1,300. The arithmetic explains why small public benchmarks rarely settle close comparisons, and why product teams that care about two-point improvements need evaluation sets in the thousands, or larger effects.

7. Clustering and multiple comparisons

The formulas assume questions are independent. Often they are not: reading-comprehension benchmarks ask several questions about one passage, templated benchmarks generate many questions from one template, and coding benchmarks draw several tasks from one repository. Correlated questions carry less information than their count suggests, and Miller (2024) shows that ignoring this can make intervals noticeably too narrow. The fix is a clustered standard error: compute the variance over cluster means (or use the clustered formula) and report the number of clusters. A second trap is testing many models, prompts or checkpoints on one set and reporting the best: with 20 comparisons at 95% confidence, one is expected to look significant by chance. Keep a final held-out set that is used once.

8. Contamination: how scores get inflated

Contamination is test material in the training data. It arrives in many forms: verbatim copies of benchmarks on web pages scraped into pretraining data; solutions discussed on forums; translations and paraphrases; problems from the same competition years; and, increasingly, synthetic training data generated by other models that were themselves trained on benchmark material. Exact-match decontamination (removing training documents that share long n-grams with test items) catches verbatim copies and misses most of the rest.

The effect is easy to model. If a fraction f of a benchmark leaked and the model is right on leaked items with probability near 1, while its true accuracy on new items is a, the observed score is about f × 1 + (1 − f) × a. By hand: with f = 0.15, near-perfect recall (0.98) and a = 0.60, the observed score is 0.15 × 0.98 + 0.85 × 0.60 = 0.147 + 0.510 = 0.657, a 5.7-point inflation; the lab's simulation of exactly this setting shows 5.5. The inflation is not noise: it does not shrink with more questions or more samples, and it differs between models depending on what each saw in training.

Detection therefore relies on fresh questions of the same kind. GSM1k (Zhang et al., 2024) commissioned about 1,000 new grade-school problems matched to GSM8K in difficulty and style; several model families scored up to 8% lower on GSM1k than on GSM8K, and the gap correlated with how likely each model was to generate GSM8K examples verbatim, a sign of memorisation. GSM-Symbolic, the public case below, generated perturbed variants of GSM8K problems. For competition benchmarks, evaluating only on problems released after a model's training cutoff (the idea behind LiveCodeBench and newer AIME years) is the most direct defence.

9. Perturbation as a test of reasoning

Fresh variants also test something beyond contamination: robustness. A model that has learned to solve a type of problem should be unaffected by a change of name, should cope with changed numbers, and should ignore irrelevant information. GSM-Symbolic found the first mostly held, the second less so, and the third failed badly. Engineers can borrow the method directly: for any evaluation set, generate perturbed variants (swap entities, change numbers, add irrelevant details, reorder clauses) and check that scores hold. A model whose score drops on harmless perturbations will drop on real users' phrasing too.

10. Grading reasoning outputs

The last source of error is the grader. Reasoning models produce long outputs with the answer somewhere at the end, in varying formats. Answer extraction must find the final answer (not an intermediate value), normalise equivalent forms (1/2, 0.5 and \frac{1}{2}), and handle truncated outputs as wrong rather than parsing whatever number appears last. Symbolic equivalence checkers help for maths; for free-form answers, LLM judges are used and bring their own biases (lm14). Grading errors of a few percent are common and can exceed the differences being measured, so spot-check a sample of graded outputs by hand, especially disagreements between models.

11. Evaluating at a thinking budget

A reasoning model's score is a function of how long it may think, so a single number hides a curve. Evaluate at the budgets or effort levels you might deploy and report accuracy against mean reasoning tokens for each. Two models can cross: model A may be better at low budgets and model B at high ones, and the right choice depends on your latency and cost limits (rs7). When comparing with a non-reasoning model, compare at matched cost or latency as well as at each model's default, since a reasoning model's advantage at unlimited budget may vanish under a product's constraints. And check the truncation rate at each budget: a model that is cut off before answering on 10% of items loses those points to the harness, not to its reasoning, and the fix is a setting, not a different model.

12. Building a private reasoning evaluation set

For product decisions, a private set beats any public benchmark, and building one is mostly careful bookkeeping. Sample candidate items from real traffic (anonymised) and from known failure reports, so the set reflects what users actually ask. Label each with a verified answer and, where possible, a programmatic check, and record its use case and an estimated difficulty so you can stratify. Size it with section 6: if you need to detect 4-point changes at around 75% accuracy with paired comparisons, plan for several hundred items per stratum you care about. Split it into a development part, used freely while iterating on prompts and settings, and a held-out part, used only for final decisions, so that iterating does not overfit the set. Generate perturbed variants of a subset to test robustness. Never publish it, refresh a slice of it each quarter with new items, and track scores on old versus new items: a widening gap is your own contamination or overfitting signal. Finally, version everything: items, labels, grader code and settings, so a score from six months ago can be reproduced and compared.

Where the binomial formula comes from, and when it breaks

If each question is an independent draw and the model's correctness on it is a Bernoulli outcome with population mean p, the count of correct answers is binomial and its proportion has variance p(1 − p)/n. With repeated samples per question, the outcomes are no longer identically distributed (each question has its own p_i), and the law of total variance gives the two-term formula in the body: the between-question variance of p_i plus the average within-question variance divided by k, all over n. Two consequences are worth stating in interviews. First, when p_i is near 0 or 1 for most questions (the model either knows a question or not), the within term is small and extra samples add little; when many p_i are near 0.5, as on hard reasoning sets, extra samples help a lot. Second, the Central Limit Theorem approximation behind ±1.96 SE is poor for small n and for p near 0 or 1; for a 30-question benchmark, a Wilson or exact binomial interval, or a bootstrap, is more honest.

Paired analysis with multiple samples

With k samples per question for two models, compute each question's mean correctness for each model, take the per-question differences, and use their mean and standard error across questions. This handles both the pairing (shared question difficulty cancels) and the within-question sampling (averaged into each question's mean). Miller's recommendations for LLM evaluations follow this pattern: report standard errors computed from per-question results, use clustered errors where questions are grouped, use paired differences when comparing models on the same questions, resample to reduce within-question variance, and plan sample sizes with power analysis.

Why AIME numbers move so much between reports

Different reports of the same model's AIME score differ by several points for reasons beyond randomness: different maximum output lengths (truncation removes hard-won correct answers), different temperatures and top-p, different numbers of samples, different system prompts and answer formats, and different extraction rules. With 30 questions, one question is 3.3 points, so a single extraction rule that mishandles one answer format can move the score by more than the difference being claimed. Reproducing a published number on your own harness, before comparing your model with it, is the only reliable approach.

Contamination detection methods and their limits

Methods fall into three groups. Data-side: search the training corpus for test items (exact or fuzzy n-gram overlap, embedding similarity); precise when you have the data, impossible for closed models, and blind to paraphrase. Model-side: check whether the model reproduces test items verbatim from a prefix, assigns them unusually low perplexity compared with similar fresh items, or prefers the original ordering of multiple-choice options; useful for open models, but each has false positives and misses. Outcome-side: compare performance on the original items with performance on fresh or perturbed items of matched difficulty, as GSM1k and GSM-Symbolic did; this works for any model and measures the effect that matters, but requires building matched items and attributing gaps carefully, since a harder-than-intended fresh set also produces a gap. Teams usually combine an outcome-side test with whatever data-side checks they can run.

Reasoning models and contamination

Reasoning models add new paths for contamination. RL training sets are built from competition problems, many of them from the same sources as benchmarks; distillation data generated by a teacher on such problems carries the teacher's memorised solutions; and long chains of thought can reconstruct a remembered solution while looking like fresh reasoning. Conversely, reasoning models' strong performance on problems released after their training cutoff (new AIME years, new LiveCodeBench problems) is the best evidence that their gains are not mainly memorisation. The practical rule is to give more weight to post-cutoff results and to treat any benchmark older than a model's data cutoff with suspicion.

Benchmarks versus your product

Public benchmarks answer "how good is this model at competition maths, graduate science or contest programming?". A product needs "how good is it at our users' problems, at our latency and cost budget?". The statistics of this lesson apply unchanged to private evaluation sets, and private sets have the great advantage of never having been published. A good private set is drawn from real traffic, labelled carefully, stratified by difficulty and use case, large enough for the differences that matter (section 6), refreshed periodically, and scored with the same settings as deployment.

Preference arenas are a different instrument

Leaderboards built from crowd-sourced pairwise votes, where users compare two anonymous models' answers to their own prompts and ratings are fitted with a Bradley–Terry or Elo model (rs5), avoid fixed test sets and therefore much of the contamination problem, and they reflect what real users prefer. They measure something different from reasoning accuracy, though: preferences mix correctness with style, length and tone, most prompts are not hard reasoning problems, and votes are noisy, so rating intervals for closely matched models overlap. They are best read as a complement to checkable benchmarks, not a substitute, and their confidence intervals deserve the same attention as any other score's.

The public case: GSM-Symbolic (Mirzadeh et al., Apple, 2024)

"GSM-Symbolic: Understanding the Limitations of Mathematical Reasoning in Large Language Models", by Iman Mirzadeh, Keivan Alizadeh, Hooman Shahrokhi, Oncel Tuzel, Samy Bengio and Mehrdad Farajtabar (Apple and Washington State University), appeared in October 2024 and was published at ICLR 2025. It asked whether GSM8K scores still measured mathematical reasoning, given rapid score increases and concerns about contamination.

Method. The authors took 100 questions from the GSM8K test set and turned each into a symbolic template, marking the names, numbers and other variables and writing constraints so that every instance remained valid and its answer computable. From each template they generated 50 instances, then grouped them into 50 benchmark sets of 100 questions each, so each set was a mutation of the original 100. Their study covered 25 open and closed models (including GPT-4o, o1-mini and o1-preview) with 8-shot chain-of-thought prompting and greedy decoding, and studied variants: changing only names, only numbers, or both; harder versions with one or two added clauses (GSM-P1, GSM-P2) and an easier one with a clause removed; and GSM-NoOp.

Findings. First, variance: scores varied noticeably across the 50 sets even though every set tested the same reasoning; for Gemma2-9B the gap between the best and worst set was more than 12 points, for Phi-3.5-mini around 15. Second, a drop: for most models the original GSM8K score sat on the right-hand side of the distribution of GSM-Symbolic scores, which, the authors note, should be statistically unlikely if GSM8K were just one more instance. Gemma2-9B-it fell from 87.0% on GSM8K to 79.1% on average (±3.0) and Phi-3.5-mini-instruct from 88.0% to 82.1% (±3.4), while GPT-4o (95.0% to 94.9%) and Llama3-8B-instruct (74.0% to 74.6%) barely moved. Third, models were more sensitive to changed numbers than to changed names, and performance fell and variance rose as clauses were added. Fourth, and most striking, GSM-NoOp: adding one clause that seems relevant but does not affect the answer (in the paper's example, that five of the kiwis picked on Sunday "were a bit smaller than average") caused large drops across all models, over 65% for Phi-3-mini, and 17.5 points even for o1-preview, because models tended to turn every statement into an operation, here subtracting the five smaller kiwis. Providing eight examples of the same question's variants in the prompt did not fix it.

Interpretation and limits. The authors argued that current models perform a kind of probabilistic pattern-matching on reasoning steps seen in training rather than formal reasoning. One need not accept the strongest version of that claim to use the results: the variance shows that a single 100-question score carries several points of uncertainty from question selection alone; the GSM8K-versus-variant gap is consistent with overfitting or contamination for some model families; and the NoOp results show brittleness to irrelevant information that matters directly for products, whose users routinely include irrelevant details. The evaluation used greedy decoding and few-shot prompting, and the strongest reasoning models released after the paper were not tested, so their robustness has to be measured the same way rather than assumed: re-test with fresh variants rather than trusting old scores.

Applying this to your own evaluations

Four practices follow directly. Report every score with a confidence interval and the settings used. Compare models with paired analyses on the same questions, with several samples per question for reasoning models. Generate perturbed variants of your evaluation items (names, numbers, irrelevant details) and check robustness. And keep a private, never-published set refreshed with new items, so that your decisions do not depend on numbers that the models may have memorised.

A reporting template

Every evaluation result your team circulates can follow one short template, which removes most of the ambiguity this lesson describes: the model and version; the evaluation set and its size (and number of clusters, if grouped); the metric (pass@1 as avg@k with k stated, maj@k, or pass@k); sampling settings (temperature, top-p, thinking budget or effort, maximum output tokens); the score with a 95% interval; for comparisons, the paired difference with its interval and the number of discordant items; the truncation rate and grading method; and the contamination status (decontaminated against what, and any post-cutoff subset). It takes a few minutes to fill in, and it makes results comparable across people and months, which is the point of measuring at all.

Failure modes in evaluation pipelines

Extraction bugs that read an intermediate number as the answer, or parse truncated outputs. Silent setting drift: a changed maximum length or temperature between runs. Leaky dev sets: prompts tuned on the same items used for the final comparison. Benchmark mismatch: a model chosen on public maths benchmarks for a product whose users ask about contracts. Over-reading small differences: shipping a model change on a one-point gain on 200 items. Each has the same remedy: treat evaluation as an experiment, with a written protocol, recorded settings, and error bars.

# rs8 lab: error bars, paired comparisons, repeated sampling and contamination. Pure NumPy.
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 1) Standard error and a bootstrap CI for a 500-question benchmark at 80% accuracy.
n = 500; correct = rng.random(n) < 0.80
p = correct.mean(); se = np.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n)
boot = [rng.choice(correct, n).mean() for _ in range(10_000)]
print(f"n=500: acc={p:.3f}  SE={se:.4f}  95% CI by formula +/-{1.96*se:.3f}  "
      f"bootstrap [{np.percentile(boot, 2.5):.3f}, {np.percentile(boot, 97.5):.3f}]")
for n_q in (30, 60, 198, 500, 2000):
    print(f"  {n_q:5d} questions at 50%: +/-{1.96*np.sqrt(0.25/n_q)*100:.1f} points")

# 2) Comparing two models on the SAME questions: paired beats unpaired.
q_easy = rng.beta(2, 0.6, 500)                         # per-question difficulty shared by both
a = rng.random(500) < q_easy; b = rng.random(500) < np.clip(q_easy + 0.03, 0, 1)
d = b.astype(int) - a.astype(int)
se_unpaired = np.sqrt(a.mean()*(1-a.mean())/500 + b.mean()*(1-b.mean())/500)
se_paired = d.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(500)
print(f"A={a.mean():.3f} B={b.mean():.3f} diff={d.mean():+.3f}  SE unpaired={se_unpaired:.4f}  "
      f"SE paired={se_paired:.4f}  discordant: A-only={np.sum(d<0)} B-only={np.sum(d>0)}")

# 3) Repeated sampling: 30 AIME-like questions, k samples each. Within-question noise shrinks
#    with k; between-question noise (which questions you happened to get) does not.
pq = rng.beta(0.6, 0.6, 30)                            # per-question solve rates
for k in (1, 4, 16, 64):
    runs = [(rng.random((30, k)) < pq[:, None]).mean() for _ in range(4000)]
    print(f"k={k:2d}: re-run SD on the same 30 questions = {np.std(runs)*100:.1f} points")
print(f"SD from which 30 questions you drew (not reduced by k): {np.std(pq)/np.sqrt(30)*100:.1f} points")

# 4) Contamination: 15% of a benchmark leaked; the model is right on leaked items 98% of the time.

Read the table

A vendor's table shows: Model X, AIME 2024 = 73.3 (pass@1); Model Y, AIME 2024 = 80.0 (cons@64); both "at default settings". Model Y's vendor says Y is clearly better. What questions do you ask, and what can you conclude?

Answer

The metrics differ: cons@64 is a majority vote over 64 samples, a system using 64 times the compute, while pass@1 is single-sample accuracy; Y's pass@1 could be lower than X's. Ask for both models' pass@1 averaged over many samples, with temperature, maximum output length and thinking budget. Then the size: 73.3% and 80.0% on 30 questions are 22 and 24 correct answers, a two-question difference, while the 95% interval for one score is about ±16 points at these accuracies. Even with matched metrics, a paired comparison on 30 questions cannot separate them; ask for results on AIME 2025 (post-cutoff for both, if true), GPQA Diamond and a larger maths set, and run your own evaluation on your own tasks. Conclusion: the table supports no claim that Y is better.

Your team wants to know whether a new prompt improves your reasoning feature's accuracy (currently about 75%) by at least 4 points. How large should the evaluation set be, and how would you run the comparison?

Answer

Unpaired: n ≈ 7.84 × 2 × 0.75 × 0.25 / 0.04² = 7.84 × 0.375 / 0.0016 ≈ 1,840 items. Paired on the same items, if the prompts disagree on about 12% of them: n ≈ 7.84 × 0.12 / 0.0016 ≈ 590. Run both prompts on the same items with the deployment's model, effort and temperature, 4-8 samples per item to cut sampling noise, compute per-item mean correctness for each prompt, and report the mean paired difference with its standard error and a 95% interval. Check robustness on perturbed variants of a subset, and keep a separate held-out set for the final confirmation so that the prompt is not tuned on the items that decide it.
