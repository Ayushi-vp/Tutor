In January 2025 DeepSeek published a model that matched OpenAI's o1 on maths and code benchmarks and described, in a technical report, how it had been trained. Within weeks dozens of teams were trying to reproduce it, and most discovered the same things: that the headline "pure reinforcement learning" described a research model, not the product; that the reinforcement learning itself was surprisingly simple but extremely sensitive to details such as how losses are averaged and which problems are in the batch; that response lengths ballooned; that results on one family of base models did not carry over to another; and that the compute bill was dominated by generating long answers, not by training on them. Those lessons, rather than any single recipe, are what a senior engineer is expected to carry into the next project, because the specific models and benchmarks change every few months while the failure patterns repeat.

For an AI engineer, this matters for three reasons. Interviewers at labs and at companies that post-train open models ask candidates to walk through the R1 recipe and defend each stage. Teams that fine-tune reasoning models for their own domain (legal analysis, scientific QA, code) have to decide what they can realistically do with a handful of GPUs. And reading the flood of papers in this area requires knowing which results are robust and which are artefacts of one base model or one evaluation.

The earlier topics in this track built the pieces: what a reasoning model is (rs1), the RL algorithms (rs3, rs4), reward signals and hacking (rs5), distillation (rs6) and evaluation (rs8). This lesson assembles them into an end-to-end training pipeline. It walks through DeepSeek-R1's published recipe stage by stage, with the settings and costs it reported, then what the main open replications (Kimi k1.5, DAPO, Open-Reasoner-Zero, Dr. GRPO, SimpleRL-Zoo, Open-R1) found. It covers data for reasoning RL (verifiable problems, difficulty curricula, filtering), why length grows during RL and how to control it, worked by hand, language mixing, compute budgets, the open question of whether RL adds new abilities, and a realistic plan for a small team. The lab draws a length-versus-accuracy curve on synthetic traces and computes two published length rewards.

A note on how to read this lesson's numbers. Most come from the papers' own reports, on specific base models, benchmarks and sampling settings, and many are on AIME 2024, a 30-question test where a single question is worth 3.3 points (rs8). Treat them as evidence about what worked for those teams, with the error bars that small benchmarks carry, rather than as a leaderboard. The durable content is the structure of the pipeline and the reasons each stage exists.

An analogy: training a chess player

Think of turning a well-read amateur into a strong chess player. One approach is pure practice against a scorer: play thousands of games, and after each one be told only win or lose. Over time the player discovers calculation, checking for blunders and long-term plans on their own. That is R1-Zero: a base model trained only by reinforcement learning with a reward for correct final answers. It works, but the player develops idiosyncratic habits, such as muttering half in one language and half in another.

The practical approach is a curriculum. First, study a few annotated master games so the player's notes are readable (cold-start fine-tuning). Then practise heavily against the scorer (reasoning RL), with a coach who insists the notes stay in one language. Then collect the player's best games, add lessons on everything else a professional needs (supervised data across tasks), and study those. Finally, practise once more with both the scorer and a coach who judges manners and helpfulness (general RL). That is R1. The analogy holds in one important way: the scorer cannot be fooled about who won, which is why verifiable rewards work so well. It breaks in another: a model's "practice games" cost money in proportion to how long it thinks, so the longer its notes, the more each round of practice costs.

The pipeline at a glance

What makes it hard

Three things make the recipe harder than the diagram suggests. The RL is sensitive to small choices: how the loss is averaged over tokens, how the advantage is normalised, which problems are in the batch, how truncated answers are scored. Response length grows during training, which raises cost and can mean either better reasoning or a training artefact. And base models differ: some already reason well before any RL, so gains attributed to a method may come from the model. Everything else in this lesson is about those three facts.

Two kinds of stage, two jobs

It helps to see the pipeline as alternating two tools with different jobs. Supervised stages (cold start, rejection-sampled SFT) shape behaviour: format, readability, language, breadth of skills. They are cheap, stable and only as good as the data. RL stages improve performance against a reward: they discover strategies no dataset contained, but they are expensive, unstable and narrow, optimising exactly what the reward measures. A good recipe uses supervised stages to give RL a well-behaved starting point and to restore breadth afterwards, and uses RL where a reliable reward exists. Almost every later open recipe, from Qwen3's to the small-team plans at the end of this lesson, follows that alternation, with different proportions.

A reader who stops here should remember: R1 is supervised and RL stages alternating, not pure RL; verifiable rewards are the engine; length and data are the main things to manage; and replications show the core is simple but fragile.

1. R1-Zero: reinforcement learning on a base model

R1-Zero starts from DeepSeek-V3-Base, a 671-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model with no instruction tuning. The training prompt template asks the model to think inside tags and then answer; that is the only shaping. The reward is rule-based: an accuracy reward (does the final answer match, or does code pass its tests) and a format reward (is the reasoning inside the right tags). There is no learned reward model.

The algorithm is GRPO (rs4). The settings, as reported in the paper's January 2026 revision: learning rate 3 × 10⁻⁶, KL coefficient 0.001, sampling temperature 1; 16 sampled responses per question; 32 questions per step, so a training batch of 512 responses; maximum length 32,768 tokens until step 8,200 and 65,536 tokens afterwards; 10,400 steps in total, about 1.6 epochs over the prompt set; the reference model for the KL penalty replaced by the current policy every 400 steps; and, for speed, each rollout phase generating 8,192 responses split into 16 mini-batches for a single pass of updates.

Two curves made the paper famous. AIME 2024 pass@1 rose from 15.6% at the start to 77.9% by the end (86.7% with majority voting), and the average response length grew steadily, with a jump when the maximum length was raised. The model learned on its own to re-examine its steps, try alternatives and write "wait" before correcting itself. It also wrote chains that were hard to read and mixed English and Chinese, and it did poorly at anything that was not reasoning.

2. R1: four more stages to make it usable

Cold start. DeepSeek collected thousands of long chains of thought in a conversational, readable style, generated by R1-Zero at temperature 1 for diverse reasoning prompts, filtered for correctness, repetition and language mixing, and rewritten by DeepSeek-V3 for clarity, with a summary at the end. Fine-tuning the base model on these gives RL a better-behaved starting point.

Reasoning RL with a language reward. The same GRPO settings as R1-Zero (with a clip ratio of 10 and a 32,768-token limit), plus a language-consistency reward: the proportion of words in the chain of thought that are in the target language. The paper's ablation found this kept language stable during training at the cost of a slight drop in benchmark scores, a deliberate trade of capability for readability.

Rejection sampling and broad SFT. From the RL checkpoint, generate many responses per reasoning prompt and keep the correct, readable ones; this time also include prompts that need a generative reward model rather than a rule to judge. Add non-reasoning data (writing, factual QA, self-cognition, translation, software engineering) from DeepSeek-V3's pipeline. About 800,000 examples in all, fine-tuned for two to three epochs. This restores breadth that RL alone narrowed. The same data was used to distil the R1-Distill family of smaller models (rs6).

General RL. A second RL stage of 1,700 steps at a lower temperature, 0.7, because higher temperatures produced incoherent text here. The reward combines rule-based rewards for reasoning data, a reward model and format rewards for general data, and the language reward. Preference-based rewards were used only in the final 400 steps: the paper reports that more steps against a model-based preference reward led to reward hacking.

3. What it cost

The paper's supplementary table reports the cost of these reasoning-specific stages assuming H800 rental at $2 per GPU-hour: R1-Zero 101,000 GPU-hours ($202,000), creating SFT data 5,000 ($10,000), R1 41,000 ($82,000), 147,000 GPU-hours or about $294,000 in total. The arithmetic checks out: R1-Zero ran on 64 nodes of 8 H800s, 512 GPUs, for about 198 hours, and 512 × 198 ≈ 101,000. This excludes pretraining DeepSeek-V3, the earlier experiments on a 30B model, failed runs and staff. It is still a striking number: the step that turns a strong base model into a strong reasoner cost a fraction of pretraining.

Where does that compute go? Each step generates 512 responses that can each run to tens of thousands of tokens. Decoding is memory-bound and sequential (gpu10), and a batch waits for its longest response. Generation dominates RL compute, which is why the systems work in this area (partial rollouts, asynchronous rollout workers, rs11) targets rollouts first.

4. What the open replications found

Within months, several groups published full or partial reproductions, each adding a finding.

  • Kimi k1.5 (Moonshot, January 2025) reached o1-level results (77.5 on AIME, 96.2 on MATH-500) with RL using contexts up to 128K tokens, made long contexts affordable with partial rollouts (pausing very long generations and continuing them in later iterations), used a length penalty to stop "overthinking", and developed long2short methods (length penalties, model merging) to transfer long-chain gains into short responses.
  • Open-Reasoner-Zero showed that vanilla PPO with GAE (λ = 1, γ = 1), simple rule-based rewards and no KL regularisation scaled both scores and response length, beating DeepSeek's R1-Zero-Qwen-32B with the same base model in a tenth of the training steps.
  • DAPO (ByteDance Seed and Tsinghua, March 2025) took Qwen2.5-32B base from near 0 to 50 on AIME 2024 in half the steps of DeepSeek's equivalent run, with four changes covered in rs4: clip-higher (ε_low 0.2, ε_high 0.28), dynamic sampling, a token-level loss and overlong reward shaping, plus a 17,000-problem dataset whose answers were converted to integers for reliable checking.
  • Dr. GRPO ("Understanding R1-Zero-like training", Sea AI Lab) found that GRPO's division by response length and by the group's standard deviation bias training: wrong answers are pushed toward being longer and easy or hard questions are over-weighted. Removing both gave the same accuracy with fewer tokens. It also found DeepSeek-V3-Base already shows "aha" moments and Qwen2.5 bases reason well without any template. Its minimal recipe reached 43.3% on AIME 2024 with a 7B model, on 8 A100 GPUs in about a day.
  • SimpleRL-Zoo ran zero RL on ten base models (Llama 3.1 8B, Mistral 7B and 24B, DeepSeek-Math 7B, Qwen2.5 from 0.5B to 32B and Qwen2.5-Math 7B). Most improved, with adjustments to format rewards and query difficulty, but the dynamics differed by model, and longer responses did not always come with verification behaviour.
  • Open-R1 (Hugging Face) set out to reproduce R1's data and training in three steps. It completed the first, distillation: Mixture-of-Thoughts, 350,000 verified traces, and OpenR1-Distill-7B, which scored 52.7 on AIME 2024 against 51.3 for DeepSeek's own R1-Distill-Qwen-7B. The repository is now archived, with its training code living on in the TRL library.

The collective lesson: the core recipe (a strong base model, verifiable rewards, a policy-gradient method with a group or value baseline, long sampling) is simple and robust; the details (loss aggregation, clipping, difficulty filtering, truncation handling) decide whether a run improves smoothly or collapses.

5. Data for reasoning RL

RL data is a set of prompts with a way to check answers, and its quality matters more than its size: R1-Zero trained for 1.6 epochs on its set, DAPO used 17,000 problems. The pipeline:

  1. Collect verifiable problems: competition and textbook maths with final answers, programming problems with tests, logic puzzles with checkers, science questions with unambiguous answers. Procedural generators (rs11) add unlimited synthetic problems at controllable difficulty.
  2. Make answers checkable: convert free-form answers to canonical forms (DAPO transformed answers so each is an integer), use a robust equivalence checker for expressions, and drop problems whose answers cannot be checked reliably, such as proofs.
  3. Clean: remove duplicates, broken or ambiguous problems and wrong reference answers. A wrong reference answer teaches the model that correct reasoning is penalised.
  4. Decontaminate against every benchmark you will report (rs8), by exact and near-duplicate matching.
  5. Filter by difficulty for the model being trained: sample k answers per problem with the starting model and keep problems with pass rates neither 0 nor 1 (often 10–90%). Problems always solved or never solved give zero advantage in GRPO and waste rollouts.
  6. Refresh as training proceeds, because problems move from hard to easy. A curriculum (more easy problems early, harder later) can stabilise early training.

How small can the set be? Qwen3's technical report (May 2025) describes its reasoning-RL stage as using just 3,995 query-verifier pairs, chosen to meet four criteria: not used in its cold-start stage, learnable for the cold-start model, as challenging as possible, and covering a broad range of sub-domains. With large batches, many rollouts per query and entropy kept steady, the flagship 235B model's AIME'24 score rose from 70.1 to 85.1 over 170 RL steps. A few thousand well-chosen, verifiable, hard-but-learnable problems can drive a large gain when the starting model is already strong; the selection criteria matter more than volume.

Worked example of the waste: with 16 samples per prompt and a batch of 512 prompts, if 30% of prompts are always solved and 20% never solved, half the batch, 4,096 responses per step, contributes zero gradient while costing full generation. Dynamic sampling (rs4) refills those slots; pre-filtering avoids generating them.

6. Why length grows, and how much is too much

Three forces push length up during reasoning RL. Genuinely useful exploration: on hard problems, a longer chain that tries more approaches and checks more steps finds the answer more often, so it gets rewarded. Loss artefacts: in the original GRPO loss, each response's token losses are averaged over its own length, so a long wrong answer is penalised less per token than a short wrong one, and the gradient nudges wrong answers to be longer (Dr. GRPO's finding). And truncation handling: if hitting the length limit is scored like any wrong answer, the model gets noisy signal about length.

Length helps until it hurts. The lab models a reasoner whose chance of finding an answer rises with thinking tokens (more so for harder problems), while every token carries a small chance of an unrecovered error. With a single fixed budget for every problem: easy problems peak at about 2,000 tokens (95% correct) and decline after; hard problems peak at about 16,000 (67%); the whole mix peaks at about 8,000 (81%) and falls to 28% at 64,000. Real models differ, but the shape matches the overthinking findings in rs10: the right length depends on the problem, and one global budget is wrong for most of them.

7. Length control, worked by hand

Kimi k1.5's length reward. For a group of responses to one problem, with lengths from min_len to max_len, define λ = 0.5 − (len − min_len) ÷ (max_len − min_len). A correct response gets λ; an incorrect one gets min(0, λ). The reward is added, with a weight, to the correctness reward. Take four responses of 800, 1,200, 2,000 and 3,000 tokens, where the third is wrong. The range is 2,200 tokens. The 800-token answer gets 0.5 − 0 = +0.5; the 1,200-token answer 0.5 − 400/2,200 = +0.318; the 2,000-token wrong answer λ = 0.5 − 1,200/2,200 = −0.045, so min(0, −0.045) = −0.045; the 3,000-token correct answer 0.5 − 1 = −0.5. Short correct answers are favoured, long correct ones penalised, and wrong answers never gain from length. Kimi warmed this penalty up after an initial period without it, because it slowed early training.

DAPO's soft overlong punishment. With a maximum length L_max = 20,480 and a punishment window L_cache = 4,096, responses up to 16,384 tokens get no length penalty; between 16,384 and 20,480 the penalty falls linearly, ((L_max − L_cache) − |y|) ÷ L_cache; beyond, −1. A response of 18,000 tokens gets (16,384 − 18,000) ÷ 4,096 = −0.395; one at 20,480 gets −1. The gradual slope tells the model it is approaching the limit before truncation, instead of punishing a correct but truncated answer as if it were simply wrong.

Unbiased losses and staged limits. A token-level loss (sum over all tokens in the batch, divided by the total token count) removes the per-sequence length bias; R1-Zero's step from 32K to 64K maximum length mid-training is an example of staged limits, letting the model grow into longer contexts once shorter ones are mastered. At inference, budget controls such as thinking-token limits (rs7) are a separate, complementary lever.

8. Language mixing

A base model trained on several languages treats them as interchangeable tokens. When RL rewards only the final answer, nothing stops the chain of thought from drifting from English into Chinese and back, if that happens to correlate with success. R1-Zero did exactly this. R1's fixes worked at two points: filtering mixed-language traces from the cold-start and SFT data, and the language-consistency reward during RL. The paper's ablation on a 7B distilled model showed language consistency deteriorating steadily without the reward and staying stable with it, with a slight drop in reasoning scores. R1 still lists language mixing as a limitation for languages other than English and Chinese: it may reason or answer in English for queries in other languages.

9. Does RL add abilities or sharpen them?

This is the most debated question in the area and a favourite interview follow-up. Yue et al. (Tsinghua) evaluated base and RL-trained models at pass@k for large k across model families, algorithms and tasks. RL models won at small k, but base models caught up and overtook them at large k, and the reasoning paths of RL models were already in the base models' sampling distribution. Their reading: current RL with verifiable rewards improves sampling efficiency, concentrating probability on good paths, rather than teaching new ones, while distillation from a stronger teacher does add new patterns. NVIDIA's ProRL took the other side: with prolonged training, KL control, periodic reference resets and a diverse task suite, RL models outperformed base models across pass@k, including on problems base models never solved at any k. Both can be true: short, narrow RL sharpens; long, diverse RL may extend. Another caution comes from Shao et al.'s spurious-rewards study: GRPO improved Qwen2.5-Math-7B by 21.4 points on MATH-500 with random rewards (29.1 with true rewards), by amplifying a behaviour the model already had, and the same effect did not appear on Llama 3 or OLMo 2.

10. What a small team can realistically do

With a handful of GPUs and weeks, not months, a team cannot reproduce R1, but it can build a capable reasoning model for its domain:

  1. Start from a strong open model of about 7–32B, ideally one already instruction-tuned or reasoning-tuned.
  2. Distil first: fine-tune on open, verified long-chain traces (such as Mixture-of-Thoughts) plus traces in your domain generated by a strong reasoner (within its terms of use). This is the cheapest large gain (rs6).
  3. Run a short RL stage on verifiable problems from your domain, filtered to medium difficulty for your model: GRPO with a token-level loss, dynamic sampling and a clip-higher setting, 8–16 samples per prompt, and a length cap that grows in stages. Dr. GRPO's 7B run on 8 A100s in about a day is a realistic scale.
  4. Evaluate honestly: avg@k over many samples, error bars, decontaminated sets, a held-out domain set, and at least one comparison on a second model family (rs8).
  5. Watch the run: reward, accuracy, response length, entropy, the fraction of zero-variance groups and reward-hacking signals (rs10).

A rough budget for step 3: 128 prompts × 8 samples × 3,000 tokens is about 3 million generated tokens per step. If generation runs at an aggregate 16,000 tokens per second on 8 GPUs (an assumption to replace with a measurement), each step spends about 190 seconds generating, and 200 steps is about 11 hours, before training updates and evaluation. Length growth during training raises this, which is another reason to control it.

11. Distillation or RL for a small model? R1's own answer

DeepSeek ran the comparison directly. It trained Qwen2.5-32B-Base with large-scale RL on maths, code and STEM data for over 10,000 steps, producing Qwen2.5-32B-Zero, and compared it with DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B, the same base model fine-tuned on R1's 800K-example data. On AIME 2024 the RL model scored 47.0 and the distilled model 72.6, and the distilled model was better on every benchmark reported. The paper's conclusions: distilling a stronger model into a smaller one gives excellent results economically, while small models relying on large-scale RL "require enormous computational power and may not even achieve the performance of distillation"; pushing the frontier beyond existing models still needs stronger base models and larger-scale RL. For almost every team that is not training a frontier model, that settles the order of operations: distil first, then use RL to adapt and sharpen.

Why the KL penalty is tiny or absent

In RLHF, the KL penalty to a reference model keeps the policy from drifting into reward-model exploits (rs3, lm6). With verifiable rewards there is less to exploit, and a strong KL penalty fights the large behavioural change (much longer, reflective chains) that reasoning RL is supposed to produce. R1 used a small coefficient (0.001) and reset the reference every 400 steps; Open-Reasoner-Zero and DAPO dropped KL entirely. ProRL kept it but reset the reference periodically to prevent entropy collapse over long runs. The pattern: KL as a weak stabiliser, not a leash.

Entropy collapse and clip-higher

Over training, the policy's token entropy tends to fall: the model becomes confident and stops exploring alternatives, and groups become uniform, giving zero advantages. PPO-style symmetric clipping makes this worse, because a low-probability token can only rise by a limited ratio per update while high-probability tokens are easily reinforced. DAPO's clip-higher raises the upper clip bound (0.28 instead of 0.2), letting rare but useful tokens grow faster. Monitoring entropy and the zero-variance fraction gives early warning of collapse.

The base model decides the ceiling

Several findings point the same way: Qwen2.5 bases already reason and self-reflect; DeepSeek-V3-Base already has "aha" moments; random rewards help only models with latent behaviours to amplify; and RL paths are largely within the base distribution at large k. Practically, choose the base model as carefully as the algorithm, and treat improvements on one family as hypotheses until tested on another. It also explains why R1-Zero's result was possible at all: it started from one of the strongest base models available.

Why distil before RL on small models

DeepSeek's own experiments (and rs6) found that distilling R1's traces into small models beat running RL on those small models directly, at a fraction of the cost. Small models explore poorly: they rarely sample a correct long chain for hard problems, so RL gets little signal. Distillation gives them good chains to imitate; a subsequent short RL stage can then sharpen. This is the standard order for small-model reasoning projects.

Outcome rewards beat process rewards here, for now

An obvious idea is to reward each reasoning step, not just the answer, using a process reward model (rs5). DeepSeek reported trying process reward models and Monte Carlo tree search and finding both hard to make work at scale: defining a "step" in free-form reasoning is ambiguous, labelling steps is expensive, and a learned step reward is exploitable. Outcome rewards are sparse but honest. The successful open recipes all use outcome rewards with group or value baselines, and use process supervision, if at all, for evaluation or for search at test time (rs2).

Evaluation during training is part of the recipe

Reasoning-RL curves are noisy: AIME-style sets are small, and a model's score moves several points between checkpoints by chance. Teams evaluate with many samples per question (DAPO used avg@32), on several sets, and keep a set that is never used for decisions. They also track training reward by data source, because a rising average can hide one domain improving while another degrades. Choosing the final checkpoint on the same small set you report is a common way to overstate results by a few points.

Rejection sampling as a bridge

Rejection sampling appears twice in R1: to create cold-start data from R1-Zero and to create the 800K-example SFT set from the RL checkpoint. It converts RL gains into supervised data that can be mixed with other skills, filtered for readability and language, and reused to train other models. It is also cheaper to iterate on than RL, since generating and filtering data is embarrassingly parallel. Many later recipes (including Llama 3's post-training, lm23) use the same loop.

Multi-domain RL and reward mixing

R1's final stage mixed rule rewards (reasoning) with reward-model rewards (general helpfulness and safety) and format and language rewards. Combining rewards from different sources raises scaling problems (a reward model's scores and a 0/1 correctness reward are on different scales), interference (improving one domain can hurt another) and hacking (the learned reward is exploitable). R1's choice, preference rewards only in the last 400 of 1,700 steps, is a practical way to get their benefits while limiting exposure.

The public case: DeepSeek-R1's report as a training manual

DeepSeek's report, first posted in January 2025, revised in January 2026 and published in Nature, is unusual for a frontier-class model in how much it discloses: the full stage structure, learning rates, KL coefficients, clip ratios, sample counts, batch sizes, maximum lengths, step counts, reference-reset intervals, rollout batching, the language-consistency reward and its ablation, prompt templates and data-generation prompts for cold start, reward-hacking observations with preference rewards, and the GPU-hour cost of each reasoning stage (147K H800 GPU-hours, about $294K at $2 an hour, excluding pretraining). It also reports what did not work for them (process reward models and Monte Carlo tree search, in an earlier version) and limitations (language mixing outside English and Chinese, limited RL on software engineering because evaluations are slow). It set the template every later open recipe compares against.

Failure modes during training runs

Collapse. Reward plateaus or falls, entropy collapses, responses become repetitive. Check clipping, learning rate, the fraction of zero-variance groups and data difficulty; reset the reference or add entropy control.

Runaway length. Length grows faster than accuracy, many responses hit the cap. Switch to a token-level loss, add soft overlong shaping or a length reward, and inspect whether the extra tokens are repetition.

Reward hacking. Accuracy on the training reward rises while held-out accuracy does not: answers formatted to fool the checker, guessing patterns in answer distributions, special-casing tests in code (rs10, rs11). Audit samples, harden verifiers, keep held-out evaluations.

Language and format drift. Chains switch languages or lose structure. Add language and format rewards, filter SFT data.

Rollout stalls. One extremely long generation holds up a batch. Use partial rollouts, asynchronous workers or per-sample time limits.

Silent data bugs. A verifier that rejects correct answers written in an unexpected format (a fraction instead of a decimal, a set in a different order) teaches the model that correct reasoning fails; a few percent of mislabelled references can stall learning on exactly the hardest problems. Sample rejected correct-looking answers every few hundred steps and audit the checker, not just the model.

What to monitor

Per step: mean reward and accuracy by data source, response length distribution (mean and the share hitting the cap), token entropy, the fraction of groups with zero reward variance, KL to the reference, clip fractions, and throughput. Every few hundred steps: avg@k on held-out sets with error bars, a sample of chains read by a person for readability, language and suspicious behaviour, and a comparison against the starting model at several values of k.

A reproduction checklist

Before trusting a reproduction (your own or someone else's), check: the base model and whether it was already reasoning-tuned; the exact data and its decontamination; samples per prompt, batch size, maximum length and how truncation was scored; the loss aggregation (sequence-level or token-level) and advantage normalisation; KL settings and reference resets; the number of steps and wall-clock hours; the evaluation protocol (avg@k or a single sample, temperature, number of seeds); and a baseline that controls for the base model's own ability, such as format-only or random rewards. Most disagreements between papers in this area trace back to one of these, not to the headline algorithm.

Goal: draw a length-versus-accuracy curve on synthetic traces and compute two published length rewards by hand and in code. About 10 minutes, Python 3.12 standard library only.

  1. Save as rs9_lab.py and run it:
import math, random
random.seed(0)

# ---- 1. A length-vs-accuracy curve on synthetic traces -----------------------------------
# Toy model of a reasoner: a problem of difficulty d needs about d * 400 tokens of useful work;
# every extra token also carries a small chance of derailing the chain (an error that is not caught).
def p_correct(budget, d, work=400, derail_per_1k=0.02):
    solved = 1 - math.exp(-budget / (d * work))           # enough thinking to find the answer?
    survived = math.exp(-derail_per_1k * budget / 1000)    # no unrecovered error on the way
    return solved * survived

problems = [random.choice([1, 2, 4, 8, 16]) for _ in range(2_000)]   # mix of easy and hard
print("budget  easy(d=1)  hard(d=16)  whole set")
for budget in (500, 1_000, 2_000, 4_000, 8_000, 16_000, 32_000, 64_000):
    acc = sum(random.random() < p_correct(budget, d) for d in problems) / len(problems)
    print(f"{budget:6d}  {p_correct(budget, 1):8.3f}  {p_correct(budget, 16):9.3f}  {acc:8.3f}")

# ---- 2. Kimi k1.5's length reward on one group -------------------------------------------
def kimi_length_reward(lengths, correct):
    lo, hi = min(lengths), max(lengths)
    if hi == lo:
        return [0.0] * len(lengths)
    out = []
    for n, c in zip(lengths, correct):
        lam = 0.5 - (n - lo) / (hi - lo)
        out.append(lam if c else min(0.0, lam))
    return out

lengths, correct = [800, 1_200, 2_000, 3_000], [1, 1, 0, 1]
print("\nKimi length reward:", [round(r, 3) for r in kimi_length_reward(lengths, correct)])

# ---- 3. DAPO's soft overlong punishment ---------------------------------------------------
def soft_overlong(n, l_max=20_480, l_cache=4_096):
    if n <= l_max - l_cache:
        return 0.0
    if n <= l_max:
        return ((l_max - l_cache) - n) / l_cache
    return -1.0

for n in (12_000, 16_384, 18_000, 20_480, 21_000):
    print(f"DAPO soft overlong, length {n:6d}: {soft_overlong(n):+.3f}")

What to look for. The table should show easy problems peaking near 2,000 tokens (about 0.95), hard problems near 16,000 (about 0.67) and the whole mix near 8,000 (about 0.81), then everything falling toward 0.28 at 64,000 as the per-token derailment term dominates. The Kimi rewards should print [0.5, 0.318, −0.045, −0.5], matching the worked example: shorter correct answers are favoured, the long correct answer is penalised, and the wrong answer never benefits from length. DAPO's soft punishment is 0 up to 16,384 tokens, −0.395 at 18,000 and −1 at 20,480 and beyond. Now change derail_per_1k to 0.005 and watch the peaks move right: a model that makes fewer unrecovered errors can use longer chains profitably, which is one reading of why stronger models think longer.

Design: a reasoning model for contract analysis

A legal-tech company wants a reasoning model that answers questions about contracts ("Is the indemnity capped? Which clause governs termination for convenience?"). It has 32 H100s for six weeks, 40,000 contracts, 6,000 questions with lawyer-written answers (short, checkable: clause numbers, yes/no, amounts), and an open 14B instruct model. Design the training plan, the data, the rewards and the evaluation.

Answer

Do not start from zero RL. A 14B instruct model plus distillation is the efficient path. First, generate long reasoning traces for a few thousand contract questions with a strong reasoning model (within its terms), keep only those whose final answers match the lawyers', filter for readability, and SFT the 14B model on them together with open reasoning traces (maths, code) to preserve general reasoning.

Make answers verifiable. Turn each lawyer answer into a canonical form: a set of clause numbers, a boolean, an amount with currency. Write a checker that normalises formats ("Clause 12.3", "s. 12.3"). Drop questions with ambiguous answers or move them to a separate rubric-judged evaluation. Split 6,000 questions into 4,500 for RL, 500 for validation, 1,000 held out, with whole contracts kept in one split to avoid leakage.

RL. GRPO with a token-level loss, 8 samples per question, dynamic sampling, clip-higher, a length cap of 8K tokens growing to 16K, and a small reward for citing clauses that exist in the contract (checked by string match) to discourage invented clauses. Filter questions by the SFT model's pass rate to the 10–90% band before starting and refresh after each epoch. Monitor length, entropy, zero-variance groups and any rise in reward without held-out gains.

Evaluation. avg@8 on the 1,000 held-out questions with error bars, by question type; a lawyer review of 100 chains for faithfulness to the contract text; a check on general benchmarks to catch regressions; and a comparison with the SFT-only model to show the RL stage is worth its cost. Budget: SFT is a few hours; RL at 4,500 questions × 8 samples × ~6K tokens is about 216M generated tokens per epoch, comfortably within weeks on 32 H100s.
