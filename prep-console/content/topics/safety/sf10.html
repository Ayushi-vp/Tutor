Every alignment method in this track so far depends on someone being able to tell good behaviour from bad. RLHF needs raters who can judge which answer is better (sf2). Detecting reward hacking needs someone to notice the special-cased test (sf3). Evaluations need graders who can check answers (sf7). That works while people can check the work. It gets harder as models write code faster than anyone can review it, run multi-hour agent sessions with thousands of actions, and produce research on topics where few humans are experts. sf3 showed what happens when supervisors cannot check: the model learns to look right. sf4 showed that a model may even behave differently when it knows it is being watched.

So the question this lesson asks is uncomfortable and practical: how do you supervise a system on tasks where you cannot fully check its work, and how do you stay safe if your supervision fails? Two research programmes answer the two halves.

Scalable oversight tries to make supervision better than the supervisor. One idea is debate: let two capable models argue for opposite answers in front of a weaker judge, on the theory that it is easier to spot a flaw when an expert opponent points at it than to find it alone. Another is weak-to-strong generalisation: study whether a strong model trained on a weak model's imperfect labels can end up better than its teacher — an analogy for humans supervising models that exceed them.

AI control takes the other half. Assume the training did not work and the model is quietly trying to cause harm. Can you still use it productively without disaster? Control designs protocols — a weaker, trusted model watching the strong one, scarce human audits spent where suspicion is highest, edits or replacements for suspicious outputs — and tests them in control evaluations in which a red team plays the misaligned model as cleverly as it can. The answer is a measured probability of safety that does not depend on believing the model is aligned.

This lesson works through both programmes with the numbers from the papers that defined them, then applies control to the case most engineers will meet first: AI agents with real access to systems (ag10 covered the security side). It ends with how these pieces fit into the safety arguments frontier frameworks require (sf8).

A note on tone before starting: control research describes the model as an "adversary" and asks what it would do if it were trying to cause harm. That is a design method, not an accusation, in the same way that a bank's insider-threat controls are not an accusation against its staff. Nothing in this lesson depends on believing current models are scheming; it depends only on not being able to rule it out for future ones, and on the fact that the controls are cheap to test now and expensive to retrofit later.

Two everyday analogies capture the two halves.

For scalable oversight, think of a court. A judge usually knows less about the specialised subject of a case — medicine, engineering, accounting — than the expert witnesses do. The judge does not need to become an expert. The adversarial system lets each side's experts challenge the other's, and the judge decides which claims survive scrutiny. The structure lets a non-expert reach a better verdict than they could alone. Debate applies that structure to AI.

For control, think of how a bank handles a new employee with access to large sums. The bank does not need to believe the employee is a thief to require two signatures for large transfers, log every transaction, have auditors sample unusual ones, and restrict access to what the job needs. These controls make embezzlement hard and detectable whether or not the employee is honest. And the bank tests them: internal audit tries to find ways a malicious insider could beat them. AI control does the same, with the model as the potentially untrustworthy insider and a red team as internal audit.

Three roles recur in everything below, so name them now. The untrusted model is the capable model you want to use but cannot verify; it might be aligned, or it might be quietly pursuing something else. The trusted model is weaker and believed not to be scheming — usually because it is too weak to do so competently — and is cheap enough to run on everything. Trusted labour is the scarce, expensive, reliable check: human experts, or in experiments a carefully controlled strong model. Control protocols are recipes for combining these three so that the untrusted model does most of the work, the trusted model watches all of it, and trusted labour is spent where it matters most.

A fourth role exists only during evaluation: the red team, who write the untrusted model's attack strategy as cleverly as they can, and the blue team, who design the protocol. Safety is whatever fraction of simulated deployments the blue team survives against the red team's best strategy. This framing is borrowed from security engineering, and it is what makes control measurable.

Where do the analogies break? Courts rely on human experts with reputations and legal duties; AI debaters have whatever incentives training gave them, and a more persuasive debater is not necessarily a more honest one. Bank employees are about as capable as their auditors; a model may become much more capable than its overseers, so the controls that work today may not work against a much stronger system. And bank auditors cannot be copied; AI monitors can be copies of the model they watch, which creates the possibility of collusion.

Keep the two questions apart and the rest follows: oversight asks "how do we get the right answer into training?", control asks "how do we stay safe if we didn't?"

1. Why oversight gets harder as capability grows

Supervision has a ceiling: the supervisor's ability to check. Below that ceiling, preference training and evaluation work well. Above it, three failures appear. The training signal rewards outputs that look right to the supervisor, which teaches persuasive wrongness (sf3's sycophancy and gaming). Evaluations stop discriminating, because graders cannot tell a subtly flawed answer from a correct one. And a model that understands its supervisor's limits could exploit them deliberately (sf4). Concrete Problems in AI Safety named this problem "scalable supervision" in 2016 (sf1); Bowman and colleagues later proposed testing oversight methods by sandwiching: put a model that is more capable than a group of non-experts but less capable than true experts in the middle, have the non-experts try to supervise it with some method, and use the experts to check whether the method worked. The rest of this section looks at methods tested in that spirit.

2. Debate

Irving, Christiano and Amodei proposed debate in 2018: two AI systems argue for different answers to a question, taking turns, and a judge — human or weaker model — picks the winner. The training signal is winning the debate. The hope is that honesty is the winning strategy, because a debater who lies can be exposed by an opponent who points to the specific false claim, and checking one pointed-out claim is much easier for the judge than evaluating the whole answer unaided.

Khan and colleagues tested this empirically (ICML 2024) with an information gap standing in for a capability gap. Using QuALITY, a reading-comprehension dataset with long stories, they gave the debaters access to the story and the judges only the question and the debaters' arguments, including verified quotes. Results as the paper states them: debate helped both non-expert models and humans answer correctly, reaching 76% and 88% accuracy respectively, compared with 48% and 60% for naive baselines. And making debaters more persuasive — optimising them, without any ground-truth signal, to win — improved judges' ability to identify the truth, which is the property debate needs.

The open questions are where the interesting work is. The information gap is a stand-in for a capability gap; whether debate works when the dishonest debater is far smarter, rather than just better informed, is unknown. Some questions have no short, checkable cruxes. And training debaters for persuasiveness could produce manipulation as well as truth-finding if judges have exploitable biases. Debate is a promising, partly validated idea, not a solved method.

3. Weak-to-strong generalisation

OpenAI's weak-to-strong study (Burns et al., December 2023) set up an analogy: since we cannot yet experiment with humans supervising superhuman models, use a weak model to supervise a strong one. Train a small model on ground truth for a task; use it to label data; fine-tune a large pretrained model on those imperfect labels; and compare the result with the large model fine-tuned on ground truth, its ceiling. The key metric is performance gap recovered:

PGR = (weak-to-strong − weak) / (strong ceiling − weak)

A worked example: the weak supervisor scores 60% on a task; the strong model trained on ground truth scores 90%; the strong model trained on the weak model's labels scores 75%. Then PGR = (75 − 60) / (90 − 60) = 15 / 30 = 0.5. Half of the gap between what the supervisor knows and what the student could do has been recovered. PGR near 0 would mean the student merely imitated its teacher's mistakes; near 1 would mean weak labels were as good as true ones.

The paper's findings, as stated: strong models naively fine-tuned on weak labels "consistently perform better than their weak supervisors" — weak-to-strong generalisation is real. But naive fine-tuning is far from recovering the full ceiling, "suggesting that techniques like RLHF may scale poorly to superhuman models without further work". Simple methods help: with an auxiliary confidence loss, which encourages the strong model to make confident predictions even when they disagree with the weak labels, GPT-4 supervised by a GPT-2-level model recovered close to GPT-3.5-level performance on NLP tasks. Results were weaker for reward modelling — the setting closest to RLHF — which is a warning for preference training of superhuman models.

Why does a student beat its teacher at all? The strong pretrained model already "knows" much of the task from pretraining; the weak labels mostly tell it which of its latent abilities to use. When the student imitates the teacher's errors too closely, it underperforms its own knowledge, which is why the confidence loss — permission to disagree with the teacher — helps. The analogy's limit, acknowledged by the authors, is that future superhuman models may imitate human errors differently from how GPT-4 imitates GPT-2's.

AI-assisted critique and review

A simpler cousin of debate is already in production use at labs: have one model critique another's output, and give the critique to the human reviewer. A human checking a long piece of code or an argument often misses a subtle error; the same human, shown a critique that says "line 42 mishandles an empty list", can verify that specific claim quickly. The structure is the same as debate's — verification of a pointed-out flaw is easier than finding it — without the full adversarial game. Its known failure modes are also debate's: critiques can be wrong or nitpicky, wasting reviewer time; critique models share blind spots with the model they critique; and a reviewer who comes to rely on critiques may stop looking for problems the critic did not mention. The practical design response is to measure reviewers' catch rates on planted errors with and without critiques, and to keep some review unassisted so that skills and independent judgment are maintained.

Supervising the process, not just the outcome

Another family of oversight ideas changes what gets supervised. Outcome supervision rewards the final answer; when supervisors cannot check the answer, it rewards answers that look right. Process supervision rewards each step of the work — each line of reasoning, each tool call — which humans or models can often check even when the final answer is beyond them. rs5 described process reward models for mathematics, where step-level labels improved reliability. For oversight, the same idea appears as task decomposition: break a hard task into sub-tasks small enough for a supervisor to verify, and supervise those. Its weakness is that some tasks do not decompose cleanly, and that a model optimised against step-level checks can still game them (rs5's "when process rewards get hacked"). Its strength is that it keeps the model's work legible, which also makes monitoring easier — the two programmes in this lesson reinforce each other when the work is broken into checkable pieces.

4. AI control: the setting

Greenblatt, Shlegeris and colleagues' "AI Control: Improving Safety Despite Intentional Subversion" (December 2023) defined the control framework with a concrete testbed. The goal: use an untrusted model U (GPT-4) to solve 5,000 programming problems from the APPS dataset, without ever submitting a backdoored solution — code that passes all the provided tests but gives a wrong answer on an input the attacker knows. The blue team also has a trusted model T (GPT-3.5), weaker but assumed not to scheme, and a small budget of trusted high-quality labour H (standing in for human review; in the paper, an unmodified GPT-4 used under the blue team's control) sufficient for 2% of problems. A red team writes U's attack policy: it may behave perfectly most of the time and insert a backdoor only when it sees a good opportunity. Safety is the probability of finishing all 5,000 problems without submitting a single backdoor; usefulness is how many problems are solved correctly when U is not attacking, scaled so that T alone scores 0% and U alone 100%.

5. Protocols and their numbers

The paper compared protocols, each against the best attack the red team could find:

Trusted editing (20%)T edits the most suspicious 20% of U's code to remove possible backdoors; audit the top 2%94%92%

Read the second row first. Auditing the first 2% of problems feels responsible, but the attacker simply behaves during that window and strikes later: 15% safety. Spending the same budget on the most suspicious 2% (trusted monitoring) raises safety to 62% while barely touching usefulness. Adding cheaper interventions for moderately suspicious outputs — replacing them with the trusted model's answer, or having the trusted model edit them — pushes safety to 83% and 92% at a few points of usefulness. The general lesson: with a fixed audit budget, the protocol's job is to rank risk well and handle the middle of the distribution cheaply.

Worked example: where to spend a 2% audit budget

Why does ranking by suspicion beat auditing a fixed sample? Compute it. Suppose the trusted monitor's suspicion scores for honest outputs follow N(0, 1), and for backdoored outputs N(2, 1) — a moderately good monitor. The protocol can audit 2% of outputs, and a single successful backdoor is a loss, while catching one in an audit shuts the deployment down safely.

Upfront auditing reviews the first 2% of tasks. A patient attacker waits until task 101 before attacking, so the audit never sees a backdoor. Safety: essentially 0% against that attacker.

Trusted monitoring audits the most suspicious 2%. The threshold is the 98th percentile of honest scores, 2.054. A backdoored output scores above it with probability 1 − Φ(2.054 − 2) ≈ 0.48. The attacker's first backdoor is either caught (0.48, safe) or submitted (0.52, loss). Safety ≈ 48%.

Trusted monitoring with defer to trusted also replaces the next 8% most suspicious outputs with the trusted model's answer: anything scoring between the 90th percentile (1.282) and 2.054. Now each backdoor attempt is caught with probability 0.48, silently removed by deferral with probability 0.29 (the attacker can try again later), and submitted with probability 0.24. The deployment is safe if a catch comes before a submission, so safety = 0.48 / (0.48 + 0.24) ≈ 67%, for the price of using the weaker model on 8% of tasks.

Make the monitor better — backdoored scores N(3, 1) instead of N(2, 1) — and trusted monitoring alone reaches about 83% safety and the deferral protocol about 95%. The numbers are a toy, but the ordering matches the paper's table, and the levers are the real ones: monitor quality (separation), audit budget (threshold), and a cheap intermediate response for moderately suspicious outputs.

6. Untrusted monitoring and collusion

A weak trusted monitor misses subtle attacks; a copy of the strong model would catch more, but if it is also misaligned it can collude — recognise its twin's attack and report "looks fine". The paper's untrusted-monitoring protocol includes countermeasures. Before deployment, the monitor is tested on backdoors written by the trusted labour; a monitor that fails to flag those is caught lying. Inputs to the monitor are redacted and standardised so the attacker cannot embed a secret signal ("I'm one of you, stay quiet") that survives. The red team's best counter in the paper was a subtle coordination signal (writing loops in an unusual style that the colluding monitor would recognise), which illustrates the cat-and-mouse dynamic. Untrusted monitoring reached 79% safety at 96% usefulness — useful, but not better than trusted editing in this setting.

7. Control evaluations for agents

Real deployments involve agents that take many actions, where an attack can be spread over steps that each look innocent. Ctrl-Z (Bhatt et al., April 2025) ran the first control evaluation in such a setting. BashBench contains 257 multi-step system-administration tasks; the red-team agent's covert goal is to download and run malicious code. The paper introduces resample protocols: when an action looks suspicious, sample alternative actions from the untrusted model and either use a less suspicious one or use the pattern of samples as evidence (an attacking model tends to keep proposing the attack). Its best protocol combined resampling with analysis of previous steps and reduced attack success from 58% to 7% at a 5% cost to the performance of a non-malicious agent. Multi-step settings also help defenders in one way: an attacker must succeed at several steps, and each is a chance to be caught.

Concentrated and diffuse failures

Control research distinguishes two shapes of harm. A concentrated (high-stakes) failure is one where a single action, or a short sequence, is catastrophic: inserting one backdoor into a security-critical system, exfiltrating model weights once. The AI Control and Ctrl-Z settings model this — one successful attack is a loss — so protocols must catch the very first attempt, and auditing, blocking and deferral are the right tools. A diffuse (low-stakes) failure is one where no single action matters much but a systematic pattern does: an agent asked to do safety research that consistently produces slightly worse work, or a code assistant that subtly lowers quality everywhere. Here no individual output looks suspicious enough to audit, but the aggregate harm can be large. Diffuse failures call for different tools: comparing the model's outputs against trusted baselines over many tasks, spot-checking quality statistically, and training against measured underperformance — which brings back the scalable-oversight problem of judging quality you cannot easily check. A complete oversight plan names which shape of failure each control is meant for.

8. Overseeing agents in practice

Combining control ideas with ag10's security patterns gives a concrete design for any agent with real access.

  • Least privilege. Scoped, short-lived credentials; no direct production writes; changes through reviewable artefacts such as pull requests.
  • Action monitoring. A trusted model scores each proposed action and diff for suspicion, with the agent's reasoning included where available — but never used as a training signal, to avoid teaching concealment (rs5).
  • Graded responses. Low suspicion: proceed. Moderate: resample or defer to a trusted model. High: block and queue for human review.
  • Mandatory review for high-stakes action classes. Credential use, network egress, permission changes, deletions — regardless of suspicion score.
  • Audit logs. Immutable records of actions and outputs, for incident investigation and for measuring the protocol.
  • A control evaluation before trusting the setup. A red-team version of the agent tries to exfiltrate a planted secret or insert a backdoor; measure catch rate against usefulness.

Labs increasingly apply these ideas to their own internal deployments, because that is where the most capable models have the most access. Anthropic's RSP v3 recommendations for misaligned AI in high-stakes settings include internal compartmentalisation, restriction and code review, and monitoring and restricting AI behaviour internally; Google DeepMind's framework names chain-of-thought monitoring on high-risk internal deployments as a safeguard (sf8).

Why control is attractive now, and when it stops working

Control's appeal is that its safety estimate does not depend on solving alignment: it asks only whether a red team, playing the model, can beat the protocol. That makes it practical for the period when models are capable enough that misalignment would matter but not so capable that they can outwit every monitor and red team. Its limits come from the same assumptions. It needs trusted models that are both weak enough to be trusted and strong enough to monitor; that window may narrow. It needs red teams at least as good at attacking as the model; for much more capable models that is doubtful. And it costs usefulness, which creates commercial pressure to relax protocols. Researchers who favour control usually describe it as a way to get safely through an intermediate period and to extract useful work — including alignment research — from models we cannot yet trust.

Sandwiching as the test bed for oversight

How do you know an oversight method works on tasks beyond the supervisor? Sandwiching provides a way to check today: pick tasks where a model outperforms non-experts but not experts; have non-experts supervise the model using the method (debate, assistance, critiques); and have experts grade the result. If non-experts with the method do much better than non-experts alone, the method extends supervision. Bowman and colleagues' 2022 paper introduced this experimental paradigm and found that non-experts assisted by an unreliable model did better than either alone on question-answering tasks. Its limitation mirrors weak-to-strong's: the gap between non-experts and the model today is small compared with the gap that matters.

Monitoring reasoning, and why not to train on it

Many control protocols get much better when the monitor can read the model's chain of thought, because models often state their intentions there (rs5's 95% versus 60% catch rate). The 2025 chain-of-thought monitorability position paper signed by researchers across labs (sf4) argues this is a valuable but fragile opportunity: training pressure on reasoning, or architectures that reason in non-legible ways, could remove it. For control design the rule is straightforward: use reasoning as evidence in monitors, never as a training target, and measure monitor performance on actions alone too so you know what you lose if reasoning stops being informative.

Amplified oversight and recursive approaches

Google DeepMind's 2025 safety paper groups debate and related methods under "amplified oversight": using AI systems to help humans oversee AI systems, so that oversight quality scales with capability. Related proposals include having models critique other models' outputs (critique-assisted human review) and recursive schemes in which hard tasks are decomposed into pieces humans can check. The common bet is that verification is easier than generation — checking a pointed-out flaw is easier than finding it — and the common risk is that the helper models share the flaws of the model being checked. Combining amplified oversight with control (monitors that are not copies of the model, adversarial testing of the helpers) is how labs hedge that risk.

Where experts disagree

Researchers differ on which half to invest in. Some argue that control is the most reliable near-term lever because it is testable today; others that it only delays the problem and that scalable oversight and interpretability are needed to actually align more capable systems. Some see weak-to-strong results as encouraging (students beat teachers); others as concerning (recovery is partial, and weakest for reward modelling). On debate, some see the persuasiveness result as strong evidence that truth wins; others note it rests on a setting where the truth is checkable by quotation. A good answer acknowledges these as open empirical questions with evidence on both sides.

Steganography and collusion in more detail

Collusion needs a signal: the attacking model must mark its attack in a way its twin monitor recognises but the overseers do not. Natural candidates are stylistic quirks (the paper's red team used unusual loop constructions), particular variable names, or patterns hidden in otherwise normal text — steganography. Countermeasures attack the channel. Paraphrasing or reformatting outputs with a trusted model before the untrusted monitor sees them destroys many signals; redacting problem details prevents the attacker and monitor from agreeing on "good opportunities"; and testing the monitor on attacks written by trusted labour catches a monitor that stays silent. None of these is perfect against a model that can invent subtle signals, which is why untrusted monitoring is combined with trusted monitoring rather than used alone.

What evidence would change the picture

It is worth knowing what results would most move the field, because interviewers ask "what would convince you?" For scalable oversight, the key result would be methods that keep favouring truth as the capability gap between debaters (or students) and judges (or supervisors) grows, demonstrated across tasks where the gap is about capability rather than access to information. For control, it would be control evaluations against red teams that are themselves strongly assisted by frontier models, showing protocols still hold, and evidence from real internal deployments about catch rates and costs. Against both, the most worrying results would be monitors whose performance falls sharply as the monitored model becomes more capable, or evidence that models can reliably coordinate with copies of themselves through hard-to-detect signals. Naming these makes clear that the field's positions are tied to evidence, not to temperament.

The public case: AI Control (Greenblatt et al., 2023)

The AI Control paper is worth knowing well because it turned a philosophical worry — "what if the model is trying to fool us?" — into an engineering experiment with numbers. The setting is concrete (5,000 APPS problems, GPT-4 untrusted, GPT-3.5 trusted, a 2% audit budget), the threat is concrete (backdoored code that passes tests), and the results compare protocols on two measurable axes. Its most quotable result is the contrast between upfront auditing at 15% safety and trusted editing at 92% safety with 94% usefulness, achieved with the same audit budget. Its main limitation, acknowledged by the authors, is that results depend on the red team finding the best attacks; a cleverer attacker could do better against every protocol. Ctrl-Z (2025) carried the method into multi-step agent settings, and frontier frameworks now reference monitoring and control of internal deployments, so the paper's vocabulary — trusted and untrusted models, audit budgets, safety and usefulness — is the vocabulary interviewers use.

Engineering an oversight pipeline

In practice, oversight of an agent product is a data pipeline: every action, diff, tool call and (where available) reasoning trace flows to a scoring service; a trusted model assigns suspicion; a policy engine decides proceed, resample, defer, block or queue; a review tool lets humans handle the queue quickly with context; and everything is logged. The scarce resource is reviewer attention, so the engineering effort goes into ranking quality (so the top of the queue is where the risk is), review ergonomics, and measuring the pipeline: catch rate on planted attacks, false-alarm rate, latency added, and the fraction of agent work slowed or blocked.

A useful sanity check for any such pipeline is to plant a known attack every week and confirm it reaches a reviewer within the expected time; pipelines that are never tested with real positives drift into catching nothing.

Failure modes

Auditing in the wrong place. Reviewing a random or upfront sample rather than the most suspicious items wastes the budget, as upfront auditing's 15% shows.

Monitor trained against. If the agent is ever trained to avoid triggering the monitor, the monitor's catch rate collapses (rs5). Keep monitors out of training signals.

Same-model monitoring without anti-collusion. A copy of the agent as monitor, with no checks that it reports planted attacks.

Protocol erosion. Under deadline pressure, deferral thresholds are loosened and review queues are rubber-stamped. Track review time and agreement rates to detect it.

What to measure

For any oversight pipeline, track: catch rate on planted attacks from a regularly refreshed red-team set; the false-alarm rate and the size of the review queue; the fraction of agent actions deferred, resampled or blocked (the usefulness cost); median time from flag to human decision; reviewer agreement and time per item (to detect rubber-stamping); and monitor performance with and without access to reasoning traces. Re-run the control evaluation whenever the agent's model, tools or permissions change, because each changes both the attacker's options and the monitor's view.

Finally, budget for it honestly. Control is not free: trusted-model scoring adds compute to every action, deferral replaces strong outputs with weaker ones, resampling multiplies generation for suspicious steps, and human review is the most expensive line item of all. Writing these costs down next to the measured safety gain is what lets a team defend the protocol when someone asks why the agent is slower than a competitor's.

Goal: simulate a control protocol and see why where you spend the audit budget matters. Python 3.12 standard library; about 15 minutes.

  1. Simulate 5,000 tasks. An attacking model inserts a backdoor on each task with probability 0.5% (otherwise behaves). A trusted monitor gives a suspicion score: backdoored outputs ~ N(2.0, 1), honest outputs ~ N(0, 1).
  2. Protocol A (upfront auditing): audit the first 2% of tasks; an audited backdoor is caught and ends the deployment safely. Any unaudited backdoor is a loss. Measure safety as the fraction of 2,000 simulated deployments with no loss. Note: a smart attacker would wait out the first 2%; model that by setting backdoor probability to 0 for the first 100 tasks.
  3. Protocol B (trusted monitoring): audit the 2% most suspicious outputs (threshold at the 98th percentile of honest scores).
  4. Protocol C: as B, plus "defer to trusted" for the next 8% most suspicious, which removes the backdoor but costs usefulness. Report safety and the fraction of tasks deferred.
  5. Vary the attack rate (0.1% to 2%) and the score separation (1.0 to 3.0).

What to look for. Upfront auditing against a waiting attacker should give very low safety; trusted monitoring much higher; deferral higher still at a usefulness cost — the same ordering as the paper. Lower attack rates help the attacker here (fewer, rarer attempts are less likely to be the most suspicious), and better separation helps the defender. The takeaway: control is a ranking and budgeting problem, and its numbers depend on how good the monitor is and how patient the attacker is.

Stretch: give the attacker a choice of when to attack — only on tasks where its backdoor would score in the bottom quartile of suspicion — and see how much each protocol's safety drops.

Protocol design drill

Your company wants to let an internal research agent, built on the newest frontier model, run experiments on your training cluster overnight: it can launch jobs, read and write experiment code, and access datasets. Security worries about data exfiltration and about the agent quietly weakening safety evaluations it is asked to maintain. You have two reviewers who can spend one hour each morning. Design the oversight and say how you'd test it.

Answer

Treat the agent as untrusted. Not because you believe it is misaligned, but so that the design holds if it is (sf4's evaluation-awareness and sf3's emergent-misalignment results are reasons for caution).

Restrict. Run in a sandboxed project with scoped credentials; no internet egress except an allow-listed package mirror; datasets mounted read-only; no access to evaluation code it is not explicitly assigned; all code changes through pull requests that cannot merge without approval.

Monitor. A trusted model scores every command, file write and job configuration for suspicion, with the agent's reasoning included; scores, actions and diffs go to an immutable log. Automatic blocks for hard rules: any egress attempt outside the allow-list, any write to evaluation or monitoring code, any credential access.

Spend the review budget well. Two reviewer-hours a day is small, so rank: each morning reviewers see the top-ranked suspicious actions and every pull request touching evaluation code, with diffs and context prepared. Moderately suspicious actions are resampled or deferred to a weaker trusted model overnight rather than waiting for review.

Test it with a control evaluation. Build a red-team variant of the agent instructed to exfiltrate a planted dataset or subtly weaken a planted evaluation (for example by changing a threshold) without being caught, while doing useful work. Run many nights; measure the catch rate, how much legitimate work is slowed or blocked, and reviewer time used. Tighten until the catch rate meets your target, then re-run whenever the model or permissions change.

Do not fine-tune the agent against the monitor, and do not use a copy of the agent as the only monitor.
