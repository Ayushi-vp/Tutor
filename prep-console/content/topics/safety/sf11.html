Everything in this track so far produces evidence that only exists inside a company unless someone writes it down: evaluation results, red-team findings, safeguard measurements, framework determinations. Governance is the set of rules and practices that decide what gets written down, who sees it, what a company must do about it, and what happens when something goes wrong. For a long time all of it was voluntary. Since 2025 a growing part is law.

Consider an engineer at a company that trains a large model. In 2023 the release checklist might have said "publish a model card". By late 2026 it includes items like: confirm the training-compute estimate against the EU's 10^25 FLOP presumption, because above it the model is presumed to carry systemic risk and needs evaluations, risk mitigation, serious-incident reporting and cybersecurity measures; check the company's revenue and compute against California's SB 53, because above its thresholds the company must publish a frontier AI framework, follow it, and report critical safety incidents within 15 days; prepare for New York's version from January 2027 with a 72-hour clock; publish a system card that documents the dangerous-capability evaluations; and make sure the incident on-call rota knows what counts as a reportable incident in each jurisdiction. Get it wrong and the consequences now include fines.

The details also move fast, and they are easy to get wrong in an interview. People often say the EU's general-purpose AI rules "start in 2027" (they have applied since August 2025; 2027 is for older models). They confuse the EU's deferral of high-risk system rules in July 2026 with a deferral of everything. They describe US federal policy as of 2023, before the executive order of that year was revoked. This topic gives the state of play as of October 2026, with every date checked against the legal text or an official page, and is explicit about what is settled and what is contested.

The lesson covers documentation first — model cards and system cards, and what makes them useful — then the EU AI Act's general-purpose AI obligations and timeline in detail, then California's SB 53, New York's RAISE Act, the US federal position and other jurisdictions briefly, then voluntary commitments and incident reporting. It ends with what this means for an engineer's daily work.

A word on sources: every date and threshold below comes from the legal text or an official page as checked in early October 2026, and the few points that rest on secondary analysis say so. Treat the lesson as a map with a date on it.

A helpful analogy is the pharmaceutical industry. A drug comes with a label for patients and doctors — what it is for, how to use it, side effects. Behind the label sits a much larger regulatory file: trial data, safety studies, manufacturing controls. After approval, companies must report adverse events within set deadlines, and regulators can act on them. Some rules are law, some are industry codes, and some are company practice that the law later adopted.

AI governance is converging on a similar shape:

  • The model card is the label: short, aimed at people choosing or using a model.
  • The system card and the frontier safety framework's reports are the safety file: long, detailed, aimed at experts, regulators and the public.
  • Regulation — the EU AI Act, California's SB 53, New York's RAISE Act — sets what must be documented, evaluated and reported, mostly for the largest developers.
  • Codes of practice and voluntary commitments fill in how, before formal standards exist.
  • Incident reporting is the adverse-event system: defined events, deadlines, and a regulator who receives them.

Each document serves a different reader making a different decision, and most bad documentation comes from forgetting who the reader is. A developer choosing a model wants to know what it is good at, where it fails and what it may not be used for — a model card. A downstream company building a hiring tool on top of a model needs information to meet its own legal duties — the EU Act's downstream documentation. A researcher or journalist assessing whether a frontier release was responsible wants evaluation evidence and safeguards — a system card. A regulator wants to know whether the company follows its own framework and what happened when something went wrong — framework reports, transparency reports and incident reports. Writing one document for all four readers produces a document none of them can use.

Keep the layers straight and most governance questions become answerable.

Where does the pharmaceutical analogy break? Drugs are approved before sale; no AI law yet requires approval of a model before release — the current laws require documentation, evaluation, disclosure and reporting, not a licence. Drug trials measure effects in people; AI evaluations measure capabilities through proxies (sf7). And a drug's formula is fixed at approval; a model's behaviour changes with prompts, tools and updates after release, which is why ongoing monitoring and incident reporting matter more than a one-time file.

A final framing: governance documents are only as good as the measurements behind them. Every number a system card or regulatory report contains was produced by an evaluation, a red-team exercise or a monitoring pipeline somewhere in the company — usually by engineers. That is why this topic belongs in a safety track for engineers rather than only in a legal briefing.

1. Model cards

Mitchell and colleagues proposed model cards in 2019 as short documents accompanying trained machine-learning models. The original motivation was fairness: models used in law enforcement, medicine, education and employment were being applied in contexts they were not suited to, with performance that varied across groups nobody had measured. A model card states the model's intended uses and out-of-scope uses; describes its training and evaluation data; reports performance disaggregated across conditions and groups (the paper's examples include demographic and intersectional groups); and lists ethical considerations, caveats and recommendations. The idea spread fast: model hubs made cards a standard part of releasing a model, and the EU AI Act's documentation duties for general-purpose models are, in spirit, a legal version of a detailed model card aimed at downstream providers.

A good model card is short and specific. "Not for medical diagnosis" is less useful than "accuracy drops from 91% to 74% on clinical notes; not evaluated on non-English clinical text". Its weakness is that it describes a model, while harms often arise in a deployed system — the model plus prompts, tools, safeguards and users.

Worked example: a useful model card section

Compare two versions of the same "limitations" section for a summarisation model. The weak version: "The model may produce inaccurate summaries. Users should verify outputs. Not intended for high-stakes use." Every sentence is true and none helps anyone decide anything. The useful version: "On our held-out news benchmark, 4.1% of summaries contained a factual error relative to the source; on clinical notes, 11.8%. Errors were most often wrong numbers and dates. Evaluated in English, Spanish and German only; performance in other languages is unknown. Not evaluated on documents longer than 30,000 tokens. Not intended for summarising medical or legal records without expert review." The second version gives a reader numbers, conditions, the shape of the failures and the gaps in testing — and it is no longer, just more specific. The same discipline applies at every layer of documentation: claim, evidence, conditions, unknowns.

2. System cards

Frontier labs therefore publish system cards: long documents describing a deployed system and its safety evaluation. OpenAI's GPT-4 system card (2023) helped establish the genre; Anthropic, OpenAI and Google DeepMind now publish system cards or model reports for each major frontier release. The Claude Opus 4 system card (May 2025), used in sf7, ran to well over a hundred pages and included: dangerous-capability evaluations by domain with thresholds and results; the framework determination (ASL-3 protections for Opus 4, the existing standard for Sonnet 4); alignment assessments covering behaviours such as deception, sycophancy and reward hacking; findings from external testers; and what was withheld because it was sensitive. The Claude Sonnet 4.5 card (sf6) added an interpretability investigation of evaluation awareness.

What makes a system card useful rather than promotional? Evidence attached to every claim; the conditions under which results hold (elicitation, scaffolds, sample sizes); uncertainty stated, especially near thresholds; comparison with the previous model; external evaluators named with their access; and explicit statements of what was redacted and why. Anthropic's RSP v3 Risk Reports (sf8) go further by assessing overall risk across models rather than documenting one release, with redactions disclosed and external review.

3. The EU AI Act: structure and timeline

The EU AI Act — Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, signed on 13 June 2024 — is the most comprehensive AI law in force. It is risk-based: certain practices are prohibited, "high-risk" AI systems in listed areas (employment, credit, education, critical infrastructure and others) carry heavy obligations, some systems carry transparency duties (for example disclosing that content is AI-generated), and general-purpose AI models carry their own set of obligations.

Its application is phased, and the dates matter. According to Article 113 of the original text, the Regulation applies generally from 2 August 2026, with exceptions: the general provisions and prohibitions from 2 February 2025; the chapters on general-purpose AI models, governance and penalties from 2 August 2025 — except Article 101, the fines for general-purpose AI providers, which therefore applies from 2 August 2026; and certain high-risk rules from 2 August 2027. Article 111 adds that providers of general-purpose AI models placed on the market before 2 August 2025 must comply by 2 August 2027.

In July 2026 the EU amended the timeline. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, the Digital Omnibus on AI, adopted on 8 July and published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026, entered into force three days later. It moved the high-risk obligations: AI systems that are high-risk because of an Annex III use area now face them from 2 December 2027, and those that are high-risk as components of products regulated under Annex I from 2 August 2028. The stated reason was legal certainty given the imminent general application date, with standards and authorities not ready. It did not move the general-purpose AI obligations.

4. What general-purpose AI providers must do

A general-purpose AI (GPAI) model is, roughly, a model trained on broad data that can competently perform a wide range of tasks and be built into many downstream systems — every frontier language model qualifies. Article 53 requires every GPAI provider to:

  • draw up and keep up to date technical documentation of the model, including training and testing, for the AI Office and national authorities on request;
  • provide information and documentation to downstream providers who integrate the model, so they understand its capabilities and limits and can meet their own obligations;
  • put in place a policy to comply with EU copyright law, including respecting rights-holders' opt-outs from text and data mining;
  • publish a sufficiently detailed summary of the content used for training, following a template from the AI Office.

Some of these are relaxed for models released under free and open-source licences, unless they present systemic risk.

Article 51 defines GPAI models with systemic risk: models with high-impact capabilities, presumed when cumulative training compute exceeds 10^25 floating-point operations; the Commission can update the threshold and designate models on other criteria. Providers must notify the Commission when a model meets the threshold. Article 55 adds four obligations for these models: perform model evaluation with state-of-the-art protocols, including documented adversarial testing; assess and mitigate systemic risks at Union level; track, document and report serious incidents and possible corrective measures to the AI Office without undue delay; and ensure adequate cybersecurity for the model and its physical infrastructure. Fines under Article 101 reach 3% of annual worldwide turnover or EUR 15 million, whichever is higher.

Downstream documentation in practice

The duty to inform downstream providers deserves a concrete picture, because it is where a frontier lab's documentation meets the rest of the economy. A company building a CV-screening tool on a general-purpose model is building a high-risk system under the AI Act (employment is an Annex III area, with obligations now applying from December 2027). To meet its own duties it needs to know things only the model provider knows: what the model was trained on in broad terms, how it performs on relevant tasks and across groups, known failure modes such as sensitivity to names or formatting, what the provider's usage policy forbids, and how the model may change between versions. Article 53 requires the provider to make such information available so integrators can understand the model's capabilities and limitations and comply with their obligations. Good downstream documentation therefore looks like an extended model card plus a change log, kept current across model versions, and it is one of the places where the fairness concerns that motivated the original model cards return in legal form.

6. The GPAI Code of Practice

The AI Act lets providers rely on codes of practice to demonstrate compliance until harmonised standards are published (Article 56, and Article 55(2) for systemic-risk models). The General-Purpose AI Code of Practice was published on 10 July 2025 after a drafting process with independent chairs and many stakeholders. It has three chapters. Transparency includes a model documentation form that records what information goes to downstream providers, the AI Office or national authorities. Copyright covers copyright policies and measures against infringing output. Safety and security, for systemic-risk models, centres on providers adopting and following a safety and security framework covering risk assessment, mitigation, incident reporting and security — recognisably the frontier safety frameworks of sf8. Providers sign by sending a signatory form to the AI Office; signing is voluntary, and providers who do not sign must show compliance in other ways.

7. California's SB 53

California's Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act (SB 53) was signed in September 2025 and its core obligations took effect on 1 January 2026. Its definitions, from the statute: a frontier model is a foundation model trained with more than 10^26 integer or floating-point operations; a large frontier developer is a frontier developer whose revenue, with affiliates, exceeded $500 million in the preceding year. Catastrophic risk means a foreseeable and material risk that the developer's frontier model will materially contribute to the death of or serious injury to more than 50 people, or more than $1 billion in damage, from a single incident involving expert-level help with CBRN weapons, autonomous conduct amounting to a cyberattack or serious crime without meaningful human oversight, or evading its developer's or user's control.

Large frontier developers must publish a frontier AI framework describing how they assess and manage catastrophic risk, and follow it; frontier developers must publish transparency reports when deploying new frontier models. A critical safety incident is one of four events: unauthorised access to, modification of or exfiltration of frontier model weights that results in death or bodily injury; harm from the materialisation of a catastrophic risk; loss of control of a frontier model causing death or bodily injury; or a frontier model using deceptive techniques against its developer to subvert its controls or monitoring, outside an evaluation designed to elicit this, in a way that demonstrates materially increased catastrophic risk. Incidents go to the Office of Emergency Services within 15 days of discovery, or within 24 hours to an appropriate law-enforcement or public-safety authority if there is imminent risk of death or serious physical injury. The Act protects whistleblowers, and violations, including not following one's own framework, carry civil penalties up to $1 million per violation enforced by the Attorney General.

Note the fourth incident type: it turns the deception and evaluation-awareness research of sf4 into a legal reporting category. Anthropic's RSP v3 notes that where laws such as SB 53 define catastrophic risk with specific thresholds, it handles those requirements in separate compliance frameworks.

8. New York, the US federal position and elsewhere

New York's RAISE Act was signed in December 2025 and substantially amended by a chapter amendment signed in March 2026; as amended, it takes effect on 1 January 2027. It follows SB 53's structure — frontier developers, published frameworks, incident reporting — with differences that matter operationally: an oversight office within the Department of Financial Services with rulemaking powers, incident reports within 72 hours, and civil penalties up to $1 million for a first violation and $3 million for later ones, with no private right of action. (Details are from law-firm analyses of the final text; check the statute before relying on specifics.)

At the US federal level there is no comprehensive AI statute. The 2023 executive order on AI safety was revoked in January 2025. An executive order signed on 11 December 2025, "Ensuring a National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence", directs the Attorney General to set up a task force to challenge state AI laws considered inconsistent with national policy and asks Congress for preemptive federal legislation. Its effect on state laws such as SB 53 and RAISE is contested and, as of this writing, unresolved; companies generally plan to comply with the state laws while it is litigated or legislated. The US Center for AI Standards and Innovation continues voluntary pre-deployment testing with developers (sf1, sf7).

Elsewhere, South Korea's AI Basic Act took effect on 22 January 2026, with obligations for high-impact AI and transparency for generative AI; China has rules for generative AI services and labelling of AI-generated content; and the UK has relied on its AI Security Institute and existing regulators rather than an AI-specific statute. For a frontier-model interview, the EU and California regimes are the ones to know in detail.

Reading a regulation as an engineer

Engineers do not need to become lawyers, but a few habits make legal texts much less intimidating. Start with the definitions article: almost every obligation turns on a defined term ("frontier model", "general-purpose AI model with systemic risk", "critical safety incident"), and the definition decides whether the obligation touches your work at all. Then find the application dates and transition clauses, which are usually near the end and are where most popular summaries go wrong. Then read the obligations as requirements: who must do what, by when, reported to whom, with which evidence. Finally, look for the escape valves and delegations — "the Commission may adopt delegated acts", "codes of practice", "as appropriate" — because they tell you where details will be filled in later and where you should expect change. Keep a link to the official text and the date you read it; regulation, like frontier frameworks, is a moving target, and the version matters.

9. Voluntary commitments

Before the laws, there were commitments. In July 2023, seven US companies made voluntary commitments to the White House covering pre-release security testing, information sharing, watermarking and public reporting. At the AI Seoul Summit in May 2024, sixteen companies signed the Frontier AI Safety Commitments: to identify, assess and manage risks, including by setting thresholds at which severe risks would be deemed intolerable unless adequately mitigated; to be accountable; and to publish a safety framework focused on severe risks. Those frameworks are the documents sf8 compared, and SB 53, the RAISE Act and the EU Code of Practice now require or encourage essentially the same artefact. The pattern — voluntary practice first, codified later — is likely to continue, which is one reason company frameworks matter even where no law requires them.

10. Incident reporting

Incident reporting is where governance becomes operational. Three legal regimes now apply to frontier developers with different definitions and clocks:

Beyond legal reporting, public incident databases — the AI Incident Database and the OECD AI Incidents Monitor — collect reported harms across the field, which helps researchers and regulators see patterns no single company sees. Internally, a reporting process needs: incident categories mapped to every applicable definition; one channel anyone can use; a named on-call owner who can classify quickly; the clock started at discovery, not at confirmation; evidence preservation; and a feedback loop into evaluations and safeguards.

Compute thresholds: strengths and weaknesses

Both the EU and the US state laws use training compute as their main trigger, alongside revenue in the US laws. Compute has real advantages: the developer knows it before release, it is hard to understate without actually training a smaller model, and it correlates with capability. Its weaknesses are also real. Algorithmic efficiency improves steadily, so the capability reachable at a fixed compute level rises over time and a fixed threshold catches less and less of what matters. Fine-tuning and agent scaffolding add capability with little extra training compute. Inference-time compute — letting models think longer (sf1's "inference-time scaling") — is not counted at all. Distillation can transfer a large model's abilities into a smaller one below the threshold. The EU Act responds by letting the Commission revise the threshold and designate models on other criteria; frontier frameworks respond by keying safeguards to evaluated capability rather than compute. A good interview answer treats compute as a cheap first filter, not a measure of risk.

Disclosure-based regulation

The 2025–26 laws share a design: they mostly require developers to write down how they manage risk, follow what they wrote, report incidents and protect whistleblowers, rather than prescribing technical methods. The advantages are flexibility as the science moves, and making existing good practice enforceable. The criticisms come from both directions: some argue that disclosure is too weak — a company can write a lenient framework and comply with it — while others argue that even disclosure creates compliance burdens and legal exposure that favour large incumbents. The penalty for not following one's own framework in SB 53 is the key mechanism: it turns a voluntary document into a binding one, and gives framework revisions legal significance.

What makes documentation trustworthy

Documents can be thorough and still mislead by omission. The features that make a system card or risk report trustworthy are mostly about what an outsider can check: external evaluators with stated access, specific numbers with conditions and uncertainty, disclosure of redactions with reasons, comparisons with previous models, and consistency between what the document says and what users observe. Anthropic's RSP v3.4 requirement that public Risk Reports indicate where material was redacted, and the use of external reviewers who publish their own commentary, are examples of design choices aimed at this. A useful reader's habit is to ask of every reassuring claim: what evidence, under what conditions, checked by whom?

Jurisdictional fragmentation

A frontier developer selling worldwide now faces the EU regime, two US state regimes with different clocks and definitions, a federal government seeking to challenge state laws, and different rules in Korea, China and elsewhere. Fragmentation has costs — duplicated compliance work, conflicting definitions, uncertainty about which rules will survive — and some benefits, such as regulatory experimentation. Practically, companies build one internal process that satisfies the strictest version of each requirement (the shortest incident clock, the broadest definition) and map it to each regime, rather than running separate processes.

Where informed people disagree

Disagreements run along familiar lines. On timing, some argue regulation came too early for a fast-changing technology and will entrench today's practices; others argue it came late and remains too light for the risks frameworks themselves describe. On scope, some favour targeting the largest developers by compute and revenue; others argue risk depends on capability and deployment, not size. On federal versus state, some see state laws as necessary experiments in the absence of federal action; others as a patchwork that federal law should replace. As with sf1's evidence dilemma, a strong answer names the trade-off and the evidence that would move it — for example, whether incident reports under the new laws actually surface meaningful information.

Standards and management systems

Alongside laws sit voluntary standards that many companies adopt and that regulators reference. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology published its AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0) in January 2023, organised around four functions — govern, map, measure, manage — and later a profile for generative AI. ISO/IEC 42001, published in December 2023, specifies an AI management system that organisations can be certified against, in the same family as ISO 27001 for information security. The EU AI Act expects harmonised European standards to give a presumption of conformity; until they exist, codes of practice fill the gap. For engineers these standards mostly appear as process requirements — documented risk assessments, defined roles, records of decisions — and they reward the same habits as everything else in this lesson: write down what you measured, under what conditions, and what you decided.

One cross-cutting observation ties these threads together: in every regime, the cheapest compliance strategy is to have done the safety work properly in the first place. Evaluations recorded with their conditions, safeguards measured against attackers, frameworks actually followed and incidents escalated promptly satisfy most requirements almost as a by-product; the expensive failures come from reconstructing evidence after the fact or discovering that practice and documents diverged.

The public case: the EU AI Act's general-purpose AI regime

The EU's general-purpose AI rules are the first binding obligations specifically for frontier model developers, and their history is a good case study in how AI governance actually works. The Act was signed in June 2024 and entered into force in August 2024; the GPAI obligations applied from 2 August 2025; the Code of Practice, published on 10 July 2025, gave providers a practical route to compliance through transparency, copyright and safety-and-security chapters; Commission fines for GPAI providers became possible from 2 August 2026; and models already on the market have until 2 August 2027. When implementation of the high-risk rules proved harder than expected, the EU amended the timeline through the Digital Omnibus of July 2026 — but kept the GPAI rules on schedule. For engineers, the regime's practical content is familiar from the rest of this track: documentation (sf11), evaluations with adversarial testing (sf7, ev9), risk assessment and mitigation (sf8, sf9), incident reporting and cybersecurity (sf1, sf8).

What this means for an engineer's work

Most compliance work lands on engineering teams as concrete tasks. Training teams record compute and data provenance from day one, because documentation and training-content summaries need them. Evaluation teams produce reports good enough to go into technical documentation and system cards, with elicitation and conditions recorded. Safeguards and security teams document controls so they can be described to regulators and auditors. Platform teams build the logging that incident investigation needs, within privacy limits. On-call engineers learn what counts as a reportable incident and who to call. And anyone may be asked to contribute to a system card — which is easier if results were recorded carefully when produced.

Failure modes

Documentation written at the end. Reconstructing training details, evaluation conditions or data sources months later produces vague documents. Fix: record as you go.

Incident clocks missed. The process starts at "confirmation", but the legal clock started at discovery. Fix: start timers at first report; rehearse.

Mismatch between document and practice. A framework says one thing; practice drifts. Under SB 53 this is now a violation. Fix: internal reviews of adherence and noncompliance reporting channels (sf8).

Assuming last year's rules. Dates and thresholds change by amendment. Fix: a named owner who tracks changes, and versioned internal guidance.

A release-readiness checklist

Pulling the lesson together, a governance checklist for releasing a large model might read: training compute estimated and recorded; applicable regimes determined (EU GPAI, systemic risk, SB 53 frontier and large-developer status, RAISE from 2027); technical and downstream documentation updated; training-content summary prepared; copyright policy current; dangerous-capability and alignment evaluations complete and documented with elicitation; framework determination made and recorded with the required safeguards in place; system card and transparency report drafted, with redactions listed; incident categories and on-call ownership confirmed; and a named owner for tracking legal changes. None of these items is exotic; the risk is in skipping one under deadline pressure.

One more practical point: keep a single internal source of truth for facts that appear in many documents — compute estimates, evaluation results, safeguard descriptions — and generate the external documents from it. Inconsistencies between a system card, a regulatory filing and a transparency report are among the easiest problems for outsiders to spot and the hardest to explain afterwards.

When in doubt about whether something is reportable, record it and escalate it; an incident record that turns out to be minor costs minutes, a missed clock can cost far more.

Compliance-and-safety drill

You are the safety lead at a company preparing to release a model trained with an estimated 1.2 × 10^26 FLOP. Annual revenue is $900 million. You sell in the EU and the US. During pre-release red-teaming, an engineer discovers that an internal checkpoint was briefly accessible from a misconfigured storage bucket for six hours last month; there is no evidence it was downloaded. What applies, what do you do, and what goes in the system card?

Answer

What applies. EU: a general-purpose AI model with systemic risk (above 10^25 FLOP): notify the Commission, technical documentation, downstream information, copyright policy, training-content summary, model evaluation with adversarial testing, systemic-risk assessment and mitigation, serious-incident reporting, cybersecurity; signing the Code of Practice is the usual route. California SB 53: a frontier model (above 10^26) from a large frontier developer (revenue above $500 million): publish and follow a frontier AI framework, publish a transparency report at release, report critical safety incidents. New York RAISE: similar obligations from 1 January 2027.

The bucket exposure. Under SB 53, unauthorised access to model weights is a critical safety incident only if it results in death or bodily injury, so this exposure is probably not reportable there — but check with counsel, and treat it as a serious security incident internally: investigate access logs thoroughly, rotate credentials, assess whether any download occurred, fix the control failure, and record findings. Under the EU regime, cybersecurity is an explicit obligation for systemic-risk models, so the failure and remediation belong in your documentation. Your own frontier framework likely requires a security review before release; follow it, because not following it is itself an SB 53 violation.

System card. Dangerous-capability evaluations with elicitation details and thresholds; alignment evaluations; safeguards; external testing; the framework determination; and a security section noting that a configuration error exposed a checkpoint, what the investigation found, and what changed — at a level of detail that does not create new risk. Disclosing it is uncomfortable; omitting it and having it surface later is worse for trust and possibly for compliance.

Process. Start an incident record now with the discovery time, because reporting clocks run from discovery if the investigation finds a reportable event.
