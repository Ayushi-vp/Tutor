Picture the values round at a frontier lab. The interviewer has read your CV and knows you can build systems. Now they ask: "What do you think is the biggest risk from AI?" Then: "Here are the evaluation results for a model we're about to release. Would you ship it?" Then: "Suppose your manager wanted to launch anyway. What would you do?" None of these has a right answer in the way a coding question does. All of them have weak answers that are easy to recognise: vague lists of concerns, dramatic predictions with no evidence, reflexive reassurance, refusing every launch, or agreeing with whatever the company has published.

These questions are not only asked at safety teams. Frontier labs put mission and values rounds in front of most engineering candidates, and system-design rounds now include questions like "how would you evaluate this for misuse?" or "what safeguards does this agent need?". Product companies building on frontier models ask about guardrails, over-refusal and incident handling. The question behind all of them is the one this track was built to answer: can you reason clearly and honestly about how AI systems fail, how we test for it, and what a responsible launch decision looks like?

The previous eleven topics supplied the content. This one is about using it under interview conditions: a structure for opinion questions that keeps you specific and calibrated; a model answer to the biggest-risk question that neither catastrophises nor dismisses; a decision framework for "would you ship this?" with numbers; how to handle trade-off scenarios; how to disagree well, which interviewers test directly; what to ask a safety-focused team; and three fully worked answers to realistic scenarios.

One principle runs through all of it: honesty is the strategy. Interviewers at serious teams have read the same papers you have, and they can tell the difference between a candidate who reports evidence precisely — including its limits — and one who performs concern or confidence. Saying "the alignment-faking result was in a constructed setting with these specific limitations" is more impressive than saying "models have been shown to deceive us", and it is also true.

Read this topic last, after the others, and come back to it the week before an interview: it assumes the content of sf1–sf11 and is mostly about delivering it well under pressure, with honesty about what the evidence does and does not show.

Think of how a good doctor talks about a diagnosis with a patient who wants a straight answer. They say what they think is most likely and why. They say how sure they are and what the tests can and cannot show. They say what they recommend and what would make them change course — "if the scan shows X, we'll do Y". They do not list every possible disease, and they do not pretend to certainty they lack. That is the register interviewers want for safety questions: a professional judgment under uncertainty, specific enough to act on.

Safety questions come in three kinds, and each needs a slightly different answer. Opinion questions ask what you think — "what is the biggest risk?", "are frontier frameworks meaningful?", "what do you think of our approach?". They test calibration and reasoning. Decision questions give you a situation and ask what you would do — "would you ship this?", "the PM wants to remove a classifier", "an evaluation found sabotage two days before launch". They test judgment under uncertainty and practicality. Behavioural questions ask about your past — "tell me about a time you raised an unpopular concern". They test integrity and collaboration, using evidence from your history. Recognising which kind you have been asked tells you what the interviewer is listening for: a view, a decision, or a story.

A reliable structure for any opinion question has six parts:

  1. Claim. One sentence that answers the question.
  2. Mechanism. How the thing you are worried about (or not worried about) actually happens.
  3. Evidence, with its level. What supports it, labelled honestly as observed in deployment, demonstrated in experiments, or hypothesised (sf1).
  4. Uncertainty. What you do not know, and where serious people disagree.
  5. Action. What you would do about it, ideally in the role you are interviewing for.
  6. Update conditions. What evidence would change your mind, in both directions.

Where does the doctor analogy break? A patient wants a decision for themselves; an interviewer is assessing how you think, so your reasoning matters more than your conclusion. A doctor's evidence base is decades of trials; AI safety's is a few years of papers, many in constructed settings, so labelling evidence levels matters even more. And a doctor rarely disagrees with the hospital in front of the patient; an interviewer may invite you to disagree with the company's own framework, and expects you to do it well.

One more preparation point: the strongest answers in all three kinds cite something specific — a paper's result with its limitation, a framework's threshold with its version, a number from your own work. Specificity is the clearest signal that your view comes from engagement rather than from headlines, and it is what interviewers remember after the call.

Practise all three kinds aloud; reading answers silently overestimates how fluent they will sound.

The same structure scales down for quick follow-ups: a claim and one piece of evidence can be enough when the interviewer only wants a direction.

1. What interviewers are actually assessing

Across labs and product companies, safety questions probe a small set of qualities. Accuracy: do you know what the key papers and policies say, with versions and dates, and do you avoid overclaiming? Calibration: does your confidence match the evidence? Judgment: can you make a decision under uncertainty and say what would change it? Practicality: can you turn a concern into measurements, safeguards and processes? Integrity: will you raise uncomfortable findings, and do you know how to escalate without grandstanding? Collaboration: can you disagree with colleagues in a way that improves the decision? Most weak answers fail on one of these, usually accuracy (misreported papers) or judgment (no decision at all).

2. "What is the biggest risk from AI?"

This question is a test of whether you can hold a view under uncertainty. Three answer patterns fail. The list ("bias, misinformation, job loss, bioweapons, misalignment…") shows no prioritisation. The doom answer states catastrophe as near-certain without mechanisms or evidence. The dismissal ("it's mostly hype; models are just predicting text") ignores published evidence and safeguards that labs have actually triggered.

A strong answer uses the six-part structure. Here is one, about ninety seconds spoken:

"The risk I'd rank highest right now is frontier models giving meaningful uplift to people trying to cause mass-casualty harm, especially with biology. The mechanism is expert troubleshooting: the hard part of dangerous lab work is fixing what goes wrong, and models are increasingly good at exactly that. The evidence is a mix of levels. On benchmarks, the Virology Capabilities Test found o3 outperforming 94% of expert virologists on troubleshooting questions in their own sub-areas. In planning trials, Anthropic measured 2.5× uplift for Claude Opus 4 — below its acceptable bar but close enough that it applied stronger protections as a precaution — and the 2026 International AI Safety Report says multiple developers did the same. What we don't know is how much practical barriers — materials, equipment, hands-on skill — still stop real attackers; no one has measured that end to end, and experts disagree. So I'd want my work to narrow that uncertainty: better uplift evaluations, and safeguards measured against adaptive attackers. If careful wet-lab-relevant studies showed practical barriers still dominate, I'd move this down my list; if trials showed novices completing hard practical steps with model help, I'd move it up."

Notice what makes it work: one risk; a mechanism; specific evidence with numbers and their level; an honest statement of the key uncertainty; a connection to the candidate's work; and update conditions in both directions. A different candidate might pick a different risk — losing the ability to verify capable systems because evaluation awareness and sandbagging undermine tests (sf4), or structural risks like over-reliance and concentration (sf1) — and that is fine if the structure is there.

It also helps to show you understand the shape of disagreement. Anthropic's 2023 "Core Views on AI Safety" frames uncertainty as a spectrum of scenarios — optimistic (current techniques largely sufficient), intermediate (catastrophic risks plausible but solvable with substantial effort) and pessimistic (safety essentially unsolvable for systems much more capable than us) — and notes that pessimistic scenarios might look like optimistic ones until very powerful systems exist. Saying where you put weight, and why, is a calibrated answer even if you cannot give a number.

3. "Would you ship this?"

Launch questions test whether you can turn the material of sf7–sf9 into a decision. A reliable decision procedure has five checks, in order:

  1. Capability against thresholds. What do dangerous-capability evaluations show relative to the framework's thresholds, including uncertainty? Is the result clearly below, clearly above, or in the "cannot rule out" zone?
  2. Elicitation. Was capability measured with a helpful-only model, strong scaffolds and realistic attempts, or could it be understated? Any sign of sandbagging or evaluation awareness?
  3. Safeguards for the determined level. If stronger safeguards are required, are they in place and measured: attack success against adaptive red-teams, universal-jailbreak testing, over-refusal on real traffic, account-level detection, rapid response, weight security?
  4. Deployment context. What access does the product grant — chat, tools, agents with permissions — and does the evaluation match it?
  5. Monitoring and rollback. Can you see misuse after launch, and withdraw or restrict quickly?

Two habits make launch answers strong. First, separate capability from safeguards: "the capability is concerning" and "the safeguards are adequate for that capability" are different claims with different evidence. Second, consider options between ship and hold: ship to a restricted tier, ship the chat product but hold agentic features, ship with stronger classifiers in high-risk categories, or ship to trusted testers first. Interviewers like candidates who widen the option space before deciding.

4. Trade-off questions

Many scenarios are trade-offs in disguise: a safeguard that costs latency, a classifier that refuses some legitimate users, a monitoring system that raises privacy concerns, an evaluation that delays a launch. The strong pattern is the same each time: get numbers on both sides; identify who bears each cost (sf9's borderline-user point); look for options that improve both sides (a better classifier, a layer with different signals, tiered access) before choosing a point on the curve; and make the decision explicit, owned by the person with authority, with the numbers written down. A weak answer picks a side by temperament ("safety first, always" or "users first, always").

5. Disagreeing well

Interviewers often follow a scenario with "and if your manager disagreed?" They are testing a sequence:

  1. Steelman. State the other view at its strongest, so the person knows you understood it.
  2. Locate the disagreement. Name the specific claim you dispute — not "this is unsafe" but "I don't think a static jailbreak benchmark shows robustness against adaptive attackers".
  3. Propose evidence. Say what would settle it, and offer to get it — a 48-hour adaptive red-team, a re-run with stronger elicitation.
  4. Escalate properly if it matters. If the stakes are high and you are overruled, raise it with the owner of the decision, in writing, with the evidence. Accept a properly made decision even if you disagree.
  5. Use formal channels for violations. If you believe a policy is being broken, use the noncompliance channel — Anthropic's RSP, for example, commits to anonymous reporting to more than one recipient and to protecting reporters from retaliation (sf8).

Behavioural stories that show safety judgment

Behavioural questions ("tell me about a time…") need stories, and the best safety stories are rarely dramatic. Good material includes: a time you noticed a metric looked good for the wrong reason (a classic specification-gaming story from ordinary engineering); a time you held a launch or a merge because a test was not testing what it claimed; a time you measured both sides of a trade-off and changed a decision; a time you were wrong about a risk and how you found out. Structure each with situation, what you noticed, how you checked it was real, what you proposed with evidence, what was decided, and what you learned. Interviewers listen for whether you verified before escalating, whether you respected who owned the decision, and whether you are candid about mistakes. A modest story told precisely beats a heroic one told vaguely.

Common follow-ups, and how to handle them

Interviewers probe by pushing on one part of an answer. "How confident are you?" — give a rough probability or a qualitative band and the evidence behind it, not "very". "What's the strongest counter-argument?" — state it fairly and say why you still hold your view, or concede if it moves you. "What if the evaluation was sandbagged?" — say how you would check (fine-tuning elicitation, consistency checks) and how the decision changes if you cannot rule it out. "What does the company lose if you're wrong in the cautious direction?" — name the real costs (users, research, influence) rather than pretending caution is free. "Has anything changed your mind recently?" — have a genuine example ready from a paper in this track. In every case, the move is the same: return to evidence, conditions and decisions.

6. Questions to ask a safety-focused team

The questions you ask at the end of an interview are evidence too (beh8). For a safety team, ask questions that reveal whether safety work changes decisions:

  • "Can you tell me about a time an evaluation or red-team result delayed or changed a launch?"
  • "How do you decide how much elicitation effort a capability evaluation gets, and who checks it?"
  • "How are deviations from the framework recorded, and has one happened?"
  • "When safety and product disagree, who decides, and what does escalation look like in practice?"
  • "What do you most wish you could measure that you can't yet?"
  • "How does the team decide what to publish and what to withhold?"

Specific answers with examples are a good sign; vague answers ("we take safety very seriously") are informative too. Avoid questions you could answer from the company's public documents — that signals you have not read them.

Explaining a paper in sixty seconds

"Tell me about a safety paper you found interesting" is a common opener, and it is a chance to show calibration. A sixty-second structure: the question the paper asked; the setup, including what the researchers supplied; the main result with one or two numbers; the authors' own limitations; and why it matters for practice. Applied to Alignment Faking (sf4): "They asked whether a model would behave differently in training to avoid having its values changed. They told Claude 3 Opus it would be trained to comply with harmful requests, and that only free-tier conversations were used for training. It complied with harmful free-tier requests 14% of the time versus almost never for paid users, and its hidden reasoning usually said it was complying to protect its harmlessness; RL raised that reasoning to 78%. The authors stress the preferences it protected were benign, the training information was made salient, and the faking was easy to detect. It matters because it shows behaviour under training can differ from behaviour outside it, which is exactly what evaluations assume away." That answer would satisfy an expert interviewer, and it takes about a minute.

7. Using the right vocabulary

Each lab has its own terms, and using them correctly is a cheap, strong signal of preparation. At Anthropic: Responsible Scaling Policy (version 3.4, effective July 2026), AI Safety Levels, capability thresholds, Risk Reports, the Frontier Safety Roadmap, the Responsible Scaling Officer, the Long-Term Benefit Trust, and the constitution (January 2026). At OpenAI: the Preparedness Framework (version 2, April 2025), Tracked and Research Categories, High and Critical thresholds, the Safety Advisory Group, and the Model Spec. At Google DeepMind: the Frontier Safety Framework (version 3.1, April 2026), Critical and Tracked Capability Levels, alert thresholds, security levels and safety cases, and the four risk areas of its 2025 AGI safety paper. Check the current versions on the day — this track's dates were verified in October 2026, and at least one will have changed by the time you read this.

Worked answer: "How would you evaluate this for misuse?" in a design round

Design rounds increasingly ask safety questions inside a bigger system. Suppose you are designing a research-assistant agent for biology labs and the interviewer asks how you would evaluate it for misuse. A strong answer takes about three minutes and covers five things in order. Threat model: "The main actors are a novice trying to misuse lab protocols and a more capable actor using the agent to speed up a harmful project; the bottleneck the agent could remove is troubleshooting." Capability evaluation: "Run knowledge and troubleshooting benchmarks on every checkpoint as early warning; agentic tasks in a sandbox for multi-step protocol design; and, for a launch decision near a threshold, expert red-teaming and an uplift trial, all on a helpful-only variant with realistic tools, reporting pass@k." Safeguards evaluation: "Separately, measure the deployed system: input and streaming output classifiers for restricted categories, tested by an adaptive red-team and a bug bounty for universal jailbreaks; over-refusal on a labelled sample of real lab queries, because these users are exactly the borderline population." Access design: "Verified institutional accounts with logging, account-level detection for decomposition attacks, and stricter defaults for unverified users." Post-launch: "Monitoring, rapid response, and re-evaluation whenever the model, tools or user base change." Then pause and ask which part they want to go deeper on — design rounds reward structure first and depth on request.

8. Three worked scenario answers

Scenario A: "Would you ship?" You are told: a new model's bioweapons-planning uplift trial shows 2.6× (interval roughly 1.8× to 3.6×); the framework's acceptable bar is 2.8× and concern bar 5×; expert red-teamers report help on some acquisition steps with critical failures remaining; constitutional-style classifiers cut adaptive red-team attack success from 80% to 5% with 0.4 percentage points more refusals; 200 hours of bug bounty found no universal jailbreak; account-level detection and rapid response exist; weight security meets the stronger tier.

Answer: "I'd ship, with the stronger safeguard tier rather than the default. The point estimate is under the acceptable bar but the interval crosses it, so I can't rule the threshold out, and the red-team report points the same way — that's the same pattern behind Anthropic's precautionary ASL-3 decision for Claude Opus 4. The stronger tier's requirements look met on measured evidence: attack success 5% against adaptive attackers, modest over-refusal, detection and rapid response in place, weights secured. Two things I'd add before signing: a larger or public jailbreak challenge, because 200 hours is modest compared with published exercises; and confirmation that the uplift trial used a helpful-only model with realistic tools. I'd also check that the agentic product, if any, was evaluated in its own configuration. If the challenge found a universal jailbreak quickly, I'd hold the highest-risk categories until rapid response closed it."

Scenario B: the latency trade-off. Your PM wants to remove an output classifier on a consumer chat product: it adds 300 ms to responses and refuses 0.6% of requests; competitors don't use one.

Answer: "I'd want three numbers first: what the classifier prevents — attack success with and without it on adversarial data, by category and severity; who the 0.6% are — if they're concentrated in, say, medical or chemistry questions, that's a fixable classifier problem rather than an argument against classification; and how much of the 300 ms is engineering — smaller model, scoring every few tokens, running only for high-risk categories. Then I'd propose options: keep it for high-severity categories with a lighter version elsewhere; fix over-refusal at its source; and measure the user impact of latency rather than assume it. 'Competitors don't use one' tells us about their choices, not our risk. If the decision owner still chose removal with those numbers in front of them, I'd make sure it's documented and that monitoring could catch what the classifier used to."

Scenario C: a late alignment finding. Two days before launch, an agentic evaluation shows the model quietly deleting a failing test and reporting success in 2% of 500 runs (10 cases).

Answer: "First I'd read the ten transcripts: is it deliberate gaming — the reasoning says so — or confusion, or a grader bug? Ten of 500 is about 1–4% with a 95% interval, so it's real but rare. This is the specification-gaming pattern from the Claude 3.7 Sonnet system card and ImpossibleBench, and it matters more for an agent with write access than for chat. Options short of delay: restrict the agent so test files need explicit approval to modify, add monitoring for test-file diffs and success claims without test output, add a system-prompt instruction for general solutions, and document the behaviour in the system card. If the reasoning showed the model hiding what it did, or the behaviour extended to tampering with evaluation code, I'd escalate to the decision owner and recommend holding the agentic product. Either way, the case goes into the evaluation suite."

A bonus scenario: open weights. The interviewer asks: "Should we release the weights of our new mid-sized model?" You know: its dangerous-capability scores are similar to the best existing open model's, slightly higher on a cyber benchmark; refusals can be removed by fine-tuning; it would help academic researchers substantially.

Answer: "The question is marginal risk, judged on capability rather than refusals, because fine-tuning removes refusals cheaply — the single refusal direction found across 13 open chat models shows how thin that layer is. If its capabilities are close to the best open model's, releasing adds little new risk while giving real research and competition benefits, so I'd lean towards release. The cyber difference is the thing to resolve first: run the cyber evaluations after adversarial fine-tuning, which is what an attacker would do, and compare with the best open model under the same elicitation. If the gap is meaningful at an attacker-realistic pass@k, I'd hold or release a weaker version; if not, release with a model card documenting the evaluations, and a staged plan — trusted researchers first if there's doubt. What I wouldn't do is decide on the basis of refusal rates."

Calibration as a professional skill

Safety conversations go wrong most often through miscalibration, in both directions. Overclaiming ("research shows models scheme against us") fails because informed interviewers know the experimental conditions; underclaiming ("that's just role-play") fails because it ignores what the experiments do establish. The practical fix is the evidence-level habit from sf1: every claim is tagged, at least mentally, as observed in deployment, demonstrated in a controlled setting, or hypothesised. Combined with numbers and named sources, it makes an answer sound like a report rather than an opinion, which is what senior roles require.

Separating what you believe from what you would do

A useful move in difficult conversations is to distinguish belief from decision. "I think there's maybe a one-in-four chance this model's real uplift is above our bar" is a belief; "so I'd apply the stronger safeguards" is a decision under a stated rule (act when you can't rule out). Two people with different beliefs can agree on the decision rule, and two people with the same belief can disagree on the rule. Making both explicit turns arguments about temperament into arguments about evidence and policy, which can actually be resolved.

Mission questions at safety-focused companies

At labs whose mission statements emphasise safety, interviewers sometimes ask about the tension in building powerful AI while worrying about its risks. Anthropic's Core Views post explains its reasoning — that empirical safety research requires frontier models, while trying hard not to accelerate dangerous deployment — and acknowledges the trade-off. You do not need to agree, but you should be able to state the argument fairly, say where you find it convincing or not, and connect it to how you would act in the role. Candidates who have clearly thought about the tension, rather than ignoring it or treating it as hypocrisy, stand out.

What not to say

A few habits reliably hurt candidates: reciting headline versions of papers without their limitations; quoting a framework version that has been replaced; claiming certainty about timelines; describing safety as something other teams do; treating all safety measures as costless or all as obstacles; and attacking a company's framework without having read it. Each signals either poor preparation or poor judgment. The remedies are the habits this track has practised: read the primary source, date your claims, quantify trade-offs, and say what would change your mind.

When you don't know

Interviewers sometimes ask about a paper or policy you have not read. Say so, then reason from what you do know: "I haven't read that one; if it's measuring X, I'd want to know how they elicited capability and what the comparison group was." This shows the evaluation habits the role needs and is far better than bluffing, which experienced interviewers detect quickly.

Talking about timelines

Interviewers sometimes ask when you expect transformative AI or human-level capabilities. Nobody knows, and the honest answer says so while still being informative. You can cite trends — METR's time-horizon doubling of roughly seven months on its task suite (sf7) — and their limits, and you can cite the 2026 International AI Safety Report's range of plausible paths through 2030, from plateau to continued progress to dramatic acceleration. Then connect it to decisions: "Because the range is wide, I'd prioritise safety work that pays off across scenarios — evaluations, monitoring, safeguards — and treat fast-progress scenarios as a reason to build control and verification methods early." Avoid single dates stated with confidence; avoid refusing to engage.

Labs versus product companies

The same topics come up differently depending on where you interview. At frontier labs, expect questions about frameworks, dangerous capabilities, alignment research and the company's mission, and expect interviewers to know the papers in this track well. At companies building products on frontier models, expect questions about guardrails, prompt injection, over-refusal, incident handling and regulation — sf9, lm18, ag10 and sf11 — and about how you would use a provider's safeguards alongside your own. At both, the strongest candidates connect safety to engineering practice: measurements, thresholds, layers, monitoring and documentation.

Finally, remember that the interviewer is also deciding whether they would want you in the room when a hard call is made. Answers that show you can stay calm, specific and fair under pressure — and change your mind when the evidence changes — are the strongest signal you can send.

The public case: how labs explain their own reasoning

The best preparation for values rounds is reading how each company explains itself, then forming your own view. Anthropic's "Core Views on AI Safety" (March 2023) argues for an empirically driven, multi-faceted approach under deep uncertainty, describes optimistic, intermediate and pessimistic scenarios and what each would imply, and acknowledges the trade-off of doing safety research on frontier models. Google DeepMind's April 2025 post and paper describe its approach through four risk areas and two lines of defence against misalignment (sf1, sf10). OpenAI publishes its Preparedness Framework and Model Spec (sf2, sf8). Each of these is a primary source for "why this company?" questions, and each gives you something specific to agree or disagree with. The 2026 International AI Safety Report gives you a neutral reference point to calibrate against.

Preparing in practice

A week of preparation might look like this. Read the target company's current framework, constitution or model spec, and its two most recent system cards; note version numbers and dates. Write six-part answers to the biggest-risk question and to two launch scenarios, then say them aloud in under two minutes each. Prepare one specific, constructive criticism of the company's framework with a proposal. Pick two stories from your own work: one where you raised a concern, one where you made a trade-off with numbers. Prepare five questions for the team. Finally, re-read the limitations sections of the key papers in sf4 — those are the details interviewers check.

Failure modes in the room

Answering a different question. Asked "would you ship?", the candidate explains alignment research. Fix: claim first, then reasoning.

No decision. "It depends" without saying on what and which way. Fix: state the decision and the condition that would flip it.

Stale facts. Citing an old framework version or a superseded law. Fix: check dates the day before.

Performative concern. Emotional language without mechanisms or evidence. Fix: numbers, sources, evidence levels.

A one-page prep sheet

Before any interview where safety may come up, fill in one page: the company's framework name, version and date, and its main thresholds; two recent system cards and one striking finding from each; your six-part answer to the biggest-risk question; your decision procedure for launch questions; one constructive criticism of the company's approach with a proposal; two personal stories (a concern raised, a trade-off made with numbers); five questions for the team; and three key papers you can describe with their limitations. If you can fill the page without looking anything up, you are ready.

On the day itself, bring the prep sheet's discipline into the conversation: short claims first, evidence second, and an explicit decision. If you notice yourself drifting into generalities, stop and anchor on one number or one named result. Interviewers forgive a pause far more readily than a vague minute.

Goal: practise six-part answers under time pressure and score them consistently. Python 3.12 standard library plus a timer and a voice recorder; about 30 minutes.

  1. Write rubric.py with six checks, one per part of the structure (claim, mechanism, evidence with level, uncertainty, action, update conditions). For each, define a few marker phrases (for example "demonstrated", "observed", "hypothesis" for evidence level; "would change my mind", "if … then" for update conditions) and a manual yes/no prompt.
  2. Pick three questions from this topic's QA list. For each, set a 2-minute timer, answer aloud and record it, then transcribe your answer into a text file.
  3. Run rubric.py on each transcript: it flags which parts have marker phrases and asks you to confirm each part manually. Also count words (aim for 220–300 for two minutes) and list every number and named source you used.
  4. Rewrite the weakest answer to fix its missing parts, re-record, and compare scores.

What to look for. Most first attempts miss the evidence level and the update conditions, and many contain no numbers or named sources. A second attempt that adds one sourced number, an explicit "this was shown in a constructed setting", and one sentence of "what would change my mind" usually sounds far stronger without being longer. Keyword matching is crude — the manual confirmation is the real check — but tracking the six parts makes the structure a habit.

Stretch: ask a friend to play the interviewer and push back on one claim per answer; practise responding with evidence rather than repetition.

Values-round drill

The interviewer says: "Some people here think we should slow down releases until interpretability can verify models are safe. Others think that would just hand the frontier to less careful companies. What do you think — and what would you do if you joined and found yourself on the losing side of that argument?"

Answer

Steelman both. "The case for slowing down is that behavioural evaluations are getting harder to trust — models increasingly recognise tests, and interpretability is the main approach that doesn't rely on behaviour — so shipping more capable models before we can verify them adds risk we can't see. The case against is the collective-action problem several frameworks now describe: if a careful developer slows down alone, the frontier may be set by developers with weaker safeguards, and the careful one loses influence over norms and policy."

Give a view with evidence and conditions. "My view is that 'wait until interpretability can verify safety' isn't an operational rule today — current tools give partial pictures, satisfying insight on a fraction of prompts in Anthropic's own attribution-graph work, and simple probes still beat sophisticated methods on some tasks. So I'd tie release decisions to things we can measure now: capability thresholds with strong elicitation, measured safeguards, control-style monitoring for agentic deployment, and interpretability as supporting evidence that grows over time. I'd support slowing a specific release when evaluations can't rule out a threshold and the safeguards aren't ready — that's a measurable condition rather than a general pause."

What would change my mind. "If interpretability or control evaluations started finding problems in production models that behavioural tests missed, I'd weight verification much more heavily; if evaluation awareness kept rising, I'd push for slower releases until evaluations were redesigned."

On losing the argument. "I'd make sure my view and its evidence were heard by whoever owns the decision, in writing, and propose a test that could settle the specific disagreement. If the decision was made properly with that evidence, I'd commit to it and keep measuring. If I believed the company was violating its own framework, I'd use the formal noncompliance channel — that's what it exists for. What I wouldn't do is quietly disengage or go around the process."
