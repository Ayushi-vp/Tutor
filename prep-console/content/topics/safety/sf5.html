sf4 ended on an uncomfortable note: behavioural evaluations can be fooled. A model can behave differently when it thinks it is being tested, can underperform on purpose, and can carry a conditional behaviour that safety training never touches. If you cannot fully trust what a model does under observation, the obvious alternative is to look at what it is computing. The model's internal activations are fully visible to its developers; nothing about them is hidden. In principle, a "lie detector" or "harmful-intent detector" that reads the internals could work even when the outputs look fine.

The first attempt everyone makes is to look at individual neurons. Pick a neuron in a language model, collect the text snippets that make it fire most strongly, and read them. Sometimes you find something clean — a neuron for Python indentation, or for a particular language. More often you find a jumble: the same neuron fires for academic citations, for English dialogue, for HTTP requests and for Korean text. Neurons are mostly polysemantic. Reading the model neuron by neuron is like trying to understand a city's economy by watching individual electrons in its power grid.

Two lines of work turned this from a dead end into a research programme. One is probing: skip the neurons and ask directly whether a concept can be read off the activations, by training a simple classifier on them. The other is a theory and a tool. The theory, superposition, explains why neurons are polysemantic: networks store many more concepts than they have neurons by overlapping them, exploiting the fact that most concepts are absent most of the time. The tool, the sparse autoencoder, tries to undo the overlap and recover the concepts. In 2024 Anthropic applied it to a production model and found millions of interpretable features, including ones related to deception and dangerous code, and showed that turning one up changed the model's behaviour.

This lesson builds those ideas from the ground up. It defines features and the linear representation hypothesis, trains a probe on synthetic activations and shows how a 93%-accurate probe can be measuring the wrong thing, works superposition by hand with five features in two dimensions, then builds sparse autoencoders and walks through what they found at scale. It ends with the negative results — several 2025 studies found simple probes beating autoencoder-based ones on concrete safety tasks — because an honest account of what interpretability can do today is what interviewers at labs listen for. sf6 continues with circuits, causal interventions and steering.

Imagine a small radio studio with three microphones recording a party of twenty people. If everyone talked at once, three microphones could never separate twenty voices. But at a party, at any moment only a couple of people are talking. Each person stands in a slightly different spot, so each voice reaches the three microphones in a slightly different mix. Knowing those mixes, and knowing that only a few people speak at a time, you can work out who said what most of the time. When two people whose positions are similar speak at once, you sometimes get it wrong. That is superposition: more signals than channels, made workable by sparsity, at the cost of occasional confusion.

A neural network's activations are the microphones. A layer in a language model might have a few thousand dimensions, but the concepts the model needs — every topic, entity, grammatical role, programming language, tone and fact — number far more. Most are absent from any given piece of text. The network can therefore assign each concept a direction, many more directions than dimensions, with each direction overlapping a little with others. A single neuron, which is just one axis, then picks up a mix of many concepts: it is polysemantic.

Three words carry the rest of this lesson. A feature is a property of the input the model represents — "this is base64", "this mentions the Golden Gate Bridge", "this statement is false". The linear representation hypothesis says many features correspond to directions in activation space: the stronger the feature, the further the activation points along its direction, and you read it with a dot product. And dictionary learning is the attempt to find those directions from data, by learning a large set of directions (a dictionary) such that each activation is a sparse combination of a few of them.

Why should a safety engineer care about any of this rather than leaving it to researchers? Because the practical payoffs are concrete. A direction for "the user is attempting a jailbreak" can become a monitor that costs almost nothing to run. A dictionary of features can tell you what a model was "thinking about" during a strange transcript, which is often the fastest way to debug it. And an intervention on a feature is a way to test a hypothesis about why a model behaves as it does, something no amount of prompting can do cleanly. Each of these comes with traps, and the lesson spends as much time on the traps as on the tools.

Where does the party analogy break? Microphone mixing is fixed physics; a network learns its directions, and how much overlap it tolerates depends on how often features co-occur and how much each matters to the loss. Voices are clearly separate things; a model's features may not be crisply defined at all, and the set you recover depends on your method. And at a party you can ask people who they are; inside a model, you only have the activations and your hypotheses about them.

If you stop here, keep this picture: concepts are directions, there are far more of them than dimensions, they coexist because most are inactive at any moment, and interpretability's job is to find the directions and check that the model actually uses them.

1. Activations, the residual stream and what a "representation" is

When a transformer processes text, every token gets a vector of numbers — its activation — at every layer. In a transformer the most important of these is the residual stream (lm3): a running vector for each token that each block reads, processes with attention and an MLP, and adds its output back into. Inside each MLP there is a wider hidden layer of neurons, each followed by a nonlinearity such as ReLU or GELU.

To say a model represents something means some function of these activations carries that information and the model's later computation depends on it. For example, if the model predicts "Paris" after "The Eiffel Tower is in", somewhere in the activations for "Tower" there must be information that this is a landmark in Paris, available for later layers to use. Interpretability tries to find where and how.

2. Features as directions: the linear representation hypothesis

The simplest possible form for "where and how" is a direction. If a feature corresponds to a unit vector u in activation space, then for any activation a, the dot product u · a measures how strongly the feature is present. Adding a multiple of u to the activation should increase the feature. This is the linear representation hypothesis.

The evidence for it is varied. Word embeddings famously support arithmetic like king − man + woman ≈ queen, which only works if gender and royalty are roughly directions. Marks and Tegmark (2023) found that true and false statements in a language model's activations separate along a clear linear direction, that probes trained on one dataset of statements transfer to others, and — the strongest evidence — that adding or subtracting that direction during the forward pass made the model treat false statements as true and vice versa. Many safety-relevant concepts (refusal, sentiment, deception-related text) have since been found to have usable directions.

It is a hypothesis, not a law. Some features appear to have non-linear structure, and the hypothesis says nothing about how many directions there are or how to find them. But it is the working assumption behind probes, sparse autoencoders and steering, so it is worth stating clearly.

Finding a direction without a classifier: difference of means and contrast pairs

The simplest way to find a candidate direction does not even need logistic regression. Collect activations for inputs that have a property and inputs that do not, average each group, and subtract: the difference of means points from "without" to "with". If 500 true statements average to activation vector μtrue and 500 false ones to μfalse, the direction u = (μtrue − μfalse) / ||μtrue − μfalse|| is a reasonable truth direction, and projecting a new activation onto it gives a score. Marks and Tegmark found such mass-mean directions generalised about as well as logistic-regression probes, including to other datasets, while being more causally implicated in the model's outputs; logistic regression can latch onto any direction that separates the training data, including ones that exploit quirks. (They also note that earlier truth probes failed to generalise to statements containing the word "not" — the real-world version of this lesson's negation example.)

A sharper variant uses contrast pairs: two inputs identical except for the property of interest, such as the same question followed by "yes" and by "no". Subtracting the two activations cancels everything the inputs share and leaves mostly the difference. Anthropic's sleeper-agent defection probes (sf4) were built from generic contrast pairs of this kind ("Are you doing something dangerous? yes" versus "… no"), which is part of why they needed no knowledge of the specific backdoor. sf6 shows the same trick used in the other direction: adding a contrast-pair direction to the activations to steer behaviour.

The worked numbers are easy. Suppose in a 4-dimensional toy the mean "yes" activation is (0.9, 0.2, −0.1, 0.4) and the mean "no" activation is (0.1, 0.3, −0.2, 0.4). The difference is (0.8, −0.1, 0.1, 0.0), with length √(0.64 + 0.01 + 0.01) ≈ 0.81, so the unit direction is about (0.99, −0.12, 0.12, 0.0). A new activation (0.6, 0.25, −0.15, 0.4) projects to 0.99 × 0.6 − 0.12 × 0.25 + 0.12 × (−0.15) ≈ 0.54, closer to the "yes" mean's projection (≈ 0.85) than the "no" mean's (≈ 0.04). Real activations have thousands of dimensions, but the arithmetic is identical.

3. Linear probes

A linear probe, introduced as a tool by Alain and Bengio (2016), is the most direct test of the hypothesis. Collect activations at some layer for many inputs labelled with a property (true or false; harmful intent or not), and train a logistic regression (ml6) to predict the label from the activation. If the probe is accurate on held-out inputs, the property is linearly decodable from that layer, and the probe's weight vector is a candidate feature direction.

Probes are cheap — one dot product per token at inference — so they are attractive as monitors. sf4 met an example: Anthropic's defection probes detected when backdoored "sleeper agent" models were about to act on their backdoor with AUROC above 99%. But probe accuracy is easy to over-read, and the worked example below shows how.

4. Worked example: a 93% probe that measures the wrong thing

Consider a synthetic experiment, built for this lesson so every number is known. Activations are 64-dimensional. A "truth" direction carries the label with a signal strength that grows through the layers. A second, unrelated "negation" direction is also present: in dataset A, false statements usually contain a negation ("Paris is not in France"), so the negation direction correlates with the label 90% of the time; in dataset B, negation is independent of truth. At early layers the negation direction is strong and the truth direction weak; later, the reverse. A logistic-regression probe is trained on 2,000 statements from dataset A at each layer and tested on 1,000 new statements from A, 1,000 from B, and, as a baseline, after shuffling the training labels.

Read the first row. A probe at layer 0 scores 92.8% on dataset A — impressive — and 50.8% on dataset B, which is chance. 5. Probe capacity and control tasks

A second trap is the probe itself being too clever. A large, nonlinear probe can learn to compute a property from raw information even if the model never represents it in a convenient form. Hewitt and Liang (2019) proposed control tasks: assign each input type (each word, each statement template) a random label and train the same probe. If the probe also does well on that meaningless task, its accuracy on the real task partly reflects its own memorising capacity. The honest number is selectivity: real-task accuracy minus control-task accuracy. In practice this argues for simple linear probes with regularisation, held-out types in the test set, and reporting selectivity alongside accuracy.

6. Polysemanticity and why neurons are the wrong unit

If features are directions, are neurons features? Sometimes, but usually not. Anthropic's "Towards Monosemanticity" (Bricken et al., 2023) studied a one-layer transformer whose MLP had 512 neurons and found most neurons responded to mixtures of unrelated contexts, while a learned decomposition of the same 512-dimensional space produced thousands of features that were each far more specific — base64 strings, Arabic script, DNA sequences, particular kinds of legal text. As the paper's heading puts it: "Just 512 neurons can represent tens of thousands of features." The question is how.

7. Superposition, worked with five features in two dimensions

"Toy Models of Superposition" (Elhage et al., 2022) studied the smallest possible setting. Take n features and a hidden layer of only m < n dimensions. Each input x is a vector of feature values, each feature active with some probability. The model compresses x to h = Wx (an m-dimensional vector) and reconstructs x′ = ReLU(WᵀWx + b), trained to minimise reconstruction error weighted by each feature's importance. The paper found that when features are dense the model simply keeps the most important m features and drops the rest, but as features become sparse it switches — abruptly, like a phase change — to storing more features than dimensions, arranged in geometric shapes such as antipodal pairs, triangles and pentagons.

Let's compute the pentagon case by hand: n = 5 features in m = 2 dimensions. Place feature i's direction wi at angle 72° × i on the unit circle. The model's read-out of feature j is ReLU(wj · h + b), and h is the sum of the active features' directions. The dot products are all we need:

w_j · w_j               = 1.000
adjacent  (72° apart)   = cos 72°  =  0.309
non-adjacent (144°)     = cos 144° = -0.809
bias b                  = -0.31   (just enough to cancel one neighbour)

Now three cases:

  • Only f0 active. f0 reads ReLU(1 − 0.31) = 0.69. Its neighbours f1 and f4 read ReLU(0.309 − 0.31) = 0. The others read ReLU(−0.809 − 0.31) = 0. Correct: one feature on, four off (the 0.69 is a scale the real model absorbs into its weight lengths).
  • Adjacent pair f0 and f1 active. f0 reads ReLU(1 + 0.309 − 0.31) ≈ 1.0, and so does f1. f2 reads ReLU(0.309 − 0.809 − 0.31) = 0, and likewise f3 and f4. Correct.
  • Non-adjacent pair f0 and f2 active. f0 reads ReLU(1 − 0.809 − 0.31) = 0, and f2 likewise — both real features vanish. Meanwhile f1, the neighbour of both, reads ReLU(0.309 + 0.309 − 0.31) ≈ 0.31: a phantom feature that is not present.

So this encoding is perfect for single features and adjacent pairs and badly wrong for non-adjacent pairs. How often does that matter? Suppose each feature is independently active with probability p. Counting every on/off combination of the five features and checking whether each read-out is right (active above 0.5, inactive below 0.1):

Feature density pP(two or more active)P(some feature misread)
1%0.10%0.05%
5%2.3%1.2%
10%8.2%4.5%
20%26%16%
50%81%66%

At 1% density the pentagon stores five features in two dimensions and misreads one input in two thousand — an excellent bargain compared with dropping three features entirely. At 50% density it is useless. That is the core of superposition: sparsity makes overlap cheap, and the nonlinearity with a negative bias filters out the small interference that single neighbours cause. Real language-model features are extremely sparse — most concepts are absent from most tokens — and real activations have thousands of dimensions, where vastly more nearly-orthogonal directions fit than in two. Both push towards heavy superposition.

8. Sparse autoencoders: undoing superposition

If a layer holds many sparse features in superposition, can we recover them? The inverse problem is dictionary learning: find a set of directions (the dictionary), more numerous than the dimensions, such that each activation is approximately a sparse combination of a few of them. A sparse autoencoder (SAE) is a neural-network way to do it, and the variant used in Anthropic's work looks like this, for an activation x of dimension d and a dictionary of F features (F much larger than d):

f      = ReLU(W_enc (x − b_dec) + b_enc)     # F feature activations, mostly zero
x_hat  = W_dec f + b_dec                     # reconstruction from active features
loss   = ||x − x_hat||²  +  λ · Σ_i |f_i| · ||W_dec[:, i]||

The encoder decides which features are active and how strongly; the decoder rebuilds the activation as a weighted sum of feature directions — column i of Wdec is feature i's direction. The first loss term demands faithful reconstruction; the second, an L1 penalty (ml3), pushes most feature activations to exactly zero. The trade-off parameter λ sets how sparse the code is. OpenAI's Gao et al. (2024) replaced the L1 penalty with a TopK rule — keep the k largest activations, zero the rest — which controls sparsity directly and reduced dead features.

Interpreting a feature means looking at the inputs that activate it most and at a range of activation strengths, and checking whether a concept explains them — by hand or with an LLM writing and testing explanations ("automated interpretability"). Verifying it means intervening: adding the feature's direction and seeing whether behaviour changes as the interpretation predicts.

9. What sparse autoencoders found

Towards Monosemanticity (October 2023). Anthropic trained SAEs on the 512-neuron MLP of a one-layer transformer, using 8 billion activation samples, with dictionaries from 1× (512 features) to 256× (131,072 features), and analysed a 4,096-feature run in detail. Most features were far more interpretable than neurons. Of those 4,096, 168 were dead and 292 ultra-rare (active on fewer than one in a million examples). Growing the dictionary caused feature splitting: one base64 feature at 512 became three at 4,096 and many more at 16,384 — evidence that there is no single "correct" granularity.

Scaling Monosemanticity (May 2024). Anthropic then applied SAEs to Claude 3 Sonnet, the production model at the time, on the residual stream at the middle layer. Three SAEs had about 1M, 4M and 34M features. Fewer than 300 features were active per token on average, and reconstructions explained at least 65% of the activation variance. Dead features were about 2%, 35% and 65% of the three dictionaries. Features were often highly abstract — the same feature responding to a concept across languages and in images — and included safety-relevant ones related to deception, sycophancy, bias, dangerous content and security vulnerabilities in code. Interventions worked: clamping the Golden Gate Bridge feature to 10× its maximum activation made the model talk about the bridge and even self-identify as it, and Anthropic briefly offered this "Golden Gate Claude" as a 24-hour public research demo. A code-vulnerability feature, clamped high, made the model hallucinate an error in bug-free code; clamped strongly negative, it led the model to rewrite buggy code without the bug. The paper cautions that "there's a difference (for example) between knowing about lies, being capable of lying, and actually lying in the real world".

Scaling and evaluating SAEs (June 2024). OpenAI's Gao et al. introduced TopK SAEs, found clean scaling laws in autoencoder size and sparsity, proposed metrics beyond reconstruction (recovery of hypothesised features, explainability of activation patterns, sparsity of downstream effects), and trained a 16-million-latent SAE on GPT-4 activations over 40 billion tokens.

Worked example: from probe score to alerts per day

Interpretability tools become engineering when you turn a score into a decision. Suppose a jailbreak-detection probe gives each prompt a score from 0 to 1. On a validation set of 2,000 jailbreak attempts and 20,000 benign prompts, you tabulate detection and false alarms at three thresholds:

Those two numbers, not the validation AUROC, decide whether the monitor is worth running.

10. Judging an SAE, and the negative results

Because there is no ground truth for a model's "real" features, SAEs are judged on several proxies at once. Reconstruction: variance explained, and more tellingly the increase in the language model's loss when the SAE's reconstruction replaces the true activation. Sparsity: average L0, the number of active features per token. Dead features: capacity wasted. Interpretability: whether features have clear, predictive explanations. And usefulness: whether the features help with a real task better than a simpler method.

The last test produced sobering results in 2025. Kantamneni, Engels and colleagues tested SAE-feature probes against plain logistic-regression probes on raw activations across four hard regimes — scarce data, class imbalance, label noise and distribution shift — and found the SAE probes underperformed the baseline in each when averaged across datasets. In March 2025 Google DeepMind's interpretability team reported that for detecting harmful intent in prompts, with new jailbreaks as the out-of-distribution test, dense linear probes performed nearly perfectly while probes built on SAE features did noticeably worse out of distribution; the team said it was deprioritising fundamental SAE research. The fair summary is that SAEs are a tool for unsupervised discovery — finding concepts nobody labelled, explaining what a detector is responding to, auditing for hidden objectives (sf4's auditing game used them) — while for a known, labelled detection task a simple probe is the first thing to try.

Why high dimensions make superposition so powerful

In two dimensions you can fit only a few directions before they overlap badly. In d dimensions, the number of directions you can fit with all pairwise dot products below some small ε grows exponentially in d (a consequence of the same concentration-of-measure facts behind the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma). Random unit vectors in 4,096 dimensions have dot products typically around 1/√4096 ≈ 0.016. So a residual stream with a few thousand dimensions can host millions of nearly orthogonal feature directions, each picking up only a sliver of interference from each other active feature. The toy pentagon exaggerates interference to make it visible; in real models the per-pair interference is tiny, and the problem is the sum over many simultaneously active features — which sparsity keeps manageable.

Privileged bases: neurons versus the residual stream

A basis is "privileged" if the architecture treats its directions specially. An elementwise nonlinearity applied to each neuron makes the neuron basis privileged: the network pays a cost for spreading a feature across neurons, because the nonlinearity acts on each separately. The residual stream has no elementwise nonlinearity — blocks read it with matrix multiplications and add to it — so any rotation of it would leave the model's function unchanged, and no direction is special. This is why neuron-level analysis sometimes works for MLP layers (some features partially align with neurons) but is meaningless for the residual stream, and why SAEs on the residual stream look for arbitrary directions.

SAE pathologies and fixes

An L1 penalty pushes all activations towards zero, including those of genuinely active features, so reconstructions are systematically too small ("shrinkage"). Features can also absorb parts of other features when that lowers the loss — a general "starts with S" feature may stop firing on words already covered by more specific features — which makes features less reliable as detectors. Dead features waste capacity and grow with dictionary size, as the 2%, 35% and 65% numbers show. Variants address these: TopK replaces the L1 penalty and controls sparsity exactly; other designs separate the decision of whether a feature fires from how strongly. None removes the deeper issue that the decomposition depends on choices — dictionary size, layer, data — rather than being uniquely determined by the model.

Dark matter

If an SAE explains 65% of the variance of an activation, 35% is unexplained. Splicing the reconstruction back into the model raises its loss, sometimes substantially, which means the unexplained part matters for behaviour. Some of it may be features too rare or too dense for the dictionary to capture, some may be non-linear structure, some may be noise. For safety this matters directly: an audit that inspects SAE features sees only the explained part, and a behaviour implemented in the dark matter would be invisible to it.

Probes as monitors: strengths and weaknesses

Linear probes are attractive production monitors: a dot product per token, trainable from a few thousand labelled examples, often robust out of distribution in the published comparisons. Their weaknesses are the ones the worked example showed — correlates and distribution shift — plus two more. They must be retrained whenever the model changes, since directions move between model versions. And they might be evaded: if a model's training ever optimised against a probe, the representation could shift away from the probed direction, the same dynamic as chain-of-thought obfuscation (rs5). Keep probes out of the training loss if you rely on them for oversight.

What interpretability can claim today

It is fair to say the field has shown that models contain many interpretable, linearly accessible features, including safety-relevant ones; that these can be found at production scale; that interventions on them change behaviour predictably in many cases; and that simple probes detect some properties of interest reliably. It has not shown a complete account of any large model's computation, a method guaranteed to find a hidden behaviour that was not deliberately planted, or that SAE-based tools beat simple baselines on most practical tasks. That gap is why interpretability is usually described as one layer in a safety case, contributing evidence alongside evaluations and control, rather than a replacement for them.

Not every feature is a single direction

The linear representation hypothesis is a powerful default, but the evidence that some features have richer geometry is growing. Cyclical concepts such as days of the week or months can be represented as points around a circle in a two-dimensional subspace rather than as one direction, so that "two days after Saturday" is computed by rotating. Some properties may be represented only in combination, through the interaction of several directions. For practical work the implications are modest but real: a single linear probe may underperform on such concepts, difference-of-means directions may average away the structure, and an SAE may split a circular feature into many arc-shaped pieces. When a probe for an obviously present concept performs poorly, consider that the representation may be a small subspace rather than one direction.

The public case: Scaling Monosemanticity and Golden Gate Claude (May 2024)

"Scaling Monosemanticity: Extracting Interpretable Features from Claude 3 Sonnet" is the best case to know, because it took a method shown on a one-layer toy and applied it to the exact model then in production, with numbers. Three SAEs — about 1M, 4M and 34M features — were trained on residual-stream activations at the middle layer, chosen because the residual stream is smaller than the MLP layer (cheaper) and the middle layer likely holds abstract features. Under 300 features were active per token; at least 65% of variance was explained; dead features were about 2%, 35% and 65%. The features were frequently multilingual and multimodal, and included safety-relevant ones. Interventions changed behaviour: the Golden Gate Bridge feature at 10× its maximum produced a model that steered every conversation towards the bridge and described itself as the bridge, shown publicly for 24 hours. The paper's caution about the gap between representing a concept and acting on it, and its acknowledgement that features found are a subset of what the model computes, are as important to quote as its successes.

Using these tools in practice

For an engineer at a company with access to a model's weights (an open-weight model, or a lab's own model), three uses are practical today. Probe-based monitors: train a linear probe for a property you can label — harmful intent, a jailbreak attempt, the model being about to refuse — validate it on held-out attack styles, choose a threshold for your false-positive budget, and run it alongside output classifiers (sf9). Discovery and auditing: use SAE features (open SAE suites exist for several open-weight models) to look for concepts active during odd behaviour, such as a feature for "reward model" or "being tested" firing during a suspicious transcript. Debugging behaviour: when a fine-tune causes an unexpected change, compare which features fire before and after on the same inputs.

Failure modes

Probe trained on a correlate. High validation accuracy, collapse in production when the correlate changes. Symptom: accuracy differs sharply across data sources. Fix: transfer tests, adversarial data, causal checks.

Over-interpreting a feature label. A feature named "deception" from its top activations may fire on fiction about lying, discussions of spies and the word "trick". Fix: look across the activation range, test explanations on new text, intervene before claiming the feature drives behaviour.

Stale probes and dictionaries. A model update moves directions; a probe or SAE trained on the old model silently degrades. Fix: retrain and revalidate on every model version as part of release.

Ignoring the baseline. Building an elaborate SAE-based detector without checking a logistic-regression probe first. Fix: always report the simple baseline.

What to measure

For probes: accuracy and AUROC in distribution and on held-out sources, calibration of the score, the false-positive rate on real benign traffic at the chosen threshold, and drift over time. For SAEs: variance explained, the increase in model loss when the reconstruction is spliced in, average L0, dead-feature fraction, and the fraction of a sample of features that pass an automated explanation test. Report them together so nobody can pick a flattering one.

Goal: train the toy superposition model and watch features crowd into two dimensions as they become sparse. NumPy from gpt_env (or torch CPU); about 15 minutes.

  1. Set n = 5 features, m = 2 hidden dimensions. Each training example has each feature independently active with probability p, with a value uniform in [0, 1] when active. Give features importances 1.0, 0.9, 0.8, 0.7, 0.6.
  2. Model: h = W @ x with W of shape 2 × 5, output relu(W.T @ h + b). Loss: importance-weighted mean squared error. Train with plain gradient descent (or torch and Adam, learning rate 1e-2) for 5,000 steps, batch 1,024.
  3. Train separately for p = 1.0, 0.3, 0.1 and 0.03. For each, print the column norms of W (how strongly each feature is represented), the matrix W.T @ W, and the angles between feature directions.
  4. For the sparsest run, test the pentagon cases from the body: one feature active, two adjacent, two non-adjacent. Print the read-outs.

What to look for. At p = 1.0 (dense), two features should get norm near 1 and orthogonal directions while the other three are dropped (norm near 0). As p falls, more features should get non-zero norms with off-diagonal entries in W.T @ W — superposition — and at the sparsest setting all five may be represented, roughly evenly spaced around the circle with a negative bias. The non-adjacent test should show interference much like the hand-computed example. The takeaway: whether a network uses superposition is not fixed; it depends on how sparse and how important features are.

Probe-validation drill

A teammate trained a linear probe on layer 20 of an open-weight model to detect "the user is attempting a jailbreak". On a held-out split of the same dataset it reaches 97% accuracy. The dataset was built by collecting jailbreak prompts from public forums (positives) and ordinary prompts from your product logs (negatives). They want to deploy it as a monitor. What do you check before agreeing?

The confound. Positives and negatives come from different sources, so the probe may be detecting the source — forum style, length, formatting, topics, English versus other languages — rather than jailbreak intent. This is the lesson's worked example in real life: high in-distribution accuracy with a correlate available.

Checks. (1) Build a matched test set: benign forum posts as negatives and jailbreak attempts written in the style of your product traffic as positives; accuracy that collapses here means the probe learned the source. (2) Hold out entire jailbreak families (role-play, encoding, many-shot) and test on them, since new attack styles are what a monitor must catch. (3) Measure the false-positive rate on a large sample of real benign product traffic at the intended threshold, and convert it to alerts per day. (4) Compare with a simple text classifier on the prompt; if the probe is no better, its added value is unclear. (5) Check other layers; a probe that works only at one layer can be fragile. (6) Run a causal sanity check if feasible: add and subtract the probe direction and see whether the model's tendency to comply with a borderline request moves.

Deployment conditions. Retrain and revalidate on every model update; keep the probe out of any training loss; log probe scores with outcomes so drift is visible; and treat it as one layer alongside input and output classifiers (sf9), not a sole gate.
