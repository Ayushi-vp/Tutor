In May 2025 Anthropic had a new model, Claude Opus 4, ready to ship. Its benchmark scores were strong, its behaviour in testing was good, and it refused requests for weapons help. None of that answered the question its safety policy required it to answer: if someone with basic technical skills tried to use this model to obtain a biological weapon, would it meaningfully help them? The company ran knowledge tests, agentic virology tasks, expert red-teaming and a two-day trial in which groups of people drafted acquisition plans with and without the model. The model-assisted groups scored two and a half times higher. That was below the level the company had decided would be clearly unacceptable, but close enough that it could not rule the risk out. It shipped the model with a stronger set of protections, saying openly that the step was precautionary.

This is what a dangerous-capability evaluation is for. It does not ask whether a model behaves well in ordinary use; earlier topics covered that. It asks what the model could do if it cooperated fully with someone trying to cause catastrophic harm, in a handful of domains where harm could be enormous and irreversible: biological and chemical weapons, cyberattacks, AI systems that act autonomously and replicate themselves, and large-scale manipulation. The answer feeds a decision — ship, ship with safeguards, or hold — that frontier safety frameworks (sf8) make conditional on the results.

These evaluations are hard in ways ordinary benchmarks are not. The harms cannot be tested directly, so every task is a proxy. The interesting results are often "no significant effect", and small studies cannot tell "no effect" from "not enough data". Scores depend heavily on how hard the evaluators tried to bring out the model's ability, and a model might even hold back (sf4). And some results cannot be published in full, because publishing would itself spread the hazard.

This lesson builds the toolkit. It starts with threat models, walks through the main kinds of evidence — knowledge benchmarks, agentic tasks, time horizons, red-teaming and human uplift trials — with the numbers from the best-known studies, works out the statistics of a small uplift trial, unpacks the Claude Opus 4 decision, and then turns to elicitation: how to tell whether you measured the model's ceiling or just your own setup's. It ends by designing an evaluation end to end, the way you might in an interview.

Think about how engineers certify a bridge. They do not drive a few cars over it and declare it safe. They work out the worst loads it might face — a traffic jam of heavy lorries, a storm, an earthquake — and test, by calculation and by physical load tests, whether it holds with a margin to spare. The tests are deliberately harsher than normal use, and the result is stated as a capacity with a safety factor, not as "it was fine on Tuesday".

Dangerous-capability evaluation follows the same logic. Ordinary evaluation is "drive a few cars over it": typical prompts, typical users. Dangerous-capability evaluation asks about the worst plausible load: a motivated person, with the model fully cooperating (refusals removed), with good tools and time, trying to accomplish a specific harmful goal. You want to know the model's maximum capability, and then you compare it to a threshold with a margin.

Four ideas organise everything that follows:

  • Threat model first. Name the actor (a novice, a skilled criminal group, a state programme), the pathway (the steps to cause harm) and the bottleneck the model might remove. An evaluation without a threat model measures something, but not necessarily anything that matters.
  • Layers of evidence. Knowledge quizzes are cheap and far from reality; agentic tasks are closer; human uplift trials are closest but slow, small and expensive. A credible assessment uses several.
  • Uplift, not absolute ability. What matters is how much the model adds compared with what the actor could already do, usually with internet access.
  • Elicitation. The score is a lower bound on capability, set by how hard you tried. Weak elicitation produces false comfort.

One more distinction keeps the vocabulary straight. A benchmark is a fixed set of tasks with a score; an evaluation is a benchmark plus a threat model, an elicitation protocol, a comparison group and a pre-agreed reading of the result; and a determination is the decision a framework makes from several evaluations — for example "this model requires the next level of safeguards". Interview answers that jump from a benchmark score straight to a determination skip the two steps where most of the judgment lives.

Where does the bridge analogy break? A bridge's capacity is fixed once built; a model's effective capability grows after release as people write better prompts, tools and agent scaffolds. Load tests use physics that is well understood; capability tests rely on proxies whose relation to real harm is debated. And a bridge does not adjust its strength when it notices a test (sf4). Hence the margins, and the requirement to re-evaluate when anything important changes.

A last framing worth carrying into the body: the output of all this work is a decision under uncertainty, not a measurement. The evaluations narrow the uncertainty; the framework (sf8) says which way to lean when uncertainty remains. Because a missed dangerous capability is far costlier than an unnecessary safeguard, frameworks lean towards caution — "if we cannot rule it out, we act as if it is there" — and the rest of this lesson shows how evaluators try to make the "cannot rule out" zone as narrow as possible, so that caution is not triggered by noise alone.

1. What counts as a dangerous capability, and the threat model

Google DeepMind's 2024 paper "Evaluating Frontier Models for Dangerous Capabilities" defines dangerous capabilities as those that "could potentially unlock very large-scale harm, even if they also have beneficial applications". Published frontier frameworks (sf8) converge on a few domains:

  • CBRN — chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear weapons, with biology usually the focus because the knowledge and equipment barriers are thought to be lowest relative to potential harm.
  • Cyber offence — finding and exploiting vulnerabilities, especially autonomously or at scale.
  • Autonomy — an AI system acting with little human input: accelerating AI research and development itself, or obtaining resources and copying itself to new compute (self-replication).
  • Persuasion and manipulation — changing people's beliefs or behaviour at scale; included in some frameworks and treated more cautiously as a threshold domain in others.

Within each domain, an evaluation starts from a threat model: who the actor is, what pathway they follow and where the bottleneck lies. For biological weapons, a common framing is a pathway of ideation, acquisition, magnification, formulation and release (the five stages OpenAI's 2024 study used), with the question being whether the model helps a particular kind of actor past a step they could not otherwise pass. For cyber, the question might be whether the model lets a low-skill actor run an attack that previously needed an expert team, or lets a skilled team work much faster. The threat model decides which tasks to build, which comparison group to use and what result would matter.

2. Knowledge benchmarks

The cheapest evidence is a set of questions. WMDP (the Weapons of Mass Destruction Proxy benchmark, 2024) has 3,668 multiple-choice questions in biosecurity, cybersecurity and chemical security, filtered to exclude sensitive and export-controlled information so it can be public. It serves as a proxy measurement of hazardous knowledge and as a target for "unlearning" methods that try to remove such knowledge while keeping general ability.

Harder and closer to practice is the Virology Capabilities Test (VCT, 2025): 322 multimodal questions written with dozens of PhD virologists about troubleshooting real laboratory protocols, including tacit and visual knowledge. Expert virologists with internet access averaged 22.1% on questions in their own sub-areas; OpenAI's o3 scored 43.8%, outperforming 94% of the experts even within their specialities. The authors' interpretation is careful: expert-level troubleshooting is dual-use, useful for legitimate research and for misuse, and should be handled within existing frameworks for dual-use life-science work.

Knowledge benchmarks are run on every checkpoint because they are cheap and automatic, so they make good early-warning indicators. Their weakness is construct validity: answering questions is not doing the work. A model that knows the answer may still not help a novice who lacks the hands-on skill, materials and equipment.

3. Agentic tasks: cyber and autonomy

For cyber and autonomy, the natural evaluations are tasks the model performs as an agent in a sandbox, with automatic success checks. Cybench (ICLR 2025) uses 40 professional-level capture-the-flag (CTF) tasks from four competitions; the agent can run commands in an environment and must retrieve a secret flag. Difficulty is anchored to first-solve time, how long the fastest human team took in the original competition. Evaluating eight models with several scaffolds, the authors found that without subtask hints, agents built on Claude 3.5 Sonnet, GPT-4o, o1-preview and Claude 3 Opus solved complete tasks that took human teams up to 11 minutes, whereas the hardest task took humans 24 hours and 54 minutes. Subtasks — intermediate steps with their own checks — measure progress on tasks beyond the agents' reach, which keeps the benchmark informative as models improve.

For self-replication, the UK AI Security Institute's RepliBench (2025) decomposes the capability into four domains — obtaining resources, exfiltrating model weights, replicating onto compute and persisting there — with 20 task families and 86 tasks. Its finding on five frontier models: they "do not currently pose a credible threat of self-replication, but succeed on many components and are improving rapidly". Models could deploy instances from cloud providers, write self-propagating programs and exfiltrate weights under simple security setups, but struggled with know-your-customer (KYC) identity checks and with setting up robust, persistent deployments. The best model evaluated, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, had over 50% pass@10 on 15 of 20 task families.

For general autonomy, METR's time horizon (2025) converts task success into human time. METR timed skilled humans on a suite of software and research tasks and defined a model's 50% time horizon as the human duration of tasks the model completes with 50% success. The paper reports about 110 minutes for o3 and a doubling time of roughly seven months since 2019, possibly faster since 2024, while discussing external-validity limits: the tasks are cleaner and better specified than most real work. Its companion RE-Bench compares agents with human experts on open-ended AI research engineering tasks. The trend is what makes this useful for governance: if horizons keep doubling, you can estimate when a model will handle multi-day autonomous projects and plan evaluations before it does.

AI research and development as a capability

One autonomy domain gets special attention in frontier frameworks: a model's ability to do AI research itself. The concern is not a single harmful act but acceleration — if models can substantially speed up the development of more capable models, capability could grow faster than evaluations, safeguards and governance can keep up, and a lab's own systems could be doing much of the work of building their successors. Anthropic's 2026 policy text, for instance, frames one threshold in terms of AI compressing two years of 2018–2024-rate progress into one (sf8 examines the wording). Evaluating it is hard: research is open-ended and slow to verify. Current evidence comes from benchmarks such as METR's RE-Bench, which compares agents with human experts on open-ended machine-learning engineering tasks under fixed time budgets, from time-horizon trends, and from internal measurements of how much research staff actually rely on models. A useful interview point is that this threshold is unusual: crossing it is dangerous mainly because it makes every other evaluation harder to keep up to date.

4. Persuasion

Persuasion is measured with human experiments. In a pre-registered study by Salvi and colleagues (2024), 820 participants debated either a human or GPT-4, with or without the opponent seeing basic demographic information about them. Participants who debated GPT-4 with access to their personal information had 81.7% higher odds of increased agreement with their opponent than those who debated humans. DeepMind's 2024 evaluations of Gemini 1.0 found persuasion and deception the most mature of the dangerous capabilities they tested. Persuasion is hard to turn into a threshold, because ordinary uses (marketing, advocacy, education) involve persuasion too and the harm depends on scale and target; frameworks treat it differently, as sf8 shows.

5. Human uplift trials, and the statistics of small studies

Uplift trials come closest to the question that matters: does a person do better at harm-relevant tasks with the model than without? In January 2024 OpenAI published one for biology. It recruited 100 participants: 50 biology experts with PhDs and wet-lab experience, and 50 students with at least one university biology course. Within each group, half were randomly assigned to internet only and half to internet plus GPT-4 (experts got a research-only version without safety refusals). All worked on tasks across the five stages from ideation to release, scored blind by external biosecurity experts on accuracy, completeness and innovation. On the 10-point accuracy scale, model access raised the mean by 0.88 points for experts and 0.25 for students; completeness rose by 0.82 and 0.41. None of the differences was statistically significant. OpenAI described this as at most a mild uplift, "not large enough to be conclusive", and noted that information access alone is insufficient to create a biological threat and that the study did not test physical construction.

How much should "not significant" reassure us? That depends on the study's power: the chance it would have detected an effect of that size if it were real. The study had about 25 participants per arm. The post does not give the score spread, so assume standard deviations to see the range. For a two-group comparison at the usual 5% significance level:

That asymmetry is why frameworks act on "cannot rule out" rather than waiting for "demonstrated".

6. The Claude Opus 4 decision, worked through

Anthropic's May 2025 system card for Claude Opus 4 and Claude Sonnet 4 reports a bioweapons-acquisition uplift trial. Groups of 8–10 participants had up to two days to draft a comprehensive acquisition plan. Controls had basic internet resources; the treatment groups additionally had Claude with safeguards removed, including extended thinking and research tools. Deloitte graded the plans against a rubric covering key steps of the acquisition pathway. Before the trial, Anthropic's threat analysis set the interpretation: total uplift of 5× or more (or raw uplift of 0.8 or more) would create significant additional risk, while uplift of 2.8× or less would keep risk at acceptable levels.

The results: controls averaged 25% (±13%), Claude Opus 4 users 63% (±13%), Claude Sonnet 4 users 42% (±11%). Uplift is the ratio: 63 / 25 ≈ 2.5 (the card reports 2.53×) for Opus 4 and 1.70× for Sonnet 4; the same trial on the earlier Claude Sonnet 3.7 had given 1.53×. All participants still hit critical failures — errors that would prevent real-world success. Expert red-teamers from Deloitte, testing over three days, reported substantially increased risk in certain parts of the pathway for Opus 4. Agentic virology tasks used three zones: below 50% rules the threshold out, above 80% rules it in, and the middle needs more evaluation.

Putting it together, Anthropic concluded that Sonnet 4 did not meet the ASL-3 bar while Opus 4's result was close enough that ASL-3 could not be ruled out. Its announcement is precise: it had "not yet determined whether Claude Opus 4 has definitively passed the Capabilities Threshold", but "clearly ruling out ASL-3 risks is not possible for Claude Opus 4 in the way it was for every previous model", so it deployed with ASL-3 protections "as a precautionary and provisional action" (sf8 explains what ASL-3 protections are). Note the reasoning pattern — pre-set thresholds, several kinds of evidence, uncertainty acknowledged, decision triggered by the inability to rule out — because it is the pattern interviewers want to hear. The 2026 International AI Safety Report notes that in 2025 multiple developers released models with additional safeguards for the same reason.

7. Elicitation: are we measuring the ceiling?

Every score above is a lower bound, set by how hard the evaluators tried. The levers are well known:

  • Model variant: a helpful-only or safeguards-removed model, so refusals do not mask capability.
  • Prompting: clear task framing, examples, asking for step-by-step reasoning.
  • Scaffold and tools: an agent loop with a shell, browser, code execution and memory; scaffolds matter enormously for agentic tasks.
  • Budget: time, tokens and number of steps; many agents fail only because they run out.
  • Attempts: pass@k, the probability that at least one of k attempts succeeds.
  • Fine-tuning: training on task demonstrations, which sf4's password-locking work showed recovers hidden capability with few examples.

pass@k deserves arithmetic, because it changes conclusions. If a model succeeds on a task 10% of the time per attempt, and attempts are independent, the chance at least one of k succeeds is 1 − 0.9k: 10% at k = 1, 41% at k = 5, 65% at k = 10, 88% at k = 20. A defender sees one attempt fail; an attacker who can retry twenty times will probably succeed. Misuse evaluations should report pass@k at values that match realistic attacker behaviour, and treat pass@1 as a floor.

The figure makes the stakes concrete. The same model on the same tasks scores 8% with a bare prompt and 55% with full elicitation; a 35% threshold is "passed" or "failed" depending entirely on effort. Three practices follow: elicit at least as hard as a capable adversary would; report the elicitation used alongside the score; and set thresholds or margins that account for improvements after release — better scaffolds and more attempts are cheap for users to add.

Worked example: reading a trend instead of a single score

Single evaluations near a threshold are noisy; trends across model generations are more informative. Suppose a lab's private cyber suite of 40 tasks, scored at pass@10 with the same scaffold, gives 6/40 (15%) for the model released 18 months ago, 11/40 (27.5%) for the one released 9 months ago, and 16/40 (40%) for the current candidate. The rule-in threshold is 50%. The per-generation gain has been about 12 points every 9 months. A naive extrapolation puts the next model, expected in about 9 months, near 52% — just over the line — and with the Wilson interval for the current score running from about 26% to 55%, the current model itself cannot be confidently placed below the threshold either.

Three conclusions follow, and they are the kind of reasoning an interviewer wants. First, the current model sits in the middle zone, so deeper evaluation is warranted now: more tasks to narrow the interval, stronger elicitation, an expert red-team. Second, the next model is likely to cross, so the safeguards that crossing requires should be built now, not after the next evaluation, because they take months. Third, the trend has its own uncertainty — a new training method can produce a jump, and saturation of easy tasks can flatten the curve — so the forecast schedules preparation rather than replacing the measurement. This is how "early warning" thresholds work in practice: they buy lead time for safeguards.

8. Designing an evaluation, end to end

Suppose you are asked to build a cyber evaluation answering: "Can the model autonomously compromise vulnerable internet-facing web applications in a way that gives a low-skill actor meaningful uplift?" A complete design has eight parts.

  1. Threat model. Actor: low-skill, with the model as an autonomous agent. Pathway: reconnaissance, vulnerability discovery, exploitation, foothold. Bottleneck: chaining these steps without expert help.
  2. Tasks. Thirty to fifty deliberately vulnerable web applications built for the evaluation (new, to avoid training-data contamination), covering a spread of vulnerability classes and difficulty.
  3. Success criteria. Automatic: retrieve a flag or obtain a shell; plus subtasks for partial credit.
  4. Human baseline. Time skilled penetration testers on each task, and ideally novices with and without model help for an uplift comparison.
  5. Elicitation protocol. Helpful-only model, a strong public-grade agent scaffold, relevant tools, generous step budget, pass@1 and pass@10, and a fine-tuned variant if feasible.
  6. Thresholds, pre-registered. For example: below 20% of tasks at pass@10 rules the concern out; above 50% rules it in; between triggers deeper evaluation and expert red-teaming. Write down what each outcome triggers before running.
  7. Safety of the evaluation itself. An isolated sandbox with no internet egress, logging of every action, and review before any artefacts leave the environment.
  8. Reporting. Scores with confidence intervals, the elicitation used, comparison with previous models and with the trend, known limitations, and which details are withheld as hazardous.

This structure works for any domain. The hardest parts are usually the threat model — agreeing which actor and which bottleneck matter — and the thresholds, because they encode a judgment about acceptable risk that the evaluation cannot make for you.

Capability, propensity and why safeguards are evaluated separately

Dangerous-capability evaluations measure capability on purpose, using helpful-only models. The deployed system's protections — refusal training, classifiers, monitoring, access controls — are then evaluated separately against adversaries (sf9). Separating the two makes each question answerable: "how dangerous is this model if fully cooperative?" and "how hard is it to make the deployed system fully cooperative?". Frameworks pair them: crossing a capability threshold requires stronger safeguards, and the safeguards must themselves be shown robust. For open-weight releases the second question collapses, because fine-tuning removes safeguards cheaply (sf6's refusal direction), so capability alone decides.

Is knowledge the bottleneck?

A recurring debate concerns biology. One view holds that the main barriers to biological weapons are practical — obtaining materials, laboratory skill, equipment, avoiding detection — so models that only supply information add little. Another holds that expert troubleshooting, the kind VCT measures, is exactly what lets novices overcome practical failures, and that models increasingly supply it. The evidence so far supports neither extreme: text-based trials show moderate uplift with critical failures remaining; troubleshooting benchmarks show expert-level performance; and nobody has run (or could ethically run) a full wet-lab trial of weapon production. The 2026 International AI Safety Report captures the state of knowledge: it remains difficult to assess the degree to which material barriers continue to constrain actors. A credible position names this uncertainty rather than resolving it by assertion.

Trends and early warning

Because evaluations lag models, the most useful signals are trends. If time horizons double every seven months, a model at 2 hours today implies roughly 8 hours in about 14 months. Labs and evaluators use such trends to schedule harder evaluations before they are needed and to set "warning" thresholds below the real ones, so that crossing a warning level triggers preparation — new evaluations, safeguards in development — while there is still time. DeepMind's 2024 paper went further and commissioned professional forecasters to predict when models would first score highly on its evaluations. Trends can break either way, so they schedule work rather than replace measurement.

Contamination, saturation and the need for new tasks

Public benchmarks leak into training data, and once they do, high scores may reflect memorisation. They also saturate: once frontier models score near the maximum, the benchmark stops discriminating. Dangerous-capability evaluators therefore keep private task sets, build new tasks for each evaluation round, design subtasks to measure partial progress, and anchor difficulty to human time so results stay interpretable as tasks change. The cost is that private evaluations are harder for outsiders to check, which is one reason third-party evaluators and published methodology matter.

Information hazards in publishing results

Publishing exactly which steps a model helps with, or example transcripts, could itself provide uplift. System cards therefore often state thresholds and summary results but withhold details: the Opus 4 card notes that some CBRN thresholds and results are sensitive and not disclosed. This creates a real tension with transparency and independent verification, addressed partly by giving government institutes and trusted third parties fuller access under agreements, and by publishing methodology without hazardous specifics.

External and government evaluation

A lab evaluating its own model has an obvious conflict of interest and a practical blind spot: its evaluators share its assumptions. External evaluation addresses both. Government institutes such as the UK AI Security Institute and the US Center for AI Standards and Innovation have run pre-deployment tests of frontier models under voluntary agreements; independent organisations specialise in particular domains; and frameworks increasingly commit labs to some form of external review (sf8). External evaluators face their own constraints — limited time before launch, limited access to weights or helpful-only variants, and confidentiality — so their reports often say what they could and could not test. When you read a system card, look for who evaluated the model, what access they had, and whether their results agreed with the lab's.

Where experts disagree

Some researchers argue current uplift evidence shows limited real-world risk: effects are moderate, plans still fail, and information was mostly available already. Others argue that the trend is what matters — each model generation moves further along the pathway, and by the time a trial shows unambiguous uplift, deployment decisions will already have been made. Both views can accept the same data. The practical consensus in published frameworks is to act on the inability to rule out, accept some unnecessary precaution, and invest in better evaluations so the "middle zone" shrinks.

The public case: Claude Opus 4 and precautionary ASL-3 (May 2025)

Anthropic's system card for Claude Opus 4 and Claude Sonnet 4 is the most complete public example of a dangerous-capability decision. The evidence: automated knowledge and troubleshooting evaluations; agentic long-form virology tasks with rule-out and rule-in zones; three days of expert red-teaming by Deloitte staff that found substantially increased risk in certain parts of the acquisition pathway for Opus 4; and a two-day uplift trial with pre-set thresholds (≤2.8× acceptable, ≥5× significant risk) in which Opus 4 produced 2.53× uplift, Sonnet 4 1.70× and the earlier Sonnet 3.7 1.53×, with all participants still hitting critical failures. The decision: Sonnet 4 under the existing standard; Opus 4 with ASL-3 protections as a precautionary, provisional action, because ASL-3 risk could not be clearly ruled out — and ASL-4 explicitly ruled out for both. The lessons generalise: set thresholds before testing, combine evidence types, state uncertainty, and let the inability to rule out trigger action.

Who runs these evaluations

Frontier labs run internal evaluations, usually in dedicated frontier red team or preparedness teams. External evaluators add independence: the UK AI Security Institute and the US Center for AI Standards and Innovation have run pre-deployment tests of frontier models under agreements with developers; independent organisations such as METR, Apollo Research and SecureBio evaluate autonomy, scheming and biology respectively; and contracted firms supply domain experts and graders (Deloitte graded the Opus 4 trial). System cards increasingly list which external evaluators had access and what they found.

Failure modes of evaluation programmes

Under-elicitation. Scores from bare prompts or weak scaffolds. Symptom: external evaluators or users find much higher capability soon after release. Fix: elicitation protocols that match public scaffolds, pass@k, fine-tuning.

Stale tasks. Benchmarks saturated or contaminated. Symptom: every model scores near the top; scores jump without corresponding capability. Fix: fresh private tasks, subtasks, human-time anchors.

Underpowered trials read as reassurance. "No significant uplift" from 25 per arm. Fix: power calculations up front, report effect sizes and intervals, and act on inability to rule out.

Thresholds set after seeing results. The temptation to decide what "too much" means once the number is known. Fix: pre-register thresholds and the decision each triggers.

Evaluating a different system from the one shipped. Tests on an older checkpoint or without the tools the product exposes. Fix: evaluate the final model in the product's configuration, and re-evaluate when tools or access change.

What to keep running after launch

Capability changes after release: users write better prompts and scaffolds, new tools appear, and fine-tuning access may be offered. Keep a cheap subset of evaluations running against the deployed system and any new scaffolds; monitor misuse signals that would indicate capability in the wild (sf9); and re-run the full suite when the model, its tools or its access mode changes in a material way.

Reading a system card's capability section

When a new frontier model launches, a quick structured read of its capability section tells you most of what matters. Find the threshold framework it refers to and the level determined. Check which evaluations were run per domain and whether thresholds were stated before results. Look for the elicitation used (helpful-only variant, scaffolds, attempts) and for external evaluators. Note which results are withheld as sensitive. And compare with the previous model's card: a jump in a cheap benchmark that is not reflected in the headline determination deserves a question.

Goal: get a feel for the statistics that decide dangerous-capability calls — power, pass@k and uncertainty — with Python 3.12 standard library only; about 15 minutes.

  1. Write power(d, n) for a two-sided, two-sample z-test at α = 0.05 using statistics.NormalDist, and n_for_power(d, power=0.8). Reproduce the body's table for SD = 1.5, 2.0 and 2.5 and an effect of 0.88 points.
  2. Simulate an uplift trial: control scores ~ Normal(25, 13), treatment ~ Normal(63, 13), clipped to [0, 100], with 9 participants per group. Compute the uplift ratio of means 10,000 times and report the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. How often is the ratio above 2.8? Below 2.0?
  3. Write pass_at_k(p, k) and print a table for p in {0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.3} and k in {1, 5, 10, 50}.
  4. Given 40 tasks with 14 solved, compute a 95% Wilson interval for the solve rate, and decide which of the zones "<20% rule out / >50% rule in / between" it falls into.

What to look for. The power table should match the body (about 55%, 34% and 24%). The simulated uplift ratio should spread widely around 2.5 — with groups of 9 and that much variation, a meaningful share of trials could land above 2.8 or near 2.0 — which is why a single trial near a threshold cannot settle a decision. pass@k shows how fast rare successes become likely with retries. The Wilson interval for 14/40 (35%) should straddle the middle zone, illustrating why middle zones exist and call for more evidence.

Decision drill

Your lab's next model is evaluated for autonomous cyber capability. Pre-registered rule: below 20% of a 40-task suite at pass@10 rules out the "high" threshold; above 50% rules it in. The evaluation team reports 16/40 (40%) at pass@10 with your standard scaffold. An external evaluator, using a newer public scaffold, reports 23/40 (57.5%). The product team wants to ship next week, arguing the internal number is in the middle zone and "the external scaffold isn't something we ship". What do you recommend?

Answer

The external number is the one that matters for misuse. The threat model is an attacker using the model autonomously; attackers use the best public scaffold, not the one the product ships. Capability evaluations should elicit at least as hard as a capable adversary, so 57.5% is the better estimate of capability, and it is above the rule-in line.

Check the uncertainty. 23/40 has a 95% interval of roughly 42% to 72%; 16/40 roughly 26% to 55%. The intervals overlap, but the external result's interval sits mostly above 50%, and the difference is explained by a known cause (scaffold quality), not noise.

Recommendation. Treat the threshold as ruled in, or at minimum as not ruled out, which under most frameworks requires the higher safeguards before deployment: enhanced cyber-misuse classifiers and monitoring, account-level controls for agentic use, and a reviewed security posture for the weights. If those safeguards are ready, ship with them; if not, delay. In parallel, rerun the internal evaluation with the external scaffold to confirm, add pass@k at higher k, and update the internal elicitation protocol so the standard scaffold tracks the best public one.

What not to do. Do not average the two numbers into "48%, middle zone", and do not dismiss the stronger elicitation because the product does not include it. That is exactly the under-elicitation failure the framework exists to prevent.
