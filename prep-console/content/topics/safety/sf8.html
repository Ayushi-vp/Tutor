Imagine you lead a team at a frontier lab and your next model is three weeks from launch. Its dangerous-capability evaluations (sf7) come back: the model gives noticeably more help on a biology uplift task than the last one, solves more of the offensive-cyber suite, and is starting to speed up your own researchers. Now what? Without a framework, the answer depends on who argues hardest in the room that week, on launch pressure, and on whatever the competition just shipped. The same results could produce a delay, a launch with extra protections, or a launch as planned — and nobody outside would know why.

Frontier safety frameworks exist to make that decision in advance. Before the results arrive, the lab writes down which capabilities it will test for, at what level each becomes a concern, what protections it will put in place when that level is reached, and who has the authority to decide. Publishing the framework lets outsiders hold the lab to it, lets employees know what they can object to, and lets governments see what industry practice looks like. The 2026 International AI Safety Report notes that twelve companies published or updated such frameworks in 2025, and that a few jurisdictions have begun to turn some of these practices into legal requirements.

The frameworks also change, often. Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy has had nine versions since September 2023, including a complete rewrite in February 2026; OpenAI's Preparedness Framework was substantially revised in April 2025; Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework reached version 3.1 in April 2026. An interview answer that describes a two-year-old version — or that cannot say what the company you are interviewing with actually commits to — is a weak answer, especially at a lab whose framework is part of its public identity.

There is a second reason to learn them well, beyond interviews. If you work on evaluations, safeguards, security or release engineering at any frontier developer, the framework decides what "done" means for your work: which evaluation must run before a launch, which classifier must reach which robustness bar, which security controls must exist before a model of a given capability is trained. Understanding the framework is understanding the requirements document your own work is measured against.

This lesson describes the three best-known frameworks as of October 2026, with their versions and dates from the companies' own documents: what each tracks, its thresholds, the required safeguards, how weight security is handled, who decides, and how each deals with competitors. It then walks a hypothetical model through all three, and finishes with the strongest criticisms and how to discuss them fairly.

A useful analogy is the building code combined with a hospital's escalation protocol. A building code says: if a building is above a certain height, it needs sprinklers; above another height, fire-protected stairwells and a firefighters' lift. You do not debate sprinklers building by building; the trigger and the requirement are written down before anyone designs the building. A hospital escalation protocol adds the human side: if a patient's score crosses a threshold, a named senior doctor must review within thirty minutes and decide. Triggers, requirements and a named decision-maker.

Every frontier safety framework has the same four parts:

  • Risk domains and capability thresholds. Which harms the framework covers (biological and chemical weapons, cyber, autonomy and AI research, sometimes manipulation) and, for each, the level of capability that makes the risk serious.
  • Evaluations. How the lab checks whether a model is near or past a threshold, and how often (sf7).
  • Required safeguards. What must be in place once a threshold is reached: deployment protections such as classifiers and monitoring (sf9), and security for the model weights, since stolen weights bypass every deployment protection.
  • Governance. Who reviews the evidence, who decides, what is published, who can object, and how the framework itself changes.

When you read any framework, a fifteen-minute pass in a fixed order gets you most of what an interviewer will ask. First the header: version and date, because everything else depends on it. Then the domains and the threshold definitions, read word for word — "meaningful counterfactual assistance to novices" and "uplift to low-to-medium-resourced actors" are not the same claim. Then what reaching each threshold requires, separating deployment safeguards from weight security and from what happens during development. Then governance: the named people or bodies, what they approve, what is published and who reviews it. Finally the escape hatches — competitor clauses, "may revise", "where appropriate" — because those are where critics will push and where you should have a view.

It also helps to keep the three companies' vocabularies apart, since each invented its own: Anthropic speaks of capability thresholds, ASLs, Risk Reports and the Responsible Scaling Officer; OpenAI of Tracked and Research Categories, High and Critical, and the Safety Advisory Group; Google DeepMind of Critical and Tracked Capability Levels, alert thresholds, security levels and safety cases. Using the right term for the right company is a cheap signal that you have read the documents.

Where does the building-code analogy break? Building codes are law, written by public bodies, enforced by inspectors; these frameworks are voluntary company policies, written and interpreted by the companies themselves, though some laws now require that they exist and are followed. Building thresholds are measurable (metres); capability thresholds are judgments built on imperfect evaluations. And building codes rarely need rewriting every few months; frameworks do, because the technology and the science of evaluating it move fast.

Keep three questions in mind for each framework below: what triggers action, what action, and who decides? Those are what interviewers probe.

1. Where these frameworks came from

Anthropic published the first Responsible Scaling Policy in September 2023, introducing AI Safety Levels modelled loosely on biosafety levels. OpenAI published a beta Preparedness Framework in December 2023, and Google DeepMind its first Frontier Safety Framework in May 2024. At the AI Seoul Summit in May 2024, sixteen companies signed the Frontier AI Safety Commitments, which included publishing a safety framework; the 2026 International AI Safety Report counted twelve companies publishing or updating frameworks in 2025. Since then, the three leading frameworks have each been revised several times, and in different directions. What follows is their state as of early October 2026, from the companies' own documents.

2. Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy, version 3.x

Versions. v1.0 (September 2023), v2.0 (October 2024), v2.1 (March 2025), v2.2 (May 2025), then v3.0 effective 24 February 2026, a comprehensive rewrite, followed by v3.1 (2 April 2026), v3.2 (29 April), v3.3 (26 May) and v3.4 (effective 8 July 2026), the current version.

The big change in v3. Earlier versions committed Anthropic to bring its own models' risks to acceptable levels regardless of what other developers did. Version 3 argues that this runs into a collective-action problem: overall catastrophic risk depends on all developers, and if one paused while others moved ahead without strong protections, "the developers with the weakest protections would set the pace". So v3 separates two things: Anthropic's plans as a company, which it expects to achieve regardless of others, and more ambitious recommendations for industry-wide safety, which it cannot commit to follow unilaterally but uses as a "north star" for its own planning and policy advocacy. A set of competitor-contingent commitments in an appendix covers scenarios such as Anthropic being clearly in the lead with a highly capable model, in which case it would delay development and deployment until it could make a strong argument that catastrophic risk is contained. Version 3.1 clarified that Anthropic may still consider pausing development or deployment even where those appendix scenarios are not triggered.

Thresholds. The policy's central table maps capability (or usage) thresholds to mitigations, in three columns: the threshold, Anthropic's plan, and the industry-wide recommendation. The rows are:

  • Non-novel chemical/biological weapons production — significantly helping people with basic technical backgrounds (for example undergraduate STEM degrees) create, obtain and deploy chemical or biological weapons with catastrophic potential. Anthropic's plan: maintain or improve its ASL-3 protections, including classifier guards at least as robust as its initial constitutional classifiers (sf9), access controls, red-teaming, bug bounties, threat intelligence and security controls.
  • Novel chemical/biological weapons production — models that can functionally substitute for the scarce world-class expertise that is the main barrier to novel weapons with pandemic-scale consequences. The industry recommendation calls for protections strong enough that even well-resourced actors could not reliably jailbreak models or steal weights, roughly in line with RAND security level 4.
  • Misaligned AI systems in high-stakes settings — systems highly relied on, with access to sensitive assets and some capacity for autonomous operation and subterfuge, such that they could plausibly carry out sabotage raising the odds of a later global catastrophe. The recommendation calls for a strong argument against such sabotage, combining internal compartmentalisation, monitoring, capability limits and evidence about propensities to deceive.
  • Automated R&D in key domains, starting with AI R&D — models that could fully substitute for Anthropic's research scientists and engineers at competitive cost (within a factor of 5), or "dramatic acceleration" of AI progress attributable to automating research, defined as roughly two years of baseline progress in one. Version 3.4 clarified that this threshold aims to capture the onset of dramatic recursive self-improvement.

ASLs now. An appendix explains that ASLs still describe the levels of protection Anthropic maintains for existing models — such as the ASL-3 protections applied to Claude Opus 4 in May 2025 (sf7) — but that for future capability levels, prescribing specific control lists proved "overly rigid"; the policy instead specifies what argument a developer should make and against which actors.

Roadmaps and Risk Reports. Two new instruments carry much of the weight. A Frontier Safety Roadmap sets ambitious goals across security, alignment, safeguards and policy, published in redacted form and graded publicly — explicitly goals, not hard commitments. Risk Reports, published every 3–6 months, cover all publicly deployed models and certain internal ones: threat models, evaluation evidence, mitigations, remaining absolute risk per threat, an overall risk assessment, and a risk-benefit determination; off-cycle analyses are required when a significantly more capable model is deployed. Anthropic published Risk Reports in February and August 2026.

Governance. A Responsible Scaling Officer implements the policy. Risk Reports go to the CEO and RSO for approval and then to the Board and the Long-Term Benefit Trust; where a marginal-risk argument — "our model adds relatively little risk given others" — is central to a decision, explicit Board and LTBT approval is required. External reviewers without financial conflicts receive near-unredacted reports and publish commentary within 30 days; since v3.2 the LTBT can request external review and approves reviewer selection. Unredacted reports go to at least 200 employees, staff can report noncompliance anonymously to more than one recipient, non-disparagement agreements may not preclude raising safety concerns, and a third party reviews procedural compliance roughly annually.

3. OpenAI's Preparedness Framework, version 2

Version. Version 2, last updated 15 April 2025, replaced the December 2023 beta. OpenAI's deployment-safety pages for its October 2026 models still link to version 2, so it is the version in force when this lesson was checked.

Tracked Categories. Three: Biological and Chemical, Cybersecurity and AI Self-improvement. Each has two thresholds. High means capabilities that significantly increase existing risk vectors for severe harm; a model reaching High must have safeguards that sufficiently minimise the associated risk before deployment, plus appropriate security during development. Critical means a meaningful risk of a qualitatively new threat vector with no ready precedent; Critical requires safeguards even during development, regardless of deployment plans. "Severe harm" is defined as the death or grave injury of thousands of people or hundreds of billions of dollars of economic damage. For example, the Biological and Chemical High threshold is meaningful counterfactual assistance — relative to tools available in 2021 — to novices with basic technical backgrounds that enables them to create known biological or chemical threats; Critical includes enabling an expert to develop a highly dangerous novel threat vector, or completing the full engineering and synthesis cycle with tools and equipment without human intervention, and the listed response includes halting further development until Critical-standard safeguards are specified.

Research Categories. Areas that do not yet meet its criteria for tracking but need work: Long-range Autonomy, Sandbagging (the listed response is to adopt elicitation that overcomes it, or use a conservative upper bound), Autonomous Replication and Adaptation, Undermining Safeguards, and Nuclear and Radiological. Persuasion was dropped from tracked risks: OpenAI says it handles persuasion through its usage policies, Model Spec and provenance work, because those risks do not fit the framework's severe-harm criteria.

Process and governance. Capabilities Reports and Safeguards Reports document evaluations and protections — OpenAI notes this parallels Anthropic's then-current approach. The Safety Advisory Group, a cross-functional group of OpenAI leaders, oversees the framework and recommends safeguards; OpenAI leadership approves or rejects; the board's Safety and Security Committee provides oversight. In practice, OpenAI has treated recent models as High capability in the Biological and Chemical domain as a precaution, and its October 2026 deployment pages describe two new models as High in Biological and Chemical and in Cybersecurity, below Critical in cyber and below High in AI Self-improvement.

Competitors. A "marginal risk" section says that if another developer releases a system with High or Critical capability without comparable safeguards, and OpenAI can rigorously confirm it, OpenAI could adjust its required safeguards in that area — but only if it assesses that overall risk does not meaningfully increase, publicly acknowledges the adjustment, and keeps its safeguards more protective than the other developer's.

4. Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework, version 3.1

Versions. v1.0 (May 2024), v2.0 (February 2025), v3.0 (September 2025) and v3.1, published 17 April 2026.

Capability levels. The framework is built around Critical Capability Levels (CCLs): the minimal capabilities a model would need to cause severe harm along the main foreseeable paths. For misuse, CCLs cover CBRN, cyber and harmful manipulation — the last labelled "exploratory", because research on manipulation as a severe risk is nascent; the manipulation CCL concerns models able to systematically and substantially change beliefs and behaviour in identified high-stakes contexts. A second family covers machine-learning R&D and misalignment: an "ML R&D acceleration" level (AI progress substantially accelerating from historical rates) and an "ML R&D automation" level (able to fully automate the work of any team of researchers at Google focused on improving AI capabilities, at comparable cost). Version 3.1 added lower Tracked Capability Levels (TCLs) for significant but not severe risks, for CBRN and for ML R&D and misalignment — including a "stealth and situational awareness" TCL for when a model's instrumental reasoning could let it undermine human control absent extra mitigations, which triggers periodic misalignment risk assessments and safeguards such as chain-of-thought monitoring on high-risk internal deployments.

Detection. Early-warning evaluations run during development; alert thresholds flag when a CCL may be near; a critical capability assessment happens before first external deployment and when capability changes materially. Like Anthropic, DeepMind notes that for ML R&D it may use information about its own research acceleration, not only evaluations.

Mitigations and risk acceptance. When a misuse CCL is reached, external deployment needs mitigations that bring residual risk to an acceptable level — considering effectiveness, scope of deployment, the capabilities and mitigations of other public models (marginal risk) and the history of related events — and an "appropriate governance function" must accept the residual risk, with a safety case where a CCL is reached. Security is graded in security levels aligned with RAND's model-weight security framework: Security Level 2+ (RAND SL2 plus protections against insiders and well-resourced non-state actors) is recommended for the CBRN, cyber and manipulation CCLs; SL3 for ML R&D acceleration; SL4 for ML R&D automation, which the framework stresses must be adopted by the frontier field as a whole. If a CCL poses an unmitigated, material risk to public safety, DeepMind aims to share information with appropriate government authorities. The framework is reviewed at least annually.

6. Worked walkthrough: one model, three frameworks

Take a hypothetical model, "Model X", and these evaluation results (invented for the exercise):

  • A bioweapons-acquisition uplift trial shows 3.1× uplift for novices with basic biology training over internet-only controls; troubleshooting benchmarks exceed expert baselines; expert red-teamers report meaningful help on some acquisition steps but critical failures remain.
  • On an autonomous cyber suite, 55% pass@10 against moderately hardened targets, including chained exploits that took professionals hours.
  • Internal measurements suggest researchers using it are perhaps 20–30% more productive on engineering tasks; no evidence of a change in the overall rate of AI progress.
  • Alignment evaluations find occasional evaluation awareness and no evidence of covert sabotage in high-stakes internal settings.

Under OpenAI's framework, the biology result looks like meaningful counterfactual assistance to novices for known threats — High in Biological and Chemical — so deployment requires High-standard misuse safeguards and security, documented in a Safeguards Report reviewed by SAG. The cyber result may meet the High threshold of removing bottlenecks to scaling cyber operations; SAG must decide. AI Self-improvement is likely below High. Undermining-safeguards and sandbagging research categories would prompt robust safeguards reasoning given the High findings.

Under DeepMind's framework, the biology result likely reaches at least the CBRN TCL and plausibly the CBRN CCL ("uplift to low-to-medium-resourced actors… additional expected harm at severe scale"); the cyber result may reach the cyber CCL. Deployment requires mitigations, a safety case, residual-risk acceptance by the governance function and SL2+ security for the weights. The ML R&D numbers sit below the acceleration CCL, though alert thresholds might trigger closer monitoring; the evaluation-awareness findings bear on the stealth and situational awareness TCL.

Under Anthropic's RSP, the biology result sits at the non-novel chemical/biological threshold, so at least ASL-3-level protections are required, and the model's analysis appears in the next Risk Report or an off-cycle update if it is significantly more capable than previously analysed models. The automated-R&D threshold (substituting for the research staff at competitive cost, or doubling the rate of progress) is clearly not met by a 20–30% productivity gain — v3.1 explicitly says the threshold is about the rate of AI progress, not researcher productivity. Cyber has no separate row; its implications would be discussed in the Risk Report and in safeguards.

The exercise shows three things. The frameworks largely agree on what to do about the biology result — strong deployment safeguards and weight security. They differ on cyber and manipulation coverage. And every determination depends on judgment at the boundary, which is why elicitation and uncertainty (sf7) matter so much.

7. What they share, and where they are vague

Read side by side, the three frameworks agree on more than their different vocabularies suggest. All three treat biological and chemical weapons uplift to relatively unsophisticated actors as the nearest severe threshold, and all three have responded to it in practice with stronger deployment safeguards rather than refusing to deploy. All three track AI research automation as a special risk because it would speed up everything else. All three tie weight security to capability. All three say a model that cannot be shown to be below a threshold should be treated with caution. And all three reserve the right to revise.

They are also vague in similar places, and that is where interview questions go. "Meaningful counterfactual assistance", "significant uplift", "substantially accelerating" are judgments, not measurements; none of the frameworks says exactly which evaluation result crosses them, partly by design, because evaluations change faster than policies. The safeguards required at higher levels are described as outcomes ("robust", "sufficiently minimise risk", "a strong argument") rather than specifications, which makes compliance hard to verify from outside. And the step from "threshold reached" to "deploy anyway with safeguards" relies on a residual-risk judgment by the company. A precise critique names one of these gaps and proposes something checkable — a pre-registered evaluation protocol, a quantitative bar for a safeguard's red-team resistance, or an external sign-off — rather than saying the frameworks are "just PR".

Why "argument-based" frameworks replaced checklists

Early frameworks listed specific evaluations and controls; recent ones ask for a safety case or a "strong argument". Anthropic's v2.0 changelog already explained the move away from pre-specified tests ("predefined tests may miss emerging risks or be overly conservative") and specific methodologies such as a fixed compute fraction for elicitation; v3 extends this to safeguards for future levels. The advantage is flexibility as the science changes. The cost is auditability: a checklist can be verified, an argument must be judged, and the judge is initially the company. That is why argument-based frameworks pair with external review — the argument becomes something outsiders can contest.

The competitor problem, stated honestly

All three frameworks wrestle with the same uncomfortable fact: a safeguard one lab adopts alone may not reduce total risk if others ship equivalent capability without it, and the lab that holds back loses influence over how the technology develops. OpenAI's answer is a conditional adjustment clause with guardrails; Anthropic's is to separate unilateral plans from industry recommendations and push for regulation that applies to everyone; DeepMind's is to state that some mitigations only work if broadly applied. Critics argue this invites a race to the bottom. Defenders argue that pretending unilateral restraint is costless leads to frameworks that are quietly abandoned instead of openly adjusted. In an interview, the strong move is to describe the mechanism precisely and say what would make it trustworthy: public acknowledgement, independent verification of the competitor claim, and staying more protective.

Security levels and the weight-theft threat model

Deployment safeguards — classifiers, monitoring, usage policies — protect a model served through an API. They are irrelevant if the weights are stolen. That is why every framework ties capability levels to security levels. RAND's framework defines SL1 (amateur attackers) through SL5 (the most capable state operations), with concrete measures at each level. The jump from SL3 to SL4 is large: protecting against well-resourced state actors requires measures such as strict insider controls and hardened infrastructure that are expensive and slow research. That cost is why DeepMind recommends SL4 only for full ML R&D automation and says it must be adopted field-wide, and why Anthropic frames roughly SL4 as an industry recommendation for its higher thresholds.

Internal deployment as a risk surface

Early frameworks focused on external deployment. Recent versions treat internal use as a risk in its own right: a model used heavily inside a lab, with access to the lab's code and training infrastructure, could cause harm through sabotage or by accelerating capabilities, without ever being released. Anthropic's Risk Reports cover internally deployed models that could pose misalignment or automated-R&D risks; DeepMind's misalignment TCL triggers assessment of "high-risk internal deployments"; OpenAI's Critical threshold requires safeguards during development. This connects directly to AI control (sf10): monitoring and limiting what an internally deployed model can do.

What a framework cannot do

A framework can make a lab's decision process predictable and public. It cannot make evaluations accurate, settle the underlying disagreements about how likely severe risks are, or bind the lab against itself — every framework can be revised by the company that wrote it, and all three say they expect to revise. Its credibility therefore rests on track record: whether the lab acts on its thresholds when it is costly (as with precautionary ASL-3 activation), whether deviations are disclosed, and whether outside reviewers have real access. Regulation that requires publishing and following a framework (sf11) adds legal weight to that track record without dictating its content.

Frameworks and regulation

Frameworks began as voluntary commitments and are being pulled into law in a specific way: rather than writing thresholds into statute, regulators increasingly require developers to have a framework, publish it, follow it and report on it. California's SB 53 and the EU's general-purpose AI obligations with their Code of Practice (both in sf11) take this approach. Anthropic's RSP notes that where laws define terms such as catastrophic risk with specific thresholds, it handles those requirements in separate compliance frameworks. The effect is that a framework's content stays company-written, but deviating from it, or misrepresenting adherence, can carry legal consequences. The open question is whether this produces convergence on good practice or merely on well-written documents.

Where informed people disagree

Three disagreements recur, and a balanced answer can name each side. First, are voluntary frameworks meaningful at all? Sceptics point out that every framework can be rewritten by its author and that the strongest commitments have tended to become recommendations over time; supporters point to concrete costly actions taken under them, such as precautionary safeguards applied before a threshold was definitively crossed, and to the transparency they create. Second, should thresholds be precise or flexible? Precise thresholds are auditable but go stale as evaluations improve and invite gaming at the boundary; flexible, argument-based ones adapt but depend on trusting the arguer. Third, is unilateral restraint the right standard? One view holds that a responsible developer should keep risk low regardless of others; the other, reflected in RSP v3's reasoning, holds that unilateral pauses can hand the frontier to less careful actors, so the priority is industry-wide rules. These are value judgments as much as empirical ones, and an interviewer will be more interested in how you reason through them than in which side you pick.

The public case: Anthropic's RSP v3 rewrite (February 2026)

Version 3.0 is the clearest recent example of a framework changing its theory of itself. Its stated reason is a collective-action problem: the previous policy committed Anthropic to keep its own models' absolute risk acceptable regardless of others, but from society's perspective what matters is risk across the ecosystem, and one developer pausing while others continued without strong protections "could result in a world that is less safe". The rewrite answered with a split between company plans and industry-wide recommendations, competitor-contingent commitments, public Frontier Safety Roadmaps, Risk Reports with external review, and a move away from fixed control lists for future ASLs. Four point releases followed within five months, each logged publicly: clarifying the automated-R&D threshold (v3.1, v3.4), giving the Long-Term Benefit Trust power over external review (v3.2), revising the novel chem/bio threshold (v3.3), and adjusting internal sharing and redaction disclosure (v3.4). Commentators were divided: some saw a more honest and more transparent policy; others read the separation of recommendations from commitments as a weakening. Either way, it shows why you must name the version you are discussing.

How these frameworks show up in practice

The visible outputs are system cards and risk reports that state which thresholds a model was assessed against and the determination reached; announcements of precautionary safeguards (Anthropic's ASL-3 for Claude Opus 4; OpenAI treating recent models as High in biology); and, increasingly, legal compliance documents mapping frameworks onto requirements such as California's SB 53 and the EU's general-purpose AI Code of Practice (sf11). For engineers, frameworks show up as evaluation gates in release pipelines, required safeguard work (classifiers, monitoring, security controls) and documentation obligations.

Failure modes to watch for

Threshold drift. A threshold is redefined shortly before a model would cross it. Signal: revisions timed with launches, especially loosening ones. Mitigation: changelogs, external review, regulatory notice requirements.

Evaluation shopping. Choosing evaluations or elicitation that keep results below thresholds. Mitigation: pre-registered evaluation protocols and external evaluators.

Paper safeguards. Safeguards that satisfy a document but not an adversary. Mitigation: red-teaming the safeguards themselves (sf9) and bug bounties.

Governance capture. The body that decides is the body under commercial pressure. Mitigation: independent members, external review, whistleblower protections.

What working under a framework looks like for an engineer

Most engineers at a frontier lab never read the framework end to end, yet it shapes their work. Evaluation engineers build and run the capability suites whose results feed determinations, and their elicitation choices decide where a model lands (sf7). Safeguards engineers build the classifiers, monitoring and account-level controls that "ASL-3-level protections" or "High-standard safeguards" mean in practice (sf9), and are asked to show those protections hold against red-teams. Security engineers implement the weight-protection controls attached to security levels. Release engineers wire the gates into the pipeline so that a model cannot ship without the required sign-offs. And anyone can be the person who notices something that should have triggered the framework; noncompliance-reporting channels exist for exactly that. In an interview, connecting your own role to a specific part of the framework shows you understand it as an operating system for the company, not a press release.

A practical habit follows from all of this: whenever you cite a framework at work or in an interview, cite it with its version and date, and check the company's policy page first, because the version you remember may already have been replaced.

Goal: practise reading frameworks precisely by building a small comparison tool. Python 3.12 standard library; about 20 minutes, plus reading.

  1. Download the three current documents (links in references). For each, write a JSON record: name, version, date, risk domains, threshold names, what reaching each requires, decision body, transparency outputs. Quote, do not paraphrase, the threshold definitions you use.
  2. Write compare.py that loads the records and prints a side-by-side table like the one in the body, flagging any domain present in one framework and absent in another.
  3. Encode the "Model X" evaluation results from the body as a dictionary, and write a function per framework that returns a tentative determination with the reason, using only rules you can cite to a specific passage.
  4. Add a field uncertain: true wherever your rule needed judgment that the document does not settle, and print those cases separately.

What to look for. The table should reproduce the differences in domains (manipulation, cyber, autonomy). The determination functions will force you to notice how many rules need judgment — what counts as "meaningful counterfactual assistance" or "uplift at severe scale" — and the uncertain list will be long. That is the point: frameworks pre-commit responses, but deciding whether a threshold is met remains a judgment, which is why governance and external review matter. Re-check the versions on the day you do this; at least one is likely to have changed.

Policy-critique drill

An interviewer at a frontier lab says: "Our framework says that if a competitor ships a High-capability model without safeguards, we may relax our own. Some people call that a loophole. What do you think, and what would you change?"

Answer

Describe it accurately first. (Using OpenAI v2 as the example.) The clause applies only if the lab can rigorously confirm the competitor released a High or Critical system without comparable safeguards; the adjustment must not meaningfully increase overall risk; it must be publicly acknowledged; and the lab must remain more protective than the competitor and share information to show it.

Give the case for it. If a capability is already widely available without safeguards, keeping expensive safeguards on one product may reduce little risk while costing users and influence; a framework that pretends otherwise may be quietly ignored when it binds. An explicit, conditional clause is better than an implicit one.

Give the case against. It creates an incentive structure in which each lab's restraint depends on the least careful lab, which is how races to the bottom start. "Does not meaningfully increase overall risk" is judged by the lab itself, and "more protective than the competitor" can be a low bar.

Suggest changes. Require an independent party (an external reviewer or government evaluator) to confirm the competitor claim and the risk assessment; limit adjustments to deployment safeguards, never weight security, since exfiltrated weights of a stronger model add risk regardless; set a time limit and a public review after adjustment; and pair the clause with active support for regulation that applies to everyone, which removes the reason for it.

Tone. Engage with the strongest version of the policy, disagree specifically, and propose something concrete — that is what "disagreeing well" (sf12) looks like.
