The dangerous-capability evaluations in sf7 end with a determination: this model could meaningfully help someone with a harmful task, so it needs stronger protections before it ships. sf8 showed that frameworks then require safeguards — "ASL-3 protections", "High-standard safeguards", "mitigations that bring residual risk to an acceptable level". This lesson is about what those words mean in practice: the engineering that sits between a capable model and millions of users, some of whom are trying to misuse it.

The problem has two sides that pull against each other. On one side are attackers. Some type a forbidden question and give up when refused. Others spend days crafting jailbreaks — ciphers, role-play, splitting a dangerous procedure into innocent-looking pieces, or hiding instructions in documents — and share what works. A safeguard that stops the first group and not the second does little against the risks frameworks care about. On the other side are the overwhelming majority of users with legitimate needs: a chemistry student asking about reaction hazards, a nurse asking about overdose thresholds, a security engineer asking how an exploit works so they can patch it. A safeguard that blocks them is not "safe"; it fails people, pushes them to worse tools and undermines the claim that safety and usefulness are compatible.

In early 2025 Anthropic published a public account of navigating exactly this tension. Its first prototype of a new classifier system held up against more than 3,000 hours of expert red-teaming without anyone finding a universal jailbreak — but it refused about 44% of ordinary traffic. The version it shipped added only a fraction of a percentage point of refusals. When it opened a public challenge, hundreds of people tried for a week, and one eventually found a universal jailbreak. Every one of those numbers is part of the lesson.

This lesson builds the safeguard stack layer by layer: classifiers on inputs and outputs, including how constitutional classifiers are trained; usage policies and enforcement; account-level detection of campaigns and how to do it while respecting privacy; how to measure robustness honestly against attackers who adapt; and how to choose an operating point between attack success and over-refusal, worked with numbers. lm18 covered jailbreaks and prompt injection as attacks, and ev9 red-teaming as evaluation; here the focus is the defender's system and its trade-offs.

One habit to take from the whole lesson: whenever someone quotes a single safety number for a safeguard, ask for its partner — the over-refusal that came with an attack-success rate, the attacker effort behind a robustness claim, the cost behind a latency figure.

Think about how a bank protects against fraud. The teller is trained to spot obvious red flags — that is the model's own refusal training. Every transaction runs through automated rules and fraud-scoring models before it clears — those are input and output classifiers. Some transactions are only allowed for verified customers or need a second approval — access controls. Behind the scenes, an analytics team looks at patterns across accounts: twenty new accounts each moving just under the reporting limit to the same destination is invisible transaction by transaction but obvious in aggregate — account-level detection. When a new scam appears, the bank updates its rules within days — rapid response. And it publishes terms of service, closes accounts that break them and reports crime — usage policy and enforcement.

The bank also lives with the trade-off this lesson is about. Make the fraud model stricter and fewer frauds succeed, but more honest customers get their cards declined at the supermarket. Banks measure both, set thresholds by the cost of each error, and accept that some fraud gets through — because declining every unusual transaction would make the service unusable.

It helps to picture the two attackers each layer is meant for. The casual attacker types a forbidden request, perhaps with a well-known jailbreak copied from a forum, and moves on when refused; refusal training and a decent input classifier stop nearly all of them. The determined attacker iterates: tries encodings, splits the task into pieces, uses many accounts, and shares what works. Against them, single-request filters eventually leak, so the system needs layers that look at outputs whatever the prompt said, at patterns across requests and accounts, and at how fast leaks are closed. Frontier frameworks care mostly about the determined attacker, because the catastrophic harms they target need sustained effort. Most product teams, by contrast, mostly face casual misuse at very high volume, where over-refusal is the bigger day-to-day cost. The same toolbox serves both; the thresholds and the investment differ.

A last piece of vocabulary: safeguards are usually split into preventive controls, which stop a harmful exchange as it happens (refusals, classifiers, access restrictions), and detective controls, which find misuse after the fact and act on the actor (monitoring, investigation, enforcement). Preventive controls protect each request; detective controls protect the system over time. A stack with only preventive controls has no way to learn from what slips through.

Where does the bank analogy break? A bank sees money, which is easy to measure; an AI provider sees language, where the line between a legitimate and a harmful request is often a matter of context and degree. Fraudsters cannot usually test a bank's rules thousands of times for free; jailbreakers can iterate cheaply and share techniques publicly. And a bank's harms are financial and reversible; the harms frontier safeguards target can be catastrophic and irreversible, which is why the trade-off is struck differently for different categories of content.

1. Why the model's own refusals are not enough

Refusal training — teaching the model to decline harmful requests (sf2) — is the first layer and handles most casual misuse. It is weak against determined attackers for structural reasons. It is applied to the same model that is being attacked, so any jailbreak that shifts the model into a different "mode" (a role-play persona, an encoded conversation, a long context full of examples) can bypass it. It cannot be updated quickly: retraining a frontier model to close a new jailbreak takes weeks. And it trades off directly against helpfulness inside one set of weights. In Anthropic's automated evaluation of advanced jailbreak attempts, an unguarded Claude blocked only 14% — 86% succeeded. Separate classifiers address all three problems: they are different models, they can be retrained in days, and their thresholds can be tuned independently of the model's general behaviour.

2. Input and output classifiers

An input classifier scores the prompt (often the whole conversation so far) before the model answers. It is cheap and can stop a request before any harmful text exists, but it only sees what the user wrote, so attacks that look harmless going in — a cipher, a request for "fiction" — can pass. An output classifier scores what the model writes. It sees the actual harmful content if the model produces it, whatever the disguise of the prompt, so it catches many jailbreaks the input classifier misses. Its weakness is latency: waiting for a full response before checking it would delay every answer. The fix is streaming classification: score the response so far at each token and halt generation the moment it crosses the threshold, keeping time-to-first-token low. Anthropic's constitutional classifier paper emphasises streaming for exactly this reason, and reports that this design, combined with an input classifier, makes the two layers complementary — the "Swiss cheese" model from sf1.

General-purpose safeguard classifiers are also available as open models. Meta's Llama Guard (2023) is a language model fine-tuned to classify both prompts and responses against a taxonomy of harm categories, with the taxonomy given in its prompt so it can be adapted. Such classifiers are a reasonable starting point for product teams; frontier labs train domain-specific classifiers for the highest-risk categories, where general classifiers are not robust enough against dedicated attackers.

3. Constitutional classifiers: training safeguards from a written policy

Anthropic's constitutional classifiers (Sharma et al., January 2025) apply the constitution idea from sf2 to safeguards. The key components:

  • A constitution for the domain — for chemical weapons, say — that lists categories of restricted content (synthesis routes for specific agents, acquisition of precursors) and, just as importantly, permitted content (general chemistry education, safety information, history). Writing the permitted side explicitly is what keeps over-refusal down.
  • Synthetic data generation. A model generates many harmful and harmless prompts and responses guided by the constitution, then applies data augmentation: translation into other languages, encodings and ciphers, paraphrases, and known jailbreak styles, so the classifier learns to recognise content however it is disguised. Pools of benign data teach what not to block.
  • Classifiers fine-tuned from Claude models: an input classifier and a streaming output classifier.
  • Adaptability. When a new threat or attack style appears, update the constitution or the augmentations and regenerate data, rather than collecting real examples.

The results are worth knowing in detail, because they include both the success and its cost. A prototype guarding Claude 3.5 Sonnet was put through a bug bounty on HackerOne: 405 invited participants, rewards up to $15,000, over 3,000 estimated hours of red-teaming, with the task of getting answers to ten harmful chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear questions. No report answered all ten at half the detail of an unrestricted model, and under stricter criteria no one answered more than six. But that prototype prioritised robustness so heavily that it refused about 44% of Claude.ai traffic. Improved classifiers with a new chemical-weapons constitution were then evaluated automatically: jailbreak success fell from 86% without classifiers to 4.4% with them — over 95% of attempts refused — with an absolute increase of 0.38 percentage points in refusals on production Claude.ai traffic and 23.7% extra inference compute.

Worked example: from constitution to classifier for a cyber domain

Suppose a safeguards team wants a classifier against serious offensive-cyber misuse for an agentic coding product. The constitution might list as restricted: writing functional malware intended to evade detection; developing exploits against systems the user does not own or administer; automating credential theft or lateral movement against third parties; and operational guidance for ransomware deployment and extortion. It would list as permitted: explaining how classes of vulnerabilities work; writing proof-of-concept code against the user's own deliberately vulnerable lab environment; defensive tooling such as detection rules and log analysis; capture-the-flag practice; and security course material. The permitted list is what separates a usable product from one that refuses every security question.

From that document, a model generates thousands of example requests and responses in each category, from blatant to subtle, then augments them: translate into twenty languages, wrap in role-play and fiction framings, encode in base64 and simple ciphers, split across turns, and embed in tool outputs. Benign examples get the same treatment, so the classifier learns that a base64 string in a security exercise is not automatically an attack. The classifiers are trained, thresholds are tuned per category on held-out adversarial data and on a labelled sample of real traffic, and the result is red-teamed before launch. When a new attack style appears after launch, the team adds it to the augmentations, regenerates, and retrains — a cycle measured in days.

Multi-turn and long-context attacks

Many of the hardest attacks do not live in a single message. In a decomposition attack, the user asks for each step of a harmful procedure in separate, innocent-looking requests, sometimes across separate conversations, and assembles the answer themselves; each request scores low on its own. In a many-shot attack, the user fills a long context window with many fabricated examples of an assistant complying with harmful requests, so the pattern overwhelms the model's training; Anthropic described this in 2024 as a risk that grows with context length. In escalation, a conversation starts benign and drifts step by step. The defences follow from the structure: classifiers that see the whole conversation rather than the last message; output classifiers, which judge what is actually produced however the request was built; limits and scrutiny on very long, example-heavy contexts; and account-level detection, which is often the only layer that sees a decomposition spread over many sessions.

4. Testing it in public: what the challenge revealed

In February 2025 Anthropic ran a public demo of the system for seven days, with eight chemical-weapons questions as levels and prizes for passing all eight. The update published on 13 February reported 339 participants, more than 300,000 chat interactions and roughly 3,700 collective hours. The system resisted for five days: until then, nobody passed all eight levels. On days six and seven, four participants cleared all levels; one used what Anthropic judged to be a universal jailbreak, another a borderline-universal one that needed parts of the answer fed in, and two obtained detailed answers to all eight with jailbreaks that did not meet the universality criteria. The most successful strategies were using ciphers and encodings to evade the output classifier, role-play scenarios often set up through system prompts, substituting harmful keywords with innocuous alternatives (for example, replacing the name of a nerve agent with "water"), and prompt-injection attacks.

Anthropic also listed two ways the demo was easier than reality. An automated grader told participants immediately whether an answer was detailed enough, a tight feedback loop a real attacker would not have — they would have to try to follow the instructions to find out. And in real deployment, rapid response would let the provider spot users repeatedly triggering classifiers and patch vulnerabilities, which the demo did not do. The honest summary is the one the post gives: the classifiers may not prevent every universal jailbreak, but they make finding one require far more effort — and robustness is always relative to effort, feedback and response time.

5. The trade-off, worked: attack success against over-refusal

Every classifier produces a score, and the threshold decides the trade-off. Here is a worked example with numbers chosen to be realistic in shape. Suppose a classifier's scores for three kinds of traffic follow these distributions (normal, standard deviation 1): attack attempts from a static jailbreak set centred at 3.0; clearly benign traffic, 95% of benign volume, centred at −2.0; and borderline benign traffic — chemistry homework, safety questions, fiction — 5% of benign volume, centred at 0.5. Requests scoring above the threshold are blocked.

The "adaptive attacker" column models attackers who iterate until their prompts score lower — centred at 1.5 instead of 3.0. Three things stand out. First, the aggregate over-refusal number looks tiny at every threshold (under 2%), but borderline legitimate users bear almost all of it: at threshold 1.0, nearly a third of them are refused. Aggregate refusal rates hide who is harmed. Second, a threshold that looks excellent on a static jailbreak set (2.3% success at 1.0) lets nearly a third of adaptive attacks through; static benchmarks overstate robustness. Third, no threshold makes both columns small — the only way to improve both is a better classifier (more separation between harmful and borderline-benign scores) or additional layers that use different information, such as account history.

How do you choose? Put costs on the errors. For catastrophic categories — synthesis routes for chemical weapons — a missed attack is so costly that a high borderline refusal rate may be acceptable, provided legitimate experts have another route (vetted access with exemptions). For lower-severity categories — profanity, mild violence in fiction — the balance tips the other way. That is why mature systems use different thresholds and even different classifiers per category, rather than one global setting.

The cost side: latency and compute

Safeguards are not free to run. Anthropic reported a 23.7% inference overhead for its improved constitutional classifiers. At scale that is real money: a service spending $10 million a month on inference would spend roughly $2.4 million more to run them, unless the classifiers are made smaller or run less often. Latency matters too: an input classifier adds its own processing time before the first token, and an output classifier that scores every token adds work on every step of generation. Engineering choices that reduce cost — smaller classifier models, scoring every few tokens instead of every token, running the heaviest classifiers only for high-risk categories or accounts — each trade some robustness, so they should be decided with the same measured approach as thresholds: estimate the robustness lost for each option on adversarial data, and choose knowingly.

Access tiers and trusted users

One way out of the over-refusal trade-off is not to give everyone the same system. Providers can offer different access to different users: verified researchers, biosecurity organisations or security firms can receive exemptions from some classifier categories under contractual terms and monitoring, while anonymous consumer access keeps the strictest settings. Anthropic's RSP v3 plan for the non-novel chemical and biological threshold explicitly mentions access controls for trusted users with exemptions to classifier guards. Tiering moves the decision from "is this request harmful?" — which is often unanswerable from the text alone — to "is this person, in this context, allowed to do this?", which identity, contracts and audit can support. The cost is friction and the risk that verified accounts are compromised or misused, so exempted access still needs logging and review.

6. Usage policies and enforcement

A usage policy is the published rulebook: what users may not do with the model (develop weapons, create malware, generate child sexual abuse material, run influence operations, and so on), and often which high-risk uses need extra safeguards such as human review or disclosure. It matters for three reasons: it tells users and developers the rules; it gives the provider a basis for action; and it is the reference that classifier constitutions and reviewer guidelines are written against.

Enforcement connects signals to consequences. A single classifier hit might just block a response. Repeated hits, or a confirmed violation, escalate: a warning, rate limits, suspension of specific capabilities, account bans, and in serious cases reports to authorities. Enforcement needs its own quality control — appeals, human review of high-impact actions, and measurement of wrongful enforcement — because false positives here cost a customer their access, not just one answer.

7. Account-level detection and threat intelligence

Per-request filters cannot see campaigns. An actor who asks one question about network scanning, another about credential formats and a third about writing a ransom note may never trigger a single-request classifier, but the sequence is unmistakable. Account-level detection aggregates signals over time and across accounts: rates of classifier hits, sequences of topics, tool usage patterns in agentic products, many new accounts with similar behaviour, payment or identity anomalies. Threat intelligence teams investigate what detection surfaces, attribute activity to actors where possible, and turn findings into new classifiers and detection rules.

Anthropic's August 2025 threat intelligence report gives concrete examples. It describes disrupting a cybercriminal who used Claude Code to automate reconnaissance, harvest credentials and penetrate networks in a data-extortion operation against at least 17 organisations, including healthcare providers, emergency services and government and religious institutions, with the model making tactical and strategic decisions such as which data to take; Anthropic banned the accounts and developed a tailored classifier and a new detection method. The same report covers North Korean operatives using Claude to obtain and keep fraudulent remote jobs at technology companies, and a criminal with basic coding skills selling AI-generated ransomware. These cases illustrate why agentic products raise the stakes: the model is not just answering questions but taking actions, so misuse detection must watch actions too.

8. Monitoring at scale with privacy

Account-level detection needs visibility into usage, which collides with user privacy. Anthropic's Clio (December 2024) is an example of a privacy-preserving approach: models, not humans, summarise and cluster conversations into aggregate patterns, with privacy protections evaluated explicitly, so analysts see topics and trends rather than individual conversations. The paper reports using it on a million Claude.ai conversations to understand usage, and for safety: identifying coordinated abuse attempts and monitoring for "unknown unknowns" during critical periods such as launches of new capabilities or major world events. The general pattern — automated aggregation first, human review only of flagged cases under policy, strict access controls and retention limits — is how safety monitoring and privacy obligations (ae5) coexist.

9. Measuring robustness honestly

Pulling the measurement lessons together: evaluate the whole system, not the classifier alone; use adaptive attackers — human red-teamers with time, feedback and incentives, plus automated attack generation — not only static jailbreak lists; test specifically for universal jailbreaks, since they are what enable non-experts; report attack success per attempt and per goal with confidence intervals (ev9); and measure over-refusal on real benign traffic, broken down by category, plus benchmarks of safe-but-scary prompts such as XSTest and OR-Bench. Then measure the operational loop: how long from a jailbreak being discovered to it being blocked (rapid-response time), and what fraction of campaigns account-level detection catches.

Why output classifiers are harder to fool than input classifiers — and their blind spot

An input classifier must infer intent from the user's text, which attackers control completely: they can phrase, encode and wrap a request however they like. An output classifier judges the model's actual response, which the attacker controls only indirectly, so plain harmful content is hard to disguise. That is why output classifiers stopped many jailbreaks in Anthropic's experiments. Their blind spot is the one the public challenge exploited: if the attacker gets the model to write its response in a cipher, or to replace key terms with innocuous words, the output looks harmless to a classifier that does not decode it. Defences include training classifiers on encoded and substituted outputs, asking the model to keep outputs in natural language, and checking for unusual encodings as a signal in themselves.

Rapid response as a design principle

If perfect robustness is unattainable, the next best property is fast repair. Rapid response treats every discovered jailbreak as training data: collect a handful of examples, generate variations automatically, retrain or adjust the classifier, and deploy within hours or days. Its effectiveness depends on detection (you must notice the jailbreak), on generalisation (a few examples must cover the family), and on attacker economics (if each new jailbreak takes days to find and minutes to patch, the defender wins on average). This is why the public challenge's lack of rapid response made it easier than reality, and why providers invest in monitoring that surfaces new attack patterns quickly.

Safeguards for agents

Agentic products change what safeguards must watch. The harmful thing may be an action — running a scan, sending data, editing a file — rather than text, and a sequence of individually reasonable actions can amount to an attack, as in the extortion case. Safeguards for agents therefore combine classifiers on text with policy on tools (which tools, against which targets, with which confirmations), monitoring of action sequences, and the security controls from ag10. The lines between safety safeguards and security controls blur here, which is one reason safeguards teams increasingly work closely with security teams.

Who bears the cost of over-refusal

The worked example showed aggregate over-refusal hiding concentrated harm: under 2% overall, a third of borderline users at a strict threshold. Borderline users are disproportionately professionals and learners in exactly the fields that matter — chemistry, medicine, security, social work — and people in difficult situations asking difficult questions. Anthropic's constitution states that unhelpful responses should never be seen as automatically safe, citing direct costs to users and indirect costs to the case that safety and helpfulness are compatible. A mature programme reports over-refusal by category and population, offers verified-access routes for legitimate experts, and reviews refused samples to find classifier errors.

Where safeguards fit in the overall argument

Safeguards supply the "control" part of a safety case (sf1): even if the model could help with a harmful task, the system prevents it. The argument is only as strong as the evidence that the safeguards hold against the relevant attackers, which is why frameworks pair "required safeguards" with requirements to red-team them. It also has a hard limit: it assumes the provider controls how the model is run. For open-weight models that assumption fails, so frameworks fall back on capability: if a model's capability would be dangerous without safeguards, releasing its weights removes the safeguards by definition.

Static benchmarks and the evaluation trap

ev9 warned that static attack-success numbers overstate robustness; for safeguards the effect is especially strong because the classifier is often trained on attacks similar to those in the evaluation set. A classifier can score near-perfect on a fixed jailbreak benchmark and still fall to a new encoding in an afternoon. Three practices reduce the trap: hold out entire attack families (not just examples) from classifier training; refresh adversarial sets continuously from red-teaming and real incidents; and report a time-to-break number from incentivised red-teaming — "how long did it take skilled people with feedback to find a universal jailbreak?" — alongside static rates. The constitutional-classifier public challenge is useful precisely because it produced that kind of number: five days with hundreds of participants and an automated grader giving instant feedback.

Safeguards when you build on someone else's model

Most companies do not train frontier models; they build products on a provider's API or on an open-weight model. The division of responsibility matters. With an API, the provider runs its own classifiers and usage policy, but those are tuned for its general population of users, not for your product's risks or your users' legitimate needs; you still need your own input and output checks for your domain, your own abuse monitoring at the level of your users' accounts (the provider sees your organisation, not your individual users), and a way to pass end-user identifiers or context so the provider's enforcement does not punish all your users for one bad actor. With an open-weight model, you are the provider: no classifiers, monitoring or enforcement exist unless you build them, and the model's refusals may be weaker than you assume. In both cases, write down which layer is whose, and test the combined stack rather than assuming someone else's safeguards cover your use case.

The public case: constitutional classifiers and the February 2025 challenge

Anthropic's constitutional classifiers are the most fully documented public example of frontier safeguards, because the company published the method, the internal red-team results, the cost, and the result of opening the system to public attack. The arc is the lesson in miniature. A robustness-maximising prototype survived 3,000+ hours of incentivised expert red-teaming but refused about 44% of traffic. Re-engineered classifiers kept most of the robustness on automated evaluations — jailbreak success 86% to 4.4% — at a cost of 0.38 percentage points of refusals and 23.7% compute. A seven-day public challenge with 339 participants and roughly 3,700 hours held for five days, then yielded one universal jailbreak and one borderline one, through ciphers, role-play, keyword substitution and injection. And the company was explicit about the ways the challenge was easier than real attacks and what real deployment adds (rapid response, monitoring). In May 2025, classifiers of this kind became part of the ASL-3 protections applied to Claude Opus 4 (sf7), and RSP v3 names classifier guards "at least as robust as our initial Constitutional Classifiers" as part of its plan for the non-novel chemical and biological weapons threshold (sf8).

Failure modes

Encoding gaps. Classifiers trained on plain text miss ciphered or substituted outputs. Signal: successful attacks share unusual formats. Fix: augmentation with encodings, format checks.

Threshold set on the wrong data. Tuned on curated benign prompts, the classifier over-refuses real users in specific professions. Signal: complaints clustered by domain. Fix: tune on labelled samples of real traffic by category.

Monitoring without action. Detection surfaces campaigns but enforcement is slow or manual. Signal: repeat offenders, long time-to-ban. Fix: playbooks, automation for clear cases, metrics on response time.

Safeguard drift after model updates. A new model version writes differently, and the output classifier's score distribution shifts. Fix: re-validate safeguards for every model change, as part of release.

What to measure in production

Classifier trigger rates by category and over time; attack success on a regularly refreshed adversarial set; over-refusal on a labelled sample of real traffic by category; time from jailbreak discovery to mitigation; number and severity of campaigns detected and actioned; wrongful-enforcement rate from appeals; and compute and latency overhead of the safeguard stack. Review them together, because improving any one in isolation is easy and misleading.

Finally, write it down. A short safeguards report per launch — what each layer is, its measured attack success and over-refusal by category, its cost, the red-team effort it withstood, and the rapid-response time — makes the safeguards part of a framework's evidence (sf8) and gives the next team a baseline to beat.

A worked launch gate

To make this concrete, here is the kind of gate a safeguards team might agree before a launch in a high-risk category. On a refreshed adversarial set with held-out attack families, attack success must be at most 5% per attempt at the chosen threshold. Over-refusal must be at most 0.5% on a labelled sample of 20,000 real requests overall and at most 5% on the borderline subset for that category. An incentivised red-team of at least 200 hours must not find a universal jailbreak. Rapid response must be shown to ship a classifier update within 72 hours of a new jailbreak family. Account-level detection must catch at least half of the campaigns in a replay of past incidents. If any gate fails, the options are explicit: improve the classifier, add a layer, narrow access (tiers), or delay. The numbers are examples; the point is that they are written before the results arrive, exactly like framework thresholds.

Goal: build an over-refusal versus attack-success analysis for a classifier, the way a safeguards team would. Python 3.12 standard library; about 15 minutes.

  1. Simulate classifier scores: 2,000 attack attempts from N(3.0, 1), 2,000 adaptive attacks from N(1.5, 1), 95,000 clearly benign requests from N(−2.0, 1) and 5,000 borderline benign requests from N(0.5, 1), using random.gauss with a fixed seed.
  2. For thresholds from 0.5 to 3.5 in steps of 0.25, compute attack success (static and adaptive), overall over-refusal and borderline over-refusal. Print a table and check it against the body's analytic numbers.
  3. Assign costs: a missed attack costs 1,000 units; refusing a borderline benign request costs 5; refusing a clearly benign request costs 1. Assume 1 attack per 1,000 requests. Find the threshold that minimises total expected cost, separately for static and adaptive attackers.
  4. Add a second, independent layer — account monitoring that catches 60% of adaptive attackers who get past the classifier — and recompute the best threshold.

What to look for. The simulated table should match the analytic one within sampling noise. The cost-optimal threshold should be stricter against adaptive attackers than against static ones, and adding the account-monitoring layer should let you relax the threshold and cut borderline over-refusal at similar overall risk — a numeric version of why layers with different signals beat one very strict filter. Change the cost of a miss from 1,000 to 100,000 (a catastrophic category) and watch the optimum move.

Record your chosen thresholds and costs in a short note, as a safeguards team would before launch.

Incident drill

Two weeks after launching an agentic coding product, your monitoring shows: (a) a 3× rise in output-classifier triggers in the cyber category, concentrated in about 40 accounts created in the same week; (b) a viral post describing a role-play prompt that gets the model to explain how to write credential-stealing malware; (c) complaints from a university security course that students are being refused for ordinary exercises. What do you do in the next 48 hours and the next month?

Answer

Next 48 hours. (a) Treat the account cluster as a likely campaign: review a sample of their transcripts and tool actions under policy, rate-limit or suspend the cluster pending review, and look for linked accounts by payment, IP and behaviour; if confirmed, ban and preserve evidence, and consider reporting if there are victims. (b) Reproduce the viral jailbreak, collect variants, add them to the classifier training data and ship an updated classifier (rapid response); meanwhile tighten the cyber-category output threshold temporarily and monitor its over-refusal impact. (c) Pull the refused samples from the university's accounts, confirm they are benign, and offer a verified-education access route or an allow-list while the classifier is fixed.

Next month. Measure attack success on a refreshed adversarial set including role-play variants, and over-refusal on labelled security-education traffic; retune per-category thresholds with those numbers rather than one global setting. Add account-level features (new-account clusters, tool-use sequences) to detection. Write a post-incident review: time to detect, time to mitigate, wrongful refusals caused by the emergency threshold change. Feed the role-play family and the education examples into the next classifier version, so both the miss and the over-refusal are corrected at the source.

The principle. Treat (b) and (c) as two sides of one classifier problem — a miss and a false positive — and (a) as a different layer's job. A good response improves both error types instead of trading one for the other in a panic.
