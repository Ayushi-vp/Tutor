A customer checks out. The checkout code calls the inventory service to reserve the item, then calls the payment service to charge the card, then saves the order in its own database. It works almost every time. But once in a while — a deploy restarts a server mid-request, the payment service times out, the database has a hiccup — something fails halfway. Over a month, support finds dozens of customers who were charged but have no order, and a warehouse full of stock "reserved" for orders that don't exist. An engineer adds a try/except that releases the stock if payment fails; the problems shrink but don't disappear, because the process can crash before the except block runs, and a timeout doesn't say whether the charge went through.

Inside one database, this is a solved problem: wrap the steps in a transaction and they commit together or not at all (sc5). But the order, the stock and the payment now live in three services with three databases, and the payment actually happens at an external card processor that will never join your transaction. As systems are split into services (sc14) and databases into shards (sc7), more and more business operations span several independent systems, and the database's atomicity no longer covers them.

This topic is about getting correctness back without that single transaction. You'll see why the obvious approach, the dual write, is fundamentally broken. You'll learn two-phase commit, the classic protocol for atomic commit across databases, how it works message by message, why it blocks and where it's still used. You'll learn the alternative that most service architectures use, the saga — a sequence of local transactions with compensating actions — and how to order its steps, coordinate it and live with its lack of isolation. And you'll learn the supporting patterns that make any of this work in practice: the transactional outbox and inbox, idempotency keys, durable workflow engines, and the reconciliation jobs that every system handling money quietly depends on.

These are some of the most frequently asked system-design topics for senior roles, because they separate designs that work in a demo from designs that work when servers crash at the worst moment.

The analogy: booking a holiday through separate companies

You're booking a trip: a flight with an airline, a hotel with a hotel chain, and a car with a rental company. You want all three or none — a flight with no hotel is useless. But the three companies don't share a booking system, and there's no single "confirm everything" button.

One approach is a travel agent who holds everything provisionally. The agent asks each company to hold a booking and promise to confirm it if asked. Only when all three have promised does the agent say "confirm" to each. If any can't hold, the agent tells the others to release. That's two-phase commit: first everyone promises (prepare), then everyone confirms (commit). The catch: while the agent is deciding, the seats and rooms are held and nobody else can book them — and if the agent goes silent after collecting the promises, the companies are stuck holding bookings they can neither confirm nor release.

The other approach is to book each for real, one by one, and cancel if something goes wrong. Book the flight. Book the hotel. Try the car; if no car is available, cancel the hotel and cancel the flight — accepting any cancellation fees. That's a saga: each booking is a real, committed step, and each has a compensating action (a cancellation) to undo it. Nobody is blocked waiting, but for a while you hold a real flight and hotel for a trip that might not happen, and some things — a non-refundable ticket — can't be fully undone.

The analogy breaks in one important way: humans notice when the agent disappears or a phone line drops. In software, a timeout gives no information about whether the other side acted, so every step must be safe to retry and every decision must be written down somewhere durable.

The three building blocks

Make every step safe to repeat. Because failures and timeouts are ambiguous, every step will sometimes be retried. An idempotency key — a unique ID for the operation, sent with each request — lets the receiver recognise a repeat and return the original result instead of charging twice.

Record progress durably. Whether it's a 2PC coordinator's decision, a saga's current step or an event to publish, the record must survive a crash, so the process can resume instead of forgetting. The transactional outbox is the basic tool: write the "what happens next" message in the same local transaction as the change that caused it.

Choose a coordination protocol. Two-phase commit when participants are databases that support it and atomicity must be real; a saga with compensations when steps span independent services and external APIs. In practice most systems use all three together: sagas for the business flow, the outbox to hand work between services, and idempotency keys on every call so that the retries all of this depends on are harmless. The rest of the lesson takes them one at a time.

1. Why one business operation now spans many systems

In a monolith with one database, "place an order" is one transaction: decrement stock, insert the order, record the payment, commit. Three forces break that. Services: when orders, inventory and payments become separate services, each owning its own database (sc14), no single database sees all three changes. Shards: when data is partitioned (sc7), a transfer between two customers on different shards touches two databases. External systems: the card processor, the email provider, the shipping carrier are someone else's systems, reachable only by API. Each of these turns one atomic transaction into several independent commits, and the job is to make the whole behave correctly anyway.

"Correctly" here usually means: either every step's effect happens, or the net effect is as if none did (possibly after some visible, temporary intermediate state); no step's effect happens twice; and every stuck operation is eventually resolved or surfaced to a human. Full ACID isolation across services is rarely achievable, so the goal is atomicity and eventual consistency, with the intermediate states handled deliberately.

2. The dual-write problem

The simplest code writes to one system, then the other:

db.save(order)              # commit in the orders database
broker.publish(OrderPlaced) # tell everyone else

If the process dies between the two lines — a crash, an out-of-memory kill, a deploy, a network error on publish — the order is saved and nobody is told. Reverse the order and a failure leaves an event announcing an order that was never saved. Adding a retry loop doesn't help if the process itself dies. Adding a try/except that "undoes" the first write doesn't help either, because the undo can fail, and because some failures — a timeout — don't tell you whether the second write happened.

The root cause is that two independent systems have no shared commit point. Every correct solution creates one: two-phase commit creates a coordinated commit across participants; the outbox moves the second write into the first system's transaction; a saga records its progress durably and treats each step as its own commit with an explicit undo. At scale, "rare" failures happen daily, so dual writes are a bug even if they've never visibly failed.

3. Two-phase commit, message by message

Two-phase commit (2PC) makes a transaction commit on all participants or none. There's a coordinator (often the database or transaction manager that started the transaction) and several participants (the databases or resource managers holding the data).

Phase 1 — prepare (voting). The coordinator sends PREPARE to every participant. Each participant does all the work of the transaction, checks it can commit (constraints satisfied, no conflicts), writes the changes and a "prepared" record durably to its log, keeps its locks, and replies yes. From that moment it has made a promise: it can no longer abort on its own, because the coordinator might decide to commit. If it can't commit, it replies no and aborts locally.

Phase 2 — commit (decision). If every participant voted yes, the coordinator writes "commit" durably to its own log — this is the point of no return — and sends COMMIT to all. Each participant commits, releases its locks and acknowledges. If any participant voted no, or didn't answer in time, the coordinator writes "abort" and sends ABORT; participants roll back. If a participant crashes after voting yes, it finds its "prepared" record on restart and asks the coordinator what was decided.

Why it blocks. Consider the window after participants have voted yes but before they receive the decision. If the coordinator crashes there, a participant is in doubt: it promised to commit if told, so it can't abort unilaterally (the coordinator may have logged "commit" and told the other participant), and it can't commit unilaterally (the coordinator may have decided to abort). It must wait, holding its locks, until the coordinator recovers. Those locks block every other transaction that touches the same rows. That's the famous blocking problem of 2PC.

Its other costs. Two round trips of messages plus several durable log writes per transaction add latency. Locks are held across those round trips, which lowers concurrency on hot rows. And every participant must implement the protocol. The standard interface for that is XA, supported by many relational databases and some message brokers, but not by most NoSQL stores, and not by any external HTTP API. A card processor won't hold a "prepared" charge on your behalf.

Where 2PC works well. Inside distributed databases. Google Spanner, CockroachDB, YugabyteDB and TiDB use two-phase commit for transactions spanning shards, but each participant — and the coordinator's record — is a consensus-replicated group (sc9). If the machine running the coordinator fails, another replica of its group already knows the decision and finishes the protocol, so the blocking problem mostly disappears. You get atomic, serializable cross-shard transactions, paying extra latency for each additional shard touched. Three-phase commit, sometimes proposed as a fix, adds a phase to avoid blocking only under crash failures with bounded message delays; under network partitions it can reach inconsistent decisions, so real systems use consensus instead.

4. Sagas: local transactions plus compensation

When steps span independent services and external APIs, the standard pattern is the saga, first described by Hector Garcia-Molina and Kenneth Salem in 1987 for long-running database transactions. A saga is a sequence of local transactions T1, T2, … Tn, each committed in one service. For each step there's a compensating action C1, C2, … that semantically undoes it. If all steps succeed, the saga is done. If step Tk fails, the saga runs Ck−1, …, C1 in reverse order, and the net effect is as if the operation had never happened.

Checkout as a saga:

StepServiceActionCompensation
T1OrdersCreate order in state PENDINGMark order CANCELLED
T2InventoryReserve 1 unit (with an expiry)Release the reservation
T3PaymentsCharge the card (idempotency key = order ID)Refund the charge
T4OrdersMark order CONFIRMED— (retriable, see pivot)
T5NotificationsSend confirmation email— (can't be undone; goes last)

A failure traced: T1 and T2 succeed. T3 fails because the card is declined. The saga runs C2 (release the stock) and C1 (cancel the order), and the user sees "payment declined". Nobody was charged, no stock is stuck, and the order record shows exactly what happened.

Compensation is not rollback. T2 really committed, and for a while other customers saw one less unit in stock. A refund is a new, visible financial transaction, not the erasure of a charge. And compensations are business logic: releasing a reservation might fail if the reservation expired and was already released, so compensations must be idempotent and tolerant of that. Some effects can't be compensated at all — an email already read, a package already shipped — which drives the ordering rules in the next section.

5. Ordering steps: compensatable, pivot, retriable

Steps fall into three kinds, and a saga's reliability depends on putting them in the right order.

Compensatable steps can be undone by a compensating action: creating a pending order, reserving stock, placing a hold on funds.

The pivot is the step that decides the outcome — the go/no-go point. Before it, a failure means compensate and stop. After it succeeds, the saga must complete: there's no going back.

Retriable steps come after the pivot and must be designed to eventually succeed if retried: marking the order confirmed, sending the email, notifying the warehouse. They must be idempotent, and the saga retries them (with backoff) until they succeed or a human intervenes.

The rule is compensatable steps → pivot → retriable steps. In checkout, capturing the payment is usually the pivot: until it succeeds, everything can be undone; once money is taken, the order will be fulfilled (or explicitly refunded as a business decision, not an automatic compensation). The classic bug is putting an uncompensatable step before the pivot: sending "your order is confirmed!" before the payment clears, then having to send "sorry, actually it isn't". A related technique is to put the step most likely to fail as early as possible, so fewer steps need compensating when it does.

6. Coordinating a saga: choreography versus orchestration

Something must drive the saga from step to step, and there are two styles (sc10 introduced them for events generally).

Choreography: each service reacts to events and emits its own. Orders creates the order and publishes OrderCreated; Inventory reacts, reserves stock and publishes StockReserved; Payments reacts and publishes PaymentCaptured or PaymentFailed; Inventory reacts to PaymentFailed by releasing stock; Orders reacts to the final events. No component knows the whole flow. It's loosely coupled and simple for two or three steps. As flows grow, the logic becomes scattered across services and topics: nobody can easily answer "which step is order 8812 on?", changing the order of steps means coordinated changes in several services, and it's easy to create cycles or forget a compensation path.

Orchestration: a single orchestrator — a state machine owned by one service, or a workflow in a dedicated engine — explicitly executes the steps: call Inventory to reserve; on success call Payments; on failure call Inventory to release; and so on. It stores the saga's state durably after each step, so if it crashes it resumes where it was. The flow is in one place, easy to read, test, monitor ("show me all sagas stuck on payment for more than 10 minutes") and change. The costs are a central component and some coupling of the orchestrator to each service's API.

In practice, orchestration is preferred for business-critical, multi-step flows with compensation, and choreography for loose reactions where no one needs to track completion (analytics, notifications). Orchestrators are increasingly built on durable execution engines such as Temporal (which grew out of Uber's Cadence), AWS Step Functions or Azure Durable Functions. You write the saga as ordinary-looking code — call reserve, then charge, and in the except block call release — and the engine records every step's result in a durable history. If the worker running it crashes, another worker replays the history to rebuild the state and continues from the next step. Steps (called activities) are retried automatically with configurable backoff, and timers can wait for hours or days. The price is that workflow code must be deterministic, so it replays the same way, and all side effects must live in idempotent activities.

7. Living without isolation

Sagas give up the "I" in ACID. Each step commits and becomes visible immediately, so other operations can see and act on intermediate states. Examples: a stock count shows a unit as taken by an order whose payment will fail (a dirty read in saga terms); two sagas both read an account balance and each debit it (a lost update across sagas); a report counts a transfer's debit but not its credit. The standard countermeasures:

Semantic locks. Put records into an explicit in-progress state — PENDING, RESERVED, PAYMENT_IN_PROGRESS — that other flows respect. An order in PENDING_PAYMENT can't be shipped or edited; a reservation counts against available stock but isn't sold. The UI can show "processing".

Commutative updates. Design changes so their order doesn't matter: increment and decrement a balance rather than setting it to a computed value, so a compensating credit and another saga's debit can interleave safely.

Pessimistic view and re-reading. Order steps so that the ones that reduce what others can do (reserving, holding funds) happen early and the ones that increase it happen late; re-read a value just before a decision rather than trusting one read at the start of the saga.

Version checks. Carry a version number and make updates conditional on it (sc5's optimistic concurrency), so a saga acting on stale data fails and retries rather than overwriting.

Keep invariants local. The best countermeasure is design: place the data that an invariant covers in one service, so the invariant is enforced by a local transaction. "Stock can't go below zero" belongs entirely to Inventory, which decrements atomically; the saga only asks it to try.

8. The transactional outbox and inbox

Sagas and event-driven flows depend on each service reliably telling others what happened, which brings back the dual write: commit the local change, then publish a message. The transactional outbox (introduced in sc8 and sc10) solves it by putting the message in the same local transaction:

BEGIN;
UPDATE inventory SET reserved = reserved + 1 WHERE sku = 'A-17' AND available >= 1;
INSERT INTO outbox (id, aggregate_id, type, payload)
VALUES ('evt-5501', 'order-8812', 'StockReserved', '{"sku":"A-17","qty":1}');
COMMIT;

Either both the reservation and the outbox row commit, or neither does. A separate relay reads committed outbox rows in order and publishes them to the broker (or delivers them to the orchestrator), then marks them sent. The relay can work by polling the table or, better at scale, by reading the database's log with change data capture (Debezium). Because the relay might publish a row, crash, and publish it again, delivery is at-least-once.

The inbox is the mirror image on the receiving side: the consumer records each processed message ID in its own database, in the same transaction as the effect, with a unique constraint, and skips messages whose ID is already there (sc10's idempotent consumer). With outbox on one side and inbox on the other, a message's effect happens exactly once even though delivery is at-least-once — the practical version of "exactly-once" between services.

9. Idempotency keys for APIs

Between a client and a service — a mobile app calling "create payment", a saga orchestrator calling "reserve stock" — the same problem appears as a timeout. The client sent the request and got no answer. Did it succeed? If the client retries, it risks doing it twice; if it doesn't, it risks not doing it at all. Idempotency keys make retrying safe.

The client generates a unique key for the logical operation (a UUID, or a natural ID like the order ID) and sends it with the request, typically in an Idempotency-Key HTTP header (an IETF draft standardises this; Stripe popularised it). The server handles it like this:

  1. In a transaction, try to insert (client_id, key, request_hash, status='in_progress') into an idempotency table with a unique constraint on (client_id, key).
  2. If the insert succeeds, this is the first attempt: do the work, and store the response in the same row, atomically with the effect.
  3. If the insert conflicts, it's a repeat. If the stored request hash differs, the client reused a key for a different request: return an error. If the stored status is complete, return the stored response — the same status code and body as the first time. If it's still in progress (the first attempt is running concurrently), return a conflict so the client retries later.
  4. Expire keys after a retention window long enough for any realistic retry (Stripe keeps them at least 24 hours).

Two details matter. The key must be stored atomically with the effect; a key recorded in Redis while the charge is written to PostgreSQL can disagree after a crash. And the key must be propagated: when the payment service calls the card processor, it passes an idempotency key derived from the original, so a retry at any layer is deduplicated all the way down. Idempotency keys turn "at-least-once" calls into "effectively once" effects, which is what makes sagas' automatic retries safe.

You'll see compensation and its absence in the lab.

10. A real-world walk-through: a reliable checkout

Here's the checkout from the opening, redesigned with the tools above. Three services — Orders, Inventory, Payments (which calls an external card processor) — plus notifications, at up to 200 checkouts per second.

Start with a durable record. The user clicks "Place order". Orders inserts the order in state PENDING and starts a checkout workflow in a Temporal-style engine, with workflow ID = order ID (starting the same workflow ID twice is rejected, so a double-click can't start two sagas). From this moment, nothing can be charged without an order record and a saga that knows about it. The API returns "processing" and the client polls or receives a push when the order settles, usually within a second or two.

Step 1 — reserve stock (compensatable). The workflow calls Inventory's reserve activity with the order ID as idempotency key. Inventory atomically moves one unit from available to reserved, with a reservation that expires in 15 minutes as a safety net, and records the order ID so a retried call returns the same reservation. If stock is unavailable, the workflow marks the order REJECTED_OUT_OF_STOCK and ends: nothing to compensate.

Step 2 — charge the card (the pivot). The workflow calls Payments' charge activity with key = order ID; Payments calls the processor with the same key. Three outcomes. Approved: continue. Declined: compensate — release the reservation (idempotent), mark the order PAYMENT_DECLINED, tell the user. Unknown (timeout, processor error): don't compensate. Retry the same call with the same key — the processor returns the original result if the charge happened — with backoff, for up to several minutes; the order shows "confirming payment". This is where many real systems went wrong before idempotency keys: they assumed a timeout meant failure, released the stock, and later found the charge had gone through.

Steps 3-5 — retriable. After a successful charge, the saga must complete. Mark the order CONFIRMED and convert the reservation into a sale; send the confirmation email with the order ID as the email provider's idempotency key; publish OrderConfirmed through the outbox for fulfilment and analytics. Each activity retries until it succeeds; if one keeps failing (the email provider is down for an hour), the workflow waits and retries, and alerts after a threshold — but it never un-charges the customer because an email failed.

Crash handling, checked. Suppose the worker running the workflow dies right after the charge succeeds but before it records the result. Another worker replays the workflow history, sees the charge activity was scheduled but not completed, and runs it again with the same idempotency key; the processor returns "already charged", and the saga continues. Suppose the whole region's Payments service is down for ten minutes: workflows wait in step 2 with retries, stock stays reserved (bounded by the 15-minute expiry as a backstop, after which the workflow would re-reserve or cancel), and no customer is charged twice or left with an unrecorded charge.

Safety nets. A nightly reconciliation job compares three records: orders marked paid, charges in the payments database, and the processor's settlement report. Any mismatch — a charge with no confirmed order, an order with no charge, amounts that differ — goes to a queue for automatic repair (refund, or re-drive the saga) or human review. A dashboard shows workflows by state and age, with alerts for any stuck longer than expected.

What we deliberately didn't use. Two-phase commit across the three services: the card processor won't participate, the services use different databases, and blocking on a coordinator would put checkout's availability at the mercy of the slowest participant. The saga accepts visible intermediate states (a reserved unit, a "processing" order) in exchange for every service staying independent — and makes those states explicit, monitored and bounded in time.

Consensus-backed commit: how distributed databases make 2PC safe

The blocking problem exists because the coordinator's decision lives on one machine. Distributed SQL databases remove that single point by making every participant and the coordinator's transaction record a replicated consensus group. In Spanner, each shard is a Paxos group; a cross-shard transaction picks one participant group as coordinator, and "prepared" and "commit" records are replicated through Paxos before they count, so if a leader fails, a new leader of the same group knows exactly where the transaction stood. CockroachDB stores a transaction record in a Raft-replicated range and writes provisional "intents" on every key it touches; its parallel commits optimisation lets a transaction be considered committed as soon as all its writes and a staging record are durably replicated, cutting commit latency to roughly one round of consensus, with the final cleanup done asynchronously. The trade-off that remains is latency proportional to the number and spread of ranges touched, which is why schemas are designed to keep common transactions within one range or region.

The formal saga model and its guarantees

Garcia-Molina and Salem defined a saga as a sequence T1 … Tn with compensations C1 … Cn−1, guaranteeing that either T1 … Tn runs, or T1 … Tj, Cj … C1 runs for some j. Two assumptions hide in that definition and matter in practice. First, compensations must always succeed eventually — a compensation that can fail permanently leaves the saga stuck halfway, which is why compensations are designed as retriable, idempotent operations and why sagas escalate to humans after bounded retries. Second, the saga's steps and compensations must be semantically sound in the presence of other concurrent sagas; the model gives no isolation, which is the origin of the countermeasures catalogued by Lars Frank and Torben Zahle and popularised in microservices literature. Knowing that the guarantee is "all or compensated", not "all or nothing", and that it depends on compensations being retriable, is the precise version of the interview answer.

Idempotency keys in depth: concurrency, scope and storage

Implementing idempotency keys well has several subtle points. Concurrent duplicates: two identical requests can arrive at the same time (a client retrying aggressively); the unique insert ensures only one proceeds, and the other must wait or receive a "request in progress" conflict — never run the operation in parallel. What to store: the full response (status and body) so replays are byte-identical, and a hash of the request parameters to detect misuse. Recovery points: Stripe's design for multi-step operations records intermediate progress ("recovery points") with the key, so a retry after a crash resumes from the last completed step rather than restarting — effectively a tiny saga per request. Scope: keys are unique per client (API key or account), not globally, and per endpoint. Errors: whether to cache error responses depends on the error — cache deterministic failures (validation errors, card declined) so retries get the same answer, but not transient server errors, which the retry should be allowed to fix. Retention: long enough for all retry paths, including offline mobile clients that retry hours later.

Deterministic replay in durable execution engines

Engines like Temporal persist a workflow's event history: every activity scheduled, completed or failed, every timer, every signal. When a worker picks up a workflow, it re-executes the workflow code from the start, and whenever the code asks to run an activity that already has a recorded result, the engine returns the recorded result instead of running it again. That's how an ordinary-looking function survives crashes. It requires the workflow code to be deterministic: no reading the clock, generating random numbers or calling external services directly in workflow code (those go through engine APIs or activities), and changes to workflow code must be versioned so running workflows replay the old logic. Understanding this explains both the power (long-running business processes as code, with retries and timers for free) and the main source of bugs (non-determinism introduced by a harmless-looking code change).

Reservations, escrow and TTLs as distributed-transaction tools

Many cross-system problems are solved by reservation: split an action into "hold" and "confirm", where the hold is local, cheap, compensatable and expires on its own. Card networks do this natively — an authorisation places a hold on funds that expires after days unless captured — which is why checkout often authorises at the pivot and captures at fulfilment. Inventory reservations with a TTL release themselves if the saga dies. Escrow (sc9's deep section) pre-allocates capacity so decisions can be made locally. The common idea is to make the compensating action automatic and time-bounded, so a saga that silently disappears can't leave resources stuck forever.

Reconciliation as architecture, not afterthought

Financial and logistics systems treat reconciliation as a first-class component. Payment processors send settlement files listing every captured charge; banks send statements; carriers send delivery manifests. Reconciliation jobs match these external records against internal ones, by idempotency keys and references, and classify every record as matched, missing internally, missing externally or mismatched in amount. The mismatches drive automatic repair (issue a refund, re-drive a saga, correct a status) or a human queue. Designing identifiers so that every external action carries an internal reference — the order ID in the payment's metadata, the transfer ID in the bank reference — is what makes reconciliation possible. It's the last line of defence against every failure mode in this lesson, and a senior design names it explicitly.

How these systems fail in production

Orphaned side effects. Charges with no order, shipments with no payment, reservations never released. Usually the fingerprint of dual writes, of treating timeouts as failures, or of a saga whose state wasn't durable. Detect them with reconciliation; prevent them with outbox, idempotency keys and durable orchestration.

Double effects. Customers charged twice, two confirmation emails, loyalty points credited twice. The cause is a retry — by a client, a broker or an orchestrator — reaching a non-idempotent operation. Every side-effecting endpoint and consumer needs an idempotency key or an inbox check.

Stuck sagas. Workflows waiting forever on a step that keeps failing (a downstream service returns a permanent error that's being retried as transient) or a compensation that can't succeed (the resource no longer exists). Symptoms: a growing count of sagas in intermediate states. Fix with bounded retries per error type, timeouts per step, and escalation to a human queue with tooling to inspect and resolve.

Blocked 2PC participants. In systems that use XA, a coordinator failure leaves in-doubt transactions holding locks, and application traffic touching those rows blocks. Databases expose in-doubt transactions (for example PostgreSQL's pg_prepared_xacts), and operators may have to resolve them manually — which is one reason XA is avoided in many modern stacks. PostgreSQL disables prepared transactions by default (max_prepared_transactions = 0) for this reason.

Outbox relay lag. The relay falls behind or stops, so events are delayed; the outbox table grows. Monitor the age of the oldest unsent row and the table's size, and clean up sent rows.

What to watch

Saga counts by state and by age (how many orders have been PENDING for more than 5 minutes?), step failure and retry rates by error type, compensation rates (a sudden rise in refunds from compensation is a signal), idempotency-key hit rates (how often retries are being deduplicated — a spike means clients or brokers are retrying heavily), outbox backlog age, inbox duplicate rates, and reconciliation mismatch counts per run with trends. For workflow engines: workflow task latency, activity schedule-to-start latency (are there enough workers?) and the number of workflows failing on non-determinism after deploys.

Knobs

Per-step timeouts and retry policies (maximum attempts, backoff, which errors are retriable); reservation and hold expiry times; idempotency-key retention; outbox relay batch size and polling interval (or CDC configuration); saga escalation thresholds; reconciliation frequency and matching tolerances.

A war-story pattern: the timeout that became a double charge

This pattern is common enough in payments systems that it's worth knowing as a story. A checkout service calls a payment processor with a 5-second timeout. During a processor slowdown, calls take 7 seconds: the charge succeeds at the processor, but the checkout service has already timed out, treated it as a failure, released the stock and shown "payment failed, please try again". The customer tries again; the second charge succeeds quickly. The customer is now charged twice, and one charge has no order. Multiplied across a thirty-minute slowdown, support receives hundreds of complaints and finance spends days matching charges to orders. Every element of the fix is in this lesson: send an idempotency key derived from the order so the retry returns the first charge instead of creating a second; treat timeouts as "unknown" and resolve them by retrying with the same key or querying the processor; make the order the durable record that exists before the charge; and run reconciliation against the processor's records daily so any remaining mismatch is found in hours, not weeks. Stripe's engineering post on idempotency (in refs) describes exactly why their API is built around this mechanism.

A two-minute simulation of a checkout saga, with and without compensation. It needs only Python 3.

import random
random.seed(3)

def checkout(state, with_compensation):
    state["stock"] -= 1                                   # step 1: reserve stock
    if random.random() < 0.2:                             # step 2: charge card, fails 20%
        if with_compensation:
            state["stock"] += 1                           # compensate step 1
        return "failed"
    state["charged"] += 1
    state["orders"] += 1                                  # step 3: confirm order
    return "ok"

for comp in (False, True):
    state = {"stock": 1000, "charged": 0, "orders": 0}
    for _ in range(500):
        checkout(state, comp)
    lost = 1000 - state["stock"] - state["orders"]        # stock gone with no order
    print(f"compensation={comp!s:5}  orders={state['orders']}  charged={state['charged']}  "
          f"stock={state['stock']}  stock leaked={lost}")

Output:

compensation=False  orders=406  charged=406  stock=500  stock leaked=94
compensation=True   orders=383  charged=383  stock=617  stock leaked=0

What it shows. Without compensation, every failed payment leaves a unit reserved forever: 94 units have vanished from stock with no order to show for them — the "reserved stock that is never released" from the opening. With compensation, each failed payment releases its reservation, so stock plus orders always adds up to what you started with. (The order counts differ only because the random failures fall differently.)

Think it through. (1) Make the compensation itself fail 5% of the time: what happens to the invariant, and why must compensations be retried until they succeed? (2) Add a crash between step 1 and step 2 (the process dies and nothing runs): which of the two versions protects you, and what would — a durable saga state, or a reservation that expires? (3) Make step 2 sometimes "time out" after the charge succeeded: what must the saga do before deciding to compensate?

Design it

Checkout spans three services: Orders, Payments (calls an external card processor) and Inventory. Today the Orders service calls Inventory.reserve, then Payments.charge, then saves the order — and in production there are charged customers with no order, and reserved stock that is never released. Design a reliable flow.

Answer
  1. Orders creates the order in state PENDING first, and starts the saga in the same step (a workflow keyed by order ID, or a CheckoutStarted event written to its outbox in the same transaction) — the order now exists before anything else happens, so nothing can be charged without a record.
  2. An orchestrator (a durable state machine or Temporal workflow keyed by order ID) drives the saga:
    • Reserve stock (Inventory, with an expiry on the reservation). Failure → mark order REJECTED, stop.
    • Charge payment — the pivot — with an idempotency key = order ID, passed through to the card processor. Declined → compensate by releasing the reservation, order PAYMENT_FAILED.
    • Confirm: commit the reservation, order CONFIRMED, and only then send the confirmation email (not compensatable, so after the pivot, retried until it succeeds).
  3. Every step is idempotent and retried with backoff, so the orchestrator can safely resume after a crash; service calls carry the order ID as the deduplication key.
  4. Timeouts are "unknown", not "failed": if the payment result is unknown, retry with the same idempotency key or query the processor by it before deciding — never release stock while money may have moved.
  5. Safety nets: reservation expiry releases abandoned stock; a reconciliation job compares orders, payments and the processor's settlement report daily and flags mismatches.

Note what we did not use: 2PC across the three services and the external processor is impossible (the card processor will not join your transaction) and would block on failures.
