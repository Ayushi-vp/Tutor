An online store's product page calls four other services: pricing, inventory, reviews and recommendations. One afternoon the recommendations service gets slow — not down, just slow, taking 20 seconds per request instead of 50 milliseconds, because its database is struggling. Nothing about the product page has changed, yet within two minutes the whole site stops loading. Every product page request waits on recommendations; each waiting request holds one of the page service's 200 worker threads; the threads run out; new requests, including ones for checkout pages that never call recommendations, queue up and time out. Users hit refresh, adding more requests. The page service's own health checks start failing, the load balancer removes instances, and the remaining ones get even more traffic. A non-essential widget has taken down the store.

This is the characteristic failure of distributed systems. At scale, something is always slow or broken — a machine, a network link, a dependency deploying a bad version, a database under load — and the dangerous failures aren't clean crashes, which are quick to detect, but slowness, which quietly consumes resources in every caller and spreads upstream. Well-meant reactions make it worse: retries add load to the struggling service, long timeouts keep resources tied up, and queues hide the problem until everything times out at once.

Resilience patterns are the standard defences, and knowing them — including when each one hurts — is expected of every senior engineer. Timeouts and deadlines bound how long you wait. Retries with backoff and jitter recover from brief failures without creating storms. Circuit breakers stop calling a dependency that's clearly failing. Bulkheads make sure one failure can only consume its own share of resources. Fallbacks and graceful degradation keep the core working without the optional parts. Rate limiting keeps any single client from overwhelming a service, and load shedding makes an overloaded service reject some work quickly rather than fail all of it slowly.

This lesson explains each pattern from the mechanism up, with the numbers that make them concrete: why slowness cascades (Little's Law again), how retries multiply load across layers, how a circuit breaker's states work, which rate-limiting algorithm to choose, why queues under overload are deadly, and what a metastable failure is — the kind of outage that keeps going after its cause is fixed. It ends with a full resilience design for that product page.

The analogy: a restaurant kitchen on a bad night

A restaurant depends on suppliers, cooks and equipment. One night the fish supplier is late. A kitchen with no rules has every cook stop and wait by the door for the fish; soon nobody is cooking anything, and even customers who ordered pasta get nothing. That's a cascading failure caused by waiting.

A well-run kitchen has rules. Don't wait forever: if the fish isn't here by 7:15, move on (a timeout). Call the supplier again, but not every ten seconds, and not all at once (retries with backoff and jitter). After three failed deliveries, stop ordering from that supplier for the night and take fish off the menu (a circuit breaker with a fallback). Only one cook handles fish, so the fish problem can't tie up the whole kitchen (a bulkhead). Serve the rest of the menu without fish (graceful degradation). Seat only as many tables as the kitchen can serve, and turn away the rest politely at the door rather than letting every table wait two hours (load shedding and rate limiting).

The analogy breaks in one way: a restaurant manager can see the whole kitchen at a glance. In software, each service sees only its own requests and responses, so each must apply these rules locally, automatically, in milliseconds, based on what it can measure.

Why slowness is worse than failure

Little's Law from sc1 explains the whole problem in one line: requests in flight = arrival rate × latency. The page service receives 1,000 requests per second, and each normally takes 60 ms, so about 60 are in flight at once — comfortably inside its 200 threads. Now recommendations slows to 20 seconds. If every page request waits for it, in-flight requests become 1,000 × 20 = 20,000, a hundred times the thread pool. The pool is exhausted within a fraction of a second.

Now the defences become obvious. A timeout of 150 ms on recommendations caps the in-flight work it can cause at 1,000 × 0.15 = 150 requests. A bulkhead of 30 threads reserved for recommendations caps it at 30, whatever happens. A circuit breaker stops calling it entirely once it's clearly failing. A fallback hides the widget. And the users' refreshes — a form of retry — show why unbounded retries turn slowness into overload. Each of these patterns is a way of making sure that the failure of one thing costs a bounded amount of everything else.

It helps to sort the patterns by what they protect. Timeouts, deadlines, circuit breakers, bulkheads and fallbacks protect a caller from its dependencies. Rate limiting, load shedding and back-pressure protect a service from its callers. Retries sit in between: they help callers recover from brief failures but can hurt the service they call, which is why they need the most care. A resilient system uses both kinds, at every layer, because every service is someone's caller and someone else's dependency.

1. Timeouts

A timeout is the maximum time a caller waits for a response. Every network call needs one, set explicitly, because many client libraries default to very long or even infinite waits. There are usually two: a connect timeout (how long to wait to establish a connection — short, since connections to a healthy server in the same datacentre take about a millisecond) and a request or read timeout (how long to wait for the response).

Choosing the value. Base it on the dependency's latency distribution under normal load. A common rule is somewhat above its p99 or p99.9: if pricing answers 99.9% of requests within 80 ms, a 150 ms timeout fails very few healthy requests while cutting off the hung ones. Setting it at the p50 would fail half the slow-but-fine requests; setting it at 30 seconds lets a single hung dependency hold resources for 30 seconds each. Remember that a timeout that fires 0.1% of the time under normal conditions will fire far more often when the dependency is loaded — which is the point.

Timeouts must shrink down the call chain. If the user's browser gives up after 5 seconds, a backend service that waits 10 seconds for its dependency is doing work nobody will use. And if service A's timeout for B is shorter than B's timeout for C plus B's own work, A gives up while B is still working, and B's result is wasted. The rule is: each layer's timeout must be shorter than its caller's, leaving room for its own processing and any retries.

A timeout is not a failure. When a call times out, you don't know whether the other side did the work. For reads that doesn't matter; for writes it does, which is why side-effecting calls need idempotency keys (sc11) before they can be retried after a timeout.

2. Deadlines and their propagation

Per-hop timeouts are a start, but they don't know how much time the user has left. A deadline fixes that: the first service to receive a request sets an absolute time by which the whole request must finish (say, now + 1 second), and every downstream call carries it. Each service computes its remaining budget ("the deadline is in 640 ms, I need 40 ms for my own work, so my calls get at most 600 ms"), sets its timeouts no longer than that, and stops immediately if the deadline has passed — including rejecting requests that arrive already expired.

This matters most during overload. Without propagation, when the front end gives up on a slow request, services further down keep working on it, consuming exactly the capacity that's in short supply. With propagation, abandoned work stops everywhere at once. gRPC propagates deadlines automatically (each call carries a deadline and servers see how much time remains); HTTP services pass one in a header and set timeouts from it.

3. Retries: helpful and dangerous

Many failures are brief: a connection reset during a deploy, a packet lost, a replica restarting, a momentary overload. Trying again after a short wait usually succeeds, so retries are a cheap way to hide transient failures from users. They're also one of the most common causes of large outages. Using them safely takes four rules.

Rule 1: retry only transient errors. Timeouts, connection errors, 503 Service Unavailable, 429 Too Many Requests (after the time the Retry-After header says), and some 502/504 gateway errors may succeed later. A 400 Bad Request, 401/403, 404 or a validation error will fail identically every time, so retrying only adds load.

Rule 2: retry only idempotent operations. Reads and naturally idempotent writes (set status to shipped) are safe. Non-idempotent operations (charge the card, send the email) are safe only with an idempotency key the server honours.

Rule 3: back off, with jitter. Wait before retrying, and wait longer each time: exponential backoff doubles the delay each attempt (100 ms, 200 ms, 400 ms…), up to a cap. Add jitter — randomness — because otherwise every client that failed at the same moment retries at the same moments too, producing synchronised waves of load. AWS's analysis of the options found that "full jitter", sleeping a random time between zero and the capped exponential value, spreads load best:

import random, time

class Transient(Exception): ...

def call_with_retries(fn, attempts=3, base=0.1, cap=2.0):
    for attempt in range(attempts):
        try:
            return fn()
        except Transient:
            if attempt == attempts - 1:
                raise                                   # out of attempts: give up
            delay = min(cap, base * 2 ** attempt)       # 0.1, 0.2, 0.4 … capped
            time.sleep(random.uniform(0, delay))        # full jitter

Rule 4: limit retries — because they multiply. This is the one that causes outages. Suppose a request passes through three services, and each retries a failed downstream call up to 3 times (4 attempts). If the bottom service fails completely, one user request produces 4 calls from the middle service, each of which produces 4 calls to the bottom: at the bottom layer that's up to 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 calls per user request. Worse, this happens exactly when the bottom service is struggling, so the extra load keeps it down. This is retry amplification, and the resulting surge is a retry storm.

The defences: retry at one layer only, typically the one nearest the user or the one with the most context about whether retrying makes sense, while inner layers fail fast. Use a retry budget: each client tracks retries as a fraction of its requests over a sliding window and stops retrying above a threshold (Linkerd's default is 20%; many teams use 10%), so retries can never add more than that much load, however bad things get. Combine with circuit breakers (next section), which stop calls to a clearly failing dependency altogether, and with deadlines, which stop retries once the user's time budget is spent.

Hedged requests are a related technique aimed at latency rather than errors: for idempotent reads, if the first request to one replica hasn't answered by, say, its p95 latency, send a second request to another replica and use whichever answers first. It costs about 5% extra load and dramatically cuts tail latency (sc1's deep section) — but it's another form of added load, so it's disabled under overload.

4. Circuit breakers

When a dependency is clearly failing, every call to it wastes the caller's time (waiting for a timeout) and adds load to the dependency (keeping it down). A circuit breaker, named after the electrical device that cuts power when current is dangerous, wraps calls to a dependency and stops making them when failures are high.

Closed (normal): calls pass through, and the breaker records outcomes over a rolling window. When the failure rate — errors and timeouts — exceeds a threshold, for example more than 50% of at least 20 calls in the last 10 seconds (the minimum count stops two failures out of three calls from tripping it), it moves to open. Open: calls don't touch the dependency; they fail immediately or go straight to a fallback. The caller stops wasting threads on timeouts, and the dependency stops receiving load. After a cool-down (say 30 seconds), the breaker moves to half-open: it lets a few trial calls through. If they succeed, it closes and normal traffic resumes; if they fail, it opens again for another cool-down.

Breakers usually live in each calling instance, per dependency (sometimes per endpoint or per backend host), so they react to what that instance sees with no coordination. Libraries such as resilience4j (Java), Polly (.NET) and Envoy's outlier detection and circuit-breaking settings in a service mesh (sc2) implement them. The important design decision isn't the breaker itself but what happens when it's open — the fallback.

5. Bulkheads

Ships are divided into watertight compartments, called bulkheads, so that a hole floods one compartment rather than sinking the ship. In software, a bulkhead is any partition of resources that stops a failure in one area from consuming everything.

The classic form is a separate, bounded pool per dependency. Instead of all outbound calls sharing one pool of 200 threads (or one connection pool, or one async concurrency limit), the page service gives pricing 60, inventory 60, reviews 30 and recommendations 30. When recommendations slows to 20 seconds, its 30 slots fill and further recommendation calls are rejected immediately (and fall back), while pricing and inventory calls continue with their own capacity untouched. Little's Law sizes each pool: pricing at 1,000 calls per second and 20 ms average needs about 20 slots on average, so 60 gives ample headroom for latency spikes.

Bulkheads exist at larger scales too: separate instance groups for interactive traffic and batch jobs, so a heavy report can't slow checkout; separate gateway or service pools per important customer or per region (cells, sc17), so one tenant's problem stays in its cell; separate databases or connection limits for background workers. The trade-off is efficiency: partitioned capacity can sit idle in one compartment while another is saturated. That's the price of guaranteed isolation.

6. Fallbacks and graceful degradation

When a dependency fails, times out, is rate-limited or has its circuit open, the caller needs something to do. A fallback is that something, and choosing it is a product decision as much as an engineering one.

  • Cached or stale data: show the last known recommendations, the price from a minute ago (clearly handled — perhaps re-verified at checkout), yesterday's trending list.
  • A default: a generic "popular items" list instead of personalised recommendations; a standard shipping estimate.
  • Hide the feature: render the page without the reviews section.
  • Defer the work: accept the order and queue the loyalty-points update for later (sc10).
  • Fail clearly: for critical data with no safe substitute, a fast, honest error ("prices are temporarily unavailable") beats a spinner that times out after 30 seconds.

Graceful degradation is the system-level version: deciding in advance which features are critical (browse, add to cart, checkout) and which are optional (recommendations, reviews, recently viewed), and making sure the critical path depends only on critical services. Many large sites also have explicit degraded modes that operators can switch on during incidents or extreme load — disabling expensive personalisation, lowering image quality, pausing non-essential background jobs — to preserve capacity for the core. Fallbacks must be exercised regularly (tests, game days, chaos experiments), because a fallback path that has never run in production is often broken when it's finally needed.

7. Rate limiting

Rate limiting caps how many requests a client may make in a period, rejecting the excess, usually with HTTP 429 Too Many Requests and a Retry-After header saying when to try again. It protects services from abusive clients, buggy clients stuck in loops, sister services in retry storms, and single tenants hogging shared capacity. It's enforced at the edge (per IP, sc2's gateway, per API key) and inside systems (per calling service, per tenant). Four algorithms are worth knowing:

Token bucket. A bucket holds up to B tokens and refills at R tokens per second. Each request takes one token; if the bucket is empty, the request is rejected. Clients get a sustained rate of R and can burst up to B after being idle. Worked example: R = 10/s, B = 20. A client idle for a while has 20 tokens. It sends 60 requests in one second: the first 20 are accepted immediately, and about 10 more are accepted as tokens refill during the second, so roughly 30 succeed and 30 are rejected. Then it's held to 10 per second. The token bucket is the most widely used algorithm (Amazon API Gateway, Stripe and many others), because bursts are natural in real traffic. It needs only two numbers per client (tokens, last refill time), computed lazily on each request.

Leaky bucket. Requests enter a queue that drains at a constant rate; if the queue is full, new requests are dropped. It smooths bursts into a steady flow, useful when the downstream needs a constant rate, but adds queueing delay and doesn't allow bursts.

Fixed window counter. Count requests per client per clock window (for example per minute) and reject beyond the limit. Very simple, but a client can send the full limit at 12:00:59 and again at 12:01:00, getting twice the limit in two seconds.

Sliding window. Count over a moving window instead: either exactly, keeping a timestamp log per client (accurate but memory-heavy), or approximately, by weighting the previous fixed window's count by how much of it overlaps the sliding window (cheap and close enough). It removes the boundary burst.

Distributed limits. With 50 gateway instances, a per-client limit must be shared somehow. Options: keep counters in a shared store like Redis, updated atomically (a small Lua script implements a token bucket in one round trip) — accurate, but adds a network call and a dependency, so decide whether to fail open (allow) or closed (deny) if Redis is unavailable; or give each instance a local share of the limit (limit ÷ instances), possibly rebalanced periodically — fast and resilient, but inexact when traffic is uneven across instances. Many systems combine a local limiter for coarse protection with a shared one for precise quotas.

8. Load shedding and back-pressure

Rate limits protect against specific clients; load shedding protects a service against its total load exceeding capacity, whatever the cause. The key insight is counter-intuitive: when a service is overloaded, rejecting some requests quickly is far better than accepting all of them.

Here's why. Suppose a service can handle 1,000 requests per second and receives 1,500. If it accepts everything into a queue, the queue grows by 500 requests every second. After ten seconds, a new request waits behind 5,000 others — five seconds — and clients, which time out at two seconds, give up and retry. The service is now spending all its capacity on requests whose clients have already left, while new ones wait behind them. Useful throughput — requests completed before their client gave up — collapses toward zero, even though the servers are 100% busy. This is congestion collapse, and it's what "the site is up but nothing works" looks like.

A service that sheds load stays useful. The techniques:

  • A concurrency limit with a short queue. Allow at most N requests in flight (sized from Little's Law and load tests: capacity × target latency), queue a small number beyond that, and reject the rest immediately with a cheap 503. Rejection must cost far less than serving, or shedding itself becomes the bottleneck.
  • Drop what nobody is waiting for. Reject requests whose deadline has already passed, and requests that have waited in the queue longer than a threshold. Some systems serve the queue last-in-first-out under overload, since the newest requests are the ones whose clients are most likely still waiting.
  • Shed by priority. Label requests by criticality — for example critical (checkout, health checks), default (browsing), sheddable (prefetch, analytics, batch) — and drop the lowest first. Google's SRE book describes exactly this criticality scheme.
  • Adaptive concurrency limits. Instead of a fixed N, adjust the limit continuously from measured latency, in the spirit of TCP congestion control (net4): raise it while latency stays near its minimum, lower it when latency rises. Netflix's concurrency-limits library and Envoy's adaptive concurrency filter do this, so the service finds its own capacity as conditions change.

Back-pressure is the same idea applied between components: when a consumer can't keep up, it signals upstream to slow down (bounded queues that block or reject, TCP flow control, gRPC flow control, sc10's consumer lag), so that pressure propagates back to the source instead of building up invisibly in memory.

Client-side throttling closes the loop from the client side. In the scheme from Google's SRE book, each client tracks, over the last couple of minutes, how many requests it sent and how many the backend accepted, and rejects a fraction of new requests locally with probability max(0, (requests − K × accepts) / (requests + 1)), with K typically 2. When the backend starts rejecting heavily, clients stop even sending most of the doomed requests, so the backend's rejection work — which isn't free — stops growing.

9. Metastable failures: outages that sustain themselves

Most failures stop when their cause is removed. Metastable failures don't. A system sits in a stable, healthy state under normal load, but a trigger — a brief network blip, a deploy, a cache flush, a traffic spike — pushes it into a bad state that a feedback loop then sustains, even after the trigger is gone. The term comes from a 2021 paper by Bronson and colleagues describing a pattern that had been behind many large outages.

The classic loop is retries: a short slowdown causes some requests to time out; clients retry; retries raise load; higher load causes more timeouts and more retries. The service is now overloaded purely by its own retry traffic. Fixing the original slowdown changes nothing, because the load has become self-sustaining. Other loops: a cache flush sends traffic to the database, which becomes too slow for requests to finish and refill the cache (sc3); a slow service triggers health-check failures, the load balancer removes instances, and the remaining ones are even more overloaded.

Recovery requires breaking the loop by bringing load below capacity: shed traffic at the edge, disable retries, rate-limit clients, warm caches gradually, then restore traffic in steps. Prevention is everything in this lesson working together: retry budgets and backoff so retries can't multiply load, circuit breakers, load shedding so overload doesn't become collapse, and capacity headroom so ordinary triggers don't push the system over the edge. Autoscaling usually can't rescue a metastable failure, because new instances take minutes and the bottleneck is often a shared dependency like a database.

10. A real-world walk-through: making the product page resilient

Back to the store from the opening. The product page service handles 1,000 requests per second at peak, with a 300 ms end-to-end target at p99, and calls pricing, inventory, reviews and recommendations. Here's the design, with the reasoning.

Classify dependencies. Pricing and inventory are critical: without a price there's no page worth showing. Reviews and recommendations are optional: the page is useful without them. Everything else follows from this classification.

Deadlines and timeouts. The gateway sets a 1-second deadline and the page service targets 300 ms. It calls all four dependencies in parallel. Pricing's p99.9 is 80 ms, so its timeout is 150 ms; inventory is similar; reviews and recommendations get 120 ms, since waiting longer for optional content isn't worth it. Each call carries the remaining deadline, and each dependency does the same for its own calls.

Retries. Pricing and inventory reads are idempotent, so the page service retries once, with jittered backoff, but only if there's time left before the deadline and the retry budget (10% of calls over 10 seconds) isn't exhausted. Optional dependencies aren't retried at all — a missing widget is better than a slower page. Nothing below the page service retries; inner services fail fast.

Circuit breakers and fallbacks. Each dependency has a breaker (open at 50% failures over at least 20 calls in 10 seconds, 30-second cool-down). Recommendations' fallback is a precomputed "popular in this category" list cached in memory; reviews' fallback hides the section with a "reviews temporarily unavailable" note. Pricing's fallback is a price cached within the last 60 seconds, shown only if the cache entry is fresh; otherwise the page shows the product with "price unavailable" and no buy button, because showing a wrong price is worse than showing none (checkout re-verifies prices anyway, "cache for display, verify for action" from sc3).

Bulkheads. Outbound calls use separate concurrency limits per dependency, sized with Little's Law and headroom: pricing and inventory 60 each, reviews and recommendations 30 each. When recommendations slows to 20 seconds, as in the opening, at most 30 slots fill (and time out at 120 ms, so they don't even stay full), its breaker opens within seconds, and the page renders with popular items — while pricing and inventory are completely unaffected.

Protecting the service itself. At its entry, the page service uses an adaptive concurrency limit and a short queue, rejecting excess requests quickly with 503. Requests carry a criticality label: page loads for real users are default, while prefetch requests from the mobile app and crawler traffic are sheddable and dropped first. The gateway enforces per-client rate limits (a token bucket per API key or IP) so a single scraper can't consume capacity.

Observability and testing. Dashboards show per-dependency latency percentiles, error and timeout rates, breaker states, fallback rates, retry-budget usage and shed request counts. Alerts fire on breaker openings for critical dependencies and on sustained fallback rates. Every quarter, a game day injects 20-second latency into each dependency in turn in a staging environment, and then in a small slice of production traffic, to confirm the page degrades as designed.

Checking against the opening incident. Recommendations slows to 20 seconds. Each call now costs at most 120 ms before timing out, so in-flight work from recommendations is bounded at about 1,000 × 0.12 = 120 calls, inside its bulkhead of 30 plus fast rejection; within seconds the breaker opens and the calls stop entirely. The page renders with popular items at normal latency. Checkout never notices. The recommendations team gets paged and fixes their database, and when the breaker's half-open trials succeed, personalised recommendations return automatically. That's what resilience looks like: the failure is contained to the thing that failed.

Choosing timeouts from percentiles, and the cost of false timeouts

A timeout trades two errors: cutting off requests that would have succeeded, and waiting on requests that won't. Setting it at a high percentile of healthy latency (p99.9) keeps false timeouts rare in normal conditions. But latency distributions shift under load, so the same timeout fires much more often exactly when the dependency is struggling — which is the desired behaviour for protecting the caller, but combined with retries it adds load at the worst time. That's why timeouts and retries must be designed together, with budgets. For calls whose latency is highly variable (a search over a varying amount of data), fixed timeouts fit poorly; deadlines and cancellation are better tools. And a timeout should cover the whole call including connection setup, DNS and TLS, or a stuck handshake can bypass it.

Why full jitter works, quantitatively

Without jitter, N clients that fail together retry together, so the dependency sees spikes of N requests at each backoff interval with silence in between — the same total work concentrated into bursts that can each overload it. AWS simulated several strategies and found that exponential backoff alone barely reduces total calls or completion time for contended resources, while adding jitter spreads retries across the interval, turning spikes into a roughly even rate. "Full jitter" (uniform between 0 and the backoff) and "decorrelated jitter" (each delay random between the base and three times the previous delay, capped) both perform well; "equal jitter" (half fixed, half random) is slightly worse. The same principle applies to anything scheduled: cron jobs, cache expiries (sc3) and client polling should all be jittered to avoid synchronised load.

Circuit breakers at scale: per-host ejection and the panic threshold

A breaker per dependency treats the dependency as one unit, but dependencies are fleets. If one of twenty backend instances is failing, a service-level breaker either ignores it (5% errors, below threshold) or, if it trips, cuts off nineteen healthy instances. Service meshes and client-side load balancers therefore also do outlier ejection (sc2): remove an individual instance from rotation after consecutive errors, for a growing ejection time, while the rest keep serving. They pair it with a cap on how much of the pool can be ejected at once and a panic threshold, beyond which the balancer assumes its health information is wrong and uses all instances anyway — because if most of the fleet looks unhealthy, it's more likely a shared cause (a bad dependency, a network issue) than a mass of individual failures, and ejecting everything would guarantee an outage.

Adaptive concurrency: borrowing from TCP

A fixed concurrency limit is either too low (wasting capacity when the service is healthy and fast) or too high (letting queues build when it slows). Adaptive limiters treat the service like a network path and apply congestion control. The gradient approach measures the minimum observed latency (the service's uncongested speed) and the current latency; while current latency stays close to the minimum, the limit grows, and when it rises the limit shrinks proportionally. Little's Law explains why it works: at a fixed throughput, extra concurrency beyond capacity only adds queueing latency, so rising latency is the signal that the limit exceeds capacity. Netflix's open-source concurrency-limits library, and adaptive concurrency in Envoy, implement variants. The result is a service that automatically sheds exactly the excess load as its capacity changes — during a deploy, a noisy neighbour, a slow dependency — without anyone tuning a number.

Fairness and multi-tenant overload

Shedding by global order punishes everyone equally, which is unfair when one tenant causes the overload. Mature systems use fair queuing — separate queues per tenant or client served round-robin, so a flood from one tenant fills only its own queue — and per-tenant concurrency limits alongside rate limits. Amazon describes shuffle sharding: assigning each customer to a small, random combination of workers from a large pool, so that a problem customer overloads only its few workers, and the chance that any other customer shares all of them is tiny. These techniques convert "one bad tenant takes down the service" into "one bad tenant degrades itself", which is the multi-tenant version of a bulkhead.

Testing resilience: chaos engineering

Resilience mechanisms are code paths that run only during failures, so they're the least tested code in most systems. Chaos engineering, popularised by Netflix's Chaos Monkey (which randomly terminates production instances), makes failure routine: define the system's steady state (for example, successful checkouts per minute), hypothesise that it will hold under a specific fault, inject that fault in a controlled way — kill instances, add latency to a dependency, drop packets, fail a zone — with a small blast radius and an abort switch, and compare. Game days do the same with people practising the response. Findings are typically mundane and valuable: a timeout nobody set, a fallback that throws an exception, a breaker threshold that never trips, a retry loop without backoff. The AWS 2021 outage in the prod section — a years-old defect in retry backoff exposed only under a rare condition — is the argument for testing these paths deliberately.

How resilience problems show up

Thread or connection pool exhaustion. CPU is low, but requests queue and time out; thread dumps show most threads waiting on one downstream call. Missing or too-long timeouts, no bulkheads. The telltale is that endpoints unrelated to the slow dependency fail too.

Retry storms. A dependency's request rate jumps to a multiple of normal right after a brief incident, and stays high; error rates don't recover when the original cause is fixed. Look at retry counts per client and whether multiple layers retry.

Breakers flapping or never tripping. A breaker that opens and closes every few seconds (thresholds too sensitive, or half-open trials passing on a partially healthy fleet), or one that never opens because its minimum call count is never reached on a low-traffic endpoint.

Fallbacks that fail. The breaker opens and the fallback throws an exception, reads from a cache that was never populated, or calls another dependency that's also down. Only testing finds these.

Congestion collapse. Servers at 100% CPU, throughput of successful requests falling, latency at the timeout value for nearly everything: the service is doing work for requests that have already been abandoned. Load shedding and deadline checks are missing.

What to watch

Per dependency: latency percentiles, error and timeout rates, retry rate and budget usage, circuit-breaker state changes, fallback invocations, and bulkhead saturation (rejections because the pool is full). Per service: in-flight requests against the concurrency limit, queue length and time in queue, shed requests by priority, 429s and 503s returned, and requests rejected for expired deadlines. At the edge: rate-limit rejections per client, which also reveal abusive or buggy clients. The combination of "high CPU, falling success rate, latency at the timeout" is the signature of overload without shedding.

Knobs

Connect and request timeouts per dependency; default deadlines at the edge; retry count, backoff base and cap, jitter strategy, retry budget; breaker failure threshold, minimum call volume, window length, cool-down and number of half-open trials; bulkhead sizes; concurrency limits (fixed or adaptive) and queue lengths; rate-limit rates and burst sizes per client class; request priority classes and shedding order.

A real incident: AWS us-east-1, December 2021

On 7 December 2021, an automated activity to scale capacity for a service in AWS's internal network triggered unexpected behaviour from a large number of clients inside that network. The result was a sudden surge of connection activity that overwhelmed the networking devices connecting the internal network to the main AWS network. A latent defect in the clients' retry logic — code that had existed for years — meant they didn't back off as they should when congestion appeared, so failed connections produced more connection attempts, keeping the devices overloaded: a textbook retry storm and metastable state. Making things harder, AWS's own real-time monitoring used the congested path, so engineers lost their dashboards and had to diagnose from logs. Impact lasted about seven hours, with elevated errors for control-plane operations across services including EC2 APIs, CloudWatch, API Gateway and authentication, while most already-running workloads continued. AWS disabled the triggering scaling activity, added network protections, and fixed the backoff behaviour. The lessons map onto this lesson almost point by point: retry and backoff logic is safety-critical and must be tested under congestion; retries can sustain an overload after its trigger; and the tools you use to observe and fix a system must not share its failure domain.

Two short calculations you can run in a minute with Python 3: how retries amplify load as a dependency fails, and how a token bucket shapes a burst.

import random
random.seed(1)

def retry_amplification(layers, retries, p_fail):
    """Expected calls reaching the bottom layer per user request."""
    per_layer = sum(p_fail ** k for k in range(retries + 1))   # 1 + p + p^2 + ... attempts
    return per_layer ** layers

for p in (0.01, 0.5, 0.99):
    print(f"failure rate {p:>4}: 3 layers x 3 retries -> {retry_amplification(3, 3, p):5.1f} calls at the bottom")

class TokenBucket:
    def __init__(self, rate, burst):
        self.rate, self.burst, self.tokens, self.last = rate, burst, burst, 0.0
    def allow(self, now):
        self.tokens = min(self.burst, self.tokens + (now - self.last) * self.rate)
        self.last = now
        if self.tokens >= 1:
            self.tokens -= 1
            return True
        return False

bucket = TokenBucket(rate=10, burst=20)            # 10 requests/s on average, bursts of 20
arrivals = sorted(random.uniform(0, 1) for _ in range(60))   # 60 requests in the first second
accepted = sum(bucket.allow(t) for t in arrivals)
print(f"token bucket: {accepted} of 60 burst requests accepted in 1 s (burst 20 + ~10 refilled)")

Output:

failure rate 0.01: 3 layers x 3 retries ->   1.0 calls at the bottom
failure rate  0.5: 3 layers x 3 retries ->   6.6 calls at the bottom
failure rate 0.99: 3 layers x 3 retries ->  61.2 calls at the bottom
token bucket: 29 of 60 burst requests accepted in 1 s (burst 20 + ~10 refilled)

What it shows. When the bottom service is healthy (1% failures), retries cost almost nothing. When it's half-failing, each user request becomes 6.6 calls at the bottom; when it's almost completely down, 61 — nearly the 64 worst case. Retries are cheap exactly when they're harmless and ruinous exactly when the dependency is struggling. The token bucket accepts the initial burst of 20, then only what refills (about 10 per second), rejecting the rest — the sustained rate and burst size are the only two numbers you tune.

Think it through. (1) Change the amplification to retry at only one layer (layers=1): what's the worst case now? (2) Add a retry budget that allows retries on at most 10% of calls: roughly what's the maximum amplification at a 99% failure rate? (3) With 50 gateway instances each running this bucket locally at rate=10, what's a client's real limit if its requests are spread evenly — and what if they all land on one instance?

Implement and reason

(a) Write a retry helper with capped exponential backoff and full jitter. (b) Service A calls B with a 10 s timeout and 3 retries; B calls C with a 10 s timeout and 3 retries. A's own callers time out after 5 s. What goes wrong when C becomes slow?

Answer 
import random, time

class Transient(Exception): ...

def retry(fn, attempts=4, base=0.1, cap=5.0):
    for i in range(attempts):
        try:
            return fn()
        except Transient:
            if i == attempts - 1:
                raise
            # full jitter: sleep a random amount up to the capped exponential
            time.sleep(random.uniform(0, min(cap, base * 2 ** i)))

(b) Everything about the configuration is inverted:

  • Timeouts longer than the caller's deadline: A's callers give up after 5 s, but A keeps waiting up to 40 s (10 s × 4 attempts) and B up to 40 s per call — all that work is wasted, while threads and connections stay occupied.
  • Retry amplification: each request to A can produce 4 calls to B, each producing 4 calls to C — 16× load on C at exactly the moment it's slow, turning slowness into an outage.
  • Cascading exhaustion: B's and then A's worker pools fill with waiting requests (Little's Law: in-flight = rate × 40 s), so even endpoints that don't use C fail.

Fixes: propagate a deadline (A passes its remaining ~4.5 s budget; B and C stop when it's spent); timeouts decrease down the stack and are based on real latency percentiles; retry at one layer only with a retry budget; add a circuit breaker on B → C with a fallback; and bulkhead C's calls into their own bounded pool so C's slowness can only consume that pool.
