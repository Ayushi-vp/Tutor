An engineering team runs the checkout service for an online store. The product manager wants to ship a new feature every week. The on-call engineers, woken three times last month, want to slow down and fix things. Nobody can settle the argument, because nobody can say how reliable checkout actually is, how reliable it needs to be, or whether last month was bad or normal. Their monitoring doesn't help: it has 140 alerts — CPU above 80%, disk above 70%, a pod restarted, a queue longer than 1,000 — that fire constantly, mostly for things users never notice, so real problems get lost in the noise. When a real outage happens, it's a customer tweet that tells them, twenty minutes in, and then six people jump into a call and all start debugging different things at once.

Site reliability engineering (SRE), the discipline Google formalised and much of the industry adopted, offers a way out of all three problems. First, define reliability in terms users feel: measure the fraction of checkout requests that succeed quickly (a service level indicator, SLI), and set a target for it (a service level objective, SLO), such as 99.9% over 28 days. Second, treat the gap to 100% as an error budget — a quantity of unreliability the team is allowed to spend — so "ship faster or slow down?" becomes a question with an answer: if there's budget left, ship; if it's gone, fix. Third, alert on the budget burning, not on every internal metric, so every page means users are affected and something must be done now.

Behind that sits observability: the metrics, logs and traces that let you understand what a system of dozens of services is doing, find which component is failing, and answer questions nobody thought of in advance. And when things do break, incident response — clear roles, mitigation before diagnosis, blameless learning afterwards — determines whether an outage lasts ten minutes or three hours, and whether it happens again.

This lesson covers all of it: choosing SLIs and SLOs, error budgets and the policies that make them bite, burn-rate alerting with the actual numbers, the golden signals, what metrics, logs and traces are each good for and how OpenTelemetry ties them together, the scaling problems of observability itself (cardinality, sampling, cost), and how to run and learn from incidents — with a worked example of setting all of this up for a checkout service.

The analogy: a delivery company's promise

A parcel company promises "99% of parcels delivered on time". That sentence contains the whole idea. The indicator is the fraction of parcels delivered on time — measured, from the customer's point of view, by counting parcels. The objective is 99%. The 1% gap is a budget: the company can afford about one late parcel in a hundred. If this month they've been late on only 0.3%, they can afford to try a new route-planning system; if they're already at 1.2%, they stop experimenting and fix the fleet. If a big business customer has a contract saying "we get a refund if on-time delivery falls below 98%", that's the agreement — deliberately looser than the internal 99%, so the company notices trouble long before it owes refunds.

The company doesn't page a manager every time a van's engine light comes on (a cause); it pages when parcels start arriving late at a rate that threatens the monthly promise (a symptom). And to find out why parcels are late, it has dashboards of van counts and depot throughput (metrics), a log of every scan (logs), and the full journey of any individual parcel through every depot (a trace).

The analogy breaks in one way: a parcel takes days and a manager can look at it. A web request takes milliseconds, passes through dozens of services, and there are millions of them a minute, so everything must be measured automatically and summarised statistically.

The pieces and how they connect

Every piece is simple on its own. The discipline is in connecting them: SLIs that genuinely reflect users, targets that product and engineering agreed on, alerts derived from the budget rather than invented one at a time, and observability data good enough that an on-call engineer woken at 3 a.m. can get from "the budget is burning" to "this deploy of this service broke this endpoint" in minutes.

A key shift in thinking is from causes to symptoms. High CPU is a cause that may or may not matter; failed checkouts are a symptom that always matters. You page on symptoms, and use causes — CPU, queue depth, replication lag — as clues during diagnosis. This single change removes most alert noise and catches failures that no cause-based alert anticipated.

A second shift is from opinion to arithmetic. Without SLOs, "is the service reliable enough?" is argued from memory of the last bad week. With them, it's a number on a dashboard — budget remaining — that everyone agreed means something in advance. That makes the conversation between product and engineering short and friendly: if the budget is healthy, ship; if it's nearly spent, the next sprint goes to the reliability fixes the post-mortems already identified. The rest of this lesson builds each piece, starting with how to measure the right thing, because every later decision depends on the SLI actually reflecting what users feel.

1. SLIs: measuring what users experience

A service level indicator is a measurement of the service as users experience it, best expressed as a ratio: good events divided by valid events. Examples:

KindSLI definitionGood for
AvailabilityRequests answered without a server error ÷ valid requestsAPIs, web pages
LatencyRequests completed in under 300 ms ÷ valid requestsInteractive endpoints
FreshnessQueries whose data is less than 5 minutes old ÷ queriesPipelines, search indexes, dashboards
CorrectnessOutputs passing a validation check ÷ outputsData processing, ML predictions
Throughput / completionJobs finished within their deadline ÷ jobs startedBatch systems

Three decisions make an SLI good or useless. What counts as valid: client errors like 400 Bad Request or 404 are usually excluded (they're the client's fault), and health checks and synthetic traffic are excluded. What counts as good: for availability, 5xx responses and timeouts are bad; for latency, anything slower than the threshold is bad. Where it's measured: as close to the user as practical. Measuring inside the application misses requests that never reached it (a crashed pod, a broken load balancer); measuring at the load balancer catches those; client-side telemetry (from browsers and apps) catches even network problems but is noisier. Many teams use the load balancer as the primary source and client telemetry as a cross-check.

The ratio form matters because it makes every SLI behave the same way: it has a target, a budget and a burn rate, and it aggregates correctly across servers and time windows (you add up good events and valid events, rather than averaging percentages).

Choose a small number of SLIs per service, tied to user journeys: "browse catalogue", "add to cart", "check out". A service with twenty SLIs has none that anyone watches.

2. SLOs: how reliable is reliable enough?

A service level objective is a target for an SLI over a window: "99.9% of checkout requests succeed, over a rolling 28 days"; "99% of search requests complete in under 400 ms and 99.9% in under 1 s, over 28 days". Rolling windows (the last 28 or 30 days) are common for engineering decisions; calendar months are often used for customer reporting.

Why not 100%? Because users can't tell the difference beyond a point: their own Wi-Fi, mobile network and devices fail far more often than 0.01% of the time, so a service at 99.99% already looks perfect to them. Because each extra nine costs roughly ten times more in redundancy, engineering effort and slower change. And because 100% means never changing anything, since every change carries risk. The target should be as reliable as users need, and no more, with the remaining margin spent on moving faster.

How to choose the number. Start from users: what failure rate and latency make them unhappy or leave? Look at history: an SLO the service has never met creates constant alarm, and one far below its real performance lets it degrade silently. Account for dependencies (sc1): if checkout needs a payment provider with 99.95% availability and a database with 99.95%, checkout can't promise 99.99% without redundancy or fallbacks. Differentiate by journey: checkout deserves a stricter SLO than product recommendations. Write the reasoning down next to the SLO and review it quarterly.

Latency SLOs are best written as a proportion of requests under a threshold, often with two thresholds for typical and tail experience, rather than "p99 must be under 400 ms". The proportion form has a budget (1% of requests may be slower than 400 ms), aggregates correctly, and is computed exactly from histogram buckets, whereas percentiles can't be averaged across servers or time windows.

An SLA (service level agreement) is different in kind: it's a contract with customers, with consequences — typically service credits — if a promised level is missed. SLAs are set looser than SLOs (an internal 99.95% SLO behind a published 99.9% SLA) so the team is alerted and responding long before contractual penalties apply. Many internal services have SLOs and no SLA at all.

3. Error budgets and the policy that makes them real

The error budget is one minus the SLO: the amount of unreliability allowed in the window. For a 99.9% SLO:

SLOBudget (fraction)Per 30 days, as full downtimeWith 100M requests per 30 days
99%1%7.2 hours1,000,000 failed requests
99.9%0.1%43.2 minutes100,000
99.95%0.05%21.6 minutes50,000
99.99%0.01%4.3 minutes10,000

Request-based budgets are usually better than time-based ones, because they weight failures by the number of users affected: a partial failure at peak and a full outage at 3 a.m. are compared by the requests that actually failed.

The budget turns the ship-versus-stabilise argument into arithmetic. Deploys, experiments and migrations all spend some budget through the failures they occasionally cause. While budget remains, the team is supposed to spend it: ship features, try risky changes, run chaos experiments. When it's exhausted, reliability work takes priority until the service is back within its SLO. Both sides get something: product gets permission to move fast most of the time; operations gets an objective trigger for slowing down.

That only works if the consequences are agreed before anyone needs them. An error budget policy, signed off by engineering and product leadership, spells them out — for example:

  • If the service exceeds its error budget over the 28-day window, feature releases pause (except security and reliability fixes) until it's back within SLO.
  • Any single incident consuming more than 20% of the budget gets a post-mortem with at least one priority action item.
  • If the budget is consistently under-used, the team may release more aggressively, and the SLO is reviewed in case it's too loose.
  • Budget burned by a dependency counts, but its post-mortem actions go to the dependency's owners.

Without such a policy, a budget is just a number on a dashboard that loses every argument with a deadline.

4. Alerting on burn rate

If the SLO defines what matters, alerts should fire when the SLO is threatened — and not otherwise. The tool is the burn rate: the rate at which the budget is being consumed, relative to the rate that would spend it exactly over the window.

burn rate = observed error rate ÷ error rate allowed by the SLO

With a 99.9% SLO (0.1% allowed), a 0.1% error rate is a burn rate of 1 — on track to use exactly the whole budget in 30 days. A 1% error rate is a burn rate of 10: the budget would be gone in 3 days. A 5% error rate is 50: gone in about 14 hours. A 100% outage is 1,000: gone in 43 minutes.

Alerting on a fixed error rate ("page if errors exceed 1% for 5 minutes") has two failure modes: a short spike pages someone at night for something that barely touched the budget, and a steady 0.5% leak never pages at all while quietly spending the whole month's budget in six days. Burn-rate alerts fix both by asking "at this rate, how much budget will we lose, and how soon?"

The Google SRE workbook's recommended setup uses multiple burn rates with multiple windows:

SeverityBurn rateLong windowShort windowBudget consumed when it fires
Page14.41 hour5 minutes2% of the 30-day budget
Page66 hours30 minutes5%
Ticket13 days6 hours10%

The long window makes the alert significant (a real fraction of the budget has been spent); the short window confirms the problem is still happening, so the alert fires quickly at the start of a serious outage and resets soon after recovery instead of staying red for an hour. With these three rules, a complete outage pages within about two minutes, a 1.5% error rate pages within an hour, and a slow 0.2% leak becomes a ticket for daytime work. The number 14.4 isn't magic: it's the burn rate that spends 2% of a 30-day budget in one hour (0.02 × 720 hours).

Whatever the thresholds, every page must be urgent (someone must act now), actionable (there's something they can do) and linked to a runbook describing how to diagnose and mitigate it. Cause-based signals — CPU, disk, queue depth — still belong on dashboards and sometimes in tickets (a disk that will be full in two days deserves a ticket), but they rarely deserve a page on their own. One exception: saturation signals that reliably predict imminent user impact, like a disk that will fill in an hour, can page, because waiting for the symptom would be too late.

5. The signals to watch: golden signals, RED and USE

For any user-facing service, Google's SRE book recommends four golden signals:

  • Latency: how long requests take, measured with histograms and reported as percentiles, with successful and failed requests separated — fast failures (an immediate 500) can make overall latency look healthy while users suffer.
  • Traffic: demand on the system — requests per second, concurrent sessions, bytes per second.
  • Errors: the rate of failed requests, including explicit errors (5xx), implicit ones (a 200 with wrong content) and policy failures (slower than the SLO threshold).
  • Saturation: how full the service is — thread and connection pool usage, queue lengths, memory, the most constrained resource. It rises before errors do, so it's the early warning.

Two shorthand methods apply the same ideas systematically (sc1 introduced them). RED — rate, errors, duration — for every service and endpoint. USE — utilisation, saturation, errors — for every resource: CPUs, disks, network interfaces, connection pools, locks. RED tells you a service is unhealthy; USE helps find which resource is the reason.

Latency deserves care. Store it as a histogram — counts of requests in latency buckets (under 10 ms, under 25 ms, …) — rather than as pre-computed percentiles, because histograms from many servers can be added together and percentiles then estimated from the total, while percentiles from different servers can't be averaged. And look at the tail: p99 and p99.9 are where fan-out and heavy users make problems visible first (sc1).

6. Metrics, logs and traces

Observability data comes in three main forms, each with different strengths and costs.

Metrics are numeric time series: a request counter, an error counter, a latency histogram, a gauge of queue length, each with a few labels (endpoint, status, region). Metrics are cheap to collect and store because they're pre-aggregated: a service handling a million requests per minute emits the same few numbers per series each scrape interval regardless of volume. They're ideal for dashboards, SLIs and alerts, and for seeing trends over months. They can't tell you about individual requests. Prometheus is the most common open-source metrics system; it scrapes metrics from services every 15-60 seconds and stores them in a time-series database.

Logs are records of individual events: a request handled, an error thrown, a user signed in. Structured logs are written as key-value records (usually JSON) with consistent fields — timestamp, level, service, trace ID, user ID, message, error — so they can be filtered and aggregated like data instead of grepped as text. Logs carry the detail that explains why something happened. Their cost grows with traffic, so at scale teams log errors fully, sample or drop routine success logs, and keep different retention for different levels.

Traces follow one request through a distributed system. When a request enters, it gets a trace ID; every operation it triggers — each service call, database query, cache lookup — records a span with the trace ID, its parent span, a start time, a duration and attributes. The trace context travels with every downstream call in headers (the W3C Trace Context standard defines the traceparent header). Assembled, the spans show the request's full path as a waterfall: which services it touched, in what order, where the time went, and which call failed.

The three work together in a typical investigation. A burn-rate alert fires on a metric: checkout errors are up. The dashboard's latency and error metrics, broken down by endpoint and version, narrow it to one endpoint after a deploy. An exemplar — a trace ID attached to a metric sample — or a trace search for failed checkouts shows the failing span is the call to the inventory service. That span's trace ID leads to inventory's structured logs for those requests, which show the actual exception. Linking by trace ID is what makes this take minutes instead of hours.

OpenTelemetry (OTel) is the open, vendor-neutral standard for producing this data: APIs and SDKs for most languages, automatic instrumentation for common frameworks and clients, the context propagation that carries trace IDs across services, and a collector that receives, processes (for example samples) and exports data to whatever back ends a team uses. Standardising on it means instrumentation is written once and back ends can change without re-instrumenting every service.

7. Observability at scale: cardinality, sampling and cost

Observability systems have their own scaling limits, and they surprise teams.

Cardinality. Every unique combination of a metric's label values is a separate time series that the metrics system must store and index. A request counter labelled by endpoint (50 values) × status code (10) × region (5) is 2,500 series — easy. Add a user_id label with a million users and it becomes 2.5 billion series, which will exhaust a Prometheus server's memory and a managed vendor's budget. Labels must have small, bounded sets of values; per-user, per-request and per-order detail belongs on spans and in logs, which are designed for high-cardinality data.

Trace sampling. Recording every span of every request is usually too expensive at scale. Head-based sampling decides at the start of each request — keep 1% at random — and propagates the decision so every service keeps or drops the same traces. It's cheap but mostly discards what you care about: the rare errors and slow requests. Tail-based sampling buffers all spans of each trace in a collector until the trace completes, then decides: keep every trace with an error or with latency above a threshold, plus a small random sample of normal ones. It keeps the interesting traces but requires a collector tier with enough memory and with all of a trace's spans routed to the same collector instance. Many teams combine a moderate head sampling rate with tail-based rules.

Log volume and retention. Logs are often the largest observability cost. Practical controls: structured logs with sensible levels; full logging of errors and warnings, sampling of routine successes; dropping noisy logs at the collector; tiered retention (days of hot, searchable logs; months in cheap object storage); and never logging secrets or unnecessary personal data, which is also a compliance requirement.

Cost as a design constraint. Observability can easily cost a noticeable fraction of infrastructure spend. Mature teams review what they collect, delete dashboards and metrics nobody uses, and prefer metrics for anything aggregated, traces for request-level questions, and logs for detail — each where it's cheapest for the job.

8. Incident response

When an alert fires and users are affected, how the response is run often matters more than how clever the responders are. The practices below come from Google's SRE book and from emergency services' incident command systems.

Declare early. If users are affected, declare an incident and open a dedicated channel. Declaring costs little; delaying while one person quietly debugs costs minutes of budget and loses the chance to get help.

Assign roles. The incident commander coordinates: sets priorities, assigns work, decides, and doesn't debug. An operations lead (and helpers) investigates and makes changes. A communications lead posts updates to stakeholders and customers on a regular cadence (for example every 30 minutes, even if there's nothing new). A scribe keeps a timeline. In small teams one person may hold two roles, but the coordination role must exist, or six people end up debugging six theories in parallel and making conflicting changes.

Mitigate first, diagnose later. The goal during an incident is to restore service, not to understand it. Most incidents are caused by changes — deploys, configuration changes, feature-flag flips, data migrations — so the first question is "what changed?", and the first action is often to roll it back. Other fast mitigations: fail over to another region or replica, shed load or rate-limit, disable a feature flag, scale up, drain a bad instance or zone. Root-cause analysis happens after users are safe.

Confirm recovery with the SLIs, not with "the error in the logs stopped". Then hand over or close, noting any temporary mitigations that need proper fixes.

Two metrics are commonly tracked: MTTD (mean time to detect) and MTTR (mean time to recover). Burn-rate alerting shortens detection; fast, safe rollback and good runbooks shorten recovery. They're useful trends but noisy averages over few incidents, so they shouldn't become targets that people game.

9. Blameless post-mortems

After significant incidents — typically any that consumed a notable share of the error budget, required paging, or affected customers visibly — the team writes a post-mortem (also called an incident review): a document with a summary, the impact (duration, users and requests affected, budget consumed), a detailed timeline, the contributing factors, what went well, what went badly, where the team got lucky, and action items with owners and due dates.

It must be blameless. Complex systems fail through combinations of factors: a change process that allowed a risky change straight to production, a missing validation, a dashboard that hid the key signal, an alert that didn't fire, a runbook that was out of date. The engineer who ran the last command is almost never the cause; if one keystroke can take down production, the system is the problem. Blame makes people hide information and avoid admitting mistakes, which destroys the learning that prevents the next incident. The question is always "how did the system allow this, and how do we change it?"

Good action items change systems: add a validation, add a canary stage, automate the rollback, add the missing alert, fix the confusing tool. Weak ones rely on people: "be more careful", "remember to check". Action items are tracked like any other work, and teams review whether they were actually completed — an unfinished action item from last quarter's post-mortem is a very common contributing factor in this quarter's.

10. A real-world walk-through: setting up SLOs and alerting for checkout

Back to the checkout team from the opening. Their service handles about 200 million requests per 30 days, peaking around 150 requests per second. Here's how they set things up, with the reasoning.

Choose SLIs from user journeys. Two journeys matter most: viewing the cart and completing a purchase. The team defines two SLIs, both measured at the load balancer: availability — requests to checkout endpoints that don't return a 5xx or time out, divided by all requests except 4xx client errors and health checks; and latency — those requests completing under 500 ms, and under 2 s, divided by the same.

Set targets. History shows 99.95% availability and 99.3% of requests under 500 ms over the past quarter. Users complain when checkout fails but rarely notice latency under half a second. Checkout depends on the payment provider, whose published SLA is 99.95%. The team sets availability at 99.9% over 28 days (achievable with margin, and above what users notice), latency at 99% under 500 ms and 99.9% under 2 s. They publish these so the teams calling checkout and the teams it calls know what to design for.

Compute the budget. 0.1% of about 187 million requests in 28 days is roughly 187,000 failed requests, or about 40 minutes of total outage.

Agree the policy. With the product manager and engineering director: if the 28-day availability SLO is breached, only reliability and security changes ship until it recovers; any incident using more than 20% of budget gets a post-mortem with a priority action; the SLO is reviewed each quarter.

Replace the alerts. The 140 cause-based alerts are deleted or turned into dashboard panels and tickets. In their place: three burn-rate alerts per SLI (page at 14.4× over 1 h confirmed over 5 min, page at 6× over 6 h confirmed over 30 min, ticket at 1× over 3 days confirmed over 6 h), each with a runbook that starts with "check recent deploys and flag changes; roll back if one correlates". A few saturation alerts remain as tickets (database disk forecast to fill in 3 days). Pages drop from about twenty a month to two or three, each one real.

Instrument for diagnosis. The service uses OpenTelemetry auto-instrumentation for its HTTP server, HTTP clients and database driver, adds spans around the payment and inventory calls with order ID and tenant as span attributes, and propagates trace context to all downstream services. RED metrics per endpoint, per deploy version and per downstream dependency go to Prometheus as histograms, with exemplars. Logs are structured JSON with trace IDs; errors are kept in full, successful requests sampled at 1%. Traces are tail-sampled: all errors, everything over 1 s, and 2% of the rest.

Test it. In a game day, they deploy a version that fails 5% of checkouts to a canary slice of traffic. The canary analysis — comparing the canary's error rate with the baseline — halts the rollout within ten minutes. They then deliberately push it to all traffic in a staging environment with production-like load: the 14.4× burn alert fires in about five minutes (5% errors is a burn rate of 50), the runbook points to the deploy, and rollback takes three minutes. Previously, the same bug in production had taken 90 minutes to notice and fix. The difference — detection in minutes, a clear first action, a practised rollback — is what this whole lesson is for.

SLOs for dependencies and the arithmetic of composition

A service's achievable availability is bounded by its critical dependencies. If a request needs services A, B and C, each at 99.9%, and failures are independent, the best case is about 99.7% — so a 99.9% SLO for the top service is impossible unless dependencies are made non-critical. The tools are the ones from sc12: fallbacks that turn a dependency failure into a degraded success, caching, asynchronous processing, and redundancy across independent providers or regions. This is why SLOs cascade downward: a service with a 99.95% SLO needs its critical dependencies at a higher level, or needs to not depend on them synchronously. Publishing SLOs for internal platforms (databases, queues, identity) lets product teams do this arithmetic rather than discovering it in incidents. Correlated failures — shared regions, shared deploy tooling, shared configuration systems — make real composition worse than the independent-failure formula suggests, which is exactly what the June 2025 Google Cloud incident illustrated: one global policy store fed every region.

Why multi-window alerts work, and their tuning

The SRE workbook evaluates alert designs on four properties: precision (a page means a real, significant problem), recall (significant problems produce pages), detection time and reset time. A single threshold on a short window has good detection and poor precision; a long window has good precision and slow detection and reset. Pairing a long window (which ensures enough budget has been spent to matter) with a short window (which ensures the burn is still happening now) gets most of both: the alert fires only when a meaningful fraction of the budget is gone and the problem persists, and it clears within minutes of recovery. Low-traffic services need adjustments, because at a few requests per minute a single failure is a huge error rate; common remedies are longer windows, synthetic traffic to raise the event count, or combining small services into one SLO.

Change is the leading cause of incidents

Across the industry, most production incidents are triggered by changes — code deploys, configuration and policy changes, feature-flag flips, infrastructure updates, data migrations. Reliability practice therefore concentrates on making change safe: progressive delivery (canary releases to a small percentage of traffic, then a zone, then a region, then everywhere, with automated analysis comparing the canary's SLIs to the baseline at each stage), feature flags that separate deploying code from enabling it and can be turned off in seconds, fast automated rollback, and treating configuration and data with the same staged rollout as code. The Google Cloud incident is a clear example of the cost of skipping these: a code path shipped enabled everywhere without a flag, triggered by a data change that replicated globally in seconds. sc15 covers deployment strategies in detail.

Observability versus monitoring

Monitoring answers questions you anticipated: is the error rate above X, is the disk full. Observability, in the sense Charity Majors and others popularised, is the ability to answer questions you didn't anticipate — "why are checkouts slow only for customers on the new mobile app version in Brazil paying with a particular card type?" — without deploying new code. That requires rich, high-cardinality context on events (spans or wide structured events with dozens of attributes), and tools that let you slice by any attribute interactively. Pre-aggregated metrics can't do this, because the question needs dimensions nobody chose as labels in advance. The practical architecture is a combination: low-cardinality metrics for SLOs, alerts and long-term trends, and high-cardinality traces or wide events for exploration, sampled intelligently to control cost.

Toil and sustainable on-call

Google's SRE model caps toil — manual, repetitive, automatable work that scales with the service, such as restarting stuck jobs, running manual failovers or hand-editing configurations — at around half of an SRE's time, so the rest goes to engineering that removes toil permanently. On-call health is tracked explicitly: pages per shift (Google's guidance aims for no more than about two incidents per 12-hour shift, so each can be handled properly), time to acknowledge, and the fraction of pages that were actionable. Noisy, non-actionable pages are treated as bugs in the alerting. This isn't a matter of comfort: exhausted, alert-fatigued engineers miss real incidents and make mistakes during them, so sustainable on-call is a reliability feature.

Learning from incidents beyond the individual post-mortem

Individual post-mortems fix individual weaknesses; patterns across them reveal systemic ones. Mature organisations tag incidents by trigger (deploy, config, dependency, capacity, data), by detection method (alert, customer report, luck) and by contributing factors, and review them quarterly: if 40% of incidents were detected by customers, alerting needs investment; if most were triggered by configuration changes, config rollout needs staging and validation. The resilience-engineering community also stresses studying what goes right — how responders adapted and recovered — since normal work contains the adaptations that usually prevent incidents, and removing them in the name of process can make systems more fragile.

How observability and SRE practice go wrong

Alert fatigue. Hundreds of cause-based alerts, most non-actionable, train people to ignore pages; a real incident is acknowledged and dismissed as "that flaky one again". Symptom-based burn-rate alerts and ruthless deletion of noisy alerts are the fix.

SLOs nobody uses. SLOs defined once for a planning document, never tied to alerts or release decisions, with no error budget policy. They drift away from reality and carry no weight in trade-offs. SLOs must drive alerting and have an agreed policy to matter.

Measuring the wrong thing. An availability SLI computed inside the application misses requests dropped by a crashed pod or broken load balancer, so the dashboard shows 100% during an outage. Or health checks and synthetic traffic are counted as good requests, diluting real failures. Measure close to users and exclude non-user traffic.

Cardinality explosions. Someone adds a request ID, user ID or raw URL path as a metric label; the metrics system runs out of memory or the monthly bill jumps. Enforce label rules in review and with limits in the collector.

Monitoring that shares the failure. Dashboards and alerting hosted in the same region, cluster or network as the system they watch go dark during the incident they should reveal — as AWS experienced in December 2021 (sc12) and Roblox in October 2021 (sc2). Keep at least minimal, independent monitoring outside the main failure domain.

Post-mortems without follow-through. Action items written, never done, and the same failure recurs months later. Track them like product work and review completion.

What to watch about the practice itself

Error budget remaining per SLO and trend; burn-rate alert counts and their precision (what fraction of pages were real); pages per on-call shift and after-hours pages; MTTD and MTTR trends; the share of incidents detected by alerts versus customers; post-mortem action item completion rate; observability cost per service and the number of active time series.

A real incident: Google Cloud, 12 June 2025

Google Cloud's API management and control plane relies on a system called Service Control, which checks authorisation, policy and quotas for API requests in every region. On 29 May 2025 a new feature adding extra quota-policy checks was deployed to it. The new code path had no feature flag to turn it off and didn't handle policies with missing fields. It lay dormant until 12 June, when a policy change containing unintended blank fields was written to the globally replicated database that Service Control reads policies from. Within seconds every region had the bad data, the new code hit a null pointer, and the Service Control binaries crashed in a loop, so API requests across many Google Cloud and Workspace products returned errors. The engineers identified the cause and deployed a mitigation that bypassed the faulty code path within about 40 minutes, and most regions recovered over the following couple of hours. The largest region, us-central1, recovered more slowly: as Service Control tasks restarted, they all hit the policy database at once without randomised backoff, overloading it — a herd effect. The full event lasted several hours for some products, and many third-party services built on Google Cloud were affected too. Google's report lists the fixes that map onto this lesson: put new code paths behind feature flags that default to off and roll out gradually; propagate data and policy changes incrementally rather than globally; fail open or safely on bad input; add randomised exponential backoff to restarts and retries; and improve how quickly customers are informed. The lesson for SLO practice is that a service can only be as reliable as its change process and its most global dependency — and that the budget spent in one afternoon can be larger than a year's allowance.

A one-minute calculator that makes error budgets and burn rates concrete. It needs only Python 3.

slo, window_days = 0.999, 30
budget = 1 - slo                                        # allowed error ratio: 0.1%
print(f"allowed downtime per {window_days} days: {budget * window_days * 24 * 60:.1f} minutes")

def burn_rate(error_ratio):
    return error_ratio / budget                         # 1.0 = spending exactly on plan

def hours_to_exhaust(error_ratio):
    return window_days * 24 / burn_rate(error_ratio)

for err in (0.0005, 0.001, 0.005, 0.0144, 0.05):
    print(f"error rate {err:6.2%}: burn rate {burn_rate(err):5.1f}x, "
          f"budget gone in {hours_to_exhaust(err):7.1f} h")

# the SRE workbook's fast-burn page: 2% of a 30-day budget spent in 1 hour
print(f"burn rate that spends 2% of the budget in 1 h: {0.02 * window_days * 24:.1f}x")

Output:

allowed downtime per 30 days: 43.2 minutes
error rate  0.05%: burn rate   0.5x, budget gone in  1440.0 h
error rate  0.10%: burn rate   1.0x, budget gone in   720.0 h
error rate  0.50%: burn rate   5.0x, budget gone in   144.0 h
error rate  1.44%: burn rate  14.4x, budget gone in    50.0 h
error rate  5.00%: burn rate  50.0x, budget gone in    14.4 h
burn rate that spends 2% of the budget in 1 h: 14.4x

What it shows. A 99.9% SLO allows 43 minutes a month. An error rate of 0.5% sounds small but empties the budget in six days — a slow leak that a fixed "page above 1%" alert would never catch. A 5% failure (a bad deploy) empties it in about 14 hours, and the 14.4× threshold is simply the burn rate that spends 2% of the budget in one hour, which is why it's the standard fast-burn page.

Think it through. (1) Change slo to 0.9999: how many minutes remain, and what does that imply about whether humans can respond before the budget is gone? (2) A deploy fails 5% of requests for 90 minutes: what fraction of the monthly budget did it use? (3) With the 14.4× over 1 hour rule plus a 5-minute confirmation window, roughly how soon after that deploy would the page fire?

Calculate and design

An API serves 200M requests per 30 days with a 99.9% availability SLO. (a) How many failed requests is the budget? (b) On day 10, a bad deploy fails 5% of requests for 90 minutes at an even ~4,600 req/min. How much budget did it burn, and what was the burn rate? (c) What should the team do now, and what alert would have caught it faster?

Answer

(a) 0.1% × 200M = 200,000 failed requests per 30 days.

(b) 90 min × 4,600 req/min ≈ 414,000 requests × 5% ≈ 20,700 failures ≈ 10% of the budget in 1.5 hours. Burn rate = error rate ÷ allowed error rate = 5% ÷ 0.1% = 50× — at that pace the whole month's budget would be gone in 30 days ÷ 50 ≈ 14 hours.

(c) About 90% of the budget remains, so no feature freeze is needed under a typical policy — but the incident used more than a trivial share, so run a blameless post-mortem: why did the deploy reach 100% of traffic without a canary catching a 5% error rate, and why did it take 90 minutes to roll back? Actions: canary analysis that gates each rollout stage on error rate; automated rollback; a runbook whose first step is checking recent changes. The alert: a fast-burn page at ≥14.4× over 1 hour, confirmed over a 5-minute window — at 50× it would have fired within a few minutes of the deploy, not after 90.
