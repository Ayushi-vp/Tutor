A company's product began as one application: one codebase, one database, one deployment. With ten engineers it was a joy — easy to understand, easy to test, easy to run. Five years later there are two hundred engineers in twenty teams, and the same monolith has become the bottleneck for everything. A release goes out once a week and bundles the work of all twenty teams; if one team's change has a bug, the whole release is rolled back and everyone waits. The test suite takes two hours. A change to the order code breaks the search code, because both reach into the same tables. The recommendation feature needs twenty times more CPU than the rest but can't be scaled on its own. Nobody understands the whole thing any more.

Splitting the system into microservices — small, independently deployable services, each owned by one team and each owning its data — promises to fix that: every team ships on its own schedule, scales its own service, and chooses its own tools. Many large companies run this way. But the same move has hurt many others. Teams of eight split a monolith into fourteen services and discovered that every feature now needs coordinated changes to four of them, that a page load triggers six network calls in a row, that one small service's outage takes down checkout, and that they spend more time on deployment pipelines and dashboards than on the product. Some companies — Segment and Amazon Prime Video among them — have publicly moved parts of their systems back to monoliths and cut their costs and pages dramatically.

The difference between those outcomes lies in a few decisions this lesson teaches: whether the organisation actually needs what microservices buy, where to draw the boundaries (and why data ownership matters more than code), how services should talk to each other, how to change their contracts without breaking anyone, and how to migrate without a risky rewrite. Along the way you'll meet Conway's law, bounded contexts, the distributed monolith, REST versus gRPC versus GraphQL, consumer-driven contract tests and the strangler fig pattern — and a worked case of splitting an e-commerce monolith the right way.

The analogy: one big restaurant versus a food court

A monolith is a single large restaurant kitchen. Everyone shares the same space, equipment and pantry. Coordinating is easy — you shout across the room — and a dish that needs pasta and sauce and cheese is assembled in one place. But as the kitchen grows to a hundred cooks, they get in each other's way: the pastry chef can't change the oven schedule without upsetting the grill, a new menu goes live only when every station is ready, and one fire in the kitchen stops all service.

Microservices are a food court. Each stall is independent: its own staff, its own equipment, its own stock room, its own opening hours. The noodle stall can change its menu tomorrow without asking anyone, and if the burger stall has a problem, the others keep serving. But anything that needs several stalls becomes harder: a combo meal means walking between counters, each stall can run out independently, and you now need a food-court manager, shared cleaning, security and signage for all of them.

The analogy points to the key rules. A stall should own its own stock room (data ownership); a food court where every stall reaches into one shared fridge isn't really independent. Stalls should be divided by what customers order (business capability), not by kitchen task — a "stall that only chops vegetables" would make every meal need several stalls. And a food court makes sense for a big mall with many independent vendors; for a small café it's absurd. The analogy breaks in one way: walking between stalls takes a customer a minute, while a network call takes a millisecond — but it can fail silently, and there may be dozens of them per request.

What microservices buy, and what they cost

What microservices buy is mostly organisational: each team can change, test, deploy and operate its service on its own schedule, so twenty teams stop waiting for one release train. Secondary benefits: independent scaling (run fifty copies of search and three of payments), fault isolation (a crash in recommendations doesn't crash checkout, if designed well), and freedom to choose a different technology where it truly helps.

What they cost is mostly technical and operational: every interaction becomes a network call that can be slow or fail (sc12), there are no transactions across services (sc11), data is duplicated and eventually consistent (sc9, sc10), a single request is harder to trace and debug (sc13), testing across services is harder, and every service needs a pipeline, monitoring, alerts, security patching and an on-call owner. Scaling alone doesn't justify them — a monolith scales horizontally too, by running many copies behind a load balancer (sc1).

That gives a practical rule of thumb: microservices pay off when many teams would otherwise collide in one codebase and release process, and when the organisation has the platform maturity — automated deployment, observability, on-call practice — to run many services. For one or two teams, a well-structured modular monolith is almost always the better choice, and services can be extracted later where a specific need is proven.

1. The spectrum: monolith, modular monolith, services

Architecture isn't a binary choice. It's a spectrum, and most of the value comes from internal structure rather than from how many deployables there are.

A big ball of mud is a monolith with no internal boundaries: any code calls any other code and reads any table. It's hard to change safely whatever its size.

A modular monolith is one deployable application divided internally into modules by business capability — orders, payments, catalogue — each with a clear public interface, its own tables that other modules don't query directly, and communication through explicit function calls or in-process events. It keeps the monolith's operational simplicity (one deploy, one database, local transactions, easy debugging) while gaining most of the design benefits of services. Boundaries are enforced in code with visibility rules and automated architecture tests. Shopify runs one of the largest Ruby on Rails applications as a modular monolith for exactly these reasons.

Microservices take the same boundaries and put a network between them, gaining independent deployment and scaling, and paying the costs above. In between sit a few larger services (sometimes called macroservices), each owned by a team and containing several related capabilities — often the sweet spot for mid-sized organisations.

A modular monolith is also the best starting point for microservices later: if modules have clean interfaces and separate data, extracting one into a service is mostly mechanical. If they don't, extraction means first untangling years of cross-module queries — the hard part of every migration.

2. Drawing boundaries: business capabilities and bounded contexts

The most important decision in a service architecture is where the boundaries go, because moving functionality between services later is expensive. Three principles guide it.

Split by business capability, not technical layer. A capability is something the business does: take orders, process payments, manage the catalogue, ship parcels, recommend products. A technical layer is something every feature needs: data access, validation, notifications. Splitting by layer ("a database service", "a validation service") means every feature crosses every layer, so every feature touches every service. Splitting by capability means most features stay inside one service.

Use bounded contexts. Domain-driven design (DDD), from Eric Evans's book, offers the key tool. The same word means different things in different parts of a business. To the catalogue team, a "product" has a title, description, images and attributes. To inventory, it has stock levels per warehouse and reorder points. To pricing, it has prices per market, discounts and tax categories. Trying to build one shared "Product" model for everyone produces a huge, fragile object that every team needs to change. A bounded context is a part of the domain within which one model and one vocabulary (the ubiquitous language) apply consistently. Each context gets its own model, and contexts refer to each other by identifiers (product ID 812), translating at the boundary. Bounded contexts are the natural candidates for services.

High cohesion inside, low coupling across. Things that change together for the same reasons belong together; interactions across the boundary should be few, coarse-grained and stable. The best evidence comes from real change history: look at the last hundred features and ask which candidate services each would have touched. If most features touch both "orders" and "order-items", they're one service. If a proposed split means every release needs coordinated changes in three services, the boundary is wrong.

Within a context, DDD's aggregate is also useful: a cluster of objects changed together as a unit, with one root — an order and its line items — that forms a natural transactional boundary. A service can contain many aggregates; an aggregate shouldn't be split across services, because its invariants need one transaction.

Two sizing heuristics: start with fewer, larger services, because splitting a service later is far easier than merging two that have diverged; and make sure every service has exactly one owning team that can understand, change and operate it. A team of eight owning fourteen services is a warning sign in itself.

3. Data ownership: the rule that makes services independent

Every service owns its data: its tables, its schema, its storage technology. Other services never read or write that data directly. They go through the owner's API, or they consume the owner's events.

This is the rule most often broken, usually "for performance" or "just for this report", and breaking it creates the distributed monolith. Here's why. If the shipping service queries the orders service's tables directly, then the orders team can't rename a column, split a table, add a partition or move to a different database without first finding and changing the shipping service — and they have to deploy in a coordinated order. The schema has become a shared contract that nobody designed as one. Heavy queries from one service also slow the other, and security becomes harder to reason about.

How then does a service get data it needs from another? Three options, in increasing order of independence:

  • Call the owner's API when it's needed. Simple and always fresh, but it couples availability and latency: if the orders service is down, shipping can't get order details.
  • Subscribe to the owner's events and keep a local read model. The orders service publishes OrderPlaced and OrderUpdated events (through an outbox, sc10); the shipping service stores the few fields it needs — address, items, weight — in its own database. Shipping now works even when orders is down, and its reads are local and fast. The cost is eventual consistency (the copy lags by a moment) and storage.
  • Receive the data in the request or event itself, so there's nothing to look up. A fat OrderPlaced event carrying everything shipping needs is the extreme case.

Reports across many services are the classic reason people break the rule. The answer is a separate analytics store fed by change data capture from every service (sc8, sc18), not queries against their production databases.

4. Conway's law and team design

In 1968, Melvin Conway observed that "organisations which design systems are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organisations". Put simply: if three teams build a compiler, you get a three-pass compiler. In service architectures this is not a curiosity but a design force.

If one service is owned jointly by three teams, every change needs three teams to agree, and the service becomes a bottleneck. If one team owns pieces of five unrelated services, its attention is fragmented and every change involves negotiation with other owners. So service boundaries and team boundaries must be designed together. The inverse Conway manoeuvre does this deliberately: organise teams around the architecture you want, each owning a coherent set of capabilities end to end — build, deploy, operate, on-call.

The book Team Topologies (Skelton and Pais) gives vocabulary for it. Stream-aligned teams own a slice of the product end to end (checkout, search). Platform teams provide self-service internal products — deployment pipelines, observability, databases as a service — that reduce the cognitive load on stream-aligned teams. The key constraint is cognitive load: a team can only own as much as it can understand and operate well, which bounds how many services, and how complex, each team should have. Microservices without a good platform make every team re-solve deployment, monitoring and networking, which is where much of their reputation for overhead comes from.

5. How services talk: synchronous and asynchronous

Synchronous request/response — REST or gRPC — is the simplest model: the caller sends a request and waits for the answer. Use it when the caller genuinely needs the answer to continue: a price to show, an authorisation to proceed, a validation. Its cost is temporal coupling: the caller works only while the callee is up and fast. Along a chain, availabilities multiply (five services at 99.9% called in sequence give about 99.5%, sc1), latencies add, and tail latencies compound, because the chance that at least one hop hits its slow tail grows with each hop — you'll see this in the lab.

Asynchronous messaging — events and queues (sc10) — lets a service announce what happened (OrderPlaced) or hand off work without waiting. The producer keeps working when consumers are down; consumers process at their own pace; new consumers can be added without changing the producer. The costs are eventual consistency, harder end-to-end tracing, and schema management for events.

A common and effective pattern combines them: state changes propagate as events, each service keeps a local read model of the data it needs from others, and synchronous calls are reserved for commands and queries that truly need an immediate, authoritative answer (authorise this payment; is this item in stock right now for checkout). Where a synchronous call remains, it gets the full resilience toolkit from sc12: a timeout derived from a deadline, a circuit breaker, a fallback, and a bulkhead.

Watch for chatty interfaces: an operation that needs many small calls between two services (get order, then get each line, then get each product) multiplies latency and failure points. Coarse-grained APIs that return what the caller needs in one call, batching, and aggregation in a backend-for-frontend (sc2) are the fixes — and a chatty interface between two services is often a sign they should be one.

6. API styles: REST, gRPC and GraphQL

Three styles dominate service APIs, each suited to different jobs (swe7 covers API design in depth).

REST models the API as resources identified by URLs (/orders/8812), manipulated with HTTP methods (GET, POST, PUT, PATCH, DELETE) and status codes, usually with JSON bodies. It's universal — every language and tool speaks it — easy to explore and debug with curl, and HTTP caches, proxies and CDNs understand it. It's the default for public APIs and a fine choice internally. Its weaknesses: JSON is verbose, contracts are often informal unless you add OpenAPI specifications, and there's no built-in streaming or deadline propagation.

gRPC defines services and messages in Protocol Buffers (Protobuf), a schema language with a compact binary encoding in which each field has a number. From the schema it generates client and server code in many languages, so calls look like typed function calls. It runs over HTTP/2 (net8), supporting streaming in both directions, and propagates deadlines and cancellation natively. Messages are typically several times smaller and faster to parse than JSON. That makes it popular for internal service-to-service traffic. Its costs: browsers can't call it directly without a proxy (gRPC-Web), binary payloads are harder to inspect, and its long-lived HTTP/2 connections need request-level load balancing (sc2's drill).

GraphQL exposes a typed schema of the whole data graph and lets clients send queries that name exactly the fields they want, possibly spanning several backend services, in one request. It solves over-fetching (getting fields you don't need) and under-fetching (needing several calls) for client applications, which is why it's often used as an aggregation layer in front of microservices, in the backend-for-frontend role. Its costs: HTTP caching is harder because queries usually go through one POST endpoint, a single query can be very expensive (so servers need depth limits, complexity scoring and timeouts), and naive resolvers cause N+1 query problems (sc4) that need batching (the DataLoader pattern).

7. Contracts and how to evolve them

A service's API and events are contracts with every consumer. The whole value of independent deployment depends on being able to change a service without coordinating with its consumers, which means changes must not break them.

Backwards-compatible changes are the default. Safe: adding a new optional field to a response, adding a new endpoint or method, adding an optional request parameter with a default, accepting a new value where consumers were told to expect more (and being careful with enumerations, since an old consumer may not know a new value). Unsafe: removing or renaming a field, changing a field's type or meaning, making an optional field required, changing error semantics. In Protobuf, fields are identified by number, so renaming is harmless on the wire but reusing a field number for a different meaning silently corrupts data for old readers — deleted field numbers are marked reserved.

Tolerant readers. Consumers should ignore fields they don't recognise and cope with optional fields being absent. Then providers can add fields freely.

Expand and contract. For changes that would otherwise break consumers, do them in steps that never break anyone. To rename zip to postal_code: first add postal_code alongside zip, populating both (expand); migrate every consumer to read postal_code; then stop sending zip (contract). The same pattern applies to database schema changes (sc15, swe9).

Versioning is for genuinely breaking changes: a new major version (/v2/orders, a new Protobuf package, a new event type or topic), run side by side with the old one, with a published deprecation timeline, and removed only when monitoring shows no remaining callers. Versions are expensive — every one must be maintained, tested and secured — so compatible evolution is always preferred. Semantic versioning (major.minor.patch) communicates the difference for libraries and schemas.

Consumer-driven contract tests make compatibility checkable. Each consumer writes tests that describe the exact requests it sends and the parts of responses it relies on; the tool (Pact is the common one) turns these into a contract shared with the provider. The provider's build verifies it still satisfies every consumer's contract before it can deploy. That catches a breaking change in the provider's pipeline, not in production, and it replaces much of the need for large, slow, flaky end-to-end test environments with dozens of services. For events, a schema registry (sc10) plays the same role, rejecting incompatible schema versions.

8. The distributed monolith and other anti-patterns

The most common failure of microservice adoption is the distributed monolith: services that can't be changed or deployed independently. Its symptoms are recognisable:

  • Most features require coordinated changes to several services, released together or in a strict order.
  • Services read each other's databases, or share one database.
  • A shared library (often of domain models) must be upgraded in lockstep across services.
  • Long synchronous call chains mean one service's outage takes down many others.
  • A single end-to-end test environment with every service is required before any release.

It has all the costs of distribution and none of the independence — worse than either a monolith or real services. Other anti-patterns: nanoservices (services so small that overhead dominates and every operation spans many of them); entity services (a "customer service" and an "order service" that are thin CRUD (create, read, update, delete) wrappers over tables, so all business logic lives in orchestrating services that call them all); shared-everything platforms where one team's change to a common gateway or library breaks everyone; and premature splitting of a domain the team doesn't yet understand, which fixes the wrong boundaries in place.

The remedies follow from the principles: merge services that always change together; enforce data ownership with events and local read models; make shared libraries small and technical (logging, auth clients), never shared domain models; replace synchronous chains on hot paths with local data; and require each service to justify its independent existence.

9. Migrating from a monolith: the strangler fig

Rewriting a large system from scratch is famously risky: the old system keeps changing during the rewrite, the new one takes longer than planned, and cut-over day is a cliff. The strangler fig pattern, named by Martin Fowler after vines that grow around a tree until they replace it, migrates gradually instead.

  1. Put a routing layer in front of the monolith — a reverse proxy or API gateway (sc2) — through which all traffic flows. Initially it sends everything to the monolith.
  2. Pick one capability to extract: one with a clear boundary, limited data entanglement, and a real reason to separate (a different scaling profile, release cadence or owning team). Notifications, search or a reporting module are common first choices; the core of checkout usually isn't.
  3. Build the new service with its own data. During transition, synchronise data between the monolith and the new service — typically with change data capture from the monolith's database, or events — and put an anti-corruption layer between them, translating the monolith's model into the new service's clean one so legacy concepts don't leak in.
  4. Shift traffic gradually: shadow traffic first (send requests to both and compare results), then a small percentage of real traffic, then all of it, with a quick path back.
  5. Remove the old code path from the monolith once the new service carries everything.
  6. Repeat, and stop when the remaining monolith no longer benefits from being split. A smaller, well-modularised monolith surrounded by a few services is a perfectly good end state.

The hard part is almost always the data: foreign keys and joins that cross the new boundary, reports that read everything, and code that reaches into other modules' tables. Modularising the monolith's data first — giving each module its own tables and removing cross-module queries — makes every later extraction far easier.

10. A real-world walk-through: splitting an e-commerce monolith

An online retailer has a Rails monolith and a PostgreSQL database, 120 engineers in 12 teams, weekly releases that are frequently rolled back, and a search feature whose load dwarfs everything else. Leadership asks for microservices. Here's how a careful architect approaches it.

Check the need. Twelve teams colliding in one release train is a genuine reason; search's different scaling profile is another. The organisation already has CI/CD (continuous integration and delivery pipelines), centralised logging and on-call rotations, and a platform team willing to provide a paved road (standard service template, deploy pipeline, dashboards, tracing). The case is real.

Find the boundaries from the domain and from history. Workshops with domain experts identify bounded contexts: catalogue, search, pricing, inventory, cart and checkout, payments, orders and fulfilment, customer accounts, notifications. Analysing a year of commits shows that cart and checkout changed together in 80% of features, so they'll be one service; pricing and promotions likewise. The result is eight target services, each mapped to one of the existing teams (some teams own two; the platform team owns none). They deliberately don't create a "product service" shared by everyone — catalogue, pricing and inventory each keep their own view of a product, referenced by ID.

Modularise first. For six months, teams enforce module boundaries inside the monolith: each module gets its own database schema, cross-module joins are replaced with calls through module interfaces, and architecture tests fail the build on violations. This alone reduces rollbacks, because changes stop leaking across modules.

Extract in order of value and ease. A gateway is put in front of the monolith. Notifications goes first: few dependencies, consumes events, and a good way to build the event infrastructure (outbox plus Kafka, sc10). Search goes second, because it has the biggest scaling benefit: it consumes catalogue, pricing and inventory change events into its own search index (sc8) and serves queries independently, scaled to forty instances while the monolith needs eight. Payments goes third, because PCI compliance (the card-industry security standard) is simpler with a small, isolated service. Checkout and orders remain in the monolith for now: they're tightly bound to the core database and the benefit of extracting them is lower.

Communication and contracts. Services publish state changes as events with schemas in a registry. Internal synchronous calls use gRPC with deadlines and circuit breakers; the public API and the mobile app go through REST at the gateway, with a backend-for-frontend for mobile. Every provider runs consumer contract tests in its pipeline. Checkout calls payments synchronously (it needs the authorisation) using an idempotency key, with a saga for the order flow (sc11).

Results after eighteen months. Search, payments and notifications deploy many times a day independently. The monolith, smaller and modular, deploys daily instead of weekly, with far fewer rollbacks. Search's scaling costs fell because only search scales with search traffic. What they didn't do is also part of the answer: they didn't split into fifty services, didn't extract checkout before its data was untangled, and didn't let any service read another's tables. That restraint is what separates this outcome from the distributed monoliths in the opening.

Measuring coupling: the change-coupling test

Boundaries can be evaluated empirically. Change coupling (also called logical or temporal coupling in code analysis) looks at version history: which files, modules or services change together in the same commits or pull requests? Tools that analyse Git history (for example CodeScene, or simple scripts over git log) reveal clusters that always change together — candidates to keep in one service — and pairs of services that change together frequently, a strong sign of a misplaced boundary. Deployment coupling can be measured too: how often do releases of one service require releases of another? Tracking these over time turns "are our boundaries right?" from opinion into data, and it's a persuasive answer to "how would you validate a service decomposition?"

The fallacies of distributed computing

Peter Deutsch and colleagues at Sun listed assumptions that engineers moving from in-process calls to networks tend to make, all false: the network is reliable; latency is zero; bandwidth is infinite; the network is secure; topology doesn't change; there is one administrator; transport cost is zero; the network is homogeneous. Every one bites microservices: calls fail and time out (sc12), latency accumulates along call chains, payloads and chatty interfaces consume bandwidth and CPU for serialisation, every hop needs authentication and encryption (mTLS via a mesh, sc2), instances move constantly (service discovery), different teams operate different parts, and data transfer between zones and regions costs money. Designing a service interaction means consciously handling each fallacy rather than assuming a network call behaves like a function call.

Data consistency across services in practice

Once each service owns its data, several patterns keep the whole system coherent. Events plus local read models give eventual consistency with high availability. Sagas coordinate multi-step business operations with compensation (sc11). The outbox and inbox make event publication and consumption reliable. Reference data that everyone needs (currencies, countries) is published as events or small, versioned datasets rather than shared tables. Queries spanning services are served by composition in an API layer for small, interactive views, or by a dedicated read store built from events (a CQRS read model, sc10) for heavy ones, and by the analytics warehouse for reports. The architectural skill is choosing, per piece of data, who owns it, who needs copies, and how stale those copies may be — and documenting that so teams don't quietly reintroduce shared tables.

Testing strategy for microservices

Full end-to-end test environments with every service deployed are slow to set up, flaky, and become shared bottlenecks — reintroducing the coordination microservices were meant to remove. Mature teams shift testing: thorough unit and component tests within each service (with dependencies replaced by test doubles, swe5), consumer-driven contract tests for every interaction, a small number of end-to-end smoke tests for the most critical journeys, and heavy reliance on production safety nets — canary releases, feature flags, fast rollback and SLO-based monitoring (sc13, sc15). Testing in production, done carefully with these controls, finds the integration problems that no staging environment reproduces, because staging never matches production's data, traffic and configuration exactly.

Cost and efficiency: when distribution is wasteful

Amazon Prime Video's 2023 case study is instructive precisely because it came from inside Amazon. A service that monitored the quality of video streams had been built as a distributed pipeline of serverless functions coordinated by a workflow service, passing video frames between components through object storage. At scale, the orchestration and data transfer between components dominated cost and hit scaling limits. Moving the components into a single process, where frames passed in memory, cut infrastructure costs by about 90% while keeping the same logical components. The lesson isn't "serverless and microservices are bad" but that distribution has a per-interaction cost, and for high-volume, tightly coupled data flows, co-locating components can be far cheaper — while the internal modularity is kept.

Cells and the limits of service independence

At very large scale, organisations sometimes find that even well-separated services share a blast radius: a bad deploy of one service affects all customers at once, and a global dependency takes everything down. Cell-based architecture partitions the whole stack — gateway, services, data — into independent copies (cells), each serving a subset of customers, with a thin routing layer assigning customers to cells (sc17). Changes roll out cell by cell, and a failure is contained to one cell's customers. It's orthogonal to service decomposition: services divide the system by capability, cells divide it by customer, and large platforms such as AWS use both. It's a natural next step once a microservice system's main risk becomes correlated failure rather than team coordination.

How microservice systems fail in production

Cascading failures across the call graph. One service slows down, its callers' threads fill up waiting, and the failure spreads upstream until the user-facing tier fails (sc12). Symptoms: many services alert at once; traces show time piling up at one dependency. Defences: timeouts from propagated deadlines, circuit breakers, bulkheads, fallbacks, and fewer synchronous dependencies on hot paths.

Coordinated deploys and version skew. A provider deploys a change that an older consumer can't handle; or two services must be released in a specific order and someone gets it wrong. Symptoms: errors immediately after a deploy, affecting only some callers. Defences: backwards-compatible changes, contract tests, expand-and-contract, and deploying consumers and providers independently in any order.

Debugging across services. An error surfaces in service A but originates three hops away; without trace propagation, teams spend hours correlating logs by timestamps. Distributed tracing with consistent context propagation is a prerequisite, not a luxury (sc13).

Retry and timeout mismatches. Each team picks its own defaults, producing retry amplification and timeouts longer than callers wait. Platform-provided defaults (in a mesh or standard client library) and deadline propagation keep behaviour consistent.

Operational sprawl. Dozens of services with unmaintained pipelines, outdated dependencies with security vulnerabilities, missing alerts and unclear owners. A service catalogue (listing every service, its owner, its on-call rotation, its SLOs and its dependencies) and a paved-road platform keep this manageable.

What to watch

Per service: SLIs against SLOs, request rates and error rates broken down by caller and by callee, dependency latency and circuit-breaker states. Across the system: the call graph from traces (fan-out, depth, cycles), deploy frequency and change failure rate per service, how many services a typical change touches, and on-call pages per team. Organisationally: services per team and how often teams are blocked on other teams' changes — the metric microservices were supposed to improve.

Real-world stories

Segment (2018). Segment delivers customer event data to over a hundred third-party destinations. With all destinations fed from one queue, a single slow or failing destination blocked delivery to all the others (head-of-line blocking). Their fix was one service and one queue per destination, which isolated failures but grew to more than 140 services and repositories. The operational cost became crushing: shared libraries diverged across dozens of codebases, each service had different load patterns that made autoscaling "more art than science", and each new destination added linear overhead. They built a new queueing system, Centrifuge, that preserved per-destination isolation inside the queue layer, and consolidated the destination code into a single service with a test recorder that made its tests run in milliseconds. Deployments went from more than 140 to one, shared-library improvements increased, and on-call load dropped. The key insight: isolation was needed in the queue, not in 140 separately deployed services.

Amazon Prime Video (2023). As described in the deep section, a stream-monitoring system built from distributed serverless components hit cost and scaling limits from orchestration and data movement between components; moving them into one process reduced infrastructure cost by about 90%. Both stories point to the same judgement: decide where boundaries go based on where independence is actually needed — by team, failure domain or scaling profile — and pay for distribution only there.

A short simulation of what happens to availability and tail latency as a request passes through more services, called in sequence versus in parallel. It needs only Python 3.

import random, statistics
random.seed(2)

def call():                       # one service hop: usually 20 ms, 0.5% of the time 300 ms
    return 300 if random.random() < 0.005 else random.gauss(20, 3)

def p99(samples):
    return statistics.quantiles(samples, n=100)[98]

for hops in (1, 3, 6):
    serial = [sum(call() for _ in range(hops)) for _ in range(20_000)]
    parallel = [max(call() for _ in range(hops)) for _ in range(20_000)]
    avail = 0.999 ** hops
    print(f"{hops} hops: availability {avail:.3%}  serial p99 {p99(serial):4.0f} ms  "
          f"parallel p99 {p99(parallel):4.0f} ms")

Output:

1 hops: availability 99.900%  serial p99   28 ms  parallel p99   28 ms
3 hops: availability 99.700%  serial p99  339 ms  parallel p99  300 ms
6 hops: availability 99.401%  serial p99  402 ms  parallel p99  300 ms

What it shows. Each service is individually excellent: 99.9% available, with only 0.5% of calls slow. With one hop, the p99 is 28 ms. With three hops the chance that at least one call is slow rises to about 1.5%, so the rare slowness of each service becomes the page's p99: over 300 ms. With six hops in sequence, availability has fallen to 99.4% (about 4.3 hours of failures a month) and the p99 is above 400 ms, because slow calls add up. Calling independent services in parallel helps latency (the total is the slowest call, not the sum) but not the tail probability — any slow call still makes the request slow.

Think it through. (1) What happens to the 6-hop numbers if three of the calls are replaced by reads from a local copy kept up to date by events? (2) Add a 100 ms timeout with a fallback to each call: how does the p99 change, and what does the user see instead? (3) If two of these services always change together, what would merging them do to both numbers?

Critique the architecture

A team of 8 split their monolith into 14 services last year. Symptoms: every feature needs coordinated deploys of 3-4 services; services read each other's tables directly "for performance"; one page load triggers a chain of 6 synchronous calls; the p99 has tripled; and an outage of the "user-profile" service takes down checkout. Diagnose and recommend.

Answer
  • It's a distributed monolith: coordinated deploys plus shared tables mean the services are coupled at the data and release level — all the costs of distribution, none of the independence.
  • Too many services for the team: 14 services for 8 engineers means each engineer owns almost two services' pipelines, alerts and on-call, and the main benefit of microservices (independent teams) doesn't exist with one team. Use change history to find services that always change together and merge them back into a few cohesive ones aligned to business capabilities — or into a modular monolith.
  • Data ownership: stop cross-service table reads; expose what's needed via APIs, or publish change events that consumers use to maintain their own local read models.
  • Synchronous chains: 6 serial calls multiply failure probability and turn each service's rare slowness into the page's p99. Parallelise independent calls, aggregate in a backend-for-frontend, and replace calls on the hot path with locally held data kept fresh by events.
  • Checkout depends on profile: checkout needs only a few fields — keep them locally (from events), set tight timeouts and a circuit breaker on any remaining call, and degrade gracefully (use the stored address) rather than failing the purchase.
  • Going forward: contract tests between the remaining services, deadline propagation and standard resilience defaults from the platform, and a rule that any new service must justify its need for independent deployment.
