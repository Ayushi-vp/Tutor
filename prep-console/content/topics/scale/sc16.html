An online retailer prepares for its biggest sale of the year. Last year's peak was 4,000 requests per second; this year marketing expects double. The team runs a load test: 200 virtual users hammering the product page in a loop, and the dashboard shows a steady 120 ms p99 at 8,000 requests per second. Everyone relaxes. On sale day, at 6,500 requests per second, checkout latency climbs to four seconds and then orders start timing out. What went wrong? The test hit the same few products over and over, so everything came from cache; the database in the test had a tenth of production's data; the virtual users politely waited whenever the servers slowed, so the test never experienced the queueing that real users create; and nobody noticed that three releases since last year had each made checkout a little slower, quietly shrinking capacity by a third.

A different team is told their API is "too slow". Two engineers spend a week rewriting the JSON parsing for speed, benchmark it at 40% faster, and ship it. The endpoint's latency doesn't change, because the time was never spent parsing JSON: 80% of each request was waiting on a database query that occasionally missed an index. A profiler and a trace would have shown that in ten minutes.

Performance engineering and capacity planning are the disciplines that prevent both stories. Performance engineering is a method: set a measurable goal, measure latency correctly, find the actual bottleneck top-down — component, then resource, then code — fix it, and verify with the same measurement. Capacity planning turns that knowledge into a forecast: how much traffic will arrive, how much each instance can really handle, how much headroom failures and bursts require, and how far ahead you must act given how long capacity takes to add.

This lesson builds on the foundations from sc1 (Little's Law, queueing, estimation) and goes deeper: defining performance goals, measuring distributions with histograms, the top-down method with USE, tracing and profilers including off-CPU analysis, the sources of tail latency, load testing that tells the truth (open models, coordinated omission, the knee), keeping performance from eroding release by release, and building a capacity plan with headroom, lead times and cost — ending with a full capacity plan for that sale.

The analogy: a traffic engineer on a motorway

A traffic engineer responsible for a motorway doesn't guess where the jams come from. They measure: sensors along the road show where cars slow down, and it's usually one place — a lane closure, a merge, a junction — the bottleneck. Widening a stretch of road that flows freely does nothing; fixing the merge fixes everything downstream. That's the first half of performance engineering: measure, find the bottleneck, fix that, measure again.

They also know that a motorway doesn't degrade gracefully. At 60% of its capacity traffic flows at full speed; at 85% it slows slightly; at 95% a single driver braking causes a wave that brings everyone to a crawl. So they plan for the busiest day of the year plus a margin, and they know that building a new lane takes two years, so the decision must be made two years before it's needed. That's the second half: capacity planning — forecast demand, know your real capacity (below the point where flow collapses), keep headroom, and account for lead time.

The analogy breaks in one way: roads don't change overnight, but software does. Every release can make each request a little more expensive, silently shrinking capacity, so measurement must be continuous rather than an annual survey.

The method in one picture

Three rules keep the loop honest. Goals before optimisation: "faster" isn't a goal; "p99 under 300 ms at 5,000 requests per second, measured at the load balancer" is, and it tells you when to stop. Top-down diagnosis: in a distributed system, first find which service or dependency the time goes to (tracing), then which resource is saturated in it (utilisation and saturation metrics), and only then which code is hot (profiling); jumping straight to code optimisation is how the JSON-parsing week happens. One change at a time, verified: measure under identical conditions before and after, with percentiles, and keep only changes that move the goal metric.

Two laws from sc1 frame every decision. Amdahl's law: speeding up a part that takes 20% of the time by any amount can improve the whole by at most 1.25×, so optimise the biggest part first. Little's law and queueing: latency explodes as utilisation approaches 100%, so capacity isn't the point where things break but the point where latency starts to climb — the knee — and plans leave headroom below it.

Put together, the two halves of this lesson answer two different questions that are often confused. Performance engineering asks "how much work does one request cost, and where does it go?" — and makes that smaller. Capacity planning asks "how many requests will arrive, and do we have enough of every resource to serve them at the latency we promised?" — and makes sure the answer stays yes. The first changes the numbers the second depends on, which is why a profiling fix can save more money than any purchasing decision, and why a capacity plan built on last year's per-instance numbers can be quietly wrong.

1. Performance goals

A performance goal has four parts: a metric (latency percentile, throughput, or resource cost per request), a threshold, a load condition, and a measurement point. "Checkout p99 under 400 ms and p99.9 under 1 s, at 3,000 requests per second, measured at the load balancer" is a complete goal. Goals usually come from the latency SLOs of sc13, which come from user needs: research on user behaviour consistently shows that interactions feel instant below roughly 100 ms, that attention starts to wander after about a second, and that users abandon slow pages, so latency targets are business targets.

Goals also exist for efficiency: CPU milliseconds per request, memory per connection, cost per thousand requests. These matter because they determine how many machines a given traffic level needs. A service whose CPU per request doubles has halved its capacity even if its latency still looks fine at today's load.

Set goals per endpoint or user journey rather than per service: a search endpoint and an admin export have very different needs, and a single service-wide number hides both.

2. Measuring latency correctly

sc1 showed why latency must be described as a distribution with percentiles. Getting those numbers right in practice takes a few more rules.

Record histograms, not pre-computed percentiles. A histogram counts requests in latency buckets. Histograms from many servers and many time intervals can be added together, and percentiles estimated from the total. Percentiles can't be averaged: the average of ten servers' p99s isn't the fleet's p99, and a dashboard that averages them hides exactly the bad server you're looking for. For precise measurement in benchmarks and load tests, HdrHistogram (high dynamic range histogram) records latencies over a wide range with a fixed relative error, so p99.99 of microsecond and multi-second values are both accurate.

Separate successes from failures, since fast errors lower overall latency. Look at several percentiles — p50, p90, p99, p99.9 — and at the maximum, because they tell different stories: a stable p50 with a rising p99 means a new source of occasional slowness, not a general slowdown.

Measure where users are. Server-side latency starts when the request reaches the application, so it misses time spent in load-balancer queues, TLS handshakes, network transfer and the client. Measuring at the load balancer is better; real-user monitoring from browsers and apps shows what users actually experience. When server-side latency is flat but users complain, the gap is in the part you aren't measuring.

Use enough samples and the right windows. A p99.9 needs thousands of requests per window to be meaningful; a p99 over one minute of low traffic is noise. Compare like with like — the same hour of the week, the same traffic mix.

3. Finding the bottleneck, top-down

The bottleneck is the one resource or component whose capacity limits the whole. Improving anything else changes nothing. In a distributed system, find it in three steps, from coarse to fine.

Step 1 — which component? Use distributed tracing (sc13). Aggregate traces of slow requests by span and see where time accumulates: in the service's own code, in a database query, in a call to another service, in a queue. A trace view often answers the question immediately: "80% of the p99 request is the SELECT in the inventory service". Without tracing, compare latency metrics of each component on the path.

Step 2 — which resource? Within the component, apply the USE method (Brendan Gregg): for every resource — CPUs, memory, disks, network interfaces, and software resources like thread pools, connection pools and locks — check Utilisation (how busy), Saturation (how much work is waiting: run-queue length, disk queue, pool wait time, lock wait time) and Errors. Saturation is the key signal: a resource with work queued for it is the constraint. A connection pool at its maximum with requests waiting for a connection is saturated even if the database's CPU is at 30%.

Step 3 — which code? Once you know the component and the resource, a profiler finds the code responsible. A sampling profiler records the call stack of running threads many times per second (for example 99 times, to avoid lining up with periodic activity) and counts how often each function appears. It has low overhead, so it can run in production. py11 covers Python's profilers (cProfile, py-spy); other ecosystems have equivalents (async-profiler and Java Flight Recorder for the JVM, pprof for Go, perf for native code), and eBPF-based profilers can sample any process on a Linux machine from the kernel with very little overhead.

A flame graph is the standard way to view profiles. Each box is a function, stacked on top of its caller, and its width is the proportion of samples in which it was on the stack. You read from the bottom (entry points) upward and look for wide boxes near the top: functions that themselves consume a lot of time. In the figure, apply_discounts takes 60% of checkout's CPU, almost all inside a regular-expression search — exactly the kind of surprise that justifies profiling first.

On-CPU versus off-CPU. An ordinary CPU profile only samples threads that are running, so it shows where computation goes. But in most services, a slow request spends much of its time not running: waiting for a database response, a lock, a network call, disk I/O, or a turn on a busy CPU. That time appears in off-CPU or wall-clock profiles, which sample threads whether running or blocked and record what they're waiting on. For I/O-heavy services, the off-CPU view is often where the answer is. Continuous profilers (Pyroscope, Parca and commercial equivalents) keep collecting profiles in production so you can compare this week's with last week's, or version 1.42 with 1.41.

Common bottlenecks found this way, roughly in order of how often they appear in real systems: database queries (N+1 patterns, missing indexes, sc4), serialisation and deserialisation of large payloads, excessive logging on hot paths, lock contention, connection pool exhaustion, chatty calls to other services, memory allocation and garbage-collection pressure, inefficient algorithms on data that grew larger than expected, and regular expressions with pathological worst cases.

4. Where tail latency comes from

Median latency is usually determined by the work a request does. Tail latency — p99 and beyond — is usually determined by variance: things that make an occasional request much slower than the rest. Knowing the usual suspects speeds up diagnosis.

  • Garbage-collection pauses in managed runtimes stop threads for milliseconds to hundreds of milliseconds; requests caught in a pause are slow.
  • Lock contention: most requests get the lock immediately; a few wait behind others.
  • Cache misses: requests for cold keys go to the database (sc3); a 95% hit ratio means 5% of requests pay the full cost.
  • CPU throttling from container CPU limits (sc15), even on an idle machine.
  • Retries and timeouts: a request that timed out once and succeeded on retry has latency of at least the timeout.
  • Network effects: packet loss causing TCP retransmission timeouts (net4), which add hundreds of milliseconds.
  • Noisy neighbours: other workloads on the same host competing for CPU, cache, memory bandwidth or disk.
  • Background work: compaction in LSM stores (sc4), vacuum, backups, log rotation, batch jobs sharing the machine.
  • Cold starts: new instances with empty caches, un-JIT-compiled code or unopened connections.
  • Fan-out: a request that waits for many backends is as slow as the slowest; with 100 backends each slow 1% of the time, the request is slow about 63% of the time (sc1, Dean and Barroso).

The fixes follow the causes: tune or replace the garbage collector, reduce allocation, shard locks, warm caches, remove CPU limits for latency-sensitive services, isolate background work onto separate resources or schedule it off-peak, keep minimum instance counts warm, and for fan-out, reduce the number of legs, return partial results by a deadline, or send hedged requests (sc1's deep section). Tail work is measured with high percentiles under realistic load; improvements to p50 say nothing about it.

5. Load testing that tells the truth

Load tests answer "how much can this handle, and how does it behave near and past that point?". They mislead in predictable ways, so good practice is mostly about avoiding those.

Kinds of test. A load test runs at expected peak to confirm the goal is met. A stress test keeps increasing load past peak to find the breaking point and — just as important — how the system breaks: does it shed load and keep serving most requests (sc12), or collapse? A soak test runs steady load for hours or days to expose slow problems: memory leaks, connection or file-handle exhaustion, growing queues, filling disks. A spike test jumps suddenly from low to high to exercise autoscaling (sc15), cold caches and overload protection.

Realistic workload. The traffic mix must resemble production — the same proportion of reads, writes, searches and checkouts — with realistic parameters: many different users and products, not one product ID repeated, or you're measuring the cache. The data volume must be production-like, because a query that's fast on 10,000 rows can be slow on 100 million, and an index that fits in memory for a small dataset won't for the real one. Dependencies should be real or faithfully simulated, including their latency. Replaying sampled production traffic is often the best way to get this right.

Open versus closed models, and coordinated omission. This is the most common and least understood flaw. A closed-model test runs N virtual users, each sending a request, waiting for the response, and sending the next. When the server slows down, the virtual users slow down with it: offered load automatically falls. Real traffic doesn't behave like that — millions of independent users keep arriving at the same rate whether the server is slow or not — which is an open model. The consequence is coordinated omission, a term coined by Gil Tene: suppose the server stalls for two seconds. In a closed test, only the few requests in flight during the stall record two seconds; the virtual users simply don't send the hundreds of requests that real users would have sent during those two seconds, all of which would have queued behind the stall. The tool "omits" exactly the measurements that matter, in coordination with the system's worst moments, and reports a p99 that looks fine.

The fixes: use tools that send at a constant arrival rate — k6's arrival-rate executors, wrk2, Vegeta, Gatling's open injection profiles — and measure each request's latency from when it should have been sent; always check that the achieved throughput matched the target (if the tool intended 8,000 requests per second but managed 6,500, the test didn't test 8,000); and record with HdrHistogram-style precision. The lab in sc1 used a closed loop deliberately, to show smooth behaviour; production behaves like the open model.

Finding the knee. The most useful output of a capacity test is the throughput-latency curve. Step the arrival rate up in increments — say 10% of expected peak each step, several minutes per step — and record throughput achieved, p50 and p99 latency, error rate and the utilisation of key resources at each step. Plot p99 against throughput: it stays roughly flat, then turns sharply upward. That turn is the knee, where the bottleneck resource approaches saturation and queues start to form (sc1's hockey stick). Note which resource saturated at the knee: that's the bottleneck to fix if more capacity per instance is needed. Past the knee, keep going in a stress test to see whether the system degrades gracefully.

Environment. Test on production-like hardware and configuration, or in production itself during quiet periods with synthetic traffic tagged so it can be excluded from business metrics. Many large companies load-test in production precisely because no staging environment matches its data, traffic and dependencies; Shopify, for example, runs large-scale production load tests before Black Friday. Isolated component tests (one instance, dependencies stubbed) are still valuable for measuring per-instance capacity precisely.

6. Keeping performance from eroding

Performance rarely collapses in one release; it erodes. Each change adds a little work per request — a new field serialised, an extra query, more logging, a heavier library — and after a year each instance handles a third less traffic, which nobody notices until the peak. Three practices stop this.

Performance budgets: agreed limits that changes must stay within — p99 latency for key endpoints at a given load, CPU milliseconds or allocations per request, response payload size, page weight for web front ends. Budgets turn "it feels slower" into a test that fails.

Regression tests in CI: microbenchmarks of hot functions and short load tests of key endpoints run on every change or nightly, compared with a baseline measured on the same runner in the same job to cancel out machine variation, with thresholds that flag meaningful regressions rather than noise. They catch the obvious regressions before production.

Production comparison: canary analysis (sc15) compares a new version's latency and CPU per request with the old version under real traffic, and continuous profiling lets you diff flame graphs between versions to see exactly which function got more expensive. Re-measuring per-instance capacity after major releases keeps the capacity plan honest.

7. Capacity planning

Capacity planning answers: will we have enough of every resource at the next peak, and what must we do, by when, to make sure? It has five inputs.

Demand forecast. Start from the current peak — the busiest minute or five minutes, never the daily average — for each important metric (requests per second per endpoint, data growth, message rates). Project it over the planning horizon using organic growth (fit a trend to history, often in business terms such as active users and requests per user), plus known events: launches, marketing campaigns, seasonal peaks such as Black Friday or the new tax year, and big customers onboarding. Planning from business drivers ("each new enterprise customer adds about 50 requests per second") makes forecasts easier to update when plans change. Because forecasts are uncertain, plan for a range — an expected and a high case.

Measured capacity per unit. From load tests, the throughput one instance (or one database, one partition, one broker) sustains while meeting the latency goal, and which resource limits it. This is the number that erodes with releases, so it's re-measured regularly.

Utilisation headroom. Run below the knee — commonly 60-70% of measured capacity at peak — so bursts within the peak minute and ordinary variance don't push latency up the curve.

Failure headroom. The system must still meet its goals at peak while something is broken. With three availability zones, surviving the loss of one means the other two must carry the whole peak, so each zone needs half of the peak capacity, and the fleet as a whole 1.5 times the peak requirement. The same reasoning applies to instances (N+1 or N+2), database replicas and regions (sc17).

Lead time. How long it takes to add each resource: autoscaled instances in minutes (if the account quotas and node capacity exist), new database replicas in hours, a larger database instance or a new shard in days to weeks (sc7's resharding), reserved capacity, hardware or a new region in weeks to months, and vendor quota increases on the vendor's schedule. You must act at least one lead time before the forecast says you'll run out — so the resources with the longest lead times need the most headroom and the earliest decisions.

Two further points separate a good plan from a spreadsheet. Shared dependencies run out first. Stateless services autoscale; databases, caches, message brokers, connection limits, third-party API quotas and cloud account limits usually don't, and they're where capacity failures actually happen. Every critical dependency needs its own line in the plan. Triggers beat dates. Rather than "expand in March", set triggers: "when peak exceeds 70% of capacity, start the next expansion" — which keeps the plan working even when the forecast is wrong.

You'll compute a plan like this in the lab.

8. Capacity and cost

Every capacity decision is also a cost decision, and senior engineers make both explicit. Express capacity in unit costs — cost per thousand requests, per active user, per gigabyte stored — and watch how they move as traffic grows: flat or falling is healthy; rising means inefficiency is compounding. Efficiency work — fixing the hot spots profilers find, caching, better data layout, cheaper serialisation — reduces resources per request, and its savings grow with traffic, so it's often worth more than it looks. Right-sizing matches instance types and container requests to measured usage (sc15), since over-provisioned requests are pure waste. Capacity types can be mixed: committed or reserved capacity for the steady baseline (substantially cheaper per hour), on-demand capacity for daily peaks, and spot or preemptible instances (much cheaper but reclaimable at short notice) for fault-tolerant batch work. And hidden costs — data transfer between zones and regions, egress to the internet, managed-service request charges, observability data (sc13) — can rival compute, so they belong in the plan. Headroom is a deliberate cost you choose for reliability; idle, oversized capacity nobody asked for is waste. Knowing the difference, and being able to state the cost of an extra nine or an extra zone of headroom, is part of the job.

9. A real-world walk-through: capacity for the big sale

Back to the retailer from the opening, doing it properly this time. The sale is in eight weeks.

Goal. Checkout and product pages must meet their SLOs — product page p99 under 300 ms, checkout p99 under 500 ms, 99.9% availability — at the sale's peak minute.

Forecast. Last year's sale peaked at 4,000 requests per second, 5.5 times a normal evening. Since then, active users have grown 35%, and marketing plans a larger campaign with a push notification at 9:00 sharp. Using last year's per-user request rate and conversion data, the expected peak is 4,000 × 1.35 × 1.3 (bigger campaign) ≈ 7,000 requests per second, with the push notification creating a surge in the first two minutes; the high case, planned for, is 9,000.

Measure real capacity. The team builds an open-model k6 test from sampled production traffic: realistic mix of browsing, search, cart and checkout; product IDs drawn from real popularity so cache hit ratios match production (about 92%); a staging database restored from an anonymised production snapshot; and payment and shipping providers simulated with their real latency distributions. Stepping the arrival rate on a single instance finds the knee at 320 requests per second, where p99 begins to climb; the saturated resource is CPU, and the profile shows 40% of checkout CPU in promotion-rule evaluation — a regex compiled per rule per request. Precompiling the patterns (one afternoon of work) moves the knee to 450 requests per second. They also find the checkout database's connection pool saturates at about 6,000 requests per second fleet-wide, well before the application tier.

Compute the fleet. Planning at 65% of the knee gives about 290 requests per second per instance. For the 9,000 high case: 9,000 ÷ 290 ≈ 31 instances with all zones up; to survive losing one of three zones at peak, the remaining two must carry everything, so 31 × 1.5 ≈ 47, which is 16 per zone. They'll pre-scale to 48 at 8:00 on sale day rather than rely on autoscaling, which takes minutes the push-notification surge won't allow.

Fix the shared dependencies. The database is the real risk. They add a read replica for product and inventory reads, move two heavy queries onto it, raise the pool size behind a connection pooler (checking with Little's Law that the database can serve the extra concurrency), and confirm the payment provider's rate limit covers 9,000 requests per second at the expected checkout ratio — it doesn't, so they request a temporary quota increase, which the provider needs three weeks to grant. That lead time is why the plan started eight weeks out.

Protect against being wrong. Load shedding and priority (sc12) are configured so that, beyond capacity, browse requests from crawlers and prefetches are dropped first and checkout is protected; a virtual waiting room is ready to switch on if the high case is exceeded (sc1's Eras Tour lesson). A stress test to 12,000 requests per second confirms the system sheds rather than collapses.

Stop the erosion. A CI job now runs the checkout load test nightly against a baseline and flags any drop of more than 5% in per-instance capacity; the regex regression that cost a third of capacity would have been caught the week it was introduced.

Result. The sale peaks at 8,200 requests per second, between the expected and high cases. p99 stays under 250 ms for product pages and 420 ms for checkout. The waiting room is never used. The cost of the extra capacity for the day was known in advance and approved, and the profiling fix permanently reduced the fleet needed on ordinary days by about 30%.

Queueing models beyond M/M/1

The simple formula from sc1, response time ≈ service time ÷ (1 − utilisation), comes from the M/M/1 model: random (Poisson) arrivals, exponentially distributed service times, one server. Real systems differ in ways that matter. With c parallel servers (an M/M/c model — say, a pool of 16 worker threads), queueing starts much later: a 16-worker pool at 80% utilisation queues far less than a single server at 80%, which is why larger instances and pools tolerate higher utilisation. With more variable service times (some requests 100× more expensive than others), queues are worse than M/M/1 predicts; the Pollaczek-Khinchine formula shows waiting time grows with the variance of service time, which is why a few expensive requests — exports, big searches — hurt everyone's latency and are often isolated to their own pools or queues. And bursty arrivals, more clustered than Poisson (a push notification, a cron job, a retry storm), queue more than the model predicts too. The practical lessons: reduce service-time variance, separate heavy from light work, and treat modelled headroom as a minimum, not a guarantee.

The Universal Scalability Law in capacity planning

sc1 introduced Neil Gunther's Universal Scalability Law as a reason adding nodes can make things worse. In capacity planning it's also a practical tool. Measure throughput at several cluster sizes (or concurrency levels on one node), fit the two coefficients — contention (σ, the serialised fraction) and coherency (κ, the cost of nodes coordinating) — and the fitted curve predicts where throughput peaks and how much each additional node will really add. If κ is significant, the plan can't assume linear scaling: going from 20 to 40 nodes might add only 50% more throughput, and beyond some point nothing. The fitted σ and κ also point to the cause: high contention suggests a shared lock or single leader; high coherency suggests cross-node chatter such as cache invalidation or distributed locking.

Benchmarking pitfalls

Microbenchmarks are easy to get wrong. Just-in-time compilers optimise code differently after warm-up, so early measurements mislead unless warm-up is excluded; compilers can eliminate code whose result is unused, "benchmarking" nothing; CPU frequency scaling and thermal throttling change speeds during a run; caches make repeated runs over the same small data look fast; and garbage collection may happen outside the measured window. Frameworks such as JMH (Java), pytest-benchmark or pyperf (Python), and Go's testing benchmarks handle warm-up, repetition and statistics. The bigger pitfall is benchmarking the wrong thing: a function that's 3× faster in isolation may be irrelevant if it's 2% of request time (Amdahl), or the benchmark's data may not resemble production. Benchmarks guide work; end-to-end measurements under realistic load decide whether it mattered.

Worst-case complexity as a production risk

Average-case performance is what benchmarks usually measure; worst-case performance is what takes production down. Regular expressions with nested quantifiers backtrack exponentially on crafted input (Cloudflare's 2019 outage, in prod); hash tables degrade to linear time under collisions (the basis of hash-flooding denial-of-service attacks, which is why language runtimes randomise hash seeds); sorting with a naive quicksort on already-sorted data is quadratic; parsers of nested data can recurse without bound. Defences: use algorithms and libraries with guaranteed bounds (linear-time regex engines such as RE2 or Rust's regex, balanced trees), cap input sizes and nesting depths, set CPU and time limits on untrusted or user-supplied computation, and include adversarial inputs in performance tests. This is performance engineering as reliability engineering.

Intent-based capacity planning at scale

Very large organisations automate capacity planning. Google's SRE book describes intent-based planning: service owners state intent — "this service must survive the loss of any one cluster at peak with 99.9% of requests under 200 ms" — along with dependencies and per-request resource costs, and a planning system computes where and how much capacity to allocate across clusters, taking into account failure domains, dependencies and efficiency. The shift is from managing machines to managing requirements, with the arithmetic done by software. Even small teams benefit from the idea: write capacity requirements as explicit statements tied to SLOs and failure scenarios, so the plan can be recomputed when traffic, costs or topology change.

Load-testing in production safely

Testing in production gives the most realistic results and carries real risk, so it's done with controls: synthetic requests are tagged so they're excluded from business metrics, analytics and billing, and routed to test accounts or dry-run paths for side effects like payments and emails; load ramps gradually with an automated abort tied to SLO burn rates; tests run at quiet times first, and the blast radius is limited (one cell, one region); and dependencies, especially third parties, are warned or simulated. Some companies go further and shift real traffic between regions or clusters to test capacity (running a region "hot" by draining another), which also exercises failover. The payoff is confidence that the numbers in the capacity plan describe the system that actually serves users.

How performance and capacity problems show up

Gradual latency creep. p99 rises a few percent a month; CPU per request rises with each release; the fleet grows faster than traffic. No single change is to blame, so it's only visible in trends of efficiency metrics and in version-to-version profile diffs.

The sudden cliff at peak. Everything is fine until the busiest day, when latency explodes at a traffic level only slightly above the previous peak — the system crossed the knee. It's the signature of planning from averages, of eroded capacity, or of a shared dependency (database, connection pool, third-party quota) reaching its limit before the application tier.

Load tests that passed. Production fails at a load the test "proved" was fine: closed-model testing, cache-friendly traffic, small test data or missing dependencies. Check achieved versus intended throughput and compare test cache hit ratios and query plans with production's.

Tail regressions. p99 doubles while p50 is flat after a change: a new source of variance — a GC-heavy library, a lock, a cold path, CPU throttling, or a new retry policy.

Pathological inputs. A specific request (a huge export, a crafted string, a customer with a million items) consumes a disproportionate amount of CPU or memory and slows everything on the same instance. Look for per-request cost outliers in traces and profiles.

What to watch

Latency histograms per endpoint and percentiles over time; throughput and CPU (and memory, allocations) per request as efficiency metrics, by version; saturation signals for every critical resource — CPU run queue, pool wait times, lock waits, disk queues; the ratio of current peak to measured capacity for every tier and dependency, with alerts at the trigger thresholds; cache hit ratios; GC pause times; container CPU throttling; and cost per unit of traffic. Continuous profiles, kept for weeks, make "what got slower?" answerable.

A real incident: Cloudflare, 2 July 2019

At 13:42 UTC, Cloudflare deployed an update to its web application firewall's managed rules, intended to detect a class of cross-site scripting attacks and initially running in a monitoring-only mode. One new rule contained a regular expression with the pattern .*(?:.*=.*). In a backtracking regular-expression engine like the PCRE library their firewall used, this pattern forces the engine to try enormous numbers of ways to split the input between the two .* terms when a match fails; the work grows super-linearly with input length — their post-mortem shows matching x=x took 23 steps, and 20 more characters took 555. Because every HTTP request passing through the firewall was evaluated against the rule, CPU usage jumped to nearly 100% on edge servers across Cloudflare's entire global network, and customers saw 502 errors. About 80% of traffic was lost until the firewall rules were globally disabled at 14:09 — a 27-minute outage. Three factors let it happen: firewall rule updates were deployed globally at once, bypassing the staged rollout used for code; the test suite checked rule correctness but not CPU cost; and a protection that limited excessive CPU use by rules had been removed during an earlier refactor. Cloudflare's fixes map onto this lesson: restore CPU limits, audit all 3,868 rules for backtracking, add performance profiling to the rule test suite, move to regular-expression engines with linear-time guarantees (such as RE2 or Rust's regex), and stage rule rollouts while keeping an emergency global path. The general lessons: worst-case cost on a hot path is a production risk, performance must be tested like correctness, and configuration that runs on every request needs the same gradual rollout as code.

The zone-failure headroom alone is half again as many instances; that's the cost of the reliability requirement, and it's worth being able to state.

Calculate

(a) A service handles 3,000 req/s at 40 ms average latency. How many requests are in flight? Each instance runs 16 worker threads; how many instances are needed at a target of 60% worker utilisation? (b) A search page fans out to 50 shards and waits for all of them; each shard's p99 is 200 ms and its median 20 ms. Roughly what fraction of page loads take ≥ 200 ms? (c) Your load test uses 200 virtual users in a loop and shows a steady p99 of 120 ms. Why might production be far worse at the same throughput?

Answer

(a) Little's law: L = 3,000 × 0.04 = 120 in flight. At 60% utilisation each instance usefully holds 16 × 0.6 ≈ 9.6 → 120 ÷ 9.6 = 12.5 → 13 instances, plus spare capacity for losing an instance or a zone: with three zones and zone-failure headroom, 13 × 1.5 ≈ 20, about 7 per zone.

(b) Each shard is ≥ 200 ms with probability 1%; the page is slow if any shard is: 1 − 0.99⁵⁰ ≈ 40% of page loads. The shard p99 has become roughly the page's typical experience — hedged requests, returning partial results by a deadline, fewer shards per query, or reducing shard variance is required.

(c) Coordinated omission: in a closed loop, when the server stalls, virtual users wait and stop sending, so the stall affects only a handful of recorded requests and the throughput silently drops. Real users keep arriving during a stall and queue behind it, so production latency is much worse. Rerun with a constant arrival rate (open model), check that achieved throughput matches the target, and also test realistic data volumes, traffic mix and cache-miss ratios.
