At 11:48 p.m. Pacific time on 19 October 2025, an automated process that manages DNS records for Amazon DynamoDB in AWS's us-east-1 region, in Northern Virginia, hit a race condition that left the database's regional endpoint with no IP addresses. Every application and AWS service in the region that used DynamoDB suddenly couldn't reach it. Because many of AWS's own services depend on DynamoDB, the failure spread; recovery then stalled as systems restarting all at once overwhelmed each other. For about fifteen hours, services from chat apps and games to banks and smart doorbells were degraded or down — many of them running entirely in that one region, some whose engineers discovered that the tools they'd use to recover were hosted there too.

Earlier lessons in this track made systems survive the failure of a process, a machine or a datacentre: replicas, zones, load balancers, retries, autoscaling. This lesson is about bigger failures — a whole region, a cloud provider's control plane, a bad configuration pushed everywhere, a flood, a ransomware attack — and about the harder questions they raise. How much recent data can the business afford to lose? How long can it be down? Is a second region worth doubling the infrastructure bill? If two regions share a dependency, did the second one help at all? Who decides to fail over, and has anyone ever actually tried it?

Those questions have answers in the form of objectives and designs. The recovery point objective (RPO) and recovery time objective (RTO) turn "how bad can it get?" into numbers. The disaster-recovery ladder — backup and restore, pilot light, warm standby, active-active — offers designs with known costs for each point on those numbers. Multi-region data patterns decide where writes go and how conflicts are avoided. Cells and static stability limit how much any single failure can affect. And cost is part of the design, because every extra nine and every extra region has a price that should be weighed against what downtime actually costs the business.

This lesson works through all of it: failure domains, availability arithmetic including the correlated failures that break it, RPO and RTO, the DR ladder, multi-region data and traffic patterns, failover and its risks, cells, static stability, data residency and cost — ending with a disaster-recovery design for a payments product under real constraints.

The analogy: a business with offices in several cities

A company keeps its important records in one office. A burst pipe on one floor is a nuisance: staff move to another floor of the same building (like a server failing over within a zone). A fire in the whole building is serious: if the company also rents space in a second building across town, on a different power grid, staff can move there within the day (a second availability zone). An earthquake that shuts down the whole city is a different class of event: only an office in another city helps (a second region). And if the company's records exist only in the flooded building, it doesn't matter how many offices it has.

So the company must decide: how much work can we afford to lose (the files updated since the last copy was sent to the other city — the RPO)? How long can we be closed (the RTO)? Should the other city's office be a storage room with copies of the files (backups), a small office with a skeleton crew (warm standby), or a full office sharing the work every day (active-active)? Each option costs more and recovers faster.

The analogy also shows the trap. If both offices use the same phone system, the same payroll provider and the same building-access badges issued from the first city, an earthquake there still stops the second office — shared dependencies make "redundant" offices fail together. The analogy breaks in one way: moving work between cities takes people days, while moving traffic between regions takes seconds to minutes — but only if it's been rehearsed and nothing in the recovery path depends on the region that failed.

Failure domains, from small to large

A failure domain is a set of things that fail together because they share something. A process crashes; a machine loses a disk; a rack loses its switch; an availability zone (AZ) — one or more datacentres with independent power, cooling and networking — loses power or connectivity; a region — a group of zones in one geographic area, such as eu-west-1 — suffers a software failure in a shared regional service, a natural disaster or a network cut; and occasionally a problem is global: a configuration pushed to every region, a DNS provider failing, or a provider-wide control-plane bug.

Two facts shape all multi-region design. First, zones are close and regions are far: zones in a region are typically within tens of kilometres, with round trips around a millisecond, so synchronous replication across zones is cheap (sc6); regions are hundreds or thousands of kilometres apart, with round trips of tens to over a hundred milliseconds, so anything synchronous across regions adds that to every operation (sc9's PACELC). Second, regions fail because of shared software, not just shared buildings. The large regional outages of recent years — AWS us-east-1 in December 2021 (a retry storm in the internal network, sc12) and October 2025 (the DynamoDB DNS automation race) — were caused by software and automation that all zones depended on. Zones protect well against physical failures; only separate regions protect against a region's shared software failing.

1. Availability arithmetic, and why it lies

sc1 introduced the two basic rules. Components in series — a request needs all of them — multiply: three dependencies at 99.9% each give 0.999³ ≈ 99.7%. Components in parallel — any one is enough — fail only if all fail: two independent regions at 99.9% each give 1 − 0.001² = 99.9999%, from about 8.8 hours of downtime a year to about 30 seconds.

That second number is almost never achieved, because the formula assumes three things that real systems violate:

Independence. The two regions must not fail for the same reason. But they usually share a lot: the same code and deploy pipeline (a bad release reaches both), the same configuration system, the same identity provider, the same DNS provider, the same cloud provider's global services, sometimes a single primary database. Every shared dependency sits in series with the pair. If both regions need a global login service at 99.95%, the pair can't exceed 99.95%, whatever the formula says.

Working failover. A standby region helps only if traffic and data actually move to it when needed. Failover depends on detection, decisions, runbooks, permissions, capacity and DNS changes, all of which can fail — and often do, if they haven't been exercised. If failover succeeds 90% of the time, a large part of the benefit disappears.

Capacity. The surviving region must be able to carry the whole load. If it's sized for half the traffic, failing over moves the outage rather than ending it.

The lab quantifies these effects: two independent regions at 99.9% give about 30 seconds of downtime a year; add one shared global dependency at 99.95% and it's 4.4 hours; make failover work 90% of the time and it's about an hour. The practical conclusion is that adding regions is only as valuable as your independence and your failover, which is why most of the work in multi-region design is removing shared dependencies and rehearsing failover, not deploying the second copy.

2. RPO and RTO: stating what you need

Two numbers turn "we need disaster recovery" into a design requirement (sc6 introduced them for replication).

The recovery point objective (RPO) is the maximum amount of recent data, measured as time, that may be lost in a disaster. An RPO of zero means no committed data may be lost; an RPO of 15 minutes means losing up to the last 15 minutes of changes is acceptable. RPO is determined mostly by replication: synchronous replication to the recovery site gives an RPO near zero (at the cost of latency on every write); asynchronous replication gives an RPO equal to the replication lag (usually seconds); periodic backups give an RPO equal to the backup interval.

The recovery time objective (RTO) is the maximum time to restore service. It's determined by how much must happen after the disaster: rebuilding infrastructure from scratch and restoring terabytes from backups takes hours or days; scaling up a running copy and switching traffic takes minutes; if every region already serves traffic, the RTO is the time to stop sending traffic to the failed one.

The objectives should be set per system, from business impact, not once for the whole company. A payments ledger might need an RPO of zero and an RTO of 30 minutes; the product catalogue an RPO of an hour (it can be re-imported) and an RTO of an hour; internal analytics an RPO and RTO of a day. Setting them forces the conversation that matters: what does an hour of downtime, or an hour of lost data, actually cost this business, and what is it worth paying to avoid? Most systems end up with different objectives for different data — very strict for money and orders, relaxed for everything else — and the design follows.

3. The disaster-recovery ladder

With RPO and RTO stated, pick the cheapest strategy that meets them. There are four rungs, each more expensive and faster than the last.

Backup and restore. Keep backups (database snapshots plus archived logs for point-in-time recovery, object-storage copies, infrastructure definitions in code) in another region, ideally another account so a compromised or deleted primary account can't take them too. After a disaster, rebuild the infrastructure from code in another region and restore the data. Cheapest by far — you pay mostly for storage — but the RTO is hours to days for large datasets, and the RPO is the time since the last backup or archived log. It's the right answer for systems that can be down for a day, and a necessary base layer for everything else, because it's also the only protection against corruption, deletion and ransomware (replicas faithfully copy those, sc6).

Pilot light. Keep the data continuously replicated to the recovery region — database replicas, object-storage replication — while running only the minimal core (the databases themselves, perhaps a small control component), with the rest defined in code but not running. On failover, promote the databases and launch the application tier. The RPO is replication lag (seconds); the RTO is the time to launch and scale everything, typically tens of minutes to a couple of hours. Costs are modest: storage, replicas, a little compute.

Warm standby. Run a complete, working copy of the whole system in the recovery region, scaled down — say 10-25% of production capacity — continuously receiving replicated data and ideally a trickle of real or synthetic traffic that proves it works. On failover, scale it up and switch traffic. The RTO is minutes (limited by scaling up and switching traffic); confidence is much higher because the copy is always running and tested. Cost: roughly the scaled-down fraction plus replication.

Active-active (multi-site). Every region serves live traffic all the time, each sized to absorb the others' share if one fails. Losing a region means stopping sending traffic to it, so the RTO is near zero, and failover is exercised constantly. Costs are highest — at least double capacity if two regions must each handle everything — and the data model is hardest, because writes happen in several regions at once (section 4). It's justified when even minutes of regional downtime are unacceptable, or when users are spread worldwide and latency matters.

Most organisations combine rungs per system: active-active for the stateless edge and read paths, warm standby for the core transactional services, backup and restore for internal tools and analytics.

4. Multi-region data: where do writes go?

Stateless services are easy to run in several regions: deploy the same code everywhere. Data is the hard part, because every design must answer where writes happen and how regions agree. There are four main patterns.

Single write region. One region hosts the primary database; other regions hold asynchronous read replicas (sc6). Reads are local and fast everywhere; writes from distant users cross the world (100-200 ms extra), and read-your-writes needs care. If the primary region fails, a replica in another region is promoted, losing whatever hadn't replicated yet (RPO equals the lag, usually seconds). Simple, common, and the natural basis for pilot light and warm standby.

Home-region partitioning. Each user, tenant or account is assigned a home region that owns their data and accepts their writes; other regions may hold read copies. A European customer's writes go to an EU region, an American customer's to a US region. Most writes are local, there are no write conflicts (each record has exactly one writer region), and it maps naturally onto data residency (section 8). The hard cases are operations spanning users with different homes — a payment from a European to an American account, a document shared across regions — which need a designated owner for the shared object or a cross-region protocol such as a saga (sc11). Users who move can be migrated between homes. This is effectively sharding by geography (sc7).

Multi-leader with conflict resolution. Every region accepts writes to any record and replicates asynchronously to the others; concurrent writes to the same record are resolved by last-writer-wins, merges or CRDTs (sc6, sc9). Writes are always local and every region stays available during a partition, but conflicts are real, and last-writer-wins silently loses data. It fits data where conflicts are rare or mergeable — preferences, counters, carts — and is what many "global tables" features provide in their default mode.

Consensus-based global databases. Systems like Google Spanner, CockroachDB and YugabyteDB replicate each piece of data with a consensus protocol across regions (sc9), so every committed write is in several regions: RPO zero, strong consistency, and automatic failover when a region is lost. DynamoDB added a similar option in 2025, global tables with multi-region strong consistency. The cost is latency: each write waits for a majority of replicas, so with replicas spread across distant regions, every write pays a cross-region round trip. Placement tricks reduce it — keeping a majority of each range's replicas near its main users, or pinning rows to regions by a column value — but the physics of distance can't be removed.

The choice follows from the objectives and the data: RPO zero for money usually means synchronous replication of that data (a global database, or synchronous replication of the ledger to a nearby second region); most other data is fine with asynchronous replication and a small RPO; and home regions keep most writes local and conflict-free.

5. Routing traffic between regions

Users must reach a healthy, preferably nearby region. Two mechanisms dominate (net6 and net12 cover them in depth).

DNS-based global load balancing answers the domain lookup with the address of a region chosen by the user's location, region health and configured weights. It's simple and works everywhere, but failover is limited by caching: resolvers and clients may keep an old answer for the record's TTL or longer, so moving traffic away from a failed region can take minutes and never reaches 100% quickly.

Anycast with a global load balancer announces the same IP addresses from many edge locations; the internet's routing delivers each user to the nearest edge, which forwards requests to a healthy region over the provider's network. Failover happens at the edge in seconds, with no DNS caching involved. Major cloud providers and CDNs offer this.

Whichever is used, failover has three requirements beyond the switch itself. The surviving region must have capacity for the moved traffic, already running (static stability, section 7) or able to scale quickly — and during a regional outage, everyone else is trying to scale in the same surviving regions. Data must be ready: databases promoted, caches warm enough. And failover must not flap: a health signal that oscillates can bounce traffic between regions, each time disrupting sessions and cold-starting caches; health checks need hysteresis (require sustained failure before moving, sustained health before moving back).

6. Failover: who decides, and what can go wrong

Failing over a whole region is a high-stakes action. The failure detector might be wrong — a monitoring outage, a partial network partition — and a cross-region failover with asynchronous replication may lose recent writes and create a split brain if the "failed" region is actually still running (sc6's GitHub 2018 story: a 43-second partition led to a cross-region database failover and a day of degraded service). So many organisations draw a line: automatic failover within a region (instances, zones, database replicas — fast, frequent, well understood) and deliberate failover across regions, triggered by a human incident commander against clear criteria, and then executed by automation with a single command or a small number of well-tested steps.

A good regional failover procedure is: detect and confirm (is the region truly unhealthy, and is it expected to stay so for longer than the RTO?); decide; fence the old region so it stops accepting writes, if reachable; promote data stores in the recovery region; scale up capacity; switch traffic; verify with SLIs; communicate. Then, later, fail back — often harder than failing over, because data written in the recovery region must be replicated back and reconciled with anything the old region accepted before it failed.

Two classic surprises: the recovery path depends on the failed region — the CI/CD system, the secrets manager, the identity provider for engineers' logins, the monitoring, or the DNS management API are hosted in the region that just failed; and quotas and capacity in the recovery region weren't raised to production levels, so scaling up fails when it's needed most. Both are found only by practising.

7. Cells, shuffle sharding and static stability

Regions protect against the failure of a location. Many real outages, though, aren't caused by locations: a bad deploy, a poison request that crashes servers, a noisy customer, a corrupted configuration. Those hit every region equally if every region runs the same code with the same data. Cell-based architecture addresses them.

A cell is a complete, independent copy of a service's stack — its own load balancers, compute and data stores — serving a subset of customers. A thin cell router maps each customer to a cell (by customer ID, using a small lookup table). Cells share nothing, so a failure in one — a bad release, a runaway customer, a data corruption — affects only that cell's customers, perhaps 5% of them rather than all. Deploys roll out one cell at a time, so a bad version is caught in the first cell. Cells are also the unit of scaling: add cells as customers grow, keeping each cell's size within tested limits. Amazon runs many services this way; the router and the customer-to-cell mapping must be extremely simple and robust, because they're the one shared component.

Shuffle sharding refines the idea for shared pools. Instead of assigning each customer to one of eight groups of workers (where a problem customer takes down a whole group and every other customer in it), assign each customer to a random combination of, say, two workers out of eight. There are 28 such combinations, so two customers rarely share both workers; if one customer's traffic breaks its two workers, almost every other customer still has at least one healthy worker. Amazon's Route 53 uses this to limit the impact of attacks on individual customers' domains.

Static stability is the principle that a system should keep working through a failure without having to change anything. During a zone or region failure, the actions many designs rely on — launching new instances, calling a control plane API, updating DNS, fetching fresh configuration — are exactly the ones most likely to be failing or overloaded, because the incident may be in the control plane itself and every other customer is trying the same thing. A statically stable design pre-provisions: with three zones and the goal of surviving one zone's loss, each zone runs enough capacity for half the peak, so losing a zone requires no scaling at all; data-plane components (load balancers, proxies, service-discovery clients) keep using their last known good configuration when the control plane is unreachable (sc2). The October 2025 us-east-1 outage showed why: after DynamoDB recovered, the systems responsible for launching and connecting new instances were themselves congested for hours, so anything that needed new capacity to recover waited.

8. Data residency

Laws and contracts increasingly specify where data may live. The EU's GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) restricts transfers of personal data to countries without adequate protections; several countries require certain data (financial, health, government, or all personal data of residents) to stay within their borders; enterprise and public-sector customers often write residency into contracts. That constrains multi-region design directly: you can't replicate everything to every region.

The common pattern is jurisdiction-aware home regions: a customer's home region is chosen by where their data must live, disaster recovery pairs regions within the same jurisdiction (two EU regions, for example), and global features operate on data that's allowed to move — aggregated, anonymised or explicitly non-personal. The places residency is most often broken accidentally are the ones nobody thinks of as "the database": logs and traces containing personal data shipped to a central observability platform, backups copied to a cheaper region, analytics pipelines, support tools that fetch customer records into another country, and third-party services. A residency design has to cover all of them.

9. Cost: the price of each nine

Multi-region resilience is expensive in ways that should be estimated before building it.

  • Infrastructure: a warm standby adds perhaps 20-50% (a scaled-down copy plus replication); active-active across two regions each able to carry everything adds at least 100%; static stability adds headroom in every zone.
  • Data transfer: replication between regions, cross-region calls and moving data for analytics are billed per gigabyte, and cross-zone traffic within a region is billed too on major clouds. A chatty service spread across zones can spend more on transfer than on compute.
  • Latency: synchronous cross-region replication adds tens of milliseconds to every affected write, which has product costs.
  • Engineering: multi-region data models, failover automation, game days, testing and on-call are permanent work.

The decision is an explicit trade: estimate the cost of downtime and data loss — lost revenue per hour, contractual penalties, recovery effort, reputational damage — and compare the expected annual loss under each design with its annual cost. Going from 99.9% to 99.99% saves about eight hours of downtime a year; if an hour of downtime costs the business less than the design's annual cost divided by eight, that nine isn't worth buying — or should be bought more cheaply, for example with cells and better deployment practices, which prevent many outages without duplicating regions. Other levers from sc16 apply too: reserved capacity for steady baselines, spot instances for fault-tolerant batch work, storage tiering and lifecycle policies for backups, and tagging every resource by team and purpose so costs are visible.

10. A real-world walk-through: disaster recovery for a payments dashboard

A B2B company runs a payments dashboard: merchants see transactions, issue refunds and download reports; the payments themselves are processed by an external payment provider. It runs in one region across three zones. After a competitor's regional outage makes the news, leadership asks for "multi-region active-active". The engineer's job is to turn that into requirements and a design.

Uncover the objectives. Conversations with product, finance and legal establish: the SLA promises 99.9% monthly availability; merchants can tolerate about 30 minutes of downtime in a genuine regional disaster (RTO 30 minutes); losing records of confirmed payments or refunds is unacceptable (RPO zero for the ledger); losing a few minutes of report-generation jobs or preferences is fine (RPO 15 minutes for everything else); EU merchants' data must stay in the EU; and the budget can grow by about 40%, not 100%.

Rule out what isn't needed. No objective requires active-active. It would double infrastructure, force a multi-region write model for the ledger, complicate residency, and blow the budget.

Design: warm standby per jurisdiction. EU merchants live in an EU primary region with a warm standby in a second EU region; other merchants in a US primary with a US standby. Each merchant's home region is fixed by jurisdiction, and replication never crosses it. Within each primary region, everything already runs across three zones with synchronous database replication, which handles the common failures.

Meeting RPO zero for the ledger. Two options are costed. Option A: synchronously replicate the ledger database to the standby region, adding the inter-region round trip (about 10-20 ms between two EU regions) to each ledger write — acceptable, since ledger writes are a small fraction of traffic and not latency-critical. Option B: rely on the payment provider as a second system of record and reconcile after failover. They choose A for the ledger (it's cleaner and auditable) and keep B's reconciliation job anyway as a safety net (sc11). Everything else replicates asynchronously, with alerts if lag exceeds five minutes.

Meeting RTO 30 minutes. The standby runs the full application at about 20% of production capacity, deployed from the same infrastructure code and receiving a trickle of synthetic traffic so it's known to work. Quotas in the standby region are raised to production levels in advance. Failover is one runbook, triggered by the incident commander: fence the primary if reachable, promote the standby databases, scale compute to 100% (pre-tested at about 12 minutes), switch the anycast global load balancer's routing (seconds), and verify with SLIs.

Remove shared dependencies. An audit finds that engineers' single sign-on, the CI/CD system, the secrets manager and the monitoring stack all ran only in the primary region. Each gets a plan: the identity provider is a separate SaaS with its own redundancy; a break-glass access path exists in each region; secrets are replicated to the standby; monitoring runs in a third region so it survives either.

Prove it. Every quarter, a game day fails one jurisdiction over to its standby for real, during a low-traffic window, measuring the actual RTO (the first drill takes 48 minutes because of a missing permission and a DNS record nobody knew about; the third takes 19) and confirming no ledger writes were lost. Backups are restored monthly into an isolated account to prove they work.

The cost, stated. About 35% more infrastructure, a few milliseconds on ledger writes, and a quarterly day of engineering time. The same analysis also produced two cheaper improvements that reduce everyday risk more than any region could: moving deployments to a cell-by-cell canary process and adding a static-stability headroom of one zone's capacity. That combination — per-system objectives, the cheapest strategy that meets them, shared dependencies removed, and failover practised until it's boring — is what a strong interview answer looks like.

Correlated failure is the real enemy

Most catastrophic outages are correlated failures: many supposedly independent components failing together because of one shared cause. Shared software is the most common cause — a configuration pushed to every region at once, a certificate that expires everywhere simultaneously, a bug triggered by a date or a leap second, a dependency (identity, DNS, a feature-flag service) used by every region. Shared operations come next: the same tooling, the same runbook, the same engineer's command running against every region. Defences are about breaking correlation: stagger rollouts of code and configuration by cell and region with bake time between stages; make regions as independent as possible in their dependencies and control planes; vary certificate expiry dates; keep a region's ability to operate from depending on others; and treat "global" systems (configuration, DNS, identity, the deploy pipeline) as the most critical components, with the most conservative change processes.

Why recovery is often slower than failure

The October 2025 us-east-1 outage illustrates a pattern seen in many large incidents: the initial fault is fixed in hours, but recovery drags on because the system can't absorb the rush of work that built up. When DynamoDB's DNS was restored, the EC2 subsystem that maintains leases with physical hosts tried to re-establish all of them at once, exceeded its capacity and went into congestive collapse; network configuration changes then queued behind it. The same mechanics appear as thundering herds after a cache restart (sc3), retry storms after a network blip (sc12) and reconnect storms after a gateway restart (sc2). Designing for recovery means rate-limiting catch-up work, adding jitter to reconnection, sizing recovery paths for the full backlog, and testing recovery at scale — not just testing that the system works when healthy.

Multi-region consistency, precisely

The data patterns map onto sc9's consistency models and PACELC. A single write region with async replicas is PC/EL: writes are consistent (one leader) and unavailable if the leader's region is unreachable; replica reads are low-latency and possibly stale. Home-region partitioning is the same per partition, with availability losses confined to the partitions homed in a failed region. Multi-leader global tables are PA/EL: every region stays writable during a partition at the cost of conflicts. Consensus-based global databases are PC/EC: strongly consistent and RPO-zero, paying write latency always and refusing writes on the minority side of a partition. A careful design often mixes them per table — the ledger in a consensus-replicated store or synchronously replicated to a nearby region, user profiles home-region partitioned, preferences and counters multi-leader — and says which model each piece of data has and why.

Testing disaster recovery for real

A disaster-recovery plan is a hypothesis until it's tested. Mature practice includes regular game days that cut a region off realistically (at the network or routing layer, not by gracefully shutting services down), measure actual RTO and RPO, and produce action items like a post-mortem; restore tests that recover backups into isolated environments and validate the data; chaos experiments at smaller scales (zone evacuation, dependency failure) run continuously; and in some organisations, routine region evacuations — draining a region of traffic as a normal operational procedure, which keeps the capability exercised. Netflix's Chaos Kong experiments, which evacuate an entire AWS region, are the best-known example. The reason is simple: every component of a recovery path drifts — credentials expire, quotas change, new dependencies appear, runbooks go stale — and only exercising it keeps it working.

Designing for provider-level failure

Beyond regions lies the cloud provider itself: global services (identity and access management, DNS, a global control plane) whose failure affects every region, and account-level risks (billing suspension, compromised credentials, accidental deletion). Protections range from cheap to very expensive. Cheap: backups copied to a separate account with restricted deletion rights and object locking (protection against ransomware and insider mistakes); DNS hosted with a second provider; documented break-glass access. Expensive: running on two cloud providers, which doubles the platform engineering effort and pushes designs toward the lowest common denominator of services. Most organisations take the cheap measures and accept provider-level risk; some regulated industries are required to demonstrate an exit or failover plan to another provider, which usually means a tested backup-and-restore path rather than live multi-cloud.

How multi-region and DR plans fail

The failover that never worked. A standby region existed on paper, but when needed: its quotas were too low to scale, a database replica had silently stopped replicating weeks ago, credentials had expired, the runbook referenced resources that no longer existed, or the tooling to fail over was hosted in the failed region. Only regular, realistic game days prevent this.

Hidden global dependencies. Both regions depend on one identity service, one DNS provider, one configuration store or one third-party API; when it fails, "multi-region" fails too. Dependency mapping and region-isolation tests expose them.

Unintended cross-region coupling. A service in region A quietly calls a service in region B on its hot path (added for convenience), so region B's failure breaks region A, and every request pays cross-region latency and transfer costs. Trace analysis by region finds these.

Data loss and split brain on failover. Asynchronous replication loses the last seconds of writes; an automated failover triggered by a partition creates two primaries; fail-back overwrites data written during the outage. Clear RPOs, fencing, and deliberate cross-region decisions mitigate this.

Replication lag nobody watched. Cross-region replication fell hours behind during a bulk job; the effective RPO was hours, not seconds, and nobody knew until a failover.

Residency leaks. Logs, backups or analytics copies of regulated data end up in the wrong jurisdiction.

What to watch

Cross-region replication lag for every replicated store, alerting well below the RPO; the health and readiness of standby regions (synthetic traffic success, capacity and quota headroom); the time since the last successful failover exercise and backup restore; the list of dependencies each region relies on, including those outside the region; cross-region traffic volume and cost; and per-cell health, so a failure contained to one cell is detected and its cell evacuated.

A real incident: AWS us-east-1, October 2025

At 11:48 p.m. PDT on 19 October 2025, customers began seeing errors from Amazon DynamoDB in us-east-1. DynamoDB manages its public endpoints' DNS records with two automated components: a planner that produces DNS plans and enactors that apply them, one per zone, for resilience. That night, one enactor was unusually slow applying a plan. Meanwhile another enactor applied a newer plan and then ran a clean-up that deleted plans it considered obsolete. The slow enactor, whose safety check that its plan was still current had become stale because of the delay, then applied its old plan over the newer one — and the clean-up deleted that plan, removing every IP address from the regional DynamoDB endpoint's DNS record. Clients and AWS services couldn't resolve DynamoDB at all. Engineers restored DNS in about three hours, but recovery then stalled: EC2's system for maintaining leases with the physical servers that host instances had fallen behind during the outage, and trying to re-establish thousands of leases at once pushed it into congestive collapse; network configuration for new instances queued behind it; and automated network load balancer health checks began removing capacity. Full recovery took until the afternoon of 20 October, about 15 hours. Applications that ran only in us-east-1 — including many well-known consumer services — were degraded or down, and some teams found their "global" tooling was hosted there. AWS disabled the DNS automation worldwide until the race condition was fixed, and added velocity controls and throttling to the recovery systems. The lessons for architects: a region is a shared-software failure domain even with multiple zones; recovery can take far longer than the fault; systems that must survive a region need an independent second region whose failover doesn't rely on control-plane actions in the failed one; and static stability — having enough capacity already running — beats counting on scaling during an incident.

A one-minute calculation showing why a second region is only as good as your independence and your failover. It needs only Python 3.

HOURS = 365 * 24

def downtime(avail):
    return (1 - avail) * HOURS

two_independent = 1 - (1 - region) ** 2          # either region can serve alone
shared = 0.9995                                  # a global dependency both regions need
two_with_shared = two_independent * shared       # series: all traffic still needs it
failover_works = 0.9                             # failover succeeds 90% of the time
two_with_bad_failover = region + (1 - region) * failover_works * region

for name, a in [("one region", region),
                ("two regions, independent", two_independent),
                ("two regions + shared global dependency", two_with_shared),
                ("two regions, failover works 90%", two_with_bad_failover)]:
    print(f"{name:<40} {a:.5%}  ~{downtime(a):5.1f} h/year down")

Output:

one region                               99.90000%  ~  8.8 h/year down
two regions, independent                 99.99990%  ~  0.0 h/year down
two regions + shared global dependency   99.94990%  ~  4.4 h/year down
two regions, failover works 90%          99.98991%  ~  0.9 h/year down

What it shows. On paper, two independent regions cut downtime from 8.8 hours a year to under a minute. Add a single global dependency both regions need — a login service at 99.95% — and it's 4.4 hours: that dependency now dominates everything. Make failover succeed only 90% of the time and you lose most of the gain too. The work of multi-region resilience is mostly removing shared dependencies and making failover reliable, not deploying the second copy.

Think it through. (1) Set failover_works = 0.5 — roughly what an untested failover might achieve — and see how much of the second region's value remains. (2) Your service depends on three global services at 99.95%, 99.99% and 99.9%. What's the best availability any number of regions can give you? (3) If an hour of downtime costs £20,000 and a warm standby costs £250,000 a year, is it worth it for going from 8.8 hours to 1 hour a year?

Decide

A B2B payments dashboard runs in one region across 3 AZs. Leadership asks for "multi-region active-active" after a competitor's regional outage. Requirements you uncover: the SLA is 99.9%; customers can tolerate 30 minutes of downtime in a regional disaster, but losing confirmed payment records is unacceptable; EU customers' data must stay in the EU; budget is limited. What do you propose?

Answer
  • Translate to objectives: RTO ≈ 30 min; RPO ≈ 0 for payment records (minutes acceptable for other data); residency by customer jurisdiction. Active-active isn't required by any of them, and it would add write-conflict handling or cross-region consensus latency and roughly double the cost.
  • Proposal: warm standby per jurisdiction. EU customers in an EU primary region with a standby in a second EU region; others likewise in their own pair. Residency is preserved because replication stays within the jurisdiction.
  • RPO ≈ 0: payment records must not be lost, so either replicate synchronously to the standby region for the payments ledger only (accepting 10-20 ms added to those writes between nearby regions), or treat the external payment processor as a second source of truth and reconcile after failover — state which and why; keeping reconciliation as a safety net either way is wise.
  • RTO 30 min: the standby runs scaled-down compute deployed from the same infrastructure code, with production-level quotas pre-approved; an automated, rehearsed failover runbook triggered by a human decision; anycast global load balancing or DNS with low TTLs.
  • Remove shared dependencies: identity, secrets, CI/CD and monitoring must not live only in the primary region.
  • Prove it: quarterly failover drills measuring real RTO and RPO, and monthly restore tests of backups.
  • Cost: warm standby at roughly 30-50% extra rather than 100%+; revisit active-active only if the RTO requirement shrinks to near zero. Mention cheaper everyday wins too: cell-by-cell deployments and zone headroom prevent more outages than a second region.
