A user in London changes their profile photo. The request goes to a database in Europe, which says "saved". A second later their friend in Singapore opens the profile, served from a copy of the database in Asia, and sees the old photo. Is the system broken? Now a harder case: two people, one in London and one in Singapore, try to register the username "mei" at the same moment. Each request reaches its nearest copy of the database, each copy checks "is 'mei' taken?", each sees no, and each says yes. Now two accounts have the same unique username. That one really is broken.

Both situations come from the same fact: once data has copies on several machines, and machines are connected by a network that can be slow or break, "what is the current value?" stops having an obvious answer. Keeping every copy identical at every instant requires the machines to talk to each other before answering, which takes time — tens or hundreds of milliseconds between continents — and becomes impossible when the network between them fails. So every distributed system makes choices about how much agreement to require, and those choices show up as behaviour users can see: stale photos, duplicate usernames, lost updates, or requests that fail while the network is broken.

This topic gives you the vocabulary and the mechanisms to make those choices deliberately. A consistency model is a precise contract about what reads may return; you'll meet the important ones, from linearizability (behaves like one copy) to eventual consistency (copies agree eventually), with concrete examples of what each allows. CAP describes the unavoidable choice during a network partition, and PACELC the everyday trade-off between consistency and latency. You'll see why clocks can't be trusted to order events and what systems use instead. And you'll learn how consensus — specifically the Raft algorithm — lets a group of machines agree on a single ordered history despite failures, which is what powers leader election, Kubernetes, etcd and distributed databases.

The payoff in interviews is the ability to say, for each feature in a design, exactly what consistency it needs and why — usernames linearizable, like counts eventual, a user's own profile read-your-writes — instead of declaring a whole system "CP" or "AP".

The analogy: a team editing a shared schedule by phone

Imagine a team keeping a meeting-room schedule, but instead of one whiteboard, each of three offices keeps its own paper copy. When someone books a room, their office updates its copy and phones the others. Most of the time this works. But phone calls take time, and sometimes the line to one office is down.

The team has to decide on rules. Rule one: before confirming any booking, get agreement from a majority of offices. Bookings are slower, and if your office can't reach a majority it can't confirm anything, but no room is ever double-booked, and anyone who checks with a majority sees the latest bookings. That's strong consistency, achieved through consensus. Rule two: confirm immediately and phone the others when you can. Bookings are instant and each office keeps working even when the phones are down, but two offices can book the same room for the same slot, and someone has to sort it out later. That's eventual consistency, choosing availability.

When the phone line to one office is cut, that office faces the choice CAP describes: keep booking and risk conflicts, or stop booking until the line is restored. And even on a normal day, rule one makes every booking wait for phone calls — the latency cost that PACELC points out.

The analogy breaks in one way: people on a phone can tell a dead line from a busy one. Machines usually can't tell whether another machine has crashed, is overloaded, or is just unreachable — they only see silence. That uncertainty is at the root of everything in this topic.

Three questions that organise the topic

What can a read return? Consistency models answer this. The strongest, linearizability, says the system behaves exactly as if there were one copy: once a write finishes, every later read sees it. The weakest common one, eventual consistency, says only that copies will agree if writes stop. Between them are models that match what users actually notice, such as "you always see your own changes".

What happens when the network fails? CAP answers this: during a partition, when some nodes can't reach others, a system must either refuse some requests or accept that answers may disagree. There's no third option.

How do machines agree at all? Consensus algorithms answer this: a group of typically three or five machines agrees on an ordered log of decisions, as long as a majority can talk to each other. Raft is the one to know.

Keep one picture in mind for the rest of the lesson: every guarantee has a price paid in waiting. To promise that everyone sees the latest value, some replicas must hear about each write before anyone is told it succeeded, so the stronger the promise, the more waiting per operation, and the more requests that must fail when replicas can't reach each other. Choosing a consistency model is choosing how much of that price each feature is worth.

1. What a consistency model is

When there's one copy of the data and one operation at a time, "read returns the last value written" is obviously right. With several copies, and many clients whose operations overlap in time, it isn't obvious at all. A consistency model is a contract that answers precisely: given the operations clients made and when, which results are allowed? Stronger models allow fewer surprising results and cost more coordination; weaker ones allow more surprises and cost less.

It helps to picture a history: a timeline for each client, showing each operation as an interval from when it was sent to when its answer came back. Operations from different clients can overlap. A consistency model says which values each read in the history may return. Throughout this section, imagine a single value x (a register) that starts at 0.

2. Linearizability: behaves like one copy

Linearizability is the strongest model for single objects, and what people usually mean by "strong consistency". It requires that every operation appears to take effect at a single instant somewhere between its start and its end, and that all reads and writes are consistent with that order of instants. The effect is that the system behaves as if there were exactly one copy and each operation happened atomically. In particular: once a write has completed, every read that starts afterwards returns that value or a newer one, no matter which replica it asks.

Look at history 2. B's read overlaps A's write, so B may see either 0 or 1; it saw 1. C's read starts after B's read finished. If the system behaved like one copy, x became 1 at some instant before B's read returned, so C must see 1. Seeing 0 means C was served by a replica that hadn't received the write yet — typical of asynchronous replication (sc6) — and no single timeline can explain it.

Linearizability is what you need when correctness depends on everyone agreeing on the latest value right now: uniqueness (only one account may have username "mei"), locks and leader election (only one node may think it holds the lock), balances with invariants (an account must not go below zero), and any "compare-and-set" operation. Its costs: every operation must coordinate with other replicas (usually through a leader and a majority), so it adds latency, and a node that can't reach the majority must stop answering — it can't be available during a partition.

3. Weaker models and what each allows

Sequential consistency requires that all clients see all operations in one single order, and that each client's own operations appear in the order it made them — but that order needn't match real time. In history 2, sequential consistency would allow C to read 0 if the agreed order put C's read before A's write, even though it happened later on the clock. It's weaker than linearizability but still requires global agreement on order.

Causal consistency requires only that operations related by cause and effect are seen in that order by everyone. If Alice posts "Anyone for lunch?" and Bob, having read it, replies "Yes!", then no one may see Bob's reply without Alice's post. Operations with no causal link — two unrelated posts — may be seen in different orders by different readers. Causal consistency prevents the most confusing anomalies (answers before questions, a comment on a photo you can't see) and, importantly, it's the strongest model that can stay available during a partition: each side can keep accepting writes and ordering them causally, and merge later.

Session guarantees are promises scoped to one client's session, and they're what users notice most:

  • Read-your-writes: after you change something, you see your change.
  • Monotonic reads: once you've seen a value, you never see an older one.
  • Monotonic writes: your writes are applied in the order you made them.
  • Writes-follow-reads: a write you make after reading something is ordered after what you read (your reply comes after the post you replied to).

sc6 showed how to implement the first two with replicas: route a user's reads to the leader after a write, pin a user to one replica, or track the log position their session has seen. Session guarantees are cheap because they only coordinate one client's view, not everyone's.

Eventual consistency promises only that if writes stop, all replicas eventually converge to the same value. It doesn't say when, and it allows reads to go backwards, disagree between replicas, or miss your own writes. It's the natural result of asynchronous replication and leaderless stores with low quorums, and it's perfectly adequate for data where a slightly stale or temporarily inconsistent value does no harm: like counts, view counts, recommendation lists, most feeds.

When writes can happen concurrently on different replicas, eventual consistency also needs a way to converge. Last-writer-wins by timestamp converges but loses data (and relies on clocks, section 6). CRDTs (conflict-free replicated data types) are data structures designed so that concurrent updates always merge to the same result regardless of order — for example, a counter that keeps a separate count per replica and reports the sum, so increments on both sides of a partition are all kept.

4. Linearizability versus serializability

These two are constantly confused, and interviewers like to check. Linearizability (this lesson) is about single objects and real time: each read or write on one key appears instantaneous, and a completed write is visible to everyone afterwards. Serializability (sc5) is about transactions over many objects: the outcome equals some one-at-a-time order of the transactions, but that order doesn't have to match real time — a serializable database may let a read-only transaction see a snapshot from a few seconds ago, as long as the results are consistent with some serial order. Strict serializability combines them: transactions behave as if run one at a time, in an order consistent with real time. Google Spanner provides strict serializability; a single PostgreSQL server at serializable isolation is effectively strictly serializable for transactions on the primary, but reads from asynchronous replicas are neither linearizable nor strictly serializable.

5. CAP, stated correctly, and PACELC

The CAP theorem (conjectured by Eric Brewer in 2000, proved by Gilbert and Lynch in 2002) is often quoted as "consistency, availability, partition tolerance — pick two". That phrasing misleads. Here's the precise version. Take a system with data replicated across nodes. C means linearizability. A means every request received by a non-failed node gets a non-error response. P refers to a network partition: some nodes can't communicate with others, though all may be running. The theorem says that when a partition occurs, the system can't provide both C and A.

The reason is simple. Suppose the network splits replicas into two groups, and a client writes x = 1 to a replica in group 1. A client then reads x from a replica in group 2. For availability, group 2 must answer — but it can't know about the write, so it returns the old value, violating linearizability. For linearizability, group 2 must refuse or wait until the partition heals — violating availability. There's no third option.

Since networks do partition — switch failures, misconfigurations, cut cables, overloaded links — "P" isn't optional; you don't get to choose "CA" for a distributed system. The real content of CAP is: decide in advance what each operation does during a partition. A CP choice means the side without a majority (or without the leader) refuses writes and possibly reads; a consensus-based system does this. An AP choice means both sides keep answering and reconcile later; Dynamo-style stores and multi-leader setups do this.

CAP is narrow in two ways worth knowing. It uses the strictest definitions (linearizability, and availability for every non-failed node), so many real systems are neither strictly CP nor AP. And it says nothing about the normal case, when there's no partition — which is almost all the time. PACELC (Daniel Abadi, 2012) fills that gap: if there's a Partition, choose Availability or Consistency; Else, choose Latency or Consistency. Even with a perfect network, keeping replicas consistent means waiting for them on each operation. A write that must be acknowledged by a majority across three zones waits for the second-fastest zone, every time; across regions, that's tens to hundreds of milliseconds.

The practical lesson is that consistency is a per-operation choice, and many systems expose it: Cassandra's consistency levels per query, DynamoDB's strongly consistent read flag, PostgreSQL's choice of reading from the primary or a replica. Good designs use strong consistency only where an invariant needs it.

6. Time and order: why clocks aren't enough

Many consistency problems come down to order: which write happened last? The obvious answer — compare timestamps — doesn't work across machines. Each machine's clock is a quartz oscillator that drifts (by tens of parts per million, which adds up to seconds per day without correction). Time synchronisation with NTP (the Network Time Protocol) corrects clocks periodically, but typically only to within a few milliseconds on a good network and much worse on a bad one, and corrections can make a clock jump — even backwards. Virtual machines can pause and resume with stale clocks. So a write that really happened later can carry an earlier timestamp, and "last writer wins by timestamp" silently discards it.

Distributed systems use other tools to order events.

Happened-before, defined by Leslie Lamport in 1978: event A happened before event B if they're on the same node and A came first, or if A is sending a message and B is receiving it, or through a chain of those. If neither happened before the other, they're concurrent: no information could have flowed between them, so neither could have influenced the other.

Lamport clocks: each node keeps a counter, increments it for every event, attaches it to every message, and on receiving a message sets its counter to the maximum of its own and the received value, plus one. If A happened before B, A's timestamp is smaller. Ties are broken by node ID, giving a total order consistent with causality — enough, for example, to agree on an order of operations. The limitation: a smaller timestamp doesn't prove A happened before B; they might be concurrent.

Vector clocks: each node keeps a list of counters, one per node, and merges lists on messages. Comparing two vectors tells you whether one event happened before the other or whether they were concurrent, which is exactly what you need to detect conflicting writes in a multi-leader or leaderless store (sc6's version vectors are the same idea).

Hybrid logical clocks (HLCs) combine a physical timestamp with a logical counter: timestamps stay close to real time (useful for humans and for time-based queries) while still respecting causality even when clocks disagree slightly. CockroachDB and YugabyteDB use them, together with a configured maximum clock offset; a node whose clock drifts beyond it shuts itself down rather than risk ordering errors.

Bounded-uncertainty clocks: Google Spanner's TrueTime uses GPS receivers and atomic clocks in each datacentre so that every server knows the current time as an interval, "between t − ε and t + ε", with ε usually a few milliseconds. When a transaction commits with timestamp t, Spanner waits until it's certain that t has passed everywhere (commit wait, about 2ε), which guarantees that any transaction starting afterwards gets a larger timestamp. That's how it provides strict serializability globally: it pays a few milliseconds per commit to turn clock uncertainty into certainty.

7. Consensus: agreeing on one history

Linearizability, leader election, locks and uniqueness all need a group of machines to agree on something despite failures: who the leader is, whether a lock is held, the order of writes. That's consensus. Formally, a consensus algorithm lets nodes propose values and guarantees that they all decide the same value (agreement), that the decided value was actually proposed (validity), and that every working node eventually decides (termination), even if some nodes crash.

In practice, systems use consensus repeatedly to agree on an ordered log of commands — "set x = 1", "grant lock to node 3", "partition 23 moves to shard 2". If every replica applies the same commands in the same order, and the commands are deterministic, every replica ends in the same state. This is state machine replication, and it's how etcd, ZooKeeper, Consul, and the per-range replication in Spanner, CockroachDB and TiDB work.

All practical consensus algorithms rest on majority quorums. A decision needs acknowledgement from more than half the nodes. Any two majorities of the same group overlap in at least one node, so two conflicting decisions can never both be made: the overlapping node would have to acknowledge both, and the algorithm prevents that. It also means a group of 2f + 1 nodes keeps working with up to f nodes failed or unreachable: three nodes tolerate one failure, five tolerate two. A partition that leaves no side with a majority stops the whole group — the CP choice made concrete.

A deep theoretical result, the FLP impossibility (Fischer, Lynch and Paterson, 1985), proves that in a fully asynchronous system, where messages can be delayed without bound, no deterministic algorithm can guarantee consensus will always terminate if even one node may crash. Practical algorithms get around it by assuming the network is usually timely enough (partial synchrony): they are always safe — they never decide two different values — and make progress whenever the network behaves reasonably, using timeouts to detect failures.

Paxos, invented by Leslie Lamport, is the classic consensus algorithm and underlies Google's Chubby lock service and much of Spanner. It's famously hard to understand and to turn into a complete system. Raft, published by Diego Ongaro and John Ousterhout in 2014, solves the same problem with a design explicitly aimed at being understandable, and it's now the most widely implemented: etcd, Consul, CockroachDB, TiKV, Kafka's KRaft mode (which replaced ZooKeeper; ZooKeeper support was removed in Kafka 4.0) and many others.

8. Raft, step by step

Raft splits consensus into three parts: electing a leader, replicating the log, and keeping it safe.

Roles and terms. Each node is a follower, a candidate or the leader. Time is divided into numbered terms; each term has at most one leader. Every message carries the sender's term. A node that sees a higher term immediately adopts it and becomes a follower; a message with a lower term is rejected. Terms are Raft's fencing tokens: a deposed leader that wakes up after a pause sends messages with an old term, and everyone ignores them.

Leader election. The leader sends periodic heartbeats. A follower that hears nothing for its election timeout assumes the leader is gone and becomes a candidate: it increments the term, votes for itself and asks every other node for its vote. Each node votes for at most one candidate per term, and only for a candidate whose log is at least as up to date as its own (a later last term, or the same term and at least as long). A candidate that collects votes from a majority becomes leader and starts sending heartbeats. If two candidates split the vote so neither has a majority, the term ends without a leader and, after another timeout, a new election begins. Election timeouts are randomised — the Raft paper suggests values like 150-300 ms, and etcd defaults to a 1-second election timeout with 100 ms heartbeats — so that usually one node times out first and wins before others start competing.

Log replication. Clients send commands to the leader. The leader appends each command to its log with its index and the current term, and sends it to followers in AppendEntries messages. Each message also includes the index and term of the entry just before the new ones; a follower accepts only if its log matches there, which keeps logs identical up to that point (the log matching property). If a follower's log diverged — it had entries from a deposed leader that were never committed — the leader walks back until the logs match and overwrites the follower's divergent tail.

Commit. Once a majority of nodes, counting the leader, have stored an entry, the leader marks it committed, applies it to its state machine (for example, updates the key-value map) and replies to the client. It tells followers the latest commit index in later messages, and they apply committed entries in order. Committed entries are never lost: any future leader needs votes from a majority, which overlaps the majority that stored the entry, and the voting rule refuses candidates whose logs are behind — so the new leader must already have every committed entry (the leader completeness property). One subtlety the paper handles: a leader only counts replicas to commit entries from its own term; older entries become committed indirectly when a new entry after them is committed.

A failure, traced. Five nodes, leader L in term 3. L's machine loses power. Followers stop receiving heartbeats; after about a second, follower A times out first, becomes a candidate for term 4 and requests votes. B and C, whose logs are no newer than A's, vote for A; with its own vote that's three of five, a majority, so A becomes leader for term 4, typically within one or two seconds of the failure. Clients that were talking to L time out and retry with A. When L comes back, it still believes it's leader of term 3, sends a heartbeat, receives a reply carrying term 4, and immediately steps down to follower. Any uncommitted entries it had are overwritten by A's log. Nothing committed was lost, and there was never a moment with two leaders accepting writes in the same term.

Reads. A leader answering reads from its own state could be stale if it has been deposed without knowing (it's partitioned from the rest). To make reads linearizable, the leader either confirms it's still leader by exchanging a round of heartbeats with a majority before answering (the read-index method), or relies on a lease: followers promise not to elect a new leader for a period, so the leader can serve reads locally during it — faster, but dependent on bounded clock drift. etcd uses read-index by default; some systems let clients opt into cheaper, possibly stale reads from followers.

Membership and log size. Adding or removing nodes must itself go through consensus (Raft uses joint consensus or single-server changes, so there's never a moment with two different majorities). And logs can't grow forever: nodes periodically take a snapshot of their state and discard the log before it; a far-behind follower is brought up to date by sending it the snapshot.

9. What consensus is for, and what it costs

Consensus is the tool for small, critical decisions that must be made exactly once: leader election for databases and schedulers (Patroni holds its leader key in etcd, sc6); configuration and metadata that must change atomically (Kubernetes stores all cluster state in etcd; distributed databases store their partition maps in consensus groups); service membership (sc2); and locks, always combined with fencing tokens so a paused holder can't do damage (etcd's revision numbers and ZooKeeper's zxid work as tokens).

Its costs are direct consequences of the design. Every write needs a majority round trip, so latency is at least the time to reach the second-fastest of three nodes (or the third-fastest of five). Spreading nodes across regions for disaster tolerance adds a cross-region round trip to every write. Throughput is limited by the leader and by disk flushes on a majority. So consensus stores are kept small and used for coordination; systems that need consensus for large data, like Spanner and CockroachDB, run many independent consensus groups, one per range of data, so throughput scales with the number of ranges.

10. A real-world walk-through: choosing consistency feature by feature

A social product runs in two regions, Europe and Asia, and must decide what each feature does, normally and during a partition between the regions. Here's the reasoning an interviewer wants to hear, one feature at a time.

Usernames — linearizable. Uniqueness is an invariant: two accounts with the same name is a real bug. Registration goes through one authoritative place — a unique constraint in the database whose leader lives in one region, or a small consensus-backed registry spanning three locations. Normal cost: a cross-region round trip for users registering far from the leader, which is fine for a rare operation. During a partition, the side that can't reach the leader or majority delays registration with a friendly message. CP, by choice.

Account balances for in-app purchases — linearizable per account. Each account has a home region whose database leader orders all debits for that account; a debit is a conditional update that can't take the balance below zero (sc5). If a user's home is unreachable, debits are refused (credits can be queued safely, because adding money never breaks the invariant). To avoid round trips for users who travel, the home can be moved deliberately.

A user's own profile — read-your-writes. Profiles are written in the user's home region and replicated asynchronously. The user sees their own changes immediately (reads of their own profile go to the home region, or wait for the session's last write position); others see changes within about a second. During a partition, others see slightly stale profiles; nothing breaks.

Posts and comments — causal. A reply must never appear before the post it answers. Each comment carries the version of the thread it was written against, and a region delays showing a comment until its dependencies have arrived. Both regions keep accepting posts during a partition; when it heals, the queued replication catches up in causal order.

Like counts — eventual, with a CRDT. A per-region counter is incremented locally and merged by summing, so no likes are lost during a partition and the displayed count may lag by a second. Nobody is harmed by a count that's briefly off by a few.

Feeds — eventual. Feeds are assembled from replicated data and caches; slightly stale is fine, and during a partition each region shows what it has.

Coordination — consensus. The platform's service discovery, configuration and database leader election use etcd clusters of three nodes per region (spread over zones), not stretched across regions, so a cross-region partition never stops a region's control plane.

The design answer is not "the system is CP" or "the system is AP", but a table like the one this walk-through produces, with the invariant that forces each choice — which is exactly what distinguishes a senior answer.

Why quorums alone aren't linearizable

Leaderless systems with W + R > N guarantee that a read's replicas overlap a completed write's replicas, but linearizability can still fail with concurrent operations. Suppose N = 3, W = 2, R = 2, and a write of x = 1 has so far reached only replica 1. Reader A queries replicas 1 and 2, sees 1 on replica 1 and returns the newer value, 1. Reader B, starting after A finished, queries replicas 2 and 3, both still at 0, and returns 0 — history 2 from the body. The fix, from the ABD algorithm (Attiya, Bar-Noy and Dolev), is for readers to write back the value they're about to return to a write quorum before returning, so that once any reader has returned a value, a majority has it. That costs an extra round trip on reads, which is why Dynamo-style systems usually don't do it, and why "quorum" doesn't mean "linearizable". Even with read repair, last-writer-wins conflict resolution based on clocks can reorder concurrent writes.

Pre-vote, check-quorum and the partially connected node

Basic Raft has a known weakness with partial connectivity, exactly what Cloudflare hit in 2020. A node that can't hear the leader but can reach other nodes keeps timing out, incrementing its term and starting elections. Each higher term forces the healthy leader to step down, even though a majority could still reach it, so the cluster keeps losing its leader. Two extensions fix this. Pre-vote: before incrementing its term, a would-be candidate asks whether others would vote for it; nodes that have heard from a leader recently say no, so an isolated node can't disrupt anything. Check-quorum: a leader that hasn't heard from a majority within an election timeout steps down voluntarily, so a leader cut off from the majority stops accepting writes promptly. etcd supports both. The lesson generalises: clean crash failures are the easy case, and production systems must be hardened against nodes that are half-alive.

Coordination avoidance: keeping invariants without consensus

Some invariants can be maintained without coordination at all, which is valuable because coordination is what costs latency and availability. Peter Bailis's work on invariant confluence gives the test: if two replicas that each satisfy an invariant can always be merged into a state that still satisfies it, the invariant needs no coordination. "Likes only increase" merges fine; "username is unique" doesn't (two valid states, each with one "mei", merge into an invalid one). For invariants that fail the test, techniques like escrow can still avoid coordination on the hot path: divide a global limit (1,000 tickets, a £10,000 credit line) into shares allocated to each region, let each region spend its share locally with only local coordination, and rebalance shares occasionally through a consensus step. That turns a cross-region round trip per operation into one per rebalance.

Consistency in the multi-region databases you'll be asked about

Being able to place real systems on this map is a strong signal. Spanner runs Paxos groups per data split, uses TrueTime commit-wait for strict serializability, and offers stale reads at a chosen timestamp from nearby replicas for lower latency. CockroachDB runs Raft per range with hybrid logical clocks, provides serializable isolation by default, and handles clock uncertainty by restarting reads that encounter values with timestamps inside the uncertainty window. DynamoDB offers eventually consistent reads by default, strongly consistent reads from the partition's leader on request, and global tables that replicate across regions (historically with last-writer-wins reconciliation, and more recently with an option for multi-region strong consistency). Cassandra lets each query pick a consistency level, including LOCAL_QUORUM to keep latency within a region. MongoDB's guarantees depend on read and write concerns, with causal consistency available per session. In each case, the consistency is a set of per-operation choices, not a single label.

Byzantine faults and why most systems ignore them

Everything above assumes nodes are honest: they may crash, pause or be slow, but they don't lie. Byzantine fault tolerance handles nodes that send arbitrary or malicious messages, which requires 3f + 1 nodes to tolerate f faulty ones and considerably more messaging (PBFT and its descendants). Blockchains and some aerospace systems need it; datacentre systems generally don't, because nodes are under one operator's control, and the cost isn't justified. Cloudflare's post-mortem called its partial switch failure "Byzantine" in the loose sense that different nodes saw inconsistent views of the network — a reminder that the crash-failure model's assumption of clean failures is itself an approximation.

Verifying distributed algorithms

Consensus and replication protocols are notoriously easy to get subtly wrong, because bugs appear only under rare interleavings of failures and message delays. Two practices have become standard. Formal specification with TLA+ (used at Amazon, Microsoft, MongoDB and elsewhere) lets engineers model a protocol and have a model checker explore every interleaving of a small configuration, finding bugs before code exists; Raft itself was specified in TLA+. Fault-injection testing in the style of Jepsen runs real clusters under partitions, clock skew and crashes while recording client histories, then checks those histories against the claimed consistency model. Kyle Kingsbury's Jepsen analyses have found consistency violations in a long list of databases, which is why "the documentation says linearizable" should be treated as a claim to verify.

How consistency problems show up

Stale reads users can see. "I changed it and it came back", "the count went down", "I saw a reply to a comment that doesn't exist" — session and causal violations from reading lagging replicas or caches. Diagnose by comparing the replica or cache a request used with the source's current state, and fix with session guarantees rather than global strong consistency.

Invariant violations. Duplicate usernames, oversold inventory, negative balances — linearizability was needed and not provided, often because a check read from a replica or a cache. These must be fixed at the source: route the check-and-write through the authoritative leader with a constraint or conditional write.

Consensus clusters losing their leader. Frequent leader elections in etcd or ZooKeeper, visible as leader-change metrics and failed or slow writes, usually caused by slow disks (the leader can't flush in time), network packet loss, CPU starvation or overly tight election timeouts. Everything depending on the cluster — Kubernetes, service discovery, database failover — degrades with it.

Clock problems. Nodes rejected for exceeding the maximum clock offset in distributed databases, lease-based reads returning stale data, certificates suddenly "not yet valid", or last-writer-wins discarding real updates. Monitor clock offset on every node and alert well before system limits.

What to watch and tune

For consensus stores: leader changes per hour (should be near zero), proposal commit latency, disk fsync latency (etcd is very sensitive to it — its documentation recommends fast SSDs and monitoring the WAL fsync duration), database size against its quota, and the number of pending proposals. For replicated data stores: replication lag, the fraction of reads served at each consistency level, and quorum failures. For every node: clock offset from the time source. Knobs include election timeout and heartbeat interval (longer timeouts reduce false elections at the cost of slower failover), pre-vote and check-quorum settings, per-query consistency levels, and whether reads go to leaders or followers.

A real incident: Cloudflare, November 2020

A network switch in one of Cloudflare's core datacentres degraded in an unusual way: some protocols kept working while others failed, so different machines saw different views of which peers were reachable. In a three-node etcd cluster, one node lost good connectivity to the leader while another kept it. The poorly connected node repeatedly started elections and voted for itself, while the other node kept voting for the existing leader; the cluster couldn't maintain a stable leader and so couldn't accept writes. The switch recovered after about six minutes, but the damage cascaded: the system that managed Cloudflare's database clusters used etcd to coordinate health and failover, and without a writable etcd, two database clusters couldn't confirm the health of their primaries and triggered automatic replica promotions. A defect meant promotion required rebuilding replicas, and the authentication database's primary was overloaded while a rebuild took hours; API availability fell to about 75% and the dashboard became up to 80 times slower, for six and a half hours in total. Engineers diverted read traffic to replicas in another datacentre and throttled non-critical services until a replica finished rebuilding. The lessons map directly onto this topic: partial failures are harder than clean crashes, Raft needs pre-vote and check-quorum protections against a half-connected node, and systems that depend on a consensus store must fail safe — not trigger drastic automated actions — when it can't take writes.

The best hands-on way to understand Raft is to watch it. This lab uses the official interactive visualisation — no installation, about fifteen minutes in a browser.

  1. Open https://raft.github.io/ and scroll to the interactive cluster of five servers. Each circle is a server; the ring around it shows its election timer running down. Watch the first election: one server's timer expires first, it becomes a candidate (the term number increases), requests votes, and becomes leader once it has three votes.
  2. Click the leader and choose request a few times to submit client commands. Watch entries appear in the leader's log, get sent to followers, and change from dashed to solid when committed — that happens once three of five servers have stored them.
  3. Stop the leader (stop). Watch the followers' timers expire, a new election in a higher term, and a new leader. Note how long it takes relative to the timeouts.
  4. Stop two followers as well, leaving two running servers. Try submitting requests: they're appended to the leader's log but never committed, because two of five is not a majority. That's the CP choice, visible.
  5. Restart the stopped servers and watch them catch up, including any divergent entries being overwritten by the leader's log.

What to look for. Commit happens at a majority, not when every server has an entry; elections are won by whoever times out first with an up-to-date log; a higher term always wins; and with no majority, the cluster stays safe but stops making progress.

Think it through. (1) With five servers split 3-2 by a partition, which side can commit, and what happens to requests sent to a leader on the minority side? (2) Why would a four-server cluster be no more fault-tolerant than three? (3) In history 2 from the body, which replication setup (sc6) would produce it, and what would you change to make it linearizable?

Classify and decide

For each feature, say what consistency it needs and what happens during a network partition between two regions: (a) the like count on a post; (b) a username must be unique across the platform; (c) a user's own shopping cart; (d) a bank account balance that must never go negative; (e) a news feed; (f) the configuration that says which database node is the leader.

Answer

(a) Like count: eventual consistency — approximate and briefly stale is fine. AP: both regions keep counting; merge with a CRDT counter (per-region counts summed) when the partition heals, so no likes are lost.

(b) Unique username: a linearizable check-and-set (a uniqueness constraint in one authoritative place, or a consensus-backed registry). CP: during the partition, the region that can't reach the authority must refuse or delay registrations — the alternative is two users with the same name.

(c) Shopping cart: session guarantees (read-your-writes, monotonic reads) for the user; available during partitions, merging conflicting edits by union (Dynamo's classic example) — occasionally a deleted item reappears, which is an acceptable product trade-off.

(d) Balance ≥ 0: linearizable per account — route all debits for an account to its home region's leader. CP: if the home is unreachable, reject or queue debits (credits can be queued safely); availability is sacrificed to protect the invariant. Escrow (pre-allocated spending limits per region) can soften this for small amounts.

(e) Feed: eventual consistency plus causal ordering within a thread; AP — show slightly stale content during a partition.

(f) Leader configuration: consensus (linearizable) — there must never be two leaders. CP: a minority side can't change it; keep consensus groups within a region (across zones) so a cross-region partition doesn't stop either region's coordination.

The senior move is exactly this: decide consistency per feature, from the invariant each one must protect, rather than declaring the whole system CP or AP.
