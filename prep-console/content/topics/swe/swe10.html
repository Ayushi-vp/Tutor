A startup of 40 engineers has one Django application. Deploys take 90 minutes, a heavy report query took down checkout last month, and everyone agrees the code is tangled. The CTO proposes splitting into 25 microservices this quarter. A year later there are 25 services, still 40 engineers, and a new set of problems: a single feature needs coordinated changes to five services released in the right order; the services share two database tables nobody dares move; a slow recommendation service makes checkout time out; each of the 25 pipelines is flaky in its own way; and on-call now means knowing 25 systems. The code isn't less tangled. The tangles are now network calls.

Architecture is the set of decisions that are expensive to change later: how the system is divided into parts, how those parts depend on and talk to each other, and where the data lives. Get them right and most features touch one part, teams work without constantly coordinating, and technology can be swapped at the edges. Get them wrong and every change is a negotiation, and the wrong split — too many pieces, or pieces along the wrong lines — can be more painful than no split at all. Most architecture debates are really about one question from swe1, asked at the scale of a whole system: when a requirement changes, how much has to change with it, and who has to be involved?

This lesson works up from the inside of an application to the shape of a whole organisation's systems. Layered architecture and its main flaw; hexagonal (ports-and-adapters) architecture, which fixes it; domain-driven design's bounded contexts and aggregates, which tell you where the real boundaries are; the modular monolith; microservices and their true costs; event-driven design; and Conway's law, which says your architecture will copy your org chart whether you plan it or not. The case study is Segment's 2018 account of consolidating more than 140 microservices back into a single service — one of the most-read architecture posts of its decade, because it's honest about what the split cost. The lab builds a small hexagonal application, runs one contract test against two storage adapters, and enforces the dependency rule automatically.

The analogy: planning a city

A city works because of zoning and interfaces. Residential, industrial and commercial districts each have their own rules and character (cohesion). They connect through a few standard interfaces — roads, water mains, the electricity grid — rather than every building running private pipes to every other (coupling through narrow interfaces). A power station can be replaced with a wind farm without rewiring every house, because houses connect to the grid, not to the power station (dependency inversion). And cities grow in two ways: one well-zoned town with clear districts, or several separate towns connected by highways, each with its own council. Separate towns can make decisions independently, but every trip between them takes longer and can be blocked, and coordinating anything that spans them needs agreements between councils.

Those are the choices this lesson covers: districts with clear rules (modules and bounded contexts), standard sockets at the edges (ports and adapters), and whether to be one well-planned town (a modular monolith) or several towns joined by highways (microservices). The analogy breaks on how fast software can change — a city can't be re-zoned in a sprint — which is exactly why software architecture should keep boundaries cheap to move until they've proven stable.

The spectrum of structures

Two different questions hide inside "monolith or microservices?". The first is about design: are there clear modules with narrow interfaces, each owning its data? The second is about deployment: do the modules run in one process or many? The first matters for every system; the second only when you have a specific reason — independent teams, very different scaling, isolation. Most of this lesson is about getting the first right: layered and hexagonal architecture structure the inside of each module, and domain-driven design finds where the module boundaries should go. Then we look at what changes when boundaries become network calls, and how to decide.

It also helps to know the vocabulary of costs. Moving from one process to many doesn't just add network latency; it changes what can go wrong. In one process, a function call either returns or raises, and a database transaction either commits everything or nothing. Across processes, a call can time out after the other side has already done the work, two services can disagree about the state of an order for a while, and a deployment of one can break another that depends on its old behaviour. None of that is exotic; it's the everyday texture of distributed systems, and it is the price you pay for independence.

A useful way to hold the whole lesson in your head is as three nested questions. Inside a module: which way do dependencies point — does the business logic depend on the database, or the database adapter on the business logic? Between modules: where are the boundaries, and what crosses them — calls, events, shared data? Across the organisation: which team owns which module, and does the structure let each team change its part without waiting for the others? A good architecture answers all three consistently, and the answers are connected: Conway's law, at the end of the body, explains why the third question quietly decides the second.

1. What counts as architecture

Architecture is not a diagram; it's the set of decisions that would be expensive to reverse. Choosing a variable name isn't architectural. Choosing whether the payment rules may call the database directly, whether two teams share a database schema, or whether a flow is synchronous or event-driven is — because once hundreds of files and several teams depend on the choice, changing it is a project. Architects reason about these decisions in terms of quality attributes — modifiability, testability, performance, availability, security, deployability, cost — and every style in this lesson is a different trade among them. There's no universally best style, only styles that fit particular constraints: team size and structure, rate of change, scale, and the complexity of the business domain.

2. Layered architecture

The most common starting point divides an application into horizontal layers, each allowed to depend only on the layers below it:

  • Presentation: HTTP handlers, templates, API serialisation.
  • Application: use cases that orchestrate a request — "place order": load cart, check stock, charge, save.
  • Domain: business rules and concepts — orders, prices, discounts, what's allowed.
  • Infrastructure / data access: the ORM, SQL, queues, external APIs.

Layers are easy to understand and separate concerns reasonably well. But the classic stack has a flaw that shows up in almost every mature layered codebase: because data access sits at the bottom and dependencies point down, the domain layer imports the database layer. Business rules end up written against ORM models, carry database concerns (lazy loading, session management) into logic, and can't be tested without a database. Changing persistence technology — or even the table layout — ripples into the rules. A second weakness is that a typical feature cuts through every layer (a new field touches handler, use case, domain object, repository and migration), so layers organise code by technical role rather than by what changes together.

3. Hexagonal architecture: ports and adapters

Alistair Cockburn's hexagonal architecture, also called ports and adapters, fixes the first flaw by inverting that one dependency. The application core — domain rules and use cases — sits in the middle and depends on nothing technical. It declares what it needs and offers as ports: interfaces written in the domain's own terms. Technology lives at the edges as adapters that plug into ports. There are two kinds. Driving adapters call into the core: an HTTP handler, a CLI command, a queue consumer, a test. Driven adapters are called by the core through its ports: a Postgres repository, an email sender, a payment gateway client, an LLM client. The hexagon shape has no meaning beyond giving room to draw several ports; "onion" and "clean" architecture are variations on the same rule: source-code dependencies point inward, toward the domain.

In Python, a port is typically a typing.Protocol in the domain package, and the use case receives adapters through its constructor (swe2's dependency injection, wired in a composition root). The lab builds exactly this: a PlaceOrder use case depending on an OrderRepository port, an in-memory and a SQLite adapter implementing it, and an HTTP-style driving adapter that translates the domain's OutOfStock exception into a 409 response. One contract test runs unchanged against both storage adapters — Liskov substitution (swe1) checked automatically. The payoffs: the rules can be read without framework noise; tests of the rules run in milliseconds against in-memory adapters; technology at the edge can be replaced (a new database, a new model provider) without touching the core; and the same use case can be driven from an API, a batch job or an agent's tool call.

The rule is only real if it's enforced. Nothing in Python stops a tired developer from importing the SQLite adapter into the domain "just this once". The lab's checker walks the domain package's imports with ast and fails on any import of adapters or infrastructure libraries; in real projects import-linter expresses the same rules ("the domain may not import the adapters", "these modules are independent") as contracts checked in CI.

4. Domain-driven design: finding the boundaries

Hexagonal architecture says how to structure a module. It doesn't say where modules should begin and end. That's the central contribution of domain-driven design (DDD), from Eric Evans's 2003 book: base the structure of the software on a model of the business domain, built together with domain experts, and draw boundaries where the model's meaning changes.

The key concept is the bounded context. Consider the word "customer" in an online shop. To billing, a customer is an account with a payment method, a billing address, invoices and a credit limit. To shipping, it's a delivery address and contact number. To support, a person with a history of tickets and a satisfaction score. To marketing, a lead with consent flags and campaign history. A single Customer class serving all four accumulates dozens of fields, each used by one team, and every change risks the others — swe1's low cohesion at system scale. DDD's answer is to accept that these are different concepts sharing a word, give each context its own model, and make the relationships between contexts explicit.

Inside a context, the team develops a ubiquitous language: the same terms used by domain experts, in conversations, in tickets and in code. If finance says "dunning" (chasing overdue payments), the code has DunningProcess, not PaymentReminderManager. Between contexts, a context map records relationships: which context is upstream (its model is consumed) and which downstream, whether they share a small kernel of code, whether one conforms to the other's model, or whether the downstream protects itself with an anti-corruption layer — an adapter that translates another system's model into its own, so a legacy CRM's CUST_REC and FLG_07 never leak into new code. Bounded contexts are the best candidates for module boundaries, and later, if needed, for service boundaries: they're where the model is cohesive and the coupling to the rest is narrow.

DDD also offers tactical patterns for modelling inside a context. Entities have identity that persists as attributes change (an order is the same order after its address is corrected). Value objects are defined only by their values and are usually immutable (Money(1299, "EUR"), an address). Domain events record that something meaningful happened (OrderPlaced). Repositories give collection-like access to stored aggregates. The most important is the aggregate: a cluster of objects that must stay consistent together, changed only through a root. An Order with its OrderLines is an aggregate whose invariants — "total equals the sum of lines", "at most 50 lines", "no changes after shipping" — are enforced by the Order root on every change. Two rules make aggregates powerful: modify one aggregate per transaction, and refer to other aggregates by ID, not by object reference. That keeps transactions small and makes explicit which consistency must be immediate (within an aggregate) and which can be eventual (between aggregates) — exactly the information you need before splitting anything across a network.

5. The modular monolith

A modular monolith applies bounded contexts and hexagonal structure inside a single deployable application. Each module corresponds to a context, has a small public interface (a service class, a set of functions, events it publishes), keeps everything else private, and exclusively owns its database tables — other modules can't query them directly, only call the owning module's interface. Import rules enforced in CI keep the boundaries from eroding. It deploys as one unit, runs in one process, uses one database with in-process transactions, and is debugged with one stack trace.

This captures most of what teams want from microservices — clear ownership, cohesive modules, independent reasoning — without the costs of distribution. Shopify, one of the largest Ruby on Rails codebases in existence, worked on by more than a thousand developers, published in 2019 how it restructured its monolith into components with enforced boundaries (tracked by an internal tool that flagged cross-component calls and associations), rather than splitting into services. A modular monolith is also the best preparation for microservices: when a module needs to become a service, its boundary, interface and data ownership already exist, and the extraction is mostly mechanical.

6. Microservices: what they buy and what they cost

Microservices are small, independently deployable services, each owning one business capability and its own data, communicating over the network via APIs (swe7) or messages (sc10). They buy real things. Independent deployment: a team can release its service many times a day without coordinating with others. Independent scaling: the GPU-heavy inference service scales separately from the CRUD service. Fault isolation: a memory leak in one service doesn't crash the others (if calls are designed with timeouts and fallbacks, sc12). Technology choice per service. And team autonomy: a team owns its service end to end.

They cost just as much, and the costs are structural. Every in-process function call that becomes a network call can be slow, fail, or succeed without the caller knowing (swe4's transient faults, now everywhere). Data split across services loses joins and transactions: an operation touching orders and payments needs a saga with compensating actions or an outbox (sc11), and readers see eventual consistency. Interfaces between services become versioned contracts (swe7) that can't be refactored in one commit. Debugging needs distributed tracing (swe14). Each service needs its own pipeline, monitoring, on-call and security patching. Martin Fowler summarises the trade as strong module boundaries, independent deployment and technology diversity, paid for with distribution, eventual consistency and operational complexity — and adds that the boundaries benefit is available without distribution, in a well-disciplined monolith.

The worst outcome is the distributed monolith: services split along the wrong lines, so they can't change independently. Recognise it by coordinated releases, shared databases, long chains of synchronous calls, shared libraries that force lockstep upgrades, and services that can't be tested without most of the others running. It combines the costs of both styles with the benefits of neither — the story at the start of this lesson.

7. Conway's law

In 1968 Melvin Conway published a paper whose thesis became a law: "organizations which design systems … are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations." Three teams building a compiler produce a three-pass compiler; a system built by a front-end team, a back-end team and a database team gets three technical layers with hand-offs between them. The law isn't a curse to escape but a force to use. Service and module boundaries will drift toward team boundaries, because that's where communication is cheap inside and expensive across. So design them together: give each team a cohesive bounded context it can own end to end, and if you want a particular architecture, organise teams to match it — the "inverse Conway manoeuvre". It's also why microservices make most sense for organisations with many teams: a service per team gives each its own deployable unit; 25 services for 40 engineers, as in the introduction, means each engineer is effectively on call for several services owned by no one in particular.

8. Event-driven architecture

Services and modules can interact in two ways. With commands (requests), the caller tells another component to do something and usually waits for the result: "charge this card". With events, a component announces that something happened, and whoever is interested reacts: "OrderPlaced". Events decouple the publisher from its consumers — it doesn't know or wait for them — so new reactions can be added without changing the publisher, and consumers can be down temporarily without losing work if events are queued (sc10).

For a multi-step business process there are two coordination styles. In choreography, each service reacts to events and emits its own: orders emits OrderPlaced, payments reacts and emits PaymentCaptured, shipping reacts to that. There's no central coordinator, which is simple for a few steps, but the process exists only implicitly, spread across subscribers — hard to see, monitor and change. In orchestration, a coordinator (often a workflow engine such as Temporal or AWS Step Functions) holds the process as an explicit state machine (swe3), tells each service what to do, and runs compensations on failure (refund if shipping fails). A reasonable default: choreography for independent reactions (notifications, analytics), orchestration for processes with ordering, deadlines and compensation. Either way, events need the discipline swe7 applied to APIs: versioned schemas, idempotent consumers (events are usually delivered at least once), and correlation IDs for tracing.

Two further event patterns are worth recognising. Event sourcing stores the sequence of events as the source of truth and derives current state by replaying them — valuable for audit-heavy domains, costly in complexity elsewhere. CQRS (command-query responsibility segregation) separates the model used for writes from read models optimised for queries, often kept up to date by events. Both are specialised tools, not defaults.

9. Choosing, and architecture for AI systems

A defensible default for most teams: start with a modular monolith with hexagonal modules along bounded contexts and enforced boundaries (Fowler calls this "monolith first"), and extract a service only when a specific driver appears — a separate team that needs independent deployment, a component with very different scaling or hardware, a need for stronger isolation (security, fault, compliance), or a different technology. Extract along a boundary that has been stable for a while, with its data already private, using the strangler-fig technique (swe11) to move traffic gradually.

AI products fit this pattern well. The product logic — conversations, retrieval orchestration, business rules, evaluation hooks — is a modular monolith whose core calls ports such as ChatModel, Retriever, Moderation and ConversationStore, with adapters per provider and vector database (swe2, swe3). Model inference on GPUs is a strong candidate for a separate service, because its scaling, hardware and deployment cadence genuinely differ from the web tier's. A model gateway shared across teams centralises rate limits, retries, cost accounting, provider fallback and logging. And ML pipelines — feature computation, training, evaluation — form their own bounded contexts with their own language ("feature", "label", "snapshot"), whose contract with serving (feature definitions, model interfaces) deserves the same versioning care as any API.

Architecture as trade-offs among quality attributes

Every style here optimises some qualities at the expense of others, and senior architecture discussions make that explicit. Microservices raise deployability and scalability of individual parts, lower performance (network hops) and consistency, and raise operational cost. Hexagonal architecture raises modifiability and testability at the cost of some indirection. Event-driven design raises availability and extensibility at the cost of understandability. Methods such as ATAM (the Architecture Tradeoff Analysis Method) formalise this: list the quality attributes that matter, express them as concrete scenarios ("a new payment provider can be added in two weeks by one team", "the checkout path survives the recommendations service being down"), and evaluate candidate designs against the scenarios. The practical version for a design doc (swe15): name the two or three qualities that matter most for this system and show how the design serves them.

Data ownership is the real boundary

Code boundaries are easy to draw and easy to erode; data boundaries are what make modules or services truly independent. A shared database table is an interface nobody designed: every module that reads it depends on its exact columns and meaning, so it can't change without coordinating all of them (connascence of name and meaning, swe1). The discipline is exclusive ownership: each table belongs to one module, which exposes the data through its interface or publishes events; other modules that need the data for queries keep their own read-optimised copies (replicated via events) rather than joining across boundaries. This is also what makes extracting a service feasible. Teams that split code into services while leaving a shared database have built a distributed monolith with an extra network hop.

Aggregate design and consistency boundaries

Aggregates encode a decision about which invariants need immediate, transactional consistency. Vaughn Vernon's rules of thumb: design small aggregates (large ones cause concurrency conflicts, since any change to any part locks the whole); reference other aggregates by ID; use eventual consistency between aggregates, updating others via domain events after the transaction commits. Consider an order and inventory. If "never oversell" must be absolute, stock reservation must happen in the same transaction as order placement — so inventory for a product becomes part of that consistency boundary, or reservations become their own aggregate that the order waits on. If a small oversell rate is acceptable and handled by back-ordering, order placement and stock decrement can be separate aggregates updated eventually. That's a business decision disguised as a technical one, and DDD's value is in surfacing it before the architecture hard-codes an answer.

Fitness functions: keeping architecture from eroding

Architectural intent decays under deadline pressure unless it's checked automatically. Architectural fitness functions, from Building Evolutionary Architectures, are automated tests of architectural properties: import rules (the domain doesn't import adapters; module A doesn't import module B's internals), dependency-cycle detection, maximum coupling metrics, "no module queries another module's tables" (checked by parsing SQL or ORM usage), latency budgets for key paths, and contract tests between services. They run in CI like unit tests. Shopify's component tooling and Python's import-linter are fitness functions; so is the lab's dependency-rule check. The point is that a boundary written only in a wiki will be crossed within a quarter.

Sizing services: capabilities, not nouns

A common mistake is to create a service per entity — an Order service, a Customer service, a Product service — each a thin wrapper around a table. Every business operation then crosses several services, which is the definition of high coupling. Services should instead align with business capabilities and bounded contexts — checkout, fulfilment, billing, catalogue management — each containing the entities and rules it needs, with its own copies of data it reads from others. A second sizing heuristic is team cognitive load: a service (or set of services) should be small enough for one team to understand deeply and operate, and a team shouldn't own more services than it can run well. Segment's case shows a third axis: services that are replicas of the same capability (one per destination) multiply operational cost without adding independence, because the same team owns them all and changes them together.

Cells: isolation without per-capability services

Segment's biggest loss in consolidating was fault isolation — one bad destination could crash the shared service. There's a middle path that many large systems use: keep one codebase and one service design, but run it as several independent cells or bulkheads, each serving a subset of customers or a class of workload with its own resources, queues and limits (sc12, sc17). A bug or overload in one cell affects only its slice. Isolation then comes from how the system is deployed and partitioned, not from splitting the code into dozens of services — often the better trade when the parts are homogeneous.

How architecture problems show up in production

Architecture failures rarely appear as a single incident; they appear as friction that grows. Lead time creep: features that took days take weeks because they cross many modules or services and need coordination. Coordinated deploys: releases that must go out in a particular order across several services. Cascading failures: synchronous call chains where one slow service times out everything upstream. Data inconsistency incidents after splitting a transaction across services without a saga or outbox. On-call load that grows with the number of services rather than with traffic. And erosion: boundaries crossed "just this once" until the modular design is a diagram nobody believes. The metrics that reveal them: change lead time and the number of modules or services touched per change, deploy coordination (how often releases are batched across services), pages per service per week, and fitness-function violations over time.

Case: Segment's "Goodbye Microservices"

Segment's core product ingests events from customers' websites and apps — "user signed up", "order completed" — and forwards them to over a hundred "destinations", partner APIs such as analytics and advertising tools. In a 2018 post, engineer Alexandra Noonan described how the destination-delivery system went from a simple design to more than 140 microservices, and back to one service.

The original design used a single queue: events were consumed, the customer's settings decided which destinations should receive each one, and the event was sent to each destination's API in turn, with failed requests retried later. That caused head-of-line blocking: if one destination slowed or went down, retries flooded the shared queue, and delivery to every destination was delayed. So the team created a separate service and queue for each destination, with a router distributing copies of each event — a microservice design that isolated destinations from one another, which the post calls crucial. Each destination's code needs custom transformation logic for that partner's API format, and to stop one destination's failing test from blocking deploys of all the others, each destination's code moved into its own repository.

Then the costs compounded. Over 50 new destinations meant 50 new repositories. Shared libraries for common logic made new destinations quick to build, but a change to a shared library meant testing and deploying dozens of services, so under time pressure engineers updated the library in only the destination they were working on, and versions diverged until, in the post's words, "all of them were using different versions of these shared libraries". Each service had a distinct load pattern, from a handful of events a day to thousands a second, and a distinct CPU and memory profile, which made autoscaling "more art than science"; low-traffic services had to be scaled by hand during spikes, and on-call engineers were regularly paged for load spikes. With the team adding about three destinations a month, "operational overhead increased linearly with each added destination." By the end, three full-time engineers spent most of their time just keeping the system alive.

The fix consolidated the more than 140 services into a single service. A new delivery system (which they called Centrifuge) replaced the per-destination queues. All destination code moved into one repository with a single version of each of its 120 unique dependencies, fixing destinations that broke on upgrade. The flaky tests — mostly caused by real HTTP calls to partner APIs, with some destinations taking up to five minutes and the whole suite up to an hour — were replaced by a "Traffic Recorder" that recorded each test's requests and responses once and replayed them (swe5's record-and-replay), after which the tests for all destinations ran in milliseconds. One engineer could deploy the service in minutes. Improvements to shared libraries rose from 32 in the microservice period to 46 in the following year, and pooling all destinations' mixed CPU and memory loads in one large worker pool absorbed spikes, ending the pages.

The post is valuable because it's candid about what was lost. Fault isolation: a bug in one destination that crashes the service now crashes it for all. Cache efficiency: in-memory caches are spread across thousands of processes. Dependency coupling: upgrading a library may break several destinations at once. The team judged these worth the trade and described work on isolation within the monolith. In this lesson's terms, the destinations weren't independent business capabilities owned by different teams — they were one capability, owned by one team, replicated 140 times — so the microservice split multiplied operational cost without adding team independence. And the problems that motivated each step (flaky tests, shared-library upgrades) were at least as much about testing and tooling as about architecture, which the post acknowledges.

Architecture in AI teams

AI platforms repeat Segment's pattern in a new form: one service per model, per prompt, per customer fine-tune or per tool, each with its own deployment, dashboards and on-call, where the underlying capability is one. The same reasoning applies: split where scaling, hardware or ownership genuinely differ (GPU inference versus the product API versus offline training pipelines), and keep homogeneous things together with isolation provided by deployment — separate worker pools, per-tenant rate limits, bulkheads — rather than by multiplying services. And keep the core of the product hexagonal, so the fastest-changing parts of the stack — providers, models, vector stores — are adapters at the edge that can be swapped without touching product logic.

Tools and knobs

Python: packages per bounded context, typing.Protocol ports, a composition root, and import-linter contracts in CI (layers, independence, forbidden imports). Across services: API and event schemas under version control with breaking-change checks, contract tests, distributed tracing, a service catalogue recording owners and dependencies, and workflow engines for orchestrated processes. For decisions: architecture decision records (swe15) capturing why a boundary or split was chosen, so future teams can revisit it knowingly.

(1) one contract test run against two adapters, (2) an import rule that keeps the domain free of infrastructure."""

import importlib
import pathlib
import sys

FILES = {
    "shop/__init__.py": "",
    "shop/domain/__init__.py": "",
    "shop/domain/orders.py": '''
        from dataclasses import dataclass
        from typing import Protocol

        class OrderRepository(Protocol):            # a PORT: owned by the domain, in the domain's words
            def next_id(self) -> int: ...
            def save(self, order: "Order") -> None: ...
            def get(self, order_id: int) -> "Order | None": ...

        class OutOfStock(Exception): ...

        @dataclass
        class Order:
            id: int
            sku: str
            qty: int
            status: str = "placed"

        class PlaceOrder:                            # a use case: pure policy, no I/O of its own
            def __init__(self, repo: OrderRepository, stock: dict[str, int]):
                self.repo, self.stock = repo, stock
            def __call__(self, sku: str, qty: int) -> Order:
                if qty <= 0:
                    raise ValueError("qty must be positive")
                if self.stock.get(sku, 0) < qty:
                    raise OutOfStock(sku)
                self.stock[sku] -= qty
                order = Order(self.repo.next_id(), sku, qty)
                self.repo.save(order)
                return order
    ''',
    "shop/adapters/__init__.py": "",
    "shop/adapters/memory.py": '''
        from shop.domain.orders import Order

        class InMemoryOrders:                        # an ADAPTER for tests and local runs
            def __init__(self): self.rows: dict[int, Order] = {}
            def next_id(self) -> int: return len(self.rows) + 1
            def save(self, order: Order) -> None: self.rows[order.id] = order
            def get(self, order_id: int): return self.rows.get(order_id)
    ''',
    "shop/adapters/sqlite.py": '''
        import sqlite3
        from shop.domain.orders import Order

        class SqliteOrders:                          # an ADAPTER for production-like storage
            def __init__(self, path=":memory:"):
                self.db = sqlite3.connect(path)
                self.db.execute("CREATE TABLE IF NOT EXISTS orders (id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, sku TEXT, qty INT, status TEXT)")
            def next_id(self) -> int:
                return self.db.execute("SELECT COALESCE(MAX(id), 0) + 1 FROM orders").fetchone()[0]
            def save(self, order: Order) -> None:
                self.db.execute("INSERT OR REPLACE INTO orders VALUES (?, ?, ?, ?)",
                                (order.id, order.sku, order.qty, order.status))
            def get(self, order_id: int):
                row = self.db.execute("SELECT id, sku, qty, status FROM orders WHERE id = ?", (order_id,)).fetchone()
                return Order(*row) if row else None
    ''',
    "shop/adapters/http.py": '''
        import json
        from shop.domain.orders import OutOfStock

        def handle_post_order(body: str, place_order) -> tuple[int, str]:   # a DRIVING adapter: HTTP-ish in, use case out
            try:
                req = json.loads(body)
                order = place_order(req["sku"], int(req["qty"]))
            except OutOfStock as e:
                return 409, json.dumps({"code": "out_of_stock", "sku": str(e)})
            except (ValueError, KeyError) as e:
                return 400, json.dumps({"code": "invalid_request", "detail": str(e)})
            return 201, json.dumps({"id": order.id, "status": order.status})
    ''',
}

def imports_of(path: pathlib.Path) -> set[str]:
    tree = ast.parse(path.read_text())
    mods = set()
    for n in ast.walk(tree):
        if isinstance(n, ast.Import):
            mods |= {a.name for a in n.names}
        elif isinstance(n, ast.ImportFrom) and n.module:
            mods.add(n.module)
    return mods

def check_dependency_rule(root: pathlib.Path) -> list[str]:
    """Domain code may import only the standard library and other domain modules."""
    forbidden = ("shop.adapters", "sqlite3", "json", "requests", "http")
    bad = []
    for f in (root / "shop" / "domain").rglob("*.py"):
        for m in imports_of(f):
            if m.startswith(forbidden):
                bad.append(f"{f.relative_to(root).as_posix()} imports {m}")
    return bad

Advise

A 40-engineer company runs one Django monolith. Deploys take 90 minutes, a bug in reporting took down checkout last month, and the CTO wants to split into 25 microservices this quarter. What do you recommend?

Answer

Diagnose the actual problems: slow deploys (a build and test pipeline problem) and lack of fault isolation between reporting and checkout (a resource-isolation problem). Neither requires 25 services.

  • Fix the pipeline first: parallelise tests, cache builds, test only affected modules, remove flaky tests — 90 minutes is usually a CI problem.
  • Isolate reporting: point it at a read replica or warehouse and run it as separate worker processes with their own resources, so heavy queries can't starve checkout. That fixes the incident class immediately.
  • Modularise: map bounded contexts (checkout, catalogue, accounts, reporting) with domain experts, move code into packages per context, give each its own tables accessed only through its interface, and enforce boundaries with import-linter in CI.
  • Extract selectively: move one or two contexts out when there's a concrete reason — a separate team, a different scaling profile — using the strangler-fig approach behind the same interface.

Why not 25 services now: 40 engineers can't own 25 services with on-call; the monolith's implicit transactions become distributed consistency problems (sagas, outboxes); without tracing and per-service CI you'd build a distributed monolith; and Segment's experience shows operational cost growing with every service. Frame it as a sequence with measurable goals — deploy time, incidents crossing module boundaries, lead time — not a rejection.
