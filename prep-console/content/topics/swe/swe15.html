On Monday morning a manager asks a senior engineer: "Can we add multi-currency support to checkout by the end of the month?" The engineer knows the code assumes pounds sterling in more than forty places and stores prices as floating-point numbers. One kind of engineer says "sure" and spends the month discovering why it was never going to fit. Another says "no" and is seen as unhelpful. A third says "let me come back on Wednesday with options", spends two days on a spike, and returns with a one-page design, a range — about seven weeks for everything, most likely, or three weeks for euros only behind a flag — and a note that the float prices are a correctness bug that needs fixing whatever is decided. The third engineer is doing technical leadership.

At some point in every engineer's growth, the constraint on their impact stops being how well they code and becomes how well they help a group of people build the right thing. That shift shows up in a handful of skills: writing clearly enough that a design can be reviewed and a decision can outlive the meeting; estimating honestly under uncertainty and communicating it so others can plan; turning yes-or-no questions into options with trade-offs; influencing teams you don't manage; and growing the people around you. These are learnable, and interviews for senior roles probe them directly — "tell me about a design you led", "how do you estimate", "describe a disagreement".

This lesson covers them in practical terms. Design docs and architecture decision records: what goes in them, when to write them and how review works. Estimation: why estimates are wrong in a predictable direction, and how to turn a T-shirt size into an honest range with three-point estimates and a Monte Carlo simulation — worked through in the lab. Then scope negotiation, influence without authority, and mentoring. The case study is the launch of HealthCare.gov, the US health-insurance marketplace, on 1 October 2013, whose failure the Government Accountability Office traced largely to planning and oversight: work started with key requirements unknown, costs roughly quadrupled, a readiness review slid to weeks before launch, and the site went live without verification that it met performance requirements. The last part covers what's different when the work involves ML models, where some of it is research.

The analogy: a mountain guide

A good mountain guide doesn't carry every client up the mountain. Before the trip, they study the route and write down the plan: the goal (the summit, or a safe turnaround point), what the group won't attempt, the options at each junction and why they chose this path, and what could go wrong. They give honest estimates — "six to nine hours, depending on the snow" — rather than a single optimistic number, and they set a turnaround time in advance so the decision isn't made in the moment by tired, summit-hungry people. On the mountain, they keep everyone informed, adjust when conditions change, and help each climber improve. Their success is the group's success.

Technical leadership works the same way: a written plan that others can review, estimates that respect uncertainty, decisions made in advance about when to change course, clear communication as things change, and helping others grow. The analogy breaks in one direction: a guide has authority on the mountain, while a senior engineer usually doesn't — they lead through clarity, trust and persuasion.

What an estimate really is

The single most useful idea in this lesson is that an estimate of uncertain work isn't a number; it's a distribution — and a lopsided one. A task you think will take five days might take four, but it's very unlikely to take one; it might well take ten, or twenty. Outcomes bunch up a little early and trail off far to the right: the distribution is right-skewed. That shape has an uncomfortable consequence: the value people naturally give as their estimate — the most likely outcome, the peak of the curve — is below the average and well below the level you can confidently promise.

Everything else in this lesson follows a similar pattern: replace an implicit, fragile understanding with an explicit, shareable one. A design doc makes an approach explicit so it can be reviewed. An ADR makes a decision's reasons explicit so they survive. A range makes uncertainty explicit so others can plan around it. Options make trade-offs explicit so the right person can choose. Leadership, in this sense, is largely the craft of making things clear enough for a group to act on together.

That framing also explains why these skills matter more as systems and teams grow. One engineer working alone can keep the design, the plan and the reasons in their head. Five engineers across two teams cannot: without written designs they build incompatible parts, without honest ranges their managers make promises the team can't keep, without recorded decisions they re-argue the same questions every quarter. The practices in this lesson are the minimum structure that lets a group of engineers behave like one coherent designer. None of them is heavy: a two-page doc, a half-page ADR, a range instead of a number, three options instead of a yes. Their cost is small and fixed; the cost of skipping them grows with every person who joins the effort.

1. Design docs: thinking in writing, before the code

A design doc is a written proposal for how something will be built, circulated for review before most of the code exists. Its value comes from timing and from writing itself. Timing: a wrong approach found in a document costs an afternoon of rewriting; found in code review, it costs the implementation; found in production, it costs an incident. Writing: putting a design in prose forces precision that whiteboard conversations let slide — "the service caches results" becomes "for how long, keyed on what, invalidated when?" — and it lets people who weren't in the room, or in the same time zone, contribute.

Google's practice, described by Malte Ubl, keeps design docs informal and usually a handful of pages, with a common shape that works almost anywhere:

  • Context and scope: the background a reader needs, and the problem in the user's or business's terms.
  • Goals and non-goals: what success means, measurably where possible ("p95 under 300 ms at 2,000 requests per second"), and what the design deliberately won't do. Non-goals are underrated: "no support for currency conversion at display time" prevents weeks of scope creep and misunderstanding.
  • The design: components and their responsibilities, data model, interfaces and APIs (swe7), key flows, and a diagram. Enough detail to evaluate, not a specification of every function.
  • Alternatives considered: the other plausible approaches and why they lost. This is often the most valuable section, because it shows the trade-offs and prevents reviewers from re-proposing what was already rejected.
  • Cross-cutting concerns: security and privacy (swe13's threat model), reliability and failure modes, observability (swe14), cost, migration and rollout (swe9), and how it will be tested.
  • Open questions and, for bigger projects, milestones.

When to write one: when the decision is expensive to reverse (a data model, an architecture boundary, swe10), crosses team boundaries, significantly affects security, data or cost, or when the same design argument keeps surfacing in code review. When not to: for small, reversible, conventional changes, where a good pull-request description is enough, or when the key uncertainty is best resolved by a quick prototype — then write the doc after the spike.

Review works best in two stages: share an early draft with one or two trusted reviewers to fix major flaws, then circulate more widely with a clear ask and deadline ("comments by Thursday; key question: should we store rates per order or per day?"). Resolve comments in the document so the reasoning is preserved, hold a short meeting only for genuinely contested points, and record the outcome — which is where the next tool comes in.

2. Architecture decision records

Design docs describe a system at a moment; architecture decision records (ADRs), proposed by Michael Nygard in 2011, capture individual decisions so their reasons survive. Each ADR is a short numbered file, usually stored in the repository, with five parts: a title, a status (proposed, accepted, deprecated, superseded by ADR-0014), the context (the forces and constraints at the time), the decision, and its consequences — good and bad. For example:

ADR-0012: Store money as integer minor units with an ISO 4217 currency code
Status: Accepted (2026-09-29)
Context: Prices are stored as floats in GBP. Floats cannot represent most decimal
  amounts exactly, causing penny errors in totals and refunds. Multi-currency support
  requires an explicit currency on every amount. Some currencies have 0 or 3 decimals.
Decision: All amounts are (amount_minor: int, currency: str). Conversion happens only
  at the edges via the Money type. Existing columns migrate by expand–contract.
Consequences: + exact arithmetic, explicit currency everywhere, safe refunds.
  − migration of ~40 call sites and two tables; reports must format by currency;
  − third-party SDKs expecting floats need adapters.

A year later, someone wondering why prices aren't floats finds the answer in thirty seconds, along with the context that would justify revisiting it. ADRs are also the natural endpoint of design-doc reviews and of disagreements in code review (swe6): once a decision is recorded, it isn't relitigated in every pull request, and changing it becomes a deliberate act — a new ADR that supersedes the old one.

3. Why estimates are wrong in a predictable direction

Estimates of software work are notoriously optimistic, and the reasons are structural, not personal. The planning fallacy, documented by Kahneman and Tversky, is the tendency to underestimate one's own tasks by imagining the plan going smoothly, even when similar past tasks overran. Skew: as the primer showed, uncertain work has a long right tail, so the most likely value is below the mean. Unknown work: every real project discovers tasks nobody listed — a migration, a legal review, an integration quirk. Dependence: one misunderstanding delays several tasks at once, so overruns cluster. And uncertainty narrows over time: early in a project, before requirements and design are known, the plausible range is wide, and it narrows as work is done — often drawn as a "cone of uncertainty". An early estimate stated as a single precise number is therefore wrong twice: too low, and too precise.

4. From T-shirt size to an honest range

Early on, T-shirt sizes (S, M, L, XL) are the right tool: coarse relative estimates that support prioritisation without pretending to precision. When a real commitment is needed, turn the size into a range in four steps. The lab works the multi-currency example.

Step 1 — decompose. Break the work into tasks small enough to reason about — here seven: a money type, migrating stored prices, replacing forty-plus GBP assumptions, an exchange-rate source with rate locking, the payment provider's multi-currency flow, refunds and reporting, and tests with a flagged rollout.

Step 2 — estimate each as a range. A three-point estimate gives optimistic, most likely and pessimistic values in engineer-days: migrating prices (3, 5, 12), refunds and reporting (3, 5, 11), and so on. The pessimistic value is where people are usually too timid: ask "what would make this take three times as long?" and include that.

Step 3 — combine as distributions. Adding the most-likely values gives 28 days. The PERT formula, (optimistic + 4 × most likely + pessimistic) / 6, which approximates each task's mean, gives 31.7. A Monte Carlo simulation draws a random duration for every task from its distribution (the lab uses a triangular distribution), adds them up, and repeats 20,000 times, producing the distribution of the total: P50 about 35 days, P80 about 38, P90 about 40. The probability of finishing within the naive 28 days is about 2%. (The triangular distribution's mean, (o + m + p) / 3, gives more weight to the pessimistic tail than PERT does, which is why its median sits above the PERT total; the choice of distribution matters less than using one at all.) Then add an allowance for unknown work: the lab adds a 30% chance of a surprise costing 3–10 days, moving P50 to about 37 and P80 to about 41.

Step 4 — communicate. "About 35 engineer-days at 50% confidence, 41 at 80%, assuming no changes to tax rules and that the payment provider's flow works as documented. The biggest risk is the price migration; a two-day spike this week will narrow it." With two engineers and normal interruptions, that becomes a calendar range. Commit to the P80 and aim for the P50.

Two complementary methods check the result. Reference-class forecasting asks how long similar past projects actually took, and adjusts from that baseline — the standard correction for the planning fallacy. Throughput forecasting skips effort estimates entirely for backlogs of similar-sized items: take the team's actual completed items per week over recent weeks and simulate. The lab forecasts 30 items from a history averaging 4.5 per week: P50 7 weeks, P85 8, P95 9. Because historical throughput already includes meetings, reviews, incidents and surprises, it's often more accurate than summed guesses.

5. Reducing uncertainty, and turning questions into options

A wide range isn't a failure; it's information about where to spend effort. The fastest way to narrow it is a spike: a short, time-boxed investigation of the riskiest unknown — a prototype of the price migration against a copy of production data, a test call to the payment provider's multi-currency API. Other levers: split the work so the uncertain part is isolated; deliver the riskiest part first, so the forecast improves early; and re-estimate at each milestone from actual progress rather than defending the original number.

When someone asks "can we do X by date Y?", the uncertain honest answer is rarely yes or no; it's a set of options that trade scope, date and risk explicitly. For the multi-currency request: (A) end of month — euros only, charge and display, refunds handled manually, behind a flag for 5% of EU users; (B) full scope — about seven weeks at 80% confidence; (C) end of month for the prerequisite fix (money type and migration) plus design for the rest. Each option lists what's in, what's out, the risks and the confidence. The decision then belongs to the person who owns the trade-off, with the information to make it. The anti-pattern is quietly accepting a date that the range says is unlikely and hoping — which is how teams end up cutting testing and quality instead of scope.

6. Influence without authority

Senior engineers usually lead people they don't manage — their own team's peers, other teams, sometimes their managers. What works is consistent across organisations. Understand before proposing: learn what the other team is measured on and what constrains them, so your proposal solves their problem too. Write it down: a clear one-page proposal with the problem, options and trade-offs travels further and faster than a meeting, and shows respect for readers' time. Involve people early: talk individually to the people whose support or objections matter before any group decision, so concerns surface while they can still shape the proposal — people support what they helped build. Bring evidence: incident counts, latency percentiles, lead times, cost, hotspot data (swe11). Offer help: volunteer to do part of the work or to pair. Build trust over time by being right, being candid about being wrong, and following through. And when a decision goes against you, disagree and commit: support it fully, and record what evidence would justify revisiting it.

7. Growing others

At senior levels much of the impact is through other people. Mentoring means giving people real, stretching work with support, rather than easy tasks or rescue: pair on the hardest parts, ask questions that lead them to the answer, and let them own decisions within a safe scope. Feedback should be specific, timely and about the work — what worked, what to change and why — as in code review (swe6). Delegation is how others grow and how you scale: hand over not just tasks but decisions, with context and a check-in, and resist taking the work back at the first difficulty. Sponsorship goes further than mentoring: making others' work visible, recommending them for opportunities, giving credit publicly. Will Larson's description of staff-level archetypes — tech lead, architect, solver, right hand — is a useful map of the different shapes this work can take.

8. Leading projects: plans that survive contact

For a project spanning weeks and several people or teams, a light structure prevents most failures. A design doc reviewed by all the teams involved, with interfaces agreed early and pinned by contract tests (swe5), so teams can work in parallel. Milestones that each deliver something demonstrable end to end, with the riskiest first and integration from the first milestone — the most common failure is teams that are each "done" but whose parts don't work together. A risk register (what could go wrong, likelihood, impact, mitigation, owner) and a decision log or ADRs. A short written status update on a regular cadence with progress against milestones, the current forecast range, risks, and explicit asks — especially bad news, early. And re-planning at each milestone from what actually happened, with scope-versus-date trade-offs raised as soon as the forecast moves, not at the deadline.

9. Leading ML and LLM work

AI projects add research uncertainty: nobody knows in advance whether a given approach will reach the quality bar. Three adjustments help. Design docs gain sections: the evaluation plan (the metric, the dataset and the threshold that defines success, built before the model work — swe5), data sources and their quality, privacy and safety risks (swe13), serving cost per request and latency budgets, human-in-the-loop design, and rollout and rollback (swe9). Research is timeboxed with stopping criteria agreed in advance: "two weeks to see whether retrieval plus reranking reaches 85% answer accuracy on the evaluation set; if below 75%, stop; between, one more timebox with a specific change". The engineering around the model — pipelines, serving, evaluation harness, monitoring — is estimated normally. And communication is in options and probabilities: "we're 60% confident a fine-tuned small model can match the large model's quality at a tenth of the cost; we'll know in three weeks" is a plan stakeholders can use, where a date for an unproven capability is not.

Writing for decisions

Most technical writing at work exists to enable a decision, and the structure should serve that. Lead with the conclusion or the ask ("I recommend option B; I need a decision by Friday"), then the reasoning, then the detail — busy readers may stop after the first paragraph. Make options genuinely comparable: the same dimensions (cost, risk, time, reversibility) for each, including "do nothing". Separate facts from judgements, and quantify where you can. Name the decision-maker. Amazon's practice of narrative memos read silently at the start of meetings, instead of slide decks, is a well-known version of the same idea: prose forces complete reasoning and puts everyone on the same information before discussion starts. Good writing is also the most scalable form of influence a senior engineer has — a clear document keeps working when you're not in the room.

Reversible and irreversible decisions

Not every decision deserves the same care. A useful distinction, popularised by Amazon, is between "two-way doors" — decisions that are cheap to reverse, such as a library for an internal tool or a feature behind a flag — and "one-way doors" — decisions that are expensive or impossible to reverse, such as a public API contract (swe7), a data model storing years of records, or a vendor contract. Two-way doors should be made quickly, by the people closest to the work, with minimal process; one-way doors deserve design docs, wide review and ADRs. A common failure of large organisations is applying one-way-door process to everything, which slows teams down; a common failure of young ones is treating one-way doors casually. Part of senior judgement is recognising which is which — and, where possible, engineering one-way doors into two-way ones (flags, expand–contract migrations, adapters that keep a vendor swappable).

Estimation at the portfolio level

Across many projects, systematic optimism compounds, and planning for the portfolio needs its own techniques. Track estimate accuracy: record each project's P50 and P80 and its actual outcome, and check calibration — roughly half should finish under P50 and four in five under P80; if not, adjust. Put buffers at the project or portfolio level, not on each task, because task-level padding gets consumed by Parkinson's law (work expanding to fill the time available) while a shared buffer is spent only where needed. Limit work in progress: teams running many projects at once finish each later than if they ran fewer in sequence, because of context switching and waiting. And prefer many small, independently valuable deliveries to one large one, since the variance of a large project's outcome is what makes it dangerous.

Pre-mortems

A simple technique from decision research, the pre-mortem (Gary Klein), counters the optimism that sinks plans. Before committing, the team imagines that the project has failed badly six months from now and each person writes down, independently, why it failed. Because the failure is framed as already having happened, people surface risks they would otherwise keep to themselves — "the payment provider's sandbox doesn't match production", "nobody owns the migration". The output feeds the risk register and often changes the plan: a spike on the scariest item, an earlier integration milestone, a scope cut. It takes thirty minutes and is one of the cheapest ways to widen a too-narrow estimate honestly.

Handling conflict and hard conversations

Technical disagreements become interpersonal when positions harden. Techniques that keep them productive: restate the other person's position until they agree you've understood it; separate interests (what each side actually needs) from positions (the solution each proposes), since two incompatible positions often share compatible interests; agree first on the criteria for deciding, then on the option; use a small experiment or prototype when the disagreement is about an empirical question; and escalate early and jointly, with both options written down fairly, rather than late and adversarially. Delivering bad news — a slipping date, a design that won't work — is a leadership skill of its own: say it early, say it plainly, bring the evidence and at least one option, and own your part.

Sociotechnical design

Conway's law (swe10) makes architecture and organisation inseparable, and senior engineers increasingly design both. Team structures that match the architecture — stream-aligned teams owning a bounded context end to end, platform teams providing self-service capabilities, enabling teams that help others adopt new practices, as described in Team Topologies — reduce the coordination each change requires. Cognitive load is the budget: a team that owns too many services or too broad a domain slows down regardless of skill. When a design requires constant coordination across teams, that's a signal to change either the design or the team boundaries — and a senior engineer's recommendation may be about the org chart as much as the code.

How leadership failures show up

They show up as project outcomes more than as incidents. Chronic lateness: every project finishes at two to three times its estimate, because single-number estimates are treated as commitments. Integration at the end: components built separately that don't fit together weeks before launch. Relitigated decisions: the same architecture argument in every quarter's planning because nothing was written down. Surprise at launch: risks known to engineers never reached decision-makers, or reached them too late to act. Hero dependence: one senior engineer who knows everything and is a bottleneck for every review and incident. The measures that reveal them: estimate calibration (P50 and P80 versus actuals), how often milestones deliver something integrated, the number of decisions with written records, how early risks are raised relative to deadlines, and the bus factor of key systems.

Case: HealthCare.gov, 1 October 2013

HealthCare.gov was the US federal government's online marketplace for health insurance under the Affordable Care Act, built for the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) largely by contractors. Its core components were the Federally Facilitated Marketplace (FFM), where people compared and enrolled in plans, and a data services hub connecting to other agencies to verify applicants' information. When enrolment opened on 1 October 2013, as the Government Accountability Office put it, "many users encountered challenges accessing and using the website". The GAO's July 2014 report, GAO-14-694, is a planning and oversight audit, and its findings read as a checklist of this lesson's practices done badly.

Starting without the requirements that drive the architecture. To move quickly on "a first-of-its-kind federal marketplace" with "compressed time frames", CMS issued the task orders to build the FFM and the data hub "when key technical requirements were unknown, including the number and composition of states to be supported and, importantly, the number of potential enrollees". The expected load — how many people would use the site at once — is exactly the requirement that shapes capacity, architecture and testing, and it was unknown when design began. CMS also did not develop the required acquisition strategy to identify risks and mitigations, and did not use available information such as quality-assurance plans to monitor performance.

Changing requirements, growing costs, unclear authority. The GAO found "significant cost increases, schedule slips, and delayed system functionality … due primarily to changing requirements that were exacerbated by oversight gaps". From September 2011 to February 2014, obligations for the FFM rose from $56 million to more than $209 million, and for the data hub from $30 million to nearly $85 million. Because of unclear guidance and inconsistent oversight, "there was confusion about who had the authority to approve contractor requests to expend funds for additional work"; the report identified about 40 instances in which program staff inappropriately authorised contractors to spend a total of over $30 million. New requirements and changing decisions led to delays and wasted effort.

Readiness pushed to the end. CMS "delayed key governance reviews, moving an assessment of FFM readiness from March to September 2013 — just weeks before the launch — and did not receive required approvals. As a result, CMS launched Healthcare.gov without verification that it met performance requirements."

Read against this lesson, the pattern is familiar at any scale. The number that should have anchored the design — expected concurrent users — wasn't pinned down, so neither estimates nor capacity plans could be sound. Requirements changed without a mechanism to renegotiate scope and date, so cost and schedule absorbed the change instead. Decision authority was unclear, so work proceeded without anyone owning the trade-offs. And verification — load testing against performance requirements, the readiness review — was left until too late to act on what it found, so the launch date held while readiness didn't. The fixes are this lesson's practices: identify and pin down the requirements that drive architecture early, even as ranges; maintain options that trade scope against date explicitly as requirements change; make decision authority and risks visible in writing; test the riskiest things — here, load — first; and treat a readiness review as a real gate with the authority to move the date or cut scope. The site was substantially repaired in the following months, with a much-publicised rescue effort; the GAO's point was that the failure was one of planning and oversight, not of any single technical component.

Leadership in AI teams

AI projects are especially exposed to HealthCare.gov's failure modes: success criteria that are vague ("the assistant should be helpful"), requirements that shift as stakeholders see demos, costs that scale unpredictably with usage, and "readiness" that is hard to define for probabilistic systems. The leadership response mirrors the case: define success as measurable evaluation criteria and load and cost budgets before building; manage research uncertainty with timeboxes and stopping criteria; present options that trade quality, cost, latency and scope; make risks — safety, privacy, cost overruns — visible early in writing; and make launch readiness a real gate based on evaluation results and staged rollout (swe9), not a date.

Tools and knobs

A design-doc template with goals, non-goals, alternatives and cross-cutting concerns; ADRs in the repository (adr-tools or a simple numbered folder); three-point estimates and a small Monte Carlo script or spreadsheet for ranges; throughput data from the issue tracker for forecasts; a risk register and decision log for multi-team work; a short written status update on a fixed cadence; and calibration tracking of estimates versus actuals.

Goal: turn a T-shirt estimate into an honest range. You'll combine three-point task estimates with a Monte Carlo simulation, compare the result with the naive sum of "most likely" values, add unknown work, and forecast a backlog from past throughput.   1. Save the script as swe15_lab.py and run python swe15_lab.py.
  2. The multi-currency checkout project is broken into seven tasks, each with (optimistic, most likely, pessimistic) engineer-days.
  3. Expected output: the sum of most-likely values is 28.0 days; the sum of PERT means is 31.7; the Monte Carlo P50 / P80 / P90 are about 35 / 38 / 40 days; and the chance of finishing within 28 days is about 2%.
  4. Adding a 30% chance of a 3–10-day surprise moves P50 to about 37 and P80 to about 41.
  5. The throughput forecast for 30 backlog items, drawing from ten past weeks averaging 4.5 items per week, gives about P50 7 weeks, P85 8, P95 9.

What it shows: the number people usually quote — the total of their most-likely guesses — sits in the far left tail of the real distribution, because every task's overrun potential is larger than its underrun potential and the sum inherits all of them. The fix isn't guessing better; it's estimating ranges and combining them honestly. The throughput forecast shows the alternative: use what the team actually delivered.

Try next: (1) Make the migration task's pessimistic value 25 days and watch P90 move far more than P50 — risk lives in the tail. (2) Replace random.triangular with a PERT (beta) distribution and compare. (3) Record your own team's last ten weeks of completed items in history and forecast your current backlog.

"""swe15 lab: turn a T-shirt estimate into a range. Three-point task estimates -> Monte Carlo -> P50/P80/P90,
compared with the naive 'sum of most-likely' — then a throughput forecast from past sprints."""
import random
import statistics

random.seed(2026)
# multi-currency checkout, broken into tasks: (optimistic, most likely, pessimistic) in engineer-days
TASKS = {
    "money type (int minor units + currency)": (2, 3, 6),
    "migrate stored float prices (expand-contract)": (3, 5, 12),
    "replace 40+ GBP assumptions":              (3, 5, 10),
    "FX rate source + rate locking":           (2, 4, 9),
    "payment provider multi-currency flow":    (2, 3, 8),
    "refunds and reporting":                   (3, 5, 11),
    "tests, flag, staged rollout":             (2, 3, 5),
}

def sample(o, m, p):
    return random.triangular(o, p, m)               # right-skewed when p - m > m - o, as real tasks are

naive = sum(m for _, m, _ in TASKS.values())
pert = sum((o + 4 * m + p) / 6 for o, m, p in TASKS.values())
runs = sorted(sum(sample(*t) for t in TASKS.values()) for _ in range(20_000))
pct = lambda q: runs[int(q * len(runs)) - 1]
print(f"sum of 'most likely'          : {naive:5.1f} days")
print(f"sum of PERT means (o+4m+p)/6   : {pert:5.1f} days")
print(f"Monte Carlo  P50 / P80 / P90   : {pct(.5):5.1f} / {pct(.8):5.1f} / {pct(.9):5.1f} days")
print(f"chance of finishing within the naive {naive} days: {sum(r <= naive for r in runs) / len(runs):.0%}")

# add the work nobody listed yet: 30% chance of a 'surprise' costing 3-10 days (tax rules, a legal review, …)
with_unknowns = sorted(r + (random.uniform(3, 10) if random.random() < 0.3 else 0) for r in runs)
print(f"with unknown-unknowns P50 / P80: {with_unknowns[len(runs)//2]:5.1f} / {with_unknowns[int(.8*len(runs))]:5.1f} days")

# throughput forecasting: how many weeks to finish 30 backlog items, from the last 10 weeks' completions
history = [4, 6, 3, 5, 7, 2, 5, 4, 6, 3]           # items finished per week
def weeks_to_finish(n_items):
    done, weeks = 0, 0
    while done < n_items:
        done += random.choice(history); weeks += 1
    return weeks
forecast = sorted(weeks_to_finish(30) for _ in range(20_000))
print(f"\n30 items at past throughput (mean {statistics.mean(history):.1f}/week): "
      f"P50 {forecast[len(forecast)//2]} weeks, P85 {forecast[int(.85*len(forecast))]} weeks, P95 {forecast[int(.95*len(forecast))]} weeks")

  • Write a one-page design doc with goals and non-goals: money as integer minor units plus an ISO currency code (fixing the floats is a prerequisite — floats are a correctness bug regardless), exchange-rate source and when the rate is locked, migration plan for stored prices (expand–contract), what changes in reporting. Record the money decision as an ADR.
  • Estimate as a range: three-point estimates per task, combined — roughly 35 engineer-days at 50% confidence, about 41 at 80% with an allowance for surprises — with the assumptions stated.
  • Offer options: (A) end of month — EUR only, display and charge, refunds manual, behind a flag for 5% of EU users; (B) full scope — about seven weeks at 80% confidence with two engineers; (C) end of month for the money-type fix and migration only, plus a plan for the rest. Include the confidence level and assumptions for each.

This is the leadership answer interviewers look for: clarify the problem, reduce uncertainty quickly, surface hidden risk (float money), give an honest range rather than a single number, and turn a yes/no into a scoped choice with trade-offs for the person who owns the decision.
