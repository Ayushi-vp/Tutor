"torch.OutOfMemoryError: CUDA out of memory. Tried to allocate 3.91 GiB." Every ML engineer has seen it, and most have responded the same way: halve the batch size and try again. Sometimes that works. Often it only moves the problem — the job now runs at half the throughput, or fails an hour later, or fails only on the longest sequences in the dataset. Meanwhile the GPU's memory is a fixed, expensive resource: an 80 GB card running at 50% of its capacity because nobody knows where the other half goes is a direct waste of money.

Two families of techniques exist to use that memory well, and both are built into PyTorch. Mixed precision runs most arithmetic in 16-bit formats on tensor cores, which roughly halves activation memory and multiplies matmul throughput, while keeping the numerically delicate parts in 32-bit. Memory management covers what is stored for the backward pass and how to store less (activation checkpointing), how the allocator works and why its numbers disagree with nvidia-smi, and how to record and read a memory snapshot that shows exactly which line of code allocated what.

Both families have sharp edges that appear in debugging interviews. Training in float16 without a gradient scaler silently loses small gradients. Clipping before unscaling clips the wrong numbers. Casting the whole model to bf16 makes small weight updates round to nothing, and the loss plateaus for no visible reason. Wrapping the backward pass in autocast, or the loss outside it, changes numerics in ways that are hard to see. A model that "fits" at sequence length 2,048 explodes at 8,192, because one term of activation memory grows with the square of the sequence length.

This lesson builds the picture from the ground up: what occupies memory during a step and how big each part is, with a formula for transformer activations from a NVIDIA paper (Korthikanti et al., 2022) that you can compute by hand; what autocast does operation by operation; bf16 versus fp16 and the mechanics of GradScaler; why the weights stay in float32; activation checkpointing in PyTorch 2.14; the caching allocator's statistics and fragmentation; memory snapshots as described by the PyTorch team in "Understanding GPU Memory"; and a step-by-step method for fixing out-of-memory errors.

Think of GPU memory during training as a workbench of fixed size. Some things live on the bench permanently: the model's weights, a slot for each weight's gradient, and the optimiser's notebooks (Adam keeps two numbers per weight). Other things come and go every step: as the forward pass runs, it leaves intermediate results on the bench because the backward pass will need them — these are the activations — and the pile grows layer by layer until the loss is computed. Then the backward pass works through the pile in reverse, using and clearing each intermediate, while filling in the gradient slots. Temporary scraps — a big matrix that exists for a moment inside one operation — appear and vanish on top of all that. An out-of-memory error is the moment the bench overflows, almost always at the peak: the end of the forward pass, when every activation is still there, plus whatever temporary is being created right then.

The two families of techniques act on different parts of the bench:

  • Mixed precision makes the things that come and go smaller: activations stored in bf16 or fp16 take half the space of float32, and the matmuls that produce them run on tensor cores several times faster. The permanent items mostly stay in float32 for accuracy.
  • Activation checkpointing stores fewer activations: keep only a few "bookmarks" (the input to each layer) and recompute the rest during backward, paying with extra compute.
  • Sharding (pt11) splits the permanent items across GPUs; allocator tuning reduces wasted space between items.

Where the workbench analogy breaks: PyTorch does not hand memory back to the driver when a tensor dies; it keeps the space in its own cache for the next allocation of a suitable size, because asking the driver is slow (pt1). So the bench has a second boundary — "space PyTorch is holding" — that is larger than "space currently used", and tools disagree depending on which they report. And because the cache keeps blocks of particular sizes, free space can be split into pieces too small for a large request: fragmentation, an OOM with gigabytes nominally free.

A second, numerical picture is needed for mixed precision. A 16-bit format is a ruler with fewer markings. bf16 keeps the ruler's full length (float32's range) but has coarse markings (only about three significant decimal digits); fp16 has finer markings but a much shorter ruler (it overflows at 65,504 and cannot represent magnitudes below about 6×10⁻⁸). Matmuls tolerate coarse markings because their results are accumulated in float32 inside the tensor cores. Sums over many elements, softmax's exponentials, normalisation statistics and weight updates do not, which is why they stay in float32.

If you remember one sentence: memory peaks at the end of the forward pass, activations are usually the biggest item, mixed precision makes them smaller and faster to compute while float32 protects the sensitive parts, and every OOM is fixed by finding the peak and shrinking its largest contributor.

1. The memory budget of a training step

Everything on the GPU during training falls into five groups. Take a model with N parameters trained with AdamW, mixed precision and float32 parameters (the setup torch.autocast gives you):

ItemBytesLifetime
Parameters (float32)4Nwhole run
Gradients (float32, same dtype as parameters)4Nfrom first backward until zero_grad (or longer)
AdamW moments m and v (float32)8Nwhole run, after first step
Activations saved for backwarddepends on batch, sequence, architectureforward → freed during backward
Temporaries and workspacedepends on the largest single opinside one op

That is 16 bytes per parameter before activations. For GPT-2 small (124M) it is 2.0 GB; for a 7B model, 112 GB — more than an 80 GB GPU holds, which is why such models are trained with sharded states (pt11) or memory-saving optimisers. Add the CUDA context itself (several hundred megabytes per process, outside PyTorch's accounting) and any communication buffers.

The activation term needs its own formula, because it is usually the largest and the one you can most easily change.

2. Activations: the transformer formula and a worked example

Korthikanti et al., "Reducing Activation Recomputation in Large Transformer Models" (2022), count every tensor a transformer layer saves for backward when activations are stored in 16-bit (2 bytes per element, 1 byte per dropout-mask element). With sequence length s, micro-batch b, hidden size h and a attention heads, their equation (1) is:

activation bytes per layer = s · b · h · (34 + 5 · a · s / h)

The 34 collects the linear-in-s terms: the inputs to the QKV projection, Q and K, V, the attention output projection's input, the MLP's input and its 4h-wide hidden activation and GELU output, two layer norms' inputs, and the dropout masks. The 5·a·s/h term is the attention score matrix, the softmax output and the attention-dropout mask — each a × s × s per example — which is why it grows with the square of the sequence length.

A worked example for a 7B-class layer (h = 4,096, a = 32) at s = 4,096, b = 1:

  • s·b·h = 4,096 × 4,096 = 16,777,216.
  • 5·a·s/h = 5 × 32 × 4,096 / 4,096 = 160.
  • Per layer: 16.78M × (34 + 160) = 16.78M × 194 ≈ 3.26 GB. For 32 layers: ≈ 104 GB.

Three levers change it dramatically. FlashAttention (gpu7; in PyTorch, F.scaled_dot_product_attention picks a flash or memory-efficient kernel on supported GPUs) never stores the s × s matrices, so the 160 disappears: 34 × 16.78M ≈ 0.57 GB per layer, 18 GB for 32 layers. Full activation checkpointing stores only each layer's input, 2·s·b·h bytes ≈ 34 MB per layer, ≈ 1.1 GB total, plus one layer's activations at a time during recomputation. And sequence length: doubling s doubles the 34-term and quadruples the attention term, so a model that fits at 2,048 can fail at 8,192 even with batch 1.

The logits deserve a separate line because they are not in the per-layer formula. For vocabulary V the output layer produces s·b·V values — 4,096 × 128,256 ≈ 525M per sequence, 1.05 GB in bf16 or 2.1 GB in float32 — and the cross-entropy computes a log-softmax of the same size. At the end of the forward pass, all of that coexists with every layer's activations; it is the most common place for the OOM to fire.

3. What autocast does, op by op

torch.autocast(device_type, dtype) is a context manager. Inside it, each eligible operation casts its inputs to the dtype that suits it:

  • Lower-precision list (CUDA "autocast to float16/bfloat16"): matmul, linear, conv*, bmm, baddbmm, attention — the ops that run on tensor cores and tolerate low precision because the hardware accumulates in float32.
  • Float32 list (CUDA "autocast to float32"), from the 2.14 docs: among others softmax, log_softmax, layer_norm, group_norm, exp, log, pow, sum, prod, cumsum, norm, cross_entropy, nll_loss, mse_loss, kl_div, binary_cross_entropy_with_logits — operations whose results are sensitive to rounding or range.
  • Widest-input list: ops such as add, cat and stack with mixed inputs run in the widest input dtype.
  • Everything else runs in whatever dtype its inputs have.

The lists are per device. Running on CPU with bf16 on 2.14, a linear layer's output is bf16 and cross_entropy returns float32, but softmax and layer_norm stay bf16 — the CPU float32 list is shorter than CUDA's. Never assume; check .dtype in the context you run.

The parameters are not changed. A nn.Linear's weight stays float32; autocast casts it to bf16 for the matmul and caches that cast for the rest of the autocast region, so a weight used many times (weight tying, a layer called in a loop) is converted once. Because the parameter is float32, its gradient is float32 too — verified on 2.14 by backpropagating through an autocast linear layer.

The usage pattern:

for x, y in loader:
    with torch.autocast("cuda", dtype=torch.bfloat16):
        logits = model(x)                       # matmuls in bf16, norms/softmax in fp32
        loss = F.cross_entropy(logits.view(-1, V), y.view(-1))   # fp32
    loss.backward()                             # outside autocast: backward ops use
    clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)    # the dtypes chosen in forward
    opt.step(); opt.zero_grad()

Rules: wrap forward and loss, not backward or the optimiser step (backward automatically runs each op in the dtype its forward used); do not call .half() or .bfloat16() on the model or inputs as well; and, inside an autocast region, if you need a specific op in float32 regardless, wrap it in a nested torch.autocast(..., enabled=False) and cast its inputs with .float().

4. bf16 versus fp16

Both formats are 16 bits; they split them differently (gpu4):

float32bfloat16float16
Exponent bits885
Stored mantissa bits23710
Max3.4e383.4e3865,504
Smallest normal1.2e-381.2e-386.1e-5
Spacing near 1.01.2e-77.8e-39.8e-4

fp16's problem is range. Activations can exceed 65,504 (inf), and — more often — gradients are smaller than its smallest normal value. Checked on 2.14: torch.tensor(1e-8).half() is exactly 0, and 7e-8 rounds to the smallest subnormal, 5.96e-8. In a deep network many gradient elements are around 1e-6 to 1e-9; in fp16 they vanish, and training quietly degrades. bf16's problem is precision: in bf16, 1.0 + 0.001 is still 1.0 (checked on 2.14), because the spacing near 1.0 is 2−7. That is why bf16 is used for compute and activations but not for weight updates (section 6). On GPUs with fast bf16 (NVIDIA Ampere and later, recent AMD and other accelerators), bf16 is the default for training; fp16 remains for older GPUs and some inference paths.

5. GradScaler: loss scaling for fp16

The fix for fp16's underflow is to multiply the loss by a large factor S before backward. Every gradient is then S times larger — by the chain rule — and shifted into fp16's representable range. Before the optimiser uses them, they are divided by S. If S is too large, some gradients overflow to inf; the step is then skipped and S reduced. torch.amp.GradScaler automates this; on 2.14 its defaults are init_scale=65536.0 (216), growth_factor=2.0, backoff_factor=0.5, growth_interval=2000.

scaler = torch.amp.GradScaler("cuda")
for x, y in loader:
    with torch.autocast("cuda", dtype=torch.float16):
        loss = F.cross_entropy(model(x), y)
    scaler.scale(loss).backward()          # gradients are S times too large
    scaler.unscale_(opt)                   # divide by S in place; detect inf/NaN
    clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)   # clip true-scale gradients
    scaler.step(opt)                       # skipped if inf/NaN was found
    scaler.update()                        # S /= 2 after a skip; S *= 2 after 2,000 clean steps
    opt.zero_grad()

The order matters in two places. Clipping must come after unscale_; clipping scaled gradients to norm 1.0 clips them to 1/65,536 of what you meant. And scaler.step must be used instead of opt.step, or skipped steps are not skipped. A trace of the scale over a run: it starts at 65,536; if the first backward produces an inf, that step is skipped and the scale becomes 32,768; after 2,000 clean steps it doubles; over a long run it settles where occasional overflows keep it from growing further. A few skipped steps are normal; a scale that keeps falling toward 1 means the model produces infs at any scale — a real numerical problem, not a scaling one. With bf16, don't use a scaler at all.

6. Why weights stay in float32

The 2018 paper that established mixed-precision training (Micikevicius et al., ICLR 2018) kept a float32 "master copy" of the weights for the update, for a reason you can compute. An update is learning rate × (normalised) gradient, often 1e-4 to 1e-6 relative to the weight. A bf16 weight near 1.0 can only change in steps of 0.0078; adding 1e-4 rounds back to the same value. In float32 the spacing near 1.0 is 1.2e-7, so the update survives. With torch.autocast, the parameters are the float32 master copy, so this is automatic. It goes wrong when someone calls model.bfloat16() to save memory and trains with a plain AdamW: many updates round away, the loss plateaus early, and nothing errors. Pure-bf16 training exists, but only with optimisers designed for it (stochastic rounding, compensated summation) or with float32 optimiser states and careful update paths.

7. Activation checkpointing

Checkpointing trades compute for memory: during forward, a checkpointed segment runs without saving its intermediate activations, keeping only its inputs; during backward, when gradients reach that segment, PyTorch re-runs its forward to regenerate what is needed, then backpropagates through it. In PyTorch 2.14:

from torch.utils.checkpoint import checkpoint
class Block(nn.Module):
    def forward(self, x):                        # x: (B, T, C)
        x = x + self.attn(self.ln1(x))
        return x + self.mlp(self.ln2(x))

class Model(nn.Module):
    def forward(self, x):
        for blk in self.blocks:
            x = checkpoint(blk, x, use_reentrant=False)   # store x, recompute blk in backward
        return self.head(self.ln_f(x))

Pass use_reentrant=False explicitly. The docstring on 2.14 says "It is recommended that you use use_reentrant=False" and that the argument "should be passed explicitly"; calling without it still only warns on 2.14 ("Starting in PyTorch 2.9, calling checkpoint without use_reentrant will raise an exception" — it did not, in this version), so do not rely on either behaviour. The non-reentrant version records a normal graph, supports torch.autograd.grad, works when no input requires grad, and stops recomputing as soon as the needed tensors are regenerated. Randomness is handled: by default the RNG state is saved and restored so dropout masks are identical in the recomputation.

The cost: one extra forward pass of every checkpointed segment. Since a training step is roughly one forward plus a backward costing two forwards, checkpointing everything adds about a third more compute (in practice 20–40%). The saving, from section 2, can take activations from tens of gigabytes to about one. In between: checkpoint every second layer; or use selective checkpointing — keep the expensive matmul outputs and recompute only cheap element-wise ops and attention internals — which Korthikanti et al. showed recovers most of the memory for a few percent of compute. PyTorch exposes policies for this through the context_fn argument of checkpoint (selective activation checkpoint policies).

8. Reading the allocator's numbers

PyTorch's CUDA caching allocator (pt1) keeps freed blocks for reuse. Its statistics:

  • torch.cuda.memory_allocated() — bytes in live tensors right now.
  • torch.cuda.max_memory_allocated() — the peak of that since start or since reset_peak_memory_stats(); the number that must fit under the limit.
  • torch.cuda.memory_reserved() — bytes PyTorch holds from the driver: allocated plus cached free blocks. This is roughly what nvidia-smi attributes to your process, minus the CUDA context.
  • torch.cuda.memory_summary() — a table of all of the above, broken down by block size, plus counts of allocation retries and OOMs.

The OOM message itself contains these numbers: it says how much it tried to allocate, the GPU's total capacity, how much is free, how much "is allocated by PyTorch" and how much "is reserved by PyTorch but unallocated". A large reserved-but-unallocated figure means fragmentation, and the message suggests the allocator option that addresses it. On 2.14 the environment variable is PYTORCH_ALLOC_CONF (with PYTORCH_CUDA_ALLOC_CONF kept as an alias, per the CUDA semantics notes); PYTORCH_ALLOC_CONF=expandable_segments:True lets the allocator grow segments in place rather than creating many fixed-size ones, which is especially effective when batch shapes vary (variable sequence lengths). The docs mark it experimental, default False.

torch.cuda.empty_cache() returns cached blocks to the driver. It does not give your process more memory — the cache was already available to you — and calling it in a training loop just makes later allocations slower. It is useful only when another process on the same GPU needs the memory.

9. Memory snapshots

When numbers are not enough, record everything. The PyTorch team's post "Understanding GPU Memory 1" (Aaron Shi and Zachary DeVito, December 2023) describes the workflow:

torch.cuda.memory._record_memory_history(max_entries=100000)   # start recording
for _ in range(5):
    loss = model(x).sum(); loss.backward(); opt.step(); opt.zero_grad()
torch.cuda.memory._dump_snapshot("snapshot.pickle")            # save
torch.cuda.memory._record_memory_history(enabled=None)         # stop

Drag the file into the viewer at pytorch.org/memory_viz. It plots every allocation over time as a coloured band, so you see the ramp of activations in each forward, the release in backward, optimiser state appearing at the first step, and — crucially — anything that never gets freed. Clicking a band shows the Python and C++ stack trace of the allocation, which names the line of your code. Recording adds overhead, so do it for a few steps, not for a whole run. The post walks through typical findings: a growing staircase across iterations (a leak, usually a tensor kept with its graph), and a tall spike within one step (a single large temporary).

10. Precision at evaluation and inference

The float32 master weights exist to protect weight updates. Without updates the argument disappears, so evaluation and inference can use 16-bit weights directly: model.to(torch.bfloat16).eval() under torch.inference_mode() halves weight memory and is how most serving stacks load models (pt13). Two cautions. The evaluation that decides which checkpoint to keep should use the same precision recipe as training (autocast over float32 weights) or as deployment (16-bit weights), and you should know which — results can differ in the third decimal place, enough to flip a close comparison. And numerically sensitive pieces still deserve float32 at inference: the final log-softmax when you need exact log-probabilities (for evaluation perplexity or ranking), and long-running accumulations such as a KV cache's attention over very long contexts, where kernels accumulate in float32 internally even when inputs are bf16. A quick check is to compare a batch of logits from the float32 and bf16 paths: differences of about 1e-2 in logits are normal for bf16; differences that change the argmax on many tokens point to an overflow or a sensitive op running in low precision.

Memory at inference is a different budget: no gradients, no optimiser state, no saved activations — only weights, the current layer's temporaries and, for generation, the KV cache (pt10). A 7B model in bf16 is 14 GB of weights; its KV cache at 4,096 tokens and batch 8 is 2 (K and V) × 32 layers × 8 × 4,096 × 4,096 × 2 bytes ≈ 17 GB without grouped-query attention, which is why inference memory planning is about the cache, not the activations.

11. A method for CUDA OOM

  1. Read the error. Note the attempted size and the reserved-but-unallocated amount. A small attempted allocation failing with lots reserved means fragmentation; a huge attempted allocation means one big temporary.
  2. Leak or peak? Print memory_allocated() after each step for a few steps. Rising every step: a leak — look for losses or outputs stored with graphs (pt3), views of big tensors kept alive (pt1), caches growing. Flat but OOM at the same point: a peak problem.
  3. Find the peak's contents. Take a snapshot of one step; identify the top allocations at the peak.
  4. Shrink the biggest item, cheapest fix first:
    • Large logits/loss: chunk the loss over the sequence or use a fused cross-entropy; compute logits only where needed.
    • Attention scores: use F.scaled_dot_product_attention so a fused kernel avoids s × s matrices.
    • Activations in general: autocast to bf16; activation checkpointing; smaller micro-batch with gradient accumulation (pt6) to keep the effective batch.
    • Weights, gradients, optimiser state: sharding (FSDP, pt11), 8-bit optimisers, or CPU offload.
    • Fragmentation: expandable_segments:True; pad or bucket sequence lengths to a few fixed sizes.
  5. Verify with max_memory_allocated() after a step, and keep 5–10% headroom: allocation patterns vary between steps (different sequence lengths, the first optimiser step allocating state), and running at 99% invites intermittent failures.

Where the 34 comes from, and how parallelism changes it

Counting by hand, per layer and per token, in units of h bytes at 2 bytes per element: the attention block stores its input to the QKV projection (2h), Q and K (4h), V (2h, needed for the score-times-V backward), the input to the output projection (2h) and an h-byte dropout mask; the MLP stores its input (2h), the 4h-wide output of the first linear (8h), the activation output (8h) and an h-byte dropout mask; the two layer norms store their inputs (4h). That totals 34h per token, hence 34·s·b·h. The attention internals add 2·a·s per token for the scores, 2·a·s for the softmax output and a·s for the dropout mask — 5·a·s. With tensor parallelism across t GPUs, most of these tensors are split t ways, and with sequence parallelism the rest are too, giving sbh(34 + 5as/h)/t per GPU (the paper's equation 4). Knowing where each term comes from lets you predict what a change does: removing attention dropout removes the a·s mask term; swapping GELU for an activation that can be recomputed from its input removes one 8h term.

Autocast's weight cache and its interaction with checkpointing and compile

Autocast caches the low-precision copy of each float32 parameter for the duration of the outermost autocast region, so a weight used repeatedly is cast once per forward. The cache is cleared on exiting the region. Two consequences: the cached copies are extra memory (one bf16 copy of every weight used — 2N bytes — during forward), and recomputation under activation checkpointing must happen under the same autocast state, which PyTorch's checkpoint handles by recording and restoring the autocast context. Under torch.compile (pt12), autocast is traced into the graph and the casts are fused with neighbouring operations, which removes most of their overhead.

Float8 training

NVIDIA Hopper and Blackwell GPUs have tensor cores for 8-bit floating-point formats (E4M3 and E5M2, gpu4), roughly doubling matmul throughput again over bf16. Training in float8 is not a matter of a third autocast dtype: each tensor needs a scale factor chosen so its values fit the tiny range, chosen per tensor, per row or per block, and updated as distributions shift. In the PyTorch ecosystem this lives in torchao's float8 training support, which swaps nn.Linear layers for float8 versions while keeping master weights and sensitive ops in higher precision. The same principles as this lesson apply — low precision for matmul inputs, high precision for accumulation, master weights and reductions — with the added problem of managing scales.

Memory fragmentation, mechanically

The caching allocator rounds requests up to size classes and allocates large blocks in segments obtained from the driver; a freed block can be split to serve a smaller request, and adjacent free blocks are merged. Fragmentation arises when long-lived allocations land in the middle of segments, so free space around them cannot merge into a large enough block. Variable-shape workloads make it worse: a batch of length 3,000 followed by one of length 3,100 asks for slightly larger blocks every time. Expandable segments map more physical memory into a large virtual address range on demand, so a segment can grow instead of a new one being allocated, and freed memory at the end can be unmapped. Bucketing sequence lengths to a few fixed sizes attacks the same problem from the workload side and also helps torch.compile avoid recompiles (pt12).

Offloading: CPU memory as overflow

When GPU memory is the binding constraint and the host has plenty, tensors can be parked in CPU memory. torch.autograd.graph.save_on_cpu(pin_memory=True) moves every saved activation to pinned host memory during forward and brings it back during backward (pt3's saved-tensor hooks); FSDP2 can offload parameters and optimiser state with an OffloadPolicy. The cost is PCIe bandwidth: at about 25 GB/s, moving 18 GB of activations each way takes over a second per step, so offloading helps only when compute per step is long enough to hide the transfers, or as a last resort to make something fit at all.

Optimiser state is often the cheapest big win

In the 16-bytes-per-parameter budget, AdamW's two moments are half. Three ways to shrink them: shard them across data-parallel GPUs (FSDP/ZeRO, pt11), which divides by the number of GPUs at the cost of communication; store them in 8 bits with block-wise scaling (8-bit Adam variants), which cuts 8N bytes to about 2N with little effect on training in published comparisons; or use optimisers with factored or no second moments (Adafactor-style factorisation, or momentum-only methods), which change the optimisation itself and need validation. For fine-tuning, parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA (lm11) shrink all three terms at once by training only small adapter matrices, leaving the frozen base weights with no gradients or optimiser state at all.

Numerical failure modes specific to mixed precision

Beyond underflow and overflow, three patterns recur. Attention logits that grow during training can overflow fp16 before softmax; computing softmax in float32 (autocast does) and normalising queries and keys (QK-norm) are the standard defences. Very large losses — sums instead of means over huge batches — can overflow the scaled fp16 loss; use means. And reductions written by hand in a custom module (a variance as x.pow(2).mean() in bf16) lose precision that the built-in normalisation layers protect against by computing in float32; the RMSNorm in pt4 upcasts for this reason.

Failure mode: OOM at long sequences or rare batches

Symptom: training runs for hours, then OOMs on one batch. Cause: the peak depends on the batch's shape, and the quadratic attention term (or a vocabulary-sized logit tensor) makes the longest batch far more expensive than the average. Fix: cap or bucket sequence lengths, use fused attention, and size the micro-batch for the longest sequence you will see — test that shape at start-up by running one forward/backward on a synthetic batch of the maximum length before training begins.

Failure mode: memory that grows every step

Symptom: memory_allocated() rises steadily; OOM after N steps. Causes: storing loss tensors instead of numbers for logging; keeping outputs with their graphs; caches keyed by tensors; hooks storing activations without detaching; a growing list of per-step metrics on the GPU. The snapshot shows a staircase whose steps have a common stack trace. Fix the reference, not the symptom; empty_cache() does nothing for a leak.

Failure mode: fp16 training that stops learning or diverges

Symptom: with fp16, loss plateaus higher than a float32 run, or GradScaler's scale collapses toward 1 and many steps are skipped. Causes: missing GradScaler (underflowed gradients), clipping before unscaling, attention logits or activations overflowing 65,504, or a loss computed outside autocast in fp16 tensors. Fixes: switch to bf16 if the hardware supports it; otherwise use the scaler correctly, keep softmax and losses in float32, and log the scale and the number of skipped steps.

Failure mode: "mixed precision made it slower"

Symptom: enabling autocast gives little or no speed-up. Causes: matrix dimensions that are not multiples of 8 or 16 (tensor-core kernels prefer aligned shapes — a vocabulary of 50,257 is often padded to 50,304 for this reason, as nanoGPT does), small models where the step is dominated by kernel-launch overhead rather than matmuls (pt12), CPU-bound input pipelines (pt5), or a GPU without fast bf16. Profile before concluding.

The public case: reading a real snapshot

The PyTorch team's "Understanding GPU Memory 1" post is the reference for the snapshot workflow and worth reading with this lesson's budget in mind. Its example traces show exactly the shapes predicted here: the first iteration allocating optimiser state that then persists, each iteration's forward ramp of activations and backward release, and — in its leak example — a staircase where something survives every iteration. It also shows how to record history inside a long-running job with a bounded max_entries, so that when an out-of-memory error finally happens the evidence is already there. The practical takeaway the post's authors stress is to stop guessing: a snapshot names the allocation and the line of code that pushed memory over the limit.

Observability

Log max_memory_allocated() per step (reset its peak each step) alongside the batch shape, so you can see which shapes approach the limit; log GradScaler's scale and skip count for fp16 runs; and when an OOM occurs in production, have the training script catch torch.OutOfMemoryError once, dump a memory snapshot and memory_summary(), then re-raise, so the failure arrives with its evidence. The PyTorch memory post shows exactly this pattern — recording history from the start of the run with a bounded number of entries, so a snapshot is available when the failure happens.

Find the bug: an fp16 training loop that "trains" but underperforms

A team on older GPUs without fast bf16 trains a transformer in fp16. The loss curve is worse than a float32 baseline, most of all in the first tens of thousands of steps; gradient-norm logs show values around 50,000; and raising the learning rate barely changes anything. Find the bugs.

scaler = torch.amp.GradScaler("cuda", init_scale=1.0)   # "start safe"
for x, y in loader:
    with torch.autocast("cuda", dtype=torch.float16):
        logits = model(x)
    loss = F.cross_entropy(logits.view(-1, V), y.view(-1))
    scaler.scale(loss).backward()
    gnorm = clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)
    scaler.unscale_(opt)
    scaler.step(opt)
    scaler.update()
    opt.zero_grad()
Answer

Bug 1 — clipping before unscaling. Once the scale has grown, the gradients are S times too large when clipped, so the logged norm (~50,000) is the scaled norm and clipping to 1.0 shrinks every update by a further large factor: almost every step is clipped to a tiny update, which is why the learning rate seems not to matter. Fix: scaler.unscale_(opt) first, then clip_grad_norm_ on true-scale gradients, then scaler.step(opt).

Bug 2 — init_scale=1.0 defeats loss scaling. The point of the scaler is to lift small gradients above fp16's underflow threshold; with a scale of 1 they underflow exactly as without a scaler. The scale only doubles every 2,000 clean steps, so reaching the default 65,536 (216) takes 16 × 2,000 = 32,000 steps — matching "worst in the first tens of thousands of steps". Fix: keep the default init_scale=65536.0; the scaler backs off by itself if that overflows.

Bug 3 — the loss is computed outside autocast on fp16 logits. logits left the autocast region as fp16, so cross-entropy runs its log-softmax in fp16, losing precision and risking overflow for large logits. Fix: compute the loss inside the autocast block (autocast runs cross_entropy in float32 on CUDA), or call logits.float() first. After fixing, log scaler.get_scale() and the number of skipped steps; and on hardware with bf16, switch to it and drop the scaler. (A fourth classic variant — calling model.half() as well — removes the float32 master weights; recent PyTorch refuses to unscale fp16 gradients, so it fails loudly.)

Estimate: will it fit?

You fine-tune a 1.3B-parameter model (24 layers, h = 2,048, 16 heads) with AdamW and autocast bf16 on one 40 GB GPU, at sequence length 2,048 and micro-batch 4, using SDPA (fused attention). Vocabulary 50,304. Does it fit, and what would you change if not?

Answer

States: 16 bytes × 1.3B ≈ 20.8 GB. Activations with fused attention: 34 × s·b·h per layer = 34 × 2,048 × 4 × 2,048 ≈ 0.57 GB; × 24 ≈ 13.7 GB. Logits: 2,048 × 4 × 50,304 × 2 bytes ≈ 0.82 GB in bf16, plus a float32 log-softmax of the same shape ≈ 1.65 GB during the loss. Peak ≈ 20.8 + 13.7 + 2.5 ≈ 37 GB plus the CUDA context and autocast's bf16 weight cache (≈ 2.6 GB): right at or over 40 GB, so it will likely OOM. Cheapest fixes: micro-batch 2 with accumulation 2 (activations and logits halve, ≈ 8 GB saved), or checkpoint every other layer. With either, measure max_memory_allocated() after one step and keep 10% headroom.
