Picture a team that runs a fleet of GPUs serving a chat product. Every Monday someone has to decide how many machines to reserve for the coming four weeks. Reserve too few and users see errors and slow replies during the Thursday evening peak; reserve too many and the company pays for idle hardware at several dollars per GPU-hour. So an engineer exports two years of hourly request counts, adds columns for hour of day, day of week and a few lagged values, shuffles the rows, holds out 20% as a test set, trains a gradient-boosted model, and gets an error of 3%. Everyone is delighted. The model goes live. Four weeks later the real error is 25%, and the Thursday peak was under-provisioned twice.

Nothing was wrong with the algorithm. What went wrong is that the engineer used the habits of ordinary machine learning on data that breaks those habits' central assumption. In ordinary supervised learning, rows are treated as independent draws from one fixed distribution: the order of rows means nothing, so shuffling is harmless and a random held-out set tells you how the model will do on new rows. A time series is the opposite. The order is the information. Each hour looks a lot like the hour before; a random test hour usually has training hours on both sides of it, so a model can effectively look at 2 pm and 4 pm to guess 3 pm. In production there is no 4 pm yet. The offline test measured interpolation; the job is extrapolation.

The same gap appears in other forms. A column that was perfectly available in the historical table — the actual temperature, the final revised sales figure, a competitor's price scraped the next day — does not exist yet at the moment the forecast is made. A history that ran smoothly for two years can be split in two by a product launch or a pricing change, after which the old data teaches the wrong lessons. A day with zero sales might mean nobody wanted the product, or that the shelf was empty. None of these traps exist when you classify images.

This lesson builds the vocabulary and the instincts for everything else in the track. You will learn what a time series is and why its rows are dependent; the language of forecasting (origin, horizon, lookback, step-ahead); a worked, by-hand example of how a random split fools you; the three kinds of covariate and which one leaks; univariate versus multivariate and local versus global models; the families of tasks people solve with time series; the data traps that eat most of a practitioner's time; and how to frame a forecasting problem from the decision it serves. The real case is a 2023 study that found leakage, including temporal leakage, behind headline results in published research.

A deck of cards versus a diary

Think of an ordinary machine-learning dataset as a deck of cards. Each card is one example: a photo and its label, a loan application and whether it defaulted. Shuffle the deck and nothing is lost, because the cards do not refer to each other. Deal 80% to training and 20% to testing and the test cards are a fair sample of the cards you will meet in future.

A time series is a diary. Each page is one day, and pages refer to each other: the weather today continues yesterday's, the bank balance today is yesterday's plus today's transactions, the café's croissant sales on Saturday look like last Saturday's. If you tear out random pages and ask someone to guess what was written on them, they will do very well — they can read the pages before and after. If you ask them to write next week's pages, they will do much worse. Forecasting is writing next week's pages. Any evaluation that lets the model read later pages measures the wrong skill.

The analogy breaks in one useful place: a diary has only one author, but a forecasting system often reads many diaries at once — thousands of stores, millions of products, every server in a fleet. Learning patterns shared across many diaries, such as "everyone buys more on paydays", is one of the main ideas of modern forecasting, and it appears below as the global model.

The words you need

A time series is a sequence of measurements of the same quantity taken over time, usually at regular intervals: daily sales, hourly requests, a sensor reading every second. We write the value at time t as y_t, with t = 1, 2, 3 and so on. The spacing is the frequency (hourly, daily, monthly), and it decides which repeating cycles can exist: hourly data can show a 24-hour cycle and a 168-hour weekly cycle, monthly data a 12-month yearly cycle.

When you forecast, you stand at a point in time called the forecast origin, written T: the last moment whose data you are allowed to use. You predict values h steps later, where h is the horizon. The forecast of the value at T+h made at origin T is written ŷ(T+h | T), read "y-hat at T plus h given T"; the hat means "estimate", and the bar says which information it is based on. The stretch of history the model reads is the lookback window (or context length). Inputs other than the series' own history are covariates: price, calendar, weather.

The big picture in four moves

First, respect order: train on the past, evaluate on what comes after it, and never compute a feature with data from after the origin. Second, start from a naive baseline — "tomorrow equals today", or "next Monday equals last Monday" — because any model must beat it to be worth running. Third, decide the setup from the decision the forecast serves: which series, at what level of detail, how far ahead, and which kind of error hurts more. Fourth, expect change: patterns drift, and a forecasting system needs re-training, monitoring and the humility to say how uncertain it is.

A reader who stops here should hold on to one sentence: a time series is a diary, not a deck of cards, so every choice — how you split, which features you compute, how you judge accuracy — must respect the arrow of time.

1. What a time series is, precisely

Formally a time series is an ordered sequence y_1, y_2, …, y_n where each value belongs to a timestamp. Most of this track assumes regular sampling: timestamps are equally spaced, one per hour or one per day. Real data is often messier. Sensor readings arrive at irregular moments, transactions are events with exact times, and someone has to decide how to turn them into a regular series, usually by aggregation: count the transactions per hour, average the sensor per minute. That choice is not neutral. Aggregating to daily totals hides the lunchtime peak that decides how many cooks a restaurant needs; aggregating to hourly makes the series noisier and adds a daily cycle to model.

The series can be a count (units sold, requests served), a continuous measurement (temperature, latency in milliseconds), a rate (requests per second), a price or a level (a bank balance, inventory on hand). The type matters later: counts cannot be negative and often have many zeros; levels accumulate; rates can be averaged but counts are summed when you change frequency. A practical rule: before any modelling, write down for each series what one value means, in what unit, over what window, and in which time zone.

2. Dependence: why the rows are not independent

The defining property of a time series is that neighbouring values are related. If today's sales were unusually high, tomorrow's are likely to be high too: the weather is still good, the promotion is still running, the local event is still on. Statisticians measure this with autocorrelation — the correlation between the series and a copy of itself shifted by k steps, called lag k. Lag 1 compares each day with the day before; lag 7 compares each day with the same weekday a week earlier. Topic ts3 computes autocorrelation by hand; for now, the idea is enough: in most real series it is strongly positive at small lags.

Dependence is good news and bad news. Good, because it is what makes forecasting possible: if the past carried no information about the future, there would be nothing to learn. Bad, because it breaks the assumption under almost everything in the ML track — that rows are exchangeable, meaning you could shuffle them without changing anything about the data. Cross-validation, bootstrapping, the usual confidence intervals and random train/test splits all lean on exchangeability. With dependent rows, a random split no longer gives a fair test, and standard errors computed as if you had n independent rows are too small, because n dependent rows carry less information than n independent ones.

3. A worked example: how a random split fools you

Take ten days of croissant sales at a café, rising gently as word spreads:

Day12345678910
Sales20222524272930333235

Use a deliberately silly model that knows only the date: to predict a day, it averages the two training days closest in time. It has no idea what a croissant is. The error measure is the mean absolute error (MAE): the average size of the errors, ignoring sign (ts5 covers it fully).

Random split. Hold out days 3, 6 and 9, scattered through the data as a random 30% split would scatter them. Day 3's nearest training days are 2 and 4: (22 + 24) / 2 = 23, actual 25, error 2. Day 6: (27 + 30) / 2 = 28.5, actual 29, error 0.5. Day 9: (33 + 35) / 2 = 34, actual 32, error 2. MAE = (2 + 0.5 + 2) / 3 = 1.5. That looks like an excellent forecaster.

Time split. Train on days 1–7, test on days 8–10, which is what forecasting actually asks for. The nearest training days to all three test days are 6 and 7, so the model predicts (29 + 30) / 2 = 29.5 every time. Errors: |33 − 29.5| = 3.5, |32 − 29.5| = 2.5, |35 − 29.5| = 5.5. MAE = 11.5 / 3 ≈ 3.83, more than two and a half times worse. The naive forecast "every future day equals the last known day" (30) does slightly better: errors 3, 2 and 5, MAE ≈ 3.33.

The model did not change; only the question did. Under a random split, every test day was sandwiched between training days, so the model interpolated. Under a time split it had to extrapolate past the end of the data, which is the real job. Notice also that it could not even beat the naive forecast. This is why the first rule of time-series evaluation is to split by time, and why the first number to compute is a naive baseline. The lab repeats the experiment on two years of synthetic data: the date-only model scores an MAE of 4.3 on a random split — as good as an honest model — and 10.7 on a time split.

4. The forecasting vocabulary, used precisely

With the origin, horizon and lookback defined in the primer, a few more terms complete the kit. A one-step-ahead forecast predicts h = 1; a multi-step forecast predicts h = 1 to H, for some maximum horizon H. Multi-step forecasts are produced either recursively — predict tomorrow, feed that prediction in as if it were data, predict the day after, and so on — or directly, with a separate model (or a separate output) for each horizon. Topic ts6 compares them; the key fact now is that recursive forecasts feed their own errors back, so error grows with h. In the lab, a linear model with lags 1 and 7 had a weekly MAE of 2.5 in the first week ahead and 10.1 in the fourth.

That growth is not a defect of one model. Every step ahead lets new, unpredictable shocks accumulate. If a series is a random walk — each day equals the previous day plus an independent shock of standard deviation σ — then the error of the naive forecast after h steps is the sum of h shocks, with standard deviation σ√h. Four times further ahead means twice as uncertain. This is why serious forecasts report accuracy per horizon, and why prediction intervals fan out (ts8).

A point forecast is a single number per future step; a probabilistic forecast is a distribution, or a set of quantiles such as "a 90% chance of fewer than 480 units". The distinction matters because decisions often depend on the tail: you stock to the 95th percentile of demand, not the mean.

Finally, in-sample means on the data used to fit the model and out-of-sample means after the training period. Fitting a model and quoting its in-sample error is the time-series equivalent of grading your own homework.

5. Covariates: static, known-future and past-only

Covariates are inputs other than the series' own past. They come in three kinds, and keeping them straight is the single most practical skill in this lesson.

Static covariates do not change over time for a given series: the store's floor area, the product's category, the city a server sits in. They let a model treat similar series similarly.

Known-future covariates are inputs whose future values you genuinely know at the origin, because they are fixed by the calendar or decided by your own organisation: day of week, public holidays, school terms, the planned price, the promotion calendar, a scheduled product launch. These are gold, because they tell the model about the future directly.

Past-only covariates are observed only after the fact: the actual temperature, a competitor's price you scraped, the number of visitors to your website, today's stock-market close. At the origin you know their history but not their future. A model may use their lagged values (yesterday's temperature) but not their values during the horizon.

The most common leak in forecasting is training a model with a past-only covariate as if it were known-future. Suppose ice-cream sales depend strongly on temperature. You train on rows where the temperature column holds the actual temperature for each day, and the model learns "hot day, high sales" beautifully. At serving time there is no actual temperature for next Tuesday, only a forecast, which is less accurate — so the model's inputs at serving time are noisier than anything it saw in training, and its accuracy collapses. The fix is either to use only lagged temperature, or to store the weather forecasts that were issued before each historical origin and train on those. Then training and serving see the same kind of input. This is the time-series form of the training/serving consistency principle from ml14.

A related subtlety is data revisions. Many official statistics, and many internal metrics, are revised after first publication: a first estimate of last month's revenue is corrected two weeks later when late invoices arrive. The historical table usually stores only the final revised value, which was not what anyone knew at the time. A model trained on final values and served on first estimates has the same mismatch as the weather example. The cure is a point-in-time (or "as-of") store that remembers what each value was on each date; ts12 builds one.

6. Univariate, multivariate, local and global

A univariate forecasting problem has one target series, possibly with covariates. A multivariate problem has several target series whose joint behaviour matters: CPU, memory and latency on the same host move together, and a spike in one tells you about the others. Multivariate models (vector autoregression in ts11, channel-mixing neural models in ts7) try to learn those cross-series relationships.

A separate axis is how many models you fit. A local model fits one model per series: one exponential-smoothing model for croissants at store 1, another for croissants at store 2. This is the classical approach of ts4. It works well for a few long series with their own character and is easy to explain, but it learns nothing from other series. A product launched last week has no history, so a local model has nothing to fit.

A global model fits one model across many series at once. Each training row is a (series, origin) pair: the recent history of that series, its static features, the calendar, and the target. Because the model sees thousands of series, it learns shared patterns — "promotions roughly double sales of snacks", "every store dips on Christmas Day" — and can apply them to a series with little history. Global models are how the M5 competition was won (ts6) and how deep and foundation models work (ts7, ts9). The cost is that series must be put on comparable scales (dividing each by its own recent mean, for example), and one model must serve very different series.

7. The task families

Forecasting predicts future values, as points or distributions. Anomaly detection flags points or windows that do not fit the expected pattern: a sudden drop in checkout events may mean a broken payment page. Change-point detection finds moments when the behaviour of the series shifts permanently. Classification labels a whole window: is this heartbeat recording normal, which activity is this phone's accelerometer showing? Imputation fills gaps in the record. Causal and what-if questions ask what would have happened under a different action: what would sales have been without last month's campaign? Topics ts10 and ts11 take the non-forecasting families; most of the track is about forecasting, because it is the most common and the foundation for the rest. Anomaly detection, for example, is often "forecast the next value, then flag it if the actual is far outside the forecast's interval".

8. Non-stationarity and structural breaks

Ordinary ML assumes the data comes from one fixed distribution. Time series rarely do. The level drifts (a growing business), the spread changes (a bigger business has bigger swings), seasonal patterns evolve (online shopping flattens the old weekly cycle), and sometimes everything changes at once: a pandemic closes the shops, a competitor opens next door, a new pricing plan launches. A series whose statistical behaviour changes over time is called non-stationary; ts3 gives the precise definition and the tools to handle it. A sudden lasting change is a structural break.

Breaks raise a question ordinary ML never asks: how much history should you use? Data from before a break may mislead the model; data after it may be too short to learn seasonality from. The common answers are to truncate history at the break, to down-weight older observations, to add an indicator feature for the regime ("after the price change"), or to let a global model borrow post-break behaviour from other series. Which one wins is an empirical question settled by backtesting each option over several origins.

9. The data traps

Experienced forecasters say most of the work is understanding what the numbers mean. Five traps come up constantly.

Missing timestamps are not zeros. If the log has no row for 3 am, either nothing happened (a real zero) or the logger was down (missing data). Filling every gap with 0 teaches the model fake drops; filling every gap by interpolation hides real closures. You need to know which, from the source system.

Censored demand. Sales are capped by stock. On a day the shelf was empty from noon, recorded sales are a lower bound on demand. Train on them as if they were demand and the model learns that popular items sell less than they do, which leads to smaller orders and more empty shelves — a feedback loop. Flag stock-out days and exclude or impute them.

Late and revised data. Yesterday's figure may be incomplete at 6 am and complete at noon. A model run at 6 am sees a partial last value that looks like a sudden drop.

Time zones and daylight saving. A "day" in UTC is not a day in New York, and a day on which the clocks change has 23 or 25 hours. Aggregations that ignore this produce fake dips and spikes twice a year.

Calendar effects that move. Easter, Ramadan, Diwali and Chinese New Year fall on different dates each year, so a fixed "same day last year" pattern misplaces them; ts2 handles them with holiday features.

10. Where an AI engineer meets this

Time series appear all over AI systems. Capacity planning for GPU fleets and inference services forecasts requests and tokens per hour. Cost planning forecasts API spend. Product teams forecast demand, sign-ups and churn. Reliability teams run anomaly detection on latency, error rates and model-quality metrics, and need change-point detection to tell a deploy regression from noise. Agents increasingly call forecasting models as tools ("how many units will we sell next month?"), and LLM-based products are asked to explain forecasts in plain language. In interviews, you will rarely be asked to derive a model; you will be asked to design one of these systems and to spot what would make its offline numbers lie.

11. Baselines first

Two forecasts cost nothing and must be computed before anything else. The naive forecast says every future value equals the last observed value. The seasonal-naive forecast says each future value equals the value one season earlier: next Monday equals last Monday. On the croissant example, naive scored 3.33 against the date-only model's 3.83. In the lab, seasonal-naive scores an MAE of 5.55 on the test period and the honest lag model 3.94, so the model earns its keep. If a sophisticated model cannot beat these baselines in an honest backtest, it is not ready, however impressive its architecture. Topic ts4 adds a few more baselines and the classical models built on them; ts5 turns "beat the baseline" into a scale-free metric called MASE.

Start from the decision, not the data

A forecast is only useful as an input to a decision, and the decision fixes almost every modelling choice. Ask four questions. What action does it drive — ordering stock, reserving machines, staffing a call centre, setting a budget? At what granularity — store-product-day, region-week, total-month? With what lead time — if an order takes ten days to arrive, the forecast that matters is for days 10 to 17, not tomorrow. And what does each kind of error cost — an empty shelf loses a sale and a customer's goodwill, an overfull one ties up cash and may spoil. If under-forecasting costs three times as much as over-forecasting, the right forecast to act on is not the mean but a higher quantile (ts8 and ts12 make this exact with the newsvendor rule). Candidates who open a design question with these four questions sound senior immediately.

Forecastability: some series are close to noise

Before modelling, estimate how predictable a series is. Three quick measures help. The coefficient of variation (standard deviation divided by mean) tells you how noisy the series is relative to its level. The share of zeros tells you whether it is intermittent; a product that sells on 20 days out of 365 at one store is mostly a question of whether a sale happens at all. And the gap between a naive baseline and a reasonable model in a backtest tells you how much structure there is to learn. If a careful model only beats seasonal-naive by 1-2%, more modelling will not help much. The productive moves are then to aggregate (forecast the product's weekly total, or the category's daily total, and split down), to forecast a distribution and let the decision absorb the uncertainty, or to change the decision so it tolerates error, for example by holding safety stock.

Aggregation changes the problem

Summing series makes them smoother: random ups and downs partly cancel, so the total of 1,000 noisy products is far more predictable than any one of them. This is why national demand is forecast more accurately than store demand, and weekly more accurately than daily. It cuts both ways. A decision at store level needs store-level numbers, and forecasts at different levels will not add up unless you reconcile them — the subject of ts11. Temporal aggregation (daily to weekly) works the same way, and a powerful trick is to forecast at several frequencies and combine them, so the weekly model's stable trend corrects the daily model's drift.

Dependence also breaks your statistics

Beyond splits, dependence invalidates the usual uncertainty arithmetic. A standard error computed as σ/√n assumes n independent observations. If neighbouring values are correlated, the effective number of independent observations is smaller: for a series where each value correlates 0.8 with the previous one, a common approximation puts the effective sample size near n × (1 − 0.8)/(1 + 0.8) ≈ 0.11n. A year of daily data then carries roughly the information of 40 independent days. Confidence intervals that ignore this are too narrow, and "statistically significant" differences between two forecasting models evaluated on the same correlated test period are often not. ts5 returns to this when comparing models over a backtest.

Regimes, history length and recency weighting

After a structural break, the question "how much history?" has no universal answer, but there are principled ways to choose. Exponential smoothing (ts4) answers it continuously: every observation's weight decays geometrically with age, and the decay rate is fitted. Machine-learning models can do the same with sample weights that decay with age, or with a regime feature that lets the model learn separate behaviour before and after. A global model can also learn post-break behaviour from series that broke earlier. The senior habit is to treat history length as a hyperparameter, test several values over several origins, and prefer the setting that does well across origins rather than the one that wins one period.

Feedback loops: forecasts change the data

Forecasts are often self-influencing. A demand forecast drives orders; orders fix stock; stock caps sales; sales become the next training data. If the forecast is low, stock is low, sales are capped, and the next forecast is lower still. A capacity forecast that reserves too few machines causes throttling, which suppresses measured demand. A fraud model that blocks transactions removes the evidence of what would have happened. These loops mean the training data reflects past decisions, not just the world, and they are a reason to log decisions alongside outcomes, to flag constrained periods, and occasionally to run deliberate experiments (ts11 discusses causal questions).

Event data and irregular series

Some data is not naturally a regular series: individual purchases, login events, earthquake times, server errors. Turning them into counts per interval is the usual first step, and the interval is a modelling choice with a trade-off between noise (short intervals) and lost detail (long intervals). There is a separate family of models, point processes, that model the times of events directly — for example the probability that the next error occurs within the next minute given the recent errors. They matter for highly bursty data such as alerts, where one event makes more events likely. In interviews it is enough to know they exist and why counts per interval can hide burst structure.

Multiple and long seasonalities

High-frequency data often has several cycles at once. Hourly electricity load has a 24-hour cycle, a 168-hour weekly cycle and an 8,766-hour yearly cycle (365.25 × 24). Many classical methods handle one season comfortably and struggle with three, and a model whose lookback is shorter than the longest cycle cannot see it at all from the history alone. Calendar features (hour of day, day of week, day of year) are the standard fix: they hand the model the phase of each cycle directly. This is also a reason global ML models became popular for high-frequency data.

How time-series systems fail in production

The characteristic failure is the one from the opening story: an offline score that does not survive contact with production. The causes form a short checklist. Was the evaluation split random, or by time? Did any feature use data after the origin — a centred rolling mean (which averages days before and after), a target statistic computed over the whole dataset, final revised values, actual weather? Was the offline test one-step-ahead while production forecasts weeks ahead? Did production inputs arrive late or partial at prediction time? Each of these makes the offline number optimistic in a way only production reveals. The defence is a backtest that replays history exactly as production would have seen it: same origins, same horizon, same data availability.

The second family is silent data problems. A pipeline starts filling gaps with zeros after a schema change; a time-zone bug shifts a daily aggregation by five hours; a product's identifier changes and its history appears to start from zero; a sensor is replaced and reads 3% higher. None of these crash anything. They show up as sudden shifts in forecast error for a segment, which is why monitoring in production tracks accuracy per segment and per horizon, not just a global average, and why it also tracks input health: row counts per day, share of zeros, share of missing values, and the date of the latest value received.

The third family is change in the world. A structural break makes the model's training data out of date. Error rises gradually or suddenly; a monitoring system should detect it (ts10 covers change-point detection) and trigger re-training or a fallback to a robust baseline. Mature forecasting teams keep a seasonal-naive or simple exponential-smoothing forecast running alongside the main model for exactly this reason: when the fancy model breaks, there is a sensible number to fall back on.

A real case: leakage behind published results

In 2022–2023, Sayash Kapoor and Arvind Narayanan at Princeton surveyed reproducibility problems in research that uses machine learning and found papers in 17 fields where errors had been documented, collectively affecting 329 papers. They organised the errors into a taxonomy of eight kinds of leakage. One of them, labelled L3.1, is temporal leakage: when a model is meant to predict future outcomes, the test set must not contain data from before the training set, otherwise the model is built with information from the future.

They then reproduced published papers in one field — predicting the onset of civil war, where complex ML models had been reported to vastly outperform logistic regression. Every paper claiming that superiority failed to reproduce because of leakage. Two of the four used k-fold cross-validation on data that is ordered by year, among other errors. In Wang (2019), fixing the problems shrank the advantage in AUC (a ranking-quality score from ml7) of AdaBoost over logistic regression from 0.14 to 0.01. In Kaufman et al. (2019), after correction, none of the models beat a baseline that simply predicted the previous year's outcome — the naive forecast of this lesson. The study is a warning that the same mistakes happen in peer-reviewed work, not just in hurried notebooks, and its remedy is the one this lesson argues for: say exactly what information was available when, split by time, and compare with a naive baseline.

The M5 data as a picture of a real forecasting problem

The M5 competition (2020), run on Kaggle by the University of Nicosia team behind the M competitions, is a useful mental model of industrial forecasting, and it reappears in ts5, ts6 and ts11. Walmart provided daily unit sales of 3,049 products in 3 categories and 7 departments, sold across 10 stores in 3 US states, from 29 January 2011 to 19 June 2016 — 1,969 days. Summing the product-store series up the product and location hierarchy gives 42,840 series in total. Teams forecast 28 days ahead; the dataset included known-future covariates (calendar, special events, prices, and the days on which food-stamp benefits could be used). Many product-store series are intermittent, with lots of zeros. 7,092 participants on 5,507 teams from 101 countries took part; the winning team improved on the best statistical benchmark by 22.4%, and almost all top methods used gradient-boosted trees trained across many series — global models. Every concept in this lesson — origin, horizon, covariate types, intermittency, hierarchy, local versus global — appears in that one dataset.

Knobs and habits

The knobs are mostly about framing: the aggregation level, the horizon, the lookback, how much history to keep, which covariates to trust, and how often to re-train (daily, weekly, on drift). The habits are what separate reliable teams: a written data dictionary for every series (unit, window, time zone, meaning of zero); a point-in-time feature store; backtests over many origins with per-horizon and per-segment results; a baseline forecast always running alongside; and a log of what was forecast, when, with which model version, so any forecast can be explained after the fact.

Goal: watch a random split flatter a model that knows nothing but the date, then see an honest time split, error growth with horizon, and a seasonal-naive baseline. NumPy only; run it with the existing gpt_env venv (Python 3.12). Save as ts1.py.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 730                                   # two years of daily data
t = np.arange(n)
level = 100 + 0.05 * t                    # slow upward trend
weekly = 10 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * t / 7)   # weekly cycle
noise = np.zeros(n)
for i in range(1, n):                     # AR(1) noise: today's shock leans on yesterday's
    noise[i] = 0.8 * noise[i - 1] + rng.normal(0, 3)
y = level + weekly + noise
y[400:] += 25                             # a permanent jump on day 400 (new store opens nearby)

# Features for each day: yesterday, the same weekday last week, and the day index
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n - 7), y[6:-1], y[:-7], t[7:]])
target = y[7:]

def fit_predict(train_idx, test_idx):
    w, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X[train_idx], target[train_idx], rcond=None)
    return X[test_idx] @ w

def mae(a, b):
    return np.mean(np.abs(a - b))

m = len(target)
cut = int(0.8 * m)
# 1. Random 80/20 split: test days are scattered between training days
perm = rng.permutation(m)
tr, te = perm[: int(0.8 * m)], perm[int(0.8 * m):]
print(f"random split   MAE = {mae(fit_predict(tr, te), target[te]):.2f}")

# 2. Time split: train on the first 80%, test on the last 20%
print(f"time split     MAE = {mae(fit_predict(np.arange(cut), np.arange(cut, m)), target[cut:]):.2f}")

# 3. A model that knows ONLY the date: average the 2 nearest training days in time.
#    Under a random split the nearest days sit on both sides of each test day.
def knn_by_date(train_idx, test_idx, k=2):
    tt = t[7:]
    out = []
    for j in test_idx:
        nearest = train_idx[np.argsort(np.abs(tt[train_idx] - tt[j]))[:k]]
        out.append(target[nearest].mean())
    return np.array(out)
print(f"date-only kNN, random split MAE = {mae(knn_by_date(tr, te), target[te]):.2f}")
print(f"date-only kNN, time split   MAE = {mae(knn_by_date(np.arange(cut), np.arange(cut, m)), target[cut:]):.2f}")

# 4. Honest multi-step forecast: from origin = last training day, forecast 28 days
#    recursively, feeding predictions back in as 'yesterday'
w, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X[:cut], target[:cut], rcond=None)
hist = list(y[: cut + 7])
for h in range(28):
    d = cut + 7 + h
    x = np.array([1.0, hist[d - 1], hist[d - 7], d])
    hist.append(x @ w)
pred28 = np.array(hist[cut + 7:])
err = np.abs(pred28 - y[cut + 7: cut + 35])
print("recursive 28-day MAE by week:", [round(float(err[k:k + 7].mean()), 2) for k in range(0, 28, 7)])

# 5. Seasonal-naive baseline on the same test period: tomorrow = same weekday last week
print(f"seasonal naive MAE = {mae(target[cut:], y[cut:m]):.2f}")

What to look for. The honest lag model (yesterday, same weekday last week, day index) scores about the same under both splits, because its features only look backwards. The date-only model is the trap: on a random split its MAE (4.26) is close to the honest model's, so a careless evaluation would rank it as a good forecaster; on a time split it more than doubles, because there is no future neighbour to copy. The recursive forecast shows error growing week by week as predictions are fed back as inputs. Seasonal-naive is the bar: the lag model beats it (3.94 vs 5.55), the date-only model does not.

Work it out

A grocer has 3,000 stores × 40,000 SKUs (stock-keeping units: one distinct product) of daily unit sales, 4 years of history. They want 28-day-ahead forecasts each Monday to drive ordering. Available: price plan, promotion calendar, public holidays, observed weather, a competitor price scrape, store square footage.

(a) State frequency, horizon and roughly how many series. (b) Sort each input into static / known-future / past-only. (c) Why is "fit a classical model per series" the wrong starting point?

Answer

(a) Daily frequency, h = 1…28, and 3,000 × 40,000 = 120 million series (fewer in practice — not every store stocks every SKU).

(b) Static: square footage (and SKU category). Known-future: price plan, promotion calendar, holidays — they are decided in advance. Past-only: observed weather and competitor price; at forecast time you only have their history. Weather forecasts could be a known-future input, but then you must train on the forecasts that were available at each historical origin, not on actuals, or you leak.

(c) Three reasons. Scale: 120 million separate fits and model selections every week. Sparsity: most SKU×store series are intermittent (lots of zeros), where classical Gaussian-error models fit poorly. And no sharing: a new SKU has no history, while a global model trained across all series transfers what it learned about promotions and seasonality. Start with seasonal-naive as a baseline, then a global gradient-boosted model with lag and calendar features (ts6), evaluated with rolling-origin backtests (ts5).
