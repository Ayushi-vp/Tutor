At 03:12 on a Sunday, an on-call engineer's phone buzzes: "checkout latency anomaly". It is the eleventh alert of the night. The previous ten were false — a nightly batch job, a cache refresh, a quiet hour that tripped a percentage-change rule. This one is real: a database failover has doubled latency for a fifth of users. The engineer, half-asleep and trained by ten false alarms, snoozes it. Users suffer for forty minutes before someone else notices. The detector "worked" — it fired — but a detector that cries wolf ten times a night is not detecting anything useful.

Anomaly detection on time series is one of the most common jobs an AI engineer meets: latency and error-rate alerting, fraud and abuse detection on transaction streams, sensor monitoring on machines, data-quality checks on pipelines, and monitoring of model quality itself. It is also one of the easiest to do badly, for three reasons. Normal behaviour has structure — daily cycles, weekly cycles, growth — so a value that is alarming at 4 am is ordinary at noon. Anomalies are rare, so even a tiny false-alarm rate produces more false alerts than real ones. And labels are scarce, so it is hard to know how well a detector works, which has let the research field fool itself with flawed benchmarks and generous metrics.

Closely related are two other jobs. Change-point detection asks when the behaviour of a series shifted for good — a deploy that permanently raised latency, a pricing change that lowered demand — which matters because after a change the old notion of "normal" is wrong. And time-series classification asks what a whole window is — a normal or abnormal heartbeat, a walking or running accelerometer trace, a healthy or failing machine.

For AI systems specifically, detection has new targets. Model-serving teams watch token throughput, time to first token, GPU memory and error rates; ML teams watch the distribution of model inputs and outputs, the rate of refusals or empty answers, evaluation scores on canary prompts, and costs per request. Each is a time series with daily and weekly rhythms, deploys that shift its level, and rare incidents that matter enormously. The same small toolkit — a good baseline, robust scores, change-point detection, honest thresholds — covers all of them.

This lesson builds detection from first principles: the kinds of anomaly, the predict-then-compare pattern, z-scores and robust scores worked by hand (including how one outlier can hide another), seasonal baselines for contextual anomalies, CUSUM and offline change-point methods, thresholds and alert fatigue, how to evaluate honestly (and the point-adjustment trap), what Wu and Keogh found in the popular benchmarks, and a short tour of classification methods.

A security guard who knows the building

A good night-shift security guard does not raise the alarm every time something moves. They know the building's rhythm: cleaners on floor 3 at 10 pm, the boiler kicking in at 5 am, deliveries on Tuesdays. What makes them valuable is a detailed sense of normal, so that they notice what does not fit: a light on floor 7 at 2 am, a door that is usually locked standing open, a lift going up and down with nobody in it. An anomaly detector is that guard, and its quality depends almost entirely on how good its model of normal is.

The guard also shows the three kinds of anomaly. A point anomaly is a single thing that is wrong anywhere: a fire alarm. A contextual anomaly is normal in one context and wrong in another: people on floor 3 are normal at 10 pm and suspicious at 3 am. A collective anomaly is a pattern that is wrong even though each moment looks fine: a lift going up and down twenty times in a row, each trip ordinary on its own.

Predict, compare, decide

Almost every detector follows the same three steps. Predict what the value (or window) should be if all is normal — a mean, a seasonal profile, a forecast, a reconstruction. Compare the actual with the prediction and scale the difference by how much normal values usually vary, giving an anomaly score. Decide whether the score crosses a threshold, possibly requiring it to persist for a while. All the sophistication — robust statistics, seasonal decomposition, forecasting models, neural autoencoders — goes into the first two steps; the third step is where business costs come in.

Change points need a different decision rule. A single large residual is evidence of an anomaly; a run of moderately positive residuals that never stops is evidence that normal itself has moved. Change-point methods accumulate evidence over time rather than judging one point at a time.

The guard analogy has a limit worth stating. A guard learns the building over months and is told about planned events — the fire drill on Thursday, the renovation on floor 5. Detectors need the same information explicitly: maintenance windows, deploy times, holidays and known batch jobs must be fed in, or every one of them will look like an anomaly. Much of the practical work in anomaly detection is plumbing this context into the baseline.

It also helps to know what "normal" means for different kinds of metric. For counts (requests, errors, sign-ups) normal variation grows with the level, so scores should be relative or variance-stabilised (a square root or log, as in ts2). For ratios (error rate, conversion rate) normal variation depends on how many events the ratio is computed from — an error rate based on 20 requests is far noisier than one based on 20,000 — so a sound detector widens its tolerance when volume is low. For latencies, the distribution is skewed and percentiles (p50, p99) behave differently; p99 latency is naturally spiky. Choosing the right scale and the right notion of noise for each metric type prevents most false alarms before any modelling.

A reader who stops here should remember: model normal first, threshold residuals not raw values, use robust statistics, and remember that rare events make false alarms the dominant problem.

1. The z-score, worked

The simplest anomaly score is the z-score: how many standard deviations a value lies from the mean of a reference window. Take a baseline of nine recent readings — 50, 52, 49, 51, 48, 50, 53, 49, 51 — and a new reading of 70.

The mean is 453/9 = 50.33. The deviations from the mean are −0.33, 1.67, −1.33, 0.67, −2.33, −0.33, 2.67, −1.33, 0.67; their squares sum to 20.0. The sample variance is 20.0/(9 − 1) = 2.5 and the standard deviation √2.5 ≈ 1.58. The new reading's z-score is (70 − 50.33)/1.58 ≈ 12.4. A common rule flags |z| > 3; this value is far beyond it.

Why 3? For normally distributed data, |z| > 3 happens about 0.27% of the time — roughly one point in 370. That sounds rare until you multiply: a metric sampled every minute has 1,440 points a day, so a |z| > 3 rule fires about four times a day on pure noise. With 1,000 metrics, that is 4,000 false alerts a day. This arithmetic is why thresholds in production are often 4, 5 or higher, and why the base rate of real anomalies dominates every design decision.

2. Robust scores and the masking problem

The mean and standard deviation are both pulled by extreme values — the very values we are looking for. Suppose the baseline had contained an earlier outlier: replace the 51 in position four with 90. The mean rises to 54.67 and the standard deviation balloons to about 13.3, so the new 70 now has z = (70 − 54.67)/13.4 ≈ 1.1: not flagged at all. The first outlier has masked the second. This happens constantly in practice, because anomalies cluster — an incident produces a burst of strange values.

The fix is to use statistics that ignore a few extreme values. The median of the original baseline is 50. The median absolute deviation (MAD) is the median of the absolute deviations from the median: |0|, 2, 1, 1, 2, 0, 3, 1, 1, sorted 0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, so MAD = 1. For normal data the standard deviation is about 1.4826 × MAD, so the robust z-score is (70 − 50)/(1.4826 × 1) ≈ 13.5. With the earlier 90 in the baseline, the median is still 50 and the MAD still 1: the robust z stays 13.5. Robust statistics are the single cheapest improvement to most anomaly detectors.

3. Contextual anomalies: score residuals, not raw values

Most operational metrics have strong seasonality: traffic follows the day and the week, batch jobs run on schedules. A raw threshold cannot work: set it high enough to stay quiet at the daily peak and it misses everything at night; set it low and the peak fires every day. The answer is to subtract an estimate of normal for each point's context and score the residual.

The baseline can be as simple as a seasonal profile — the median value for each hour of the day, or for each hour of the week — or as rich as an STL decomposition (ts2) or a forecasting model with prediction intervals (ts8). The lab simulates four weeks of hourly data with a daily cycle and injects three anomalies of +30 at the daily low, where the series is around 60. A rule "alert above the overall 99th percentile" produces 7 alerts — all at normal daily peaks — and catches none of the anomalies. Subtracting the hour-of-day median and thresholding the robust z of the residual at 4 produces exactly 3 alerts, all correct.

Using a forecast as the baseline adds two things: it handles trend and recent level changes, and its prediction interval gives a principled threshold ("alert if outside the 99.9% interval"). Its risk is that a forecaster fitted on recent data that includes the anomaly may learn the anomaly as normal; robust fitting or excluding flagged points from training prevents that.

4. Thresholds, persistence and alert fatigue

The threshold turns scores into decisions, and it should come from costs, not convention. Write down the cost of a missed incident (minutes of outage, lost revenue), the cost of a false page (an engineer woken up, trust eroded), and the base rate of real incidents. Then, on historical data with labelled incidents, choose the threshold that meets a false-alarm budget — say at most one false page per week per team — with the best recall.

Two techniques cut false alarms without hurting recall much. Persistence (or debouncing) requires the score to stay above threshold for several consecutive points or a minimum duration, which ignores one-point blips. Hysteresis uses a higher threshold to open an alert than to close it, so an alert does not flap open and shut as the score hovers near the line. Severity tiers help too: low-confidence anomalies go to a dashboard or a ticket, high-confidence ones page a person.

The precision arithmetic of ml7 applies with full force. If real anomalies are 1 in 1,000 points and the detector flags 1% of normal points, there are about 10 false alerts for each real one: precision under 10% even with perfect recall. Lowering the false-positive rate tenfold matters far more than raising recall a few points.

5. Change points: CUSUM, worked

A change point is a time after which a series behaves differently and keeps doing so: a new level, a new variance, a new seasonal pattern. Detecting it online — as data arrives — is a different problem from detecting spikes, because each point after a small shift may look only slightly unusual. The classic tool is CUSUM (cumulative sum), from industrial quality control in the 1950s. To detect an upward shift from a target mean μ0, keep a running statistic

S_t = max(0, S_{t−1} + (x_t − μ0 − k))      alarm when S_t > h

The allowance k (often half the size of the shift you care about, in the series' units) stops ordinary noise from accumulating; the threshold h sets the trade-off between detection delay and false alarms. A mirror-image statistic catches downward shifts.

With μ0 = 50, k = 1, h = 5 and readings 51, 49, 50, 52, 54, 55, 53, 56: the increments x − 51 are 0, −2, −1, 1, 3, 4, 2, 5. S goes 0, 0 (floored), 0, 1, 4, 8 — exceeding 5 at the sixth reading — then 10, 15. The level shifted to about 54 at the fifth reading, so CUSUM signalled one reading later, despite no single value being extreme (55 is only about three standard deviations above 50 if σ ≈ 1.6). CUSUM is optimal in a precise sense for detecting a known shift size quickly at a given false-alarm rate, which is why it remains standard.

6. Offline change-point detection

When the whole history is available — for example, to find when a metric's behaviour changed over the past year, or to segment a series before modelling — offline methods look for the set of change points that best explains the data. The framework: choose a cost function for a segment (for a change in mean, the sum of squared deviations from the segment's mean), and find the segmentation that minimises the total cost plus a penalty per change point. Without the penalty, every point would be its own segment. A common penalty is proportional to log(n) per change, as in the Bayesian information criterion.

Binary segmentation finds the single best split, then recursively splits each side; it is fast but approximate. PELT (Pruned Exact Linear Time, Killick and colleagues, 2012) finds the exact optimum by dynamic programming, pruning candidate split positions that can never be optimal, and runs in roughly linear time in practice. Python's ruptures library implements these with costs for changes in mean, variance, linear trend and more general distributions. Bayesian online change-point detection (Adams and MacKay, 2007) is a popular online alternative that maintains a probability distribution over "how long since the last change".

7. Learned detectors

When normal behaviour is too complex for profiles and decompositions, learned models take over the "predict" step. Forecasting-based detection uses any forecaster from ts4-ts9 and its prediction intervals: an observation outside the 99.9% interval is anomalous. Reconstruction-based detection trains an autoencoder (dl10) on normal windows; at test time, windows the model reconstructs badly are unlike what it learned. Isolation forests build random trees on window features; points that get isolated in few random splits are unusual. For multivariate data — many sensors on one machine, many metrics of one service — these methods can catch combinations that are individually normal but jointly odd, such as high CPU with low traffic.

For collective anomalies, a distance-based tool is the matrix profile: for every subsequence of a given length, the distance to its nearest neighbour elsewhere in the series. A subsequence whose nearest neighbour is far away — a discord — is a shape that happens nowhere else. Efficient algorithms compute it for millions of points, and it is a strong, almost parameter-free baseline that Keogh's group has long advocated.

8. Evaluating detectors honestly

Evaluation is where anomaly detection research has gone most wrong. Labels are scarce and fuzzy (when exactly does an incident start?), anomalies are rare, and metrics can be generous. The most important trap is point adjustment (PA). Many papers, following an influential 2018 paper, counted a whole labelled anomaly segment as detected if any single point in it was flagged, and then computed point-wise F1. Long segments are then easy to hit by chance. Kim and colleagues (AAAI 2022) showed theoretically and experimentally that under PA a random anomaly score can match or beat state-of-the-art methods, and that even an untrained model was competitive without PA. The lab reproduces the effect: on a series with 20 anomalous segments of 100 points, flagging 1% of points at random gives F1 = 0.018 normally and 0.845 after point adjustment.

Better practice: report point-wise metrics without adjustment, or event-level metrics that count each incident once with a penalty for detection delay (how many minutes after onset the first alert fired), or PA%K, which credits a segment only if at least K% of its points are flagged. Report the false-alarm rate per day, which is what on-call engineers feel. Compare against simple baselines — a robust z on seasonal residuals, a matrix-profile discord — because, as the next section shows, simple baselines often win.

9. Time-series classification in brief

Classification assigns a label to a whole series or window: arrhythmia or normal for an ECG beat, an activity for an accelerometer trace, a fault type for a vibration window. Three families of strong methods exist. Distance-based: 1-nearest-neighbour with dynamic time warping (DTW), which aligns two sequences by stretching and compressing time (found by dynamic programming over a cost matrix) before measuring distance; for years it was the benchmark to beat on the UCR archive of classification datasets. Feature-based: compute summary features (spectral power, autocorrelations, shapelets — short discriminative subsequences) and train a standard classifier such as gradient boosting. Random-kernel: ROCKET (Dempster, Petitjean and Webb, 2020) convolves each series with 10,000 random kernels of random length, dilation and bias, takes two features per kernel (the maximum and the proportion of positive values), and trains a linear classifier; it matched the most accurate methods of its time at a tiny fraction of their training cost, and its successors (MiniRocket, MultiRocket) are standard baselines. Deep networks (1D CNNs, InceptionTime, transformers) do well with plenty of data.

Two evaluation rules matter. Overlapping windows from the same recording must not be split across train and test, or the test set leaks — split by recording, subject or machine. And class imbalance (rare faults) needs per-class precision and recall rather than accuracy.

10. Multivariate detection, worked

Some anomalies only show up in combination. Suppose a service's CPU usage and request rate are normally strongly correlated: from history, CPU averages 50% with standard deviation 10, requests average 1,000 per second with standard deviation 200, and their correlation is 0.9. A reading of CPU 70% with 1,000 requests per second has univariate z-scores of 2.0 (CPU) and 0.0 (requests) — neither alarming alone. But CPU two standard deviations high while traffic is exactly average is very unusual given the 0.9 correlation: normally, high CPU comes with high traffic. The Mahalanobis distance measures distance from the mean taking correlation into account: for two standardised variables with correlation ρ, its square is (z₁² − 2ρz₁z₂ + z₂²)/(1 − ρ²). Here that is (4 − 0 + 0)/(1 − 0.81) = 4/0.19 ≈ 21.1, a distance of about 4.6 — far outside the usual range (for two normal variables, the squared distance exceeds 13.8 only 0.1% of the time). The joint view catches a probable runaway process that the per-metric views missed. Learned detectors such as autoencoders and isolation forests generalise this idea to many variables and nonlinear relationships.

11. The delay–false-alarm trade-off

Every online detector trades how quickly it notices a real change against how often it fires falsely. Quality-control engineers summarise this with the average run length (ARL): ARL₀ is the average number of observations between false alarms when nothing has changed, ARL₁ the average delay to detect a real shift of a given size. Raising CUSUM's threshold h lengthens both; raising the allowance k makes it ignore small shifts but react faster to large ones. A useful way to set them is to start from the false-alarm budget (for minute data and at most one false alarm a week, ARL₀ must be at least about 10,000 minutes), then choose the k that minimises delay for the smallest shift you care about. The same trade-off governs persistence rules for spike detectors: requiring three consecutive points above threshold cuts false alarms enormously but adds two minutes of delay.

12. One metric, end to end

Putting it together for a single metric — say, checkout error rate sampled each minute. Baseline: the median for the same hour-of-week over the last six weeks, excluding flagged incidents and maintenance windows. Noise model: because it is a ratio, its expected spread depends on traffic; use a binomial-style standard error √(p(1 − p)/n) from the baseline rate p and the current minute's request count n, or a robust spread of historical residuals scaled by √(1/n). Scores: the residual divided by that spread. Rules: page when the score exceeds 5 for three consecutive minutes; open a ticket when CUSUM on the scores crosses its threshold (a sustained moderate rise); suppress during declared maintenance. Feedback: every page is marked useful or not by the responder; every incident found another way is recorded as a miss; thresholds are reviewed monthly against these records. Nothing in this pipeline is sophisticated, and that is the point: most of the value comes from a good baseline, an honest noise model and a disciplined threshold.

Why anomaly detection is fundamentally hard to evaluate

Three properties make evaluation harder than for forecasting. Ground truth is incomplete: unlabelled real anomalies count as false positives, and labelled "anomalies" are sometimes just what the labeller noticed. Ground truth is fuzzy in time: an incident's start and end are judgement calls, which makes point-wise metrics arbitrary at the edges. And the objective is decision-dependent: an alert five minutes late may be worthless for fraud and fine for capacity. Mature teams therefore evaluate on curated incident sets with explicit onset times, use event-level recall with delay distributions, measure false alarms per day, and supplement offline metrics with on-call feedback ("was this alert useful?") collected in production.

Choosing the baseline window

Robust scores need a reference window, and its choice is a bias-variance trade-off. Short windows adapt quickly to new normals but are noisy and quickly contaminated by an ongoing incident (the incident becomes the new normal). Long windows are stable but slow to adapt after a legitimate change. Seasonal metrics need same-context windows (the same hour on previous days, the same hour-of-week on previous weeks). A common production design uses a long seasonal baseline for "normal for this time" and a separate change-point detector that, when it fires, resets the baseline to post-change data.

Multiple testing at scale

With 50,000 metrics each tested every minute, the number of tests per day is in the tens of millions; any fixed per-test threshold produces many false alarms in absolute terms. Ideas from multiple-hypothesis testing help: control the false discovery rate across metrics rather than the per-metric error rate, require corroboration across related metrics (the same service's latency and error rate), and aggregate alerts into incidents by time and topology. Grouping is often the biggest practical win: one incident producing 300 correlated alerts should page once.

Adversarial and drifting anomalies

In fraud and abuse, the "anomalies" are people who adapt. Once a pattern is blocked, attackers change it; a detector trained on last month's fraud misses this month's. This pushes toward detectors of unusualness relative to a user's or entity's own history (contextual by entity), toward rapid label feedback loops from investigators, and toward combining unsupervised scores with supervised classifiers trained on confirmed cases. It also means the evaluation set must be refreshed, or scores reflect a threat that no longer exists.

Change points versus regime models

Change-point detection assumes behaviour is piecewise stable. Some series instead switch back and forth between a few regimes — calm and volatile markets, normal and degraded service modes. Hidden Markov models and regime-switching models describe these with a small number of states and transition probabilities, giving the probability of being in each regime at each time. They are useful when regimes recur, while change-point methods suit one-way shifts such as deploys and policy changes.

Bayesian online change-point detection, intuitively

Adams and MacKay's method keeps, at every time step, a probability distribution over the run length — how many steps since the last change point. When a new observation arrives, each possible run length either grows by one (no change, and the observation is judged under that segment's predictive distribution) or resets to zero (a change, judged under a fresh prior). Observations that fit the current segment keep probability on long run lengths; a surprising run of observations shifts probability to short run lengths, which is the signal of a change. Its advantages are a calibrated probability of change rather than a raw statistic, natural handling of uncertainty in the segment's parameters, and no need to specify the shift size in advance; its costs are a prior (the expected time between changes) and more computation than CUSUM.

Seasonality inside change detection

Running CUSUM directly on a seasonal metric fails: the daily peak looks like a shift every afternoon. The fix is to run change detection on residuals from the seasonal baseline (or on standardised scores), so that "normal for this hour" is removed first. Conversely, a change in the seasonal pattern itself — the peak moving from 2 pm to 11 am after a product change — shows up as residuals that are positive in the morning and negative in the afternoon, which a mean-shift detector misses. Detecting such pattern changes needs a cost function over the whole seasonal profile, or a comparison of profiles estimated from recent and older windows.

LLMs and foundation models in detection

Time-series foundation models (ts9) provide zero-shot forecasts with intervals, which make convenient baselines for forecast-based detection on many heterogeneous metrics. Language models help mostly after detection: summarising a group of alerts, correlating them with recent deploys and change logs, drafting incident reports, and answering "what changed?" with links to evidence. Asking an LLM to judge whether a raw series is anomalous is unreliable and expensive compared with the statistical methods above, and its judgements need the same evaluation discipline as any detector.

The case: flawed benchmarks and generous metrics

In 2020 Renjie Wu and Eamonn Keogh (University of California, Riverside) examined the benchmark datasets most cited in time-series anomaly-detection papers — from Yahoo, Numenta, NASA and Pei's lab (OMNI) — and reported, in a paper later published in IEEE Transactions on Knowledge and Data Engineering, that most individual series suffer from at least one of four flaws. Triviality: the anomalies can be found by a "one-liner", a single line of code using basic operations such as a difference and a moving average with a threshold; for the Yahoo benchmark they showed 316 of its 367 series (86.1%) were solvable this way. Unrealistic anomaly density: some series have many anomalies or anomalies covering large fractions of the data, which blurs the line between anomaly and normal. Mislabelled ground truth: examples where labelled anomalies look no different from unlabelled regions, or obvious anomalies are unlabelled. Run-to-failure bias: anomalies tend to sit near the end of series, so a detector that simply favours late points scores well. Their conclusion was that much of the apparent progress in recent years "may be illusionary", and they released the UCR Time Series Anomaly Archive, whose series each contain one carefully placed anomaly.

A year later Siwon Kim and colleagues (Seoul National University and others, AAAI 2022) attacked the metric side. Most deep-learning detection papers since 2018 had reported F1 after point adjustment. Kim et al. showed that point adjustment can make a random anomaly score look state of the art, that rankings of methods under it are misleading, and that an untrained model achieved detection performance comparable to existing methods even without point adjustment. They proposed PA%K as a stricter alternative. Together, the two papers are the standard warning in this field: check that the benchmark is hard and the metric is honest before believing a reported improvement — and always include a simple baseline.

How detection fails in production

Alert storms. One incident triggers hundreds of correlated alerts across dependent services; responders drown. Group by topology and time, and page on incidents, not metrics.

Silent detectors. A metric stops reporting (a broken exporter) and the detector, seeing no data, raises nothing. Monitor data freshness and volume as first-class signals; absence of data is an anomaly.

Learning the incident as normal. A detector that retrains continuously on recent data absorbs a slow degradation into its baseline, and alerts stop. Exclude flagged periods from baselines, and use change-point detection to make baseline resets deliberate.

Seasonal surprises. The first holiday after launch, a daylight-saving change or a quarter-end batch job produces a burst of false alerts because the baseline never saw it. Holiday calendars and maintenance windows belong in the detector, as they do in forecasters.

Rolling out a new detector safely

Replacing an alerting rule is itself a risky change, because a detector that is worse in some corner will only reveal it during an incident. The standard practice is a shadow period: run the new detector alongside the old one for several weeks, sending its alerts to a log rather than to people. At the end, compare the two on the same timeline — which incidents each caught and how early, how many alerts each raised, and which of the new detector's extra alerts a responder would have found useful. Only then switch paging over, and keep the old rule in shadow for a while in reverse, so a regression can be spotted. Pair this with replay testing: run the new detector over the data from the last dozen real incidents and confirm it would have fired, and over a few quiet weeks to estimate its false-alarm rate. These steps turn "the new model has better F1 on a benchmark" into "the new detector would have woken us up 6 minutes earlier on 9 of the last 10 incidents with a third fewer false pages", which is the claim an operations team needs.

What to monitor about the monitor

Alerts per day per team and the fraction acknowledged as useful; detection delay on confirmed incidents; incidents found by humans or customers before the detector (missed detections); the share of metrics with stale baselines; and threshold changes over time, which should be reviewed like code changes.

# 1. The worked z-score example
x = np.array([50, 52, 49, 51, 48, 50, 53, 49, 51, 70.0])
base = x[:9]
z = (x[-1] - base.mean()) / base.std(ddof=1)
mad = np.median(np.abs(base - np.median(base)))
rz = (x[-1] - np.median(base)) / (1.4826 * mad)
print(f"mean {base.mean():.2f} sd {base.std(ddof=1):.2f} z {z:.1f} | median {np.median(base):.0f} MAD {mad:.0f} robust z {rz:.1f}")
# masking: an earlier outlier in the baseline inflates the sd and hides the new one
base2 = base.copy(); base2[3] = 90
print(f"with an earlier 90 in the window: z {(70 - base2.mean()) / base2.std(ddof=1):.1f}, "
      f"robust z {(70 - np.median(base2)) / (1.4826 * np.median(np.abs(base2 - np.median(base2)))):.1f}")

# 2. The worked CUSUM example: target 50, allowance k = 1, alarm threshold h = 5
S, path = 0.0, []
for v in [51, 49, 50, 52, 54, 55, 53, 56]:
    S = max(0.0, S + (v - 50 - 1)); path.append(S)
print("CUSUM:", path, "-> alarm at step", next(i + 1 for i, s in enumerate(path) if s > 5))

# 3. Contextual anomalies: a raw threshold vs a seasonal residual on hourly-like data
rng = np.random.default_rng(10)
n = 24 * 28
t = np.arange(n)
y = 100 + 40 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * t / 24) + rng.normal(0, 4, n)
truth = np.zeros(n, bool)
for s in (210, 402, 570):            # three injected anomalies: +30 at the daily low (contextual)
    y[s] += 30; truth[s] = True
raw_flag = y > np.quantile(y, 0.99)  # 'alert above the 99th percentile'
profile = np.array([np.median(y[t % 24 == h]) for h in range(24)])   # hour-of-day baseline
resid = y - profile[t % 24]
rmad = 1.4826 * np.median(np.abs(resid - np.median(resid)))
res_flag = np.abs(resid) / rmad > 4
def pr(flag):
    tp = (flag & truth).sum()
    return f"precision {tp / max(flag.sum(), 1):.2f} recall {tp / truth.sum():.2f} ({flag.sum()} alerts)"
print("\nraw 99th-percentile threshold:", pr(raw_flag))
print("seasonal residual, robust z>4:", pr(res_flag))

# 4. Point adjustment (PA) makes random scores look good
truth = np.zeros(10_000, bool)
for s in rng.choice(9_000, 20, replace=False):
    truth[s:s + 100] = True                       # 20 anomaly segments of length 100
def f1(pred):
    tp = (pred & truth).sum(); fp = (pred & ~truth).sum(); fn = (~pred & truth).sum()
    return 2 * tp / (2 * tp + fp + fn)
def point_adjust(pred):
    out = pred.copy(); i = 0
    while i < len(truth):
        if truth[i]:
            j = i
            while j < len(truth) and truth[j]: j += 1
            if pred[i:j].any(): out[i:j] = True       # one hit marks the whole segment detected
            i = j
        else:
            i += 1
    return out
random_pred = rng.random(10_000) > 0.99            # flag 1% of points at random
print(f"\nrandom scores: F1 {f1(random_pred):.3f}   F1 after point adjustment {f1(point_adjust(random_pred)):.3f}")

Work it out

A payments service's error rate is sampled every minute. Over the last 30 days, the hour-of-week median at 14:00 on Tuesdays is 0.20% with a robust standard deviation (1.4826 × MAD) of 0.05 percentage points. The team wants at most one false page per week from this metric, and real incidents happen about twice a month.

(a) Today at Tuesday 14:00 the error rate is 0.42%. What is its robust z-score? (b) A rule of "page if robust z > 3 for one minute" is proposed. Estimate the false pages per week, assuming normal noise. (c) Propose a better rule. (d) After a deploy, the rate settles at 0.30% and stays there. Which detector should notice, and what should happen next?

Answer

(a) (0.42 − 0.20)/0.05 = 4.4.

(b) P(z > 3) ≈ 0.135% for one-sided normal noise. A week has 10,080 minutes, so about 0.00135 × 10,080 ≈ 14 false pages per week — fourteen times the budget, before accounting for heavier-than-normal tails.

(c) Raise the threshold and require persistence: e.g. robust z > 4 for 3 consecutive minutes. Under independent normal noise, P(z > 4) ≈ 3.2 × 10⁻⁵ per minute; three in a row is vanishingly rare, so false pages come mainly from real but harmless blips — validate the rate on the last 30 days of data and tune to one per week. Real incidents (sustained errors) still trigger within a few minutes. Consider a lower-severity ticket for z > 3 for 5 minutes.

(d) A shift from 0.20% to 0.30% is a robust z of 2 — too small for the spike rule, but CUSUM on the residuals (or a change-point detector) will accumulate it within tens of minutes. The right response is an investigation of the deploy (a 50% increase in errors is significant), and if the new level is accepted, a deliberate baseline reset so the spike detector measures deviations from the new normal.
