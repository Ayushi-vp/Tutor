A supermarket chain's planning meeting goes badly. The national team's forecast says next month's sales will be 41 million; the regional managers' forecasts add up to 38.5 million; and the store-level forecasts that drive ordering add up to 36.9 million. Each was produced by a competent model at its own level. Nobody can say which is right, finance plans to one number, logistics to another and the stores order to a third. The forecasts are each reasonable and collectively incoherent.

Business forecasts nearly always come in hierarchies — product, category, department, total; store, region, country; and often both at once — and different decisions use different levels. The M5 competition (ts1, ts6) had 12 levels and scored every one. Making the levels agree while using the information in all of them is called reconciliation, and its best methods do more than tidy up: by pooling evidence across levels they make forecasts more accurate.

The same meeting raises two more questions. Logistics notes that sales in a region seem to move a week after web traffic does, and asks whether to use web traffic to forecast sales — a question about multivariate series and about what "X drives Y" can mean when all you have is time series. And marketing claims last month's TV campaign lifted sales by 12%. It ran nationally, so there was no control group; was the lift real, or would sales have risen anyway? That is a causal question, and forecasting is the tool for answering it: estimate what would have happened without the campaign — the counterfactual — and compare.

These questions arrive constantly in AI engineering. Token and GPU budgets form hierarchies — organisation, team, product, model — and finance wants forecasts that add up at every level. Model launches are interventions: did the new model version raise engagement, or did engagement rise because of a marketing push the same week? Many launches cannot be A/B tested (a model change that affects shared infrastructure, a pricing change announced publicly), so counterfactual methods are how their effects get measured. Interviewers ask about these because they separate engineers who can produce a number from those who can defend it.

This lesson covers all three. It builds the summing matrix that describes a hierarchy, works bottom-up, top-down and reconciliation by hand on a two-level example, explains MinT, introduces vector autoregressions and Granger causality (and shows how a hidden driver fakes it), and walks from before-and-after comparisons through difference-in-differences and synthetic controls to Google's CausalImpact, whose observational estimate of an ad campaign's effect matched a randomised experiment to within about 1%.

A family budget

Think of a household budget. The family plans a monthly total for food, and separately each person estimates what they will spend on their own meals. The individual estimates almost never add up to the family total: one person is optimistic, another forgets about birthdays. The family has three options. Trust the individuals and add them up (bottom-up). Trust the family total and split it by last year's shares (top-down). Or sit down with all the numbers, remember who usually over- or under-estimates, and agree on a set of figures that adds up and leans toward whoever has been more reliable (reconciliation). The third option uses all the information, and it is the one forecasting has formalised.

The analogy breaks in a useful place: in a family, people talk, so the individual estimates already contain some knowledge of the total. In forecasting systems, models at different levels are usually built independently, and their errors are related in ways that are only visible from data — which is exactly what the best reconciliation method, MinT, estimates.

When series influence each other

Some groups of series are not just sums of each other but cause or anticipate each other: web visits precede sales, CPU load precedes latency, a supplier's delays precede your stock-outs. Multivariate models such as the vector autoregression let each series' forecast use the others' history. The tempting next step — "web visits predict sales, so web visits cause sales" — is where care is needed: predictability is not causation.

When you need to know what an action did

The most valuable questions are causal: did the campaign work, did the price change reduce demand, did the new model version increase engagement? With a randomised experiment (math7), the answer is a comparison of groups. Without one, the method is to forecast the world without the action — using information that the action did not touch — and compare it with what happened.

There is a further reason reconciliation matters beyond tidiness. Each level of a hierarchy is good at seeing different things. The national total sees broad effects clearly — the economy, a national holiday, a nationwide campaign — because local noise cancels out when stores are added. A single store sees local effects that are invisible in the total — a road closure, a new competitor across the street. Bottom-up throws away the total's clear view of broad effects; top-down throws away the stores' local knowledge. Reconciliation is how both views end up in the same set of numbers.

The causal part of the lesson rests on one idea that is worth fixing in mind now: an effect is always a comparison between what happened and what would have happened otherwise. Every method — experiments, difference-in-differences, synthetic controls, CausalImpact — is a different way of estimating that "otherwise", and each is only as credible as the assumptions that make its estimate of "otherwise" trustworthy.

A reader who stops here should remember: make forecasts coherent by reconciling with information from all levels; treat Granger causality as a statement about prediction; and answer causal questions with an explicit, testable counterfactual.

1. Hierarchical and grouped structure

A hierarchical time series is a collection of series in which each parent is the sum of its children: stores sum to regions, regions to the country. A grouped structure has several crossing ways to aggregate: product categories and regions both sum to the total, and the product-by-region cells are the bottom level. M5 combined both: 3,049 products in 10 stores could be summed by store, state, category, department and their combinations, giving 12 levels and 42,840 series in total from 30,490 bottom-level series.

The structure is captured by the summing matrix S. Stack all series into a vector y_t (total first, then middle levels, then bottom) and the bottom-level series into b_t; then y_t = S b_t. For the two-level example with Total = A + B:

        [Total]   [1 1]
y_t  =  [  A  ] = [1 0]  ×  [A]
        [  B  ]   [0 1]     [B]

Each row of S says which bottom series add up to that node. Forecasts ỹ are coherent if ỹ = S b̃ for some bottom-level vector b̃ — that is, if they add up.

2. Bottom-up, top-down and middle-out, worked

Suppose independent models produce base forecasts for next month: Total 110, A 60, B 40. They are incoherent, since 60 + 40 = 100.

Bottom-up keeps the bottom forecasts and sums them: A 60, B 40, Total 100. It uses no information from the total's model. It shines when bottom series are well-behaved and differences between them matter, and suffers when bottom series are noisy or intermittent.

Top-down keeps the total and splits it by proportions. With historical shares of 60% and 40%: Total 110, A 66, B 44. Proportions can be historical averages of shares, shares of historical averages, or forecast proportions (the ratio of the children's forecasts, here 60/100 and 40/100, which gives the same 66 and 44). Top-down is stable and good at capturing aggregate patterns, but it cannot represent a child whose share is changing — if A is growing and B shrinking, fixed historical shares are wrong.

Middle-out picks a middle level (say, regions in a three-level hierarchy), forecasts it, sums upward and splits downward. It is a compromise chosen when the middle level is the most forecastable.

3. Reconciliation as a projection, worked

Optimal reconciliation treats the problem as: find coherent forecasts as close as possible to all the base forecasts. Every reconciliation method of this family can be written as

ỹ = S G ŷ     with   G = (Sᵀ W⁻¹ S)⁻¹ Sᵀ W⁻¹

G maps all base forecasts to a bottom-level estimate, and S sums it back up. W says how much to trust each base forecast. Taking W as the identity gives OLS reconciliation, the coherent vector nearest in squared distance. For our example: SᵀS = [[2, 1], [1, 2]], whose inverse is (1/3)[[2, −1], [−1, 2]]; Sᵀŷ = (110 + 60, 110 + 40) = (170, 150). So the bottom estimates are (1/3)(2 × 170 − 150, −170 + 2 × 150) = (1/3)(190, 130) = (63.33, 43.33), and the total is 106.67. The discrepancy of 10 has been split equally across the three nodes: the total moved down 3.33, each child up 3.33.

Equal splitting is right only if every base forecast is equally reliable. If the total's model is noisier — say its error variance is 25 while each child's is 9 — weighted least squares with W = diag(25, 9, 9) trusts the total less. The lab computes the result: Total 104.19, A 62.09, B 42.09, closer to bottom-up. If instead the children were much noisier than the total, the result would move toward top-down. Reconciliation interpolates between the simple methods according to evidence about reliability.

4. MinT: estimating W from errors

The question is how to choose W. Wickramasuriya, Athanasopoulos and Hyndman (2019) showed that if W is the covariance matrix of the base-forecast errors, the reconciled forecasts are unbiased (when the base forecasts are) and have the smallest possible total error variance — the smallest trace of the reconciled error covariance, hence MinT (minimum trace). In practice W is estimated from the base models' in-sample one-step errors. Estimating a full covariance for thousands of series is impossible (it has millions of entries), so the standard estimator shrinks the sample covariance toward its diagonal, with the shrinkage intensity chosen from the data (MinT-shrink). Simpler variants use only the diagonal (WLS with each series' error variance) or structural weights proportional to the number of bottom series under each node.

MinT's power comes from correlations as well as variances: if the errors of A and B tend to move together (both over-forecast when the economy weakens), the total's forecast carries information about both. Across many studies, MinT reconciliation improves accuracy at most levels compared with base forecasts and with bottom-up or top-down — reconciliation is not just a tidying step but a way to pool evidence.

5. Probabilistic and temporal reconciliation

Point reconciliation does not tell you how to reconcile intervals. Quantiles do not add (ts8), so reconciling each quantile separately is wrong. The sound approach reconciles joint sample paths: generate samples of the base forecasts that preserve their correlations (or bottom-level paths with realistic dependence), apply the reconciliation to each sample, and read intervals from the reconciled samples. Libraries such as HierarchicalForecast implement this.

Hierarchies also exist in time: daily forecasts should add up to weekly, weekly to monthly. Temporal hierarchies forecast the same series at several frequencies and reconcile across them, combining the daily model's detail with the monthly model's stable trend. This often improves accuracy at every frequency, for the same pooling reason as cross-sectional reconciliation.

6. Vector autoregression, worked

When series influence each other, a vector autoregression (VAR) forecasts them jointly. A VAR(1) for two series x and y is

x_t = c₁ + a₁₁ x_{t−1} + a₁₂ y_{t−1} + e₁t
y_t = c₂ + a₂₁ x_{t−1} + a₂₂ y_{t−1} + e₂t

Each equation is an ordinary regression on lags of both series, fitted by least squares. Suppose x is web visits (thousands) and y is sales (hundreds), with fitted coefficients c₁ = 2, a₁₁ = 0.5, a₁₂ = 0 and c₂ = 1, a₂₁ = 0.6, a₂₂ = 0.3, and today x = 10, y = 9. Tomorrow's forecasts are x = 2 + 0.5 × 10 = 7 and y = 1 + 0.6 × 10 + 0.3 × 9 = 9.7. The day after, using those forecasts, x = 2 + 3.5 = 5.5 and y = 1 + 0.6 × 7 + 0.3 × 9.7 = 8.11. Sales respond to yesterday's visits, so a forecast of falling visits produces a forecast of falling sales a day later — something a univariate sales model cannot know.

VARs need stationary inputs (difference first, ts3, or use an error-correction form when series are cointegrated), and they grow quickly: k series with p lags need k²p coefficients. For a handful of series they are a classic tool, with impulse responses describing how a shock to one series propagates to the others over time. For thousands of related series, global ML models (ts6) with other series' lags as features, or neural models with channel mixing (ts7), take over.

7. Granger causality, worked and debunked

Clive Granger proposed a precise, testable notion: X Granger-causes Y if past values of X improve forecasts of Y beyond what Y's own past provides. The test fits two regressions for Y — one on Y's own lags, one adding X's lags — and compares their residual sums of squares with an F-statistic: F = ((RSS₀ − RSS₁)/p) / (RSS₁/(n − k)), where p is the number of added lags and k the number of parameters in the larger model. In the lab, Y is generated to depend on yesterday's X; the F-statistic for X → Y is 234 and for Y → X is 0.1, against a 5% critical value of about 3.9. The test finds the true direction.

Then the lab builds two series, a and b, that do not affect each other at all; both are driven by a hidden variable, which reaches a one step before it reaches b. The F-statistic for "a Granger-causes b" is 940 — overwhelming — because a's past contains the driver's information before b shows it. Granger causality is about predictive precedence, and it cannot tell a cause from an early messenger. Other traps: non-stationary series give spurious results (difference first), effects faster than the sampling interval appear as contemporaneous correlation rather than Granger causality, and aggregating over time can hide or create apparent precedence. Use it to pick useful predictors and to generate hypotheses, not to make causal claims.

8. Causal questions need counterfactuals

"Did the campaign raise sales?" means: are sales higher than they would have been without the campaign? The second quantity, the counterfactual, is never observed. A randomised experiment estimates it with a control group. Without one, the options form a ladder of increasing sophistication.

Before and after. Compare the campaign period with the period before. This absorbs everything else that changed: seasonality, trend, competitor actions, the economy. A December campaign "works" because December sells more. Almost never acceptable alone.

Difference-in-differences. Find a control group not exposed — regions where the campaign did not run — and compute (treated after − treated before) − (control after − control before). If sales rose 15% in treated regions and 5% in control regions, the estimated effect is about 10 points. It relies on parallel trends: without the campaign, both groups would have changed by the same amount. Check that they moved in parallel for a long time before.

Synthetic control. When no single control resembles the treated unit, build one: choose non-negative weights over many untreated units so that their weighted combination tracks the treated unit closely before the intervention. That weighted combination after the intervention is the counterfactual. Abadie and colleagues developed it to estimate the effects of policies on single regions, such as a state's tobacco tax.

Forecasting with controls (CausalImpact). Model the treated series in the pre-period as a function of its own dynamics plus control series that the intervention cannot affect, then forecast the post-period using the controls' actual post-period values. Actual minus forecast is the effect, with uncertainty from the forecast. This generalises the previous two: it handles trends and seasonality, chooses controls automatically, and gives a time path of the effect.

9. CausalImpact's model

CausalImpact uses a Bayesian structural time-series model: the treated series is a local level (a random walk, capturing slow drift), optionally a trend and seasonal component, plus a regression on the control series. A spike-and-slab prior on the regression coefficients lets the model choose which of many candidate controls matter: each coefficient is exactly zero with some probability (the spike) or drawn from a broad distribution (the slab). Fitted by Markov chain Monte Carlo on the pre-period, the model produces posterior samples of the counterfactual in the post-period, given the controls' actual values. Subtracting gives samples of the pointwise effect, their running sum gives the cumulative effect, and intervals come directly from the samples. A natural consequence is that intervals widen with time after the intervention, as uncertainty about the counterfactual accumulates.

The lab builds a stripped-down version: regress the treated series on one control in the 98-day pre-period, predict the 42-day post-period, and compare. With a true lift of 20%, it estimates 20.1%. Its naive interval is too narrow because it ignores parameter uncertainty and autocorrelated errors — precisely what the full Bayesian model accounts for.

10. Validating a causal estimate

Causal estimates from observational time series rest on untestable assumptions, so validation is about building confidence. Pre-period fit: if the model cannot predict the treated series well before the intervention, its counterfactual after is not credible. Placebo in time: rerun with a fake intervention date in the pre-period; any "effect" found there measures the method's false-positive tendency. Placebo in space: run on units that were not treated; they should show nothing. Sensitivity: vary controls, model components and pre-period length; a robust effect survives. And check the exclusion assumption: could the intervention have affected the controls (a national TV campaign lifts searches for the whole category, contaminating "industry search" controls)? If yes, the effect is biased toward zero.

11. A grouped structure, worked

Real hierarchies often cross. Suppose a company sells two product lines (P and Q) in two regions (N and S). The bottom level has four series: PN, PS, QN, QS. Aggregates include P (= PN + PS), Q, N (= PN + QN), S and the total. The summing matrix has 9 rows (1 total, 2 products, 2 regions, 4 bottom) and 4 columns. The total can be reached two ways — summing products or summing regions — and coherent forecasts must agree with both. Reconciliation handles this automatically: the projection finds bottom-level values whose every aggregate is as close as possible to the corresponding base forecast, weighted by reliability. This matters in practice because different teams own different slices: the product team forecasts P and Q, the regional teams N and S, and without reconciliation the company has two different totals.

12. A synthetic control, worked

Suppose a new delivery service launched in city A in month 7, and we want its effect on orders. Cities B and C did not get the service. Before the launch, monthly orders (thousands) were: A 100, 104, 108, 110, 114, 118; B 80, 82, 86, 88, 90, 94; C 130, 136, 140, 142, 148, 152. No single city matches A, but a weighted combination might. Try weights 0.5 on B and 0.4 on C: 0.5 × 80 + 0.4 × 130 = 92, rising to 0.5 × 94 + 0.4 × 152 = 107.8 — too low. A synthetic control also needs to match A's level, so in practice the weights are chosen by optimisation (often with an intercept, or on de-meaned series) to minimise pre-period error. With weights of about 0.2 on B and 0.68 on C plus no intercept: month 1 = 16 + 88.4 = 104.4, month 6 = 18.8 + 103.4 = 122.2 — tracking A's growth closely but sitting a steady 4 to 5 thousand above it, a level gap that an intercept in the fit would remove. If in months 7-9 A records 135, 139, 144 while the synthetic control, computed from B's and C's actual post-launch values, predicts 125, 128, 131, the estimated effect is about +10, +11, +13 thousand orders a month. The credibility rests on two checks: the pre-period fit (here a constant offset of 4-5 thousand, corrected by the intercept) and that B and C were not affected by the launch (for instance, customers in C did not start using A's service). Real analyses use many more donor cities and months, and placebo tests that apply the method to each donor city in turn.

Why reconciliation improves accuracy, not just coherence

Reconciliation is a form of combination (ts4): each reconciled forecast is a weighted combination of all the base forecasts, because G mixes them. A child's reconciled forecast uses its own base forecast, its sibling's and its parent's. If the parent's forecast is accurate (aggregates are smoother) and the children's are noisy, the parent pulls each child toward a more consistent total; if a child's own model has detected a local change, its weight keeps that information. The MinT result formalises when this is optimal. In practice the gains are largest at the noisy bottom levels, and the key requirement is good base forecasts at each level — reconciliation cannot rescue uniformly bad inputs.

Reconciliation with constraints and non-negativity

Linear reconciliation can produce negative values for small bottom series (a child forecast of 0.3 adjusted down by 0.5). Fixes include setting negatives to zero and re-reconciling, solving the reconciliation as a constrained optimisation with non-negativity, or reconciling in a transformed space. Intermittent bottom levels (ts6) make this common in retail. Another practical constraint is that some levels may be "locked" — a finance-approved total that must not move — which turns into reconciliation with fixed nodes, again a constrained least-squares problem.

Learning the reconciliation end to end

Global ML and deep models can learn coherence directly: train a model on bottom-level series with a loss that includes errors at aggregated levels (as WRMSSE effectively did in M5), or add a reconciliation layer to a neural network so that outputs are coherent by construction. These approaches can capture nonlinear cross-level information, at the cost of interpretability and the clean optimality results of MinT. A sensible baseline for any of them is base forecasts plus MinT-shrink.

Structural VARs and identification

A reduced-form VAR describes correlations among series, including contemporaneous correlation of their shocks. To make causal statements — "a 1% shock to interest rates lowers output by …" — economists impose identifying assumptions on how shocks affect series within a period, such as an ordering (one variable can affect others within the period but not vice versa) or sign restrictions. These are structural VARs. The lesson for ML engineers: causal conclusions from time series always rest on assumptions that the data cannot fully verify, and stating them explicitly is part of the analysis.

Interrupted time series and regression discontinuity in time

When there is no control at all, a weaker design remains: model the series' trend and seasonality before the intervention and test for a level or slope change at the intervention date, including the usual autocorrelation corrections. This interrupted time series design is common in public health (did a policy change hospital admissions?). Its key threat is any other change at the same time; its strength is that the effect must appear sharply at a known date, which is hard for slow confounders to mimic. It is effectively CausalImpact with no control series, so its counterfactual is only as good as the extrapolation of the pre-period pattern.

Experiments on time series

When you can randomise, do — but time-series metrics complicate experiments. Network effects and shared resources mean users or regions are not independent; outcomes are autocorrelated, so standard errors from daily data are too small (ts3); and effects may build or decay over time. Designs used in practice include geo experiments (randomise regions, as in the CausalImpact case's original experiment), switchback experiments (alternate treatment and control in time blocks for marketplace systems such as ride pricing), and staggered rollouts. Analysis often uses exactly the counterfactual tools above, with randomisation making their assumptions far more credible.

Which level should a decision use?

Reconciliation makes all levels consistent, but each decision should still use the level that matches it. Store replenishment needs store-product forecasts; warehouse capacity needs regional totals; the annual budget needs the national total by month. A frequent mistake is to forecast only at the bottom and aggregate, because "the detail is there", which ignores the information available at higher levels; another is to forecast only the total and disaggregate by fixed shares, which ignores shifting mix. Reconciliation across the levels that matter, with evaluation at each of them, avoids both. Agreeing in advance which level is authoritative for each decision also prevents arguments later about whose number is right.

Granger causality in high dimensions

With hundreds of candidate series, pairwise Granger tests produce many false positives (multiple testing) and miss conditional relationships (X predicts Y only given Z). Regularised VARs (lasso on the lag coefficients) select a sparse set of predictive relationships and are used for building "influence graphs" among metrics, for example to suggest root causes in observability data. They inherit all of Granger's caveats; their output is a ranked list of hypotheses for an engineer to check, not a causal graph.

The case: CausalImpact and a Google advertising campaign

Kay Brodersen and colleagues at Google published "Inferring causal impact using Bayesian structural time-series models" in the Annals of Applied Statistics in 2015, along with the open-source CausalImpact R package. Their empirical example is unusually convincing because the truth was known. An advertiser ran search ads for six consecutive weeks, geo-targeted to a randomised set of 95 of the 190 US designated market areas (DMAs) — a randomised geo experiment, whose conventional analysis had estimated a lift of 84,700 clicks (search-related visits, organic plus paid). The authors then asked whether their method, applied as if only one treated region existed, would recover that answer.

In the first analysis, using the untreated DMAs as controls, the model fitted the pre-campaign clicks closely — including a spike two weeks before and a dip just before the campaign — and estimated 88,400 additional clicks, a 22% increase with a 95% credible interval of 13% to 30%: within about 4% of the experimental estimate. In the second, they discarded the control regions entirely and used only purely observational controls — Google Trends search volumes for keywords related to the advertiser's industry — and estimated 85,900 additional clicks, 21% with an interval of 12% to 30%, within about 1% of the experiment. In a third, placebo analysis, applying the method to the untreated regions found a non-significant 2% lift (interval −6% to 10%), as it should. The effect peaked about three weeks into the campaign and faded about a week after it ended, a time profile that a single difference-in-differences number would hide.

The case shows both the promise and the conditions. Industry search volumes worked as controls because they track seasonality and market events but a single advertiser cannot move them; a national campaign by a dominant brand could. The method's estimates are only as good as that exclusion assumption.

An AI-product example: measuring a model upgrade without an A/B test

A team upgrades the model behind a coding assistant for all users at once, because running two models side by side was too expensive. Two weeks later, accepted suggestions per active user are up 9%. Was it the model? The same fortnight included the end of a holiday period (usage always recovers then) and a marketing email. A counterfactual analysis would model the daily acceptance rate before the upgrade as a function of controls the upgrade could not touch: acceptance rates in a product surface still running the old model (if one exists), overall editor activity, and the same calendar weeks last year. Fitted on several months before the upgrade, it predicts the post-upgrade fortnight. If the prediction already includes a 5% seasonal recovery, the estimated model effect is about 4%, with an interval. A placebo run on last year's same dates should find nothing, and the analysis should be repeated with and without the marketing-affected user segments. The honest summary — "about +4% (interval +1% to +7%), assuming the old-model surface was unaffected" — is far more useful to a launch review than the raw +9%, and it tells the team when the next upgrade should get a proper experiment.

How these methods fail in production

Incoherent dashboards. Different teams pull forecasts from different levels and models, and numbers disagree in meetings. Publish reconciled forecasts from one system, with base forecasts kept for diagnosis.

Reconciliation that hurts a level. A poorly estimated W (too little history, structural change) can make a level worse than its base forecast. Backtest reconciled versus base forecasts at every level before switching.

Contaminated controls. A campaign's halo lifts the "control" products or regions, biasing the measured effect toward zero; or a simultaneous competitor action biases it either way. Choose controls by mechanism, not just correlation, and run placebo checks.

Attribution theatre. Every team runs a counterfactual analysis of its own launch, each attributing the same quarter's growth to itself, and the attributed effects sum to more than the total growth. Coordinate analyses, account for overlapping interventions, and prefer experiments for high-stakes decisions.

Tooling and monitoring

HierarchicalForecast (Nixtla) and R's fable provide bottom-up, top-down, middle-out, OLS, WLS and MinT reconciliation, including probabilistic versions. statsmodels provides VAR models, impulse responses and Granger tests. CausalImpact (R, with Python ports), and synthetic-control packages cover counterfactual analysis. In production, monitor coherence violations (should be zero after reconciliation), accuracy per level for base versus reconciled forecasts, and for causal analyses keep a register of interventions with dates, so overlapping effects are visible.

Goal: reconcile a two-level hierarchy four ways, run Granger tests on a true causal pair and on a pair linked only by a hidden driver, and estimate a campaign lift with a stripped-down CausalImpact. NumPy only, gpt_env (Python 3.12). Save as ts11.py.

import numpy as np

# 1. Reconciling a two-level hierarchy: Total = A + B
S = np.array([[1, 1], [1, 0], [0, 1]], float)        # summing matrix: rows Total, A, B
yhat = np.array([110.0, 60.0, 40.0])                 # incoherent base forecasts (60 + 40 != 110)
def reconcile(W):
    Wi = np.linalg.inv(W)
    G = np.linalg.inv(S.T @ Wi @ S) @ S.T @ Wi       # bottom-level estimate from all base forecasts
    return S @ G @ yhat
print("bottom-up :", S @ yhat[1:])
print("top-down  :", S @ (yhat[0] * np.array([0.6, 0.4])), "(historical shares 60/40)")
print("OLS       :", np.round(reconcile(np.eye(3)), 2))
print("WLS (var 25, 9, 9):", np.round(reconcile(np.diag([25.0, 9.0, 9.0])), 2))

# 2. Granger causality: does past X improve the forecast of Y beyond Y's own past?
rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
def granger_F(y, x, p=1):
    n = len(y)
    Y = y[p:]
    own = np.column_stack([np.ones(n - p)] + [y[p - k:n - k] for k in range(1, p + 1)])
    full = np.column_stack([own] + [x[p - k:n - k] for k in range(1, p + 1)])
    rss = lambda A: np.sum((Y - A @ np.linalg.lstsq(A, Y, rcond=None)[0]) ** 2)
    r0, r1 = rss(own), rss(full)
    return ((r0 - r1) / p) / (r1 / (len(Y) - full.shape[1]))
n = 500
x = np.zeros(n); y = np.zeros(n); z = np.zeros(n)
for t in range(1, n):
    x[t] = 0.5 * x[t - 1] + rng.normal()
    y[t] = 0.3 * y[t - 1] + 0.6 * x[t - 1] + rng.normal()     # X drives Y with a one-step lag
print(f"\nF(X -> Y) = {granger_F(y, x):.1f}   F(Y -> X) = {granger_F(x, y):.1f}   (5% critical value ~3.9)")
# a hidden common driver makes two unrelated series 'Granger-cause' each other
drv = np.zeros(n); a = np.zeros(n); b = np.zeros(n)
for t in range(2, n):
    drv[t] = 0.8 * drv[t - 1] + rng.normal()
    a[t] = drv[t - 1] + rng.normal(0, 0.5)                    # a sees the driver one step early
    b[t] = drv[t - 2] + rng.normal(0, 0.5)                    # b sees it two steps later
print(f"common driver: F(a -> b) = {granger_F(b, a):.1f}  although a has no effect on b")

#    use the prediction as the counterfactual after the intervention
T, T0 = 140, 98                                       # 14 weeks, campaign starts in week 15 (day 98)
control = 1000 + 50 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * np.arange(T) / 7) + np.cumsum(rng.normal(0, 5, T))
treated = 0.8 * control + 100 + rng.normal(0, 15, T)
treated[T0:] *= 1.20                                  # true lift: +20% after day 98
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(T), control])
w, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X[:T0], treated[:T0], rcond=None)
resid_sd = np.std(treated[:T0] - X[:T0] @ w, ddof=2)
cf = X[T0:] @ w
lift = treated[T0:].sum() - cf.sum()
se = resid_sd * np.sqrt(T - T0)                      # ignores parameter uncertainty and autocorrelation
print(f"\ncumulative lift {lift:,.0f} ({lift / cf.sum():.1%}), rough 95% interval "
      f"[{(lift - 1.96 * se) / cf.sum():.1%}, {(lift + 1.96 * se) / cf.sum():.1%}]")

What to look for. The reconciliation lines reproduce the worked example and show WLS leaning toward the children when the total is noisier. The Granger test finds the true direction, then finds an even stronger "causal" link where none exists. The causal estimate recovers the true 20% lift; its interval is too narrow because it treats the fitted coefficients as exact and the daily errors as independent.

Work it out

A company sells in two regions, North and South. For next quarter, base forecasts are Total 1,000, North 640, South 420 (in thousands of units). Backtests give base-forecast error standard deviations of 30 (Total), 40 (North) and 30 (South), and errors are roughly uncorrelated.

(a) What are the bottom-up and OLS-reconciled forecasts? (b) Which base forecast should WLS trust most, and in which direction will WLS move the result relative to OLS? (c) Last quarter North ran a campaign; South did not. North's sales rose 18% on the previous quarter and South's 6%; in the four quarters before, both grew about 5% a quarter. Estimate the campaign effect and state the assumption.

Answer

(a) Bottom-up: North 640, South 420, Total 1,060. OLS: the discrepancy is 640 + 420 − 1,000 = 60 (children exceed the total), split equally across three nodes: the total moves up 20 and each child down 20, giving Total 1,020, North 620, South 400 (check: 620 + 400 = 1,020).

(b) Variances are 900 (Total), 1,600 (North), 900 (South). North is least reliable, so WLS moves North more and Total and South less than OLS does. Computed: Total ≈ 1,015.9, North ≈ 611.8, South ≈ 404.1 — North lower than OLS's 620, and Total and South closer to their base values (1,000 and 420) than OLS's 1,020 and 400.

(c) Difference-in-differences: 18% − 6% = about 12 percentage points. The assumption is parallel trends: without the campaign, North would have grown like South (about 6% last quarter). The four prior quarters of similar growth (both about 5%) support it, but anything else that hit North only last quarter (a competitor closing, weather) would be counted as campaign effect. A synthetic control or CausalImpact with several control series would add robustness, and a placebo test on earlier quarters would show how big "effects" look when nothing happened.
