In 2018 the real-estate website Zillow, famous for its "Zestimate" home-value estimates, launched Zillow Offers: it would use its price models to make instant cash offers on homes, buy them, make light repairs and resell them a few months later at a small margin. The business grew fast. By the third quarter of 2021 Zillow was buying thousands of homes a quarter. Then, on 2 November 2021, the company announced it was shutting the business down. It had bought many homes at prices higher than it now expected to sell them for, wrote down about $304 million of inventory, expected another $240 to $265 million of losses on homes it was contracted to buy, and would cut about a quarter of its workforce. Its chief executive said the unpredictability in forecasting home prices far exceeded what the company had anticipated.

Zillow did not lack forecasting talent or data; few companies had more of either for US housing. What failed was the system around the forecasts: how forecast error interacted with thin margins, with a holding period during which the market moved, and with sellers who chose which offers to accept. That is the subject of this final lesson. A forecast in production is not a number in a notebook; it is an input to decisions, produced daily by a pipeline that must be correct about time, robust to bad data, monitored for drift, protected by fallbacks, and designed so that the decisions it drives do not quietly poison the data it learns from.

The same lessons apply to AI engineers who forecast GPU demand, token spend, support volume or product demand. The questions an interviewer asks in a forecasting system-design round are mostly about this system layer: How do you make training data match what was known at forecast time? How does the forecast become an order, a reservation or a staffing plan? How do you know when it has gone wrong? What happens when the model breaks at 2 am? How do overrides, reconciliation and retraining fit in?

This lesson walks through the architecture of a production forecasting system, point-in-time data with a worked as-of join, turning distributions into decisions with the newsvendor rule (worked), monitoring with a tracking signal (worked), retraining, fallbacks and safe rollouts, feedback loops including censored demand and the winner's curse (simulated in the lab), human overrides, where LLMs fit, and the Zillow Offers case read from Zillow's own earnings release. It closes the track by tying together what the earlier lessons built: honest evaluation (ts5), global models (ts6), probabilistic forecasts (ts8), detection (ts10) and reconciliation and causal measurement (ts11), assembled into one system that someone depends on every morning.

A weather service, not a weather model

A national weather service is a good picture of a production forecasting system. Its numerical models are impressive, but the service is much more: a network of stations and satellites feeding data with timestamps and quality checks; a schedule of model runs; post-processing that corrects known model biases; forecasters who adjust outputs when they know something the model does not; products tailored to different users (aviation needs winds aloft, farmers need frost risk); verification that scores every forecast against what happened; and a public that makes decisions — carry an umbrella, cancel a flight — on the outputs. When forecasts go wrong, it is as often a data feed, a missed adjustment or a badly communicated probability as the model itself.

A business forecasting system has the same parts. Data arrives from operational systems with delays and revisions. Features are built as of each forecast date. Models are trained, backtested against the current champion and registered. Scoring runs on a schedule. Post-processing reconciles hierarchies, applies business rules and human overrides. Decision rules turn forecasts into orders, reservations or staffing. Monitoring verifies every forecast and watches for drift.

Two ideas that run through everything

The first is time correctness. Every piece of the system must be honest about what was known when: features as of the origin, data as first published, plans as they stood. Without it, backtests lie and production disappoints. The second is closing the loop. Forecasts drive actions; actions change the data; and the next model learns from data shaped by the last model's decisions. A well-designed system logs decisions with outcomes and watches for the ways this loop can distort learning.

What changes between a notebook and production

In a notebook, data is a clean file, the forecast origin is wherever you split, the model runs once, and success is a number in a table. In production, data arrives late, partially and with corrections; the origin is "now", every night; the model runs thousands of times and must not fail silently; and success is fewer empty shelves, fewer idle GPUs or fewer missed staffing targets. Almost every production problem in this lesson comes from one of these differences. A useful habit is to ask, for each step of a notebook workflow, "what does this step look like at 2 am on a holiday when one data source is late?" The answer usually reveals a missing validation, a fallback, or a monitor.

Interviewers probing forecasting system design are mostly testing whether you have that habit. They will accept a simple model if you can explain how its inputs stay honest about time, how its output becomes a decision, how you would know it is failing and what happens when it does. They will be unimpressed by a sophisticated model whose training data quietly includes the future.

A reader who stops here should hold on to one sentence: a forecasting system is judged by the decisions it improves, so design it from the decision backward, keep every input honest about time, and watch it continuously.

1. Start from the decision

Every production forecasting system should begin with a written answer to four questions, extending ts1's framing. Which decision? Replenishment orders, GPU reservations, call-centre rosters, cash planning. At what granularity and lead time? Store-product-day with a 3-day lead time and a weekly order cycle means the forecasts that matter are for days 3 to 10. What do errors cost? A missed sale's lost margin and goodwill versus an unsold item's waste or holding cost. What constraints apply? Pack sizes, minimum orders, shelf space, capacity limits, budgets. These answers fix the forecast target (which quantile, at which level), the evaluation metric (the decision's cost, not just MASE), and the latency and freshness requirements of the pipeline.

2. Data with two timestamps

The most important design choice in the data layer is to store, for every fact, both when it happened (event time) and when the system learned it (arrival time or "known at"). Sales for Monday may arrive Tuesday at 10:00 and be corrected on Thursday when returns are processed. A promotion may be planned in January for March, then extended in February. Official statistics are revised. If the warehouse keeps only the latest value, the historical record silently becomes "what we know now about then", and any backtest built on it uses information no forecast ever had — the revision leak of ts1 and ts5.

Worked example. Weekly web visits are first published the day after each week ends and revised upward a week later. For the week of 2 March the first figure was 1,000 (known 3 March) and the revision 1,300 (known 10 March); for the week of 9 March, 1,100 (known 10 March) then 1,450 (known 17 March). Today's table holds 1,300 and 1,450. A forecast made on 9 March could only have seen 1,000 for the previous week; one made on 16 March saw the latest figure published by then. An as-of join — for each forecast date, take the most recent row whose known-at time is on or before it — reproduces exactly that, as pandas' merge_asof does in the lab. A model trained on the revised values would learn a relationship with data 30% higher than what it will receive at serving time.

3. Features, training and backtest gates

Features are computed by one code path for training, backtesting and serving, from the point-in-time data, ideally through a feature store that records each feature value with its timestamp. Training produces candidate models (a new architecture, new features, or simply this week's retrain). Before any candidate can replace the production champion, it must pass a backtest gate: a rolling-origin backtest (ts5) on recent origins, scored on the agreed metrics by horizon and segment, compared with the champion and with seasonal-naive. Typical gate rules: no worse overall, no segment worse by more than a set tolerance, interval coverage within bounds, no increase in forecast revisions beyond a limit. Passing models go to a model registry with their training data snapshot, code version, parameters and backtest report, so any forecast can later be traced to exactly what produced it.

4. Scoring, validation and fallbacks

Most business forecasts are produced by batch scoring: a scheduled job (nightly, weekly) forecasts every series and writes results to a table that downstream systems read. Before scoring, the job validates inputs: did today's sales arrive for all stores? Are row counts, null rates and zero rates within normal ranges? Did any series' last value jump implausibly? Series that fail validation, series too short for the main model, and any batch where the model errors get a fallback forecast — seasonal-naive or a quick ETS — and are flagged, so planners know which forecasts are degraded. Fallbacks should themselves be backtested; a fallback that is much worse than expected is a liability.

Online (request-time) forecasting is rarer but appears in interactive planning tools and agent tools, where users ask for forecasts of arbitrary series. There, latency limits model choice (ts9's foundation models are a natural fit), and caching and input validation matter more.

5. Post-processing: reconciliation, rules and overrides

Raw model outputs are rarely published as they are. Reconciliation (ts11) makes forecasts coherent across product and location hierarchies, and across time (daily forecasts that sum to the weekly). Business rules apply known constraints: no sales on days a store is closed, caps from capacity, minimum forecasts for new products. Overrides let planners adjust forecasts using information the model lacks. Every override should be logged with who, when, how much and why (a reason code), and evaluated: forecast value added compares the overridden forecast with the model's on the same metric. Research on judgemental adjustments has found that large adjustments based on specific information often help, while small frequent tweaks tend to add noise and optimistic bias. The aim is to learn from overrides — turning a recurring reason ("local festival") into a model feature — and to discourage the ones that hurt.

6. From distributions to decisions: the newsvendor, worked

A bakery sells a pastry for 3.50; it costs 1.00 to make and is thrown away if unsold. Each pastry short costs the lost margin, c_u = 3.50 − 1.00 = 2.50; each pastry over costs its making, c_o = 1.00. The order quantity that maximises expected profit is the demand quantile at the critical ratio c_u/(c_u + c_o) = 2.50/3.50 ≈ 0.714. The intuition: increasing the order by one pastry earns 2.50 if demand exceeds the order (probability 1 − F(q)) and loses 1.00 otherwise (probability F(q)); it is worth doing until 2.50(1 − F) = 1.00 F, that is, F(q) = 2.5/3.5. In the lab, with uncertain demand averaging about 80, the 71.4th percentile is 90 pastries, for an expected daily profit of 175.3, against 172.1 for baking the mean of 80 — a 2% gain from using the distribution correctly, with no change to the forecast model at all.

Real decisions add structure — multi-period inventory with lead times, review cycles, pack sizes, shelf life, substitution between products — but the principle holds: the forecast system should deliver the quantities each decision needs (often several quantiles, or sample paths for multi-period problems), and the decision rule should be explicit, versioned and evaluated on realised costs. This also tells you what to train: a pinball loss at the critical ratio aligns the model's objective with the decision's.

7. Monitoring, and the tracking signal worked

Monitoring a forecasting system means watching five things continuously. Accuracy by horizon and segment (MASE, WAPE) against the seasonal-naive baseline running alongside. Bias, separately from accuracy, because bias is the earliest symptom of a changed world. Calibration: coverage of each interval level (ts8). Inputs: freshness, completeness, distributions of key features, fallback rate. Stability and outcomes: forecast revisions, and the decision metrics — service level, waste, stock-outs, idle capacity.

A classic bias monitor is the tracking signal: the running sum of errors (actual − forecast) divided by the running mean absolute error. Unbiased errors cancel, keeping it near zero; persistent errors in one direction make it grow. Suppose weekly errors for a product are 4, −3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 6. After week 1, the sum is 4 and the mean absolute error 4: TS = 1.0. After week 2: sum 1, MAE 3.5, TS = 0.3. Continuing gives 1.5, 2.7, 3.8, 4.9, 6.0, 7.0. A common rule alarms beyond ±4, so week 6 triggers an investigation: the forecast has been too low for five straight weeks. Perhaps a competitor closed, or a stock-out period before was depressing training data. A tracking signal per series, aggregated by segment, catches drift long before average accuracy metrics move.

8. Retraining, shadow mode and safe rollout

Retraining policy has three parts. Feature refresh happens every run: new lags and rolling statistics let even an old model track recent levels. Scheduled retraining (weekly, monthly) incorporates new patterns; its cadence is set by how fast the world changes and how costly training is. Triggered retraining responds to monitoring: a tracking-signal alarm across a segment, a detected change point, a new product range. Every retrain passes the backtest gate. Larger changes — a new model family, new features — go through shadow mode: the challenger forecasts on live data for several weeks without driving decisions, and is compared with the champion on what actually happened, before a staged rollout (one region or category first). Keep the previous champion deployable for instant rollback.

9. Feedback loops: censored demand and selection

Forecasts change the world they are later trained on. Censored demand is the classic case: a low forecast leads to a small order, the shelf empties early, recorded sales are capped at stock, and the next model learns that demand was low — a downward spiral concentrated on the items that sell best. Remedies: record stock levels and flag stock-out periods, exclude or impute censored days, add an in-stock feature (set to 1 at forecast time), or use a likelihood that treats censored observations as "at least this many". Similar loops arise in capacity planning (under-provisioned services throttle requests, so measured demand understates true demand) and in pricing (discounts triggered by high forecasts inflate later sales).

Selection is the subtler loop, and it is the one that caught Zillow. When a decision is taken only for cases where the forecast clears some bar — buy if our valuation beats the seller's ask, approve if predicted risk is low, launch if predicted demand is high — the cases acted on are not a random sample; they are enriched for the forecast's errors in the favourable direction. The next section works through the mechanism.

10. The winner's curse, simulated

The lab simulates 50,000 homes. Each has a true value; the company's model estimates it with about 6% random error and no bias — its median error over all homes is +0.0%. The company offers 3% below its estimate, planning a 3% margin. Each seller knows their home roughly (their reservation price is the true value with 3% noise) and accepts only offers above it. About a third of offers are accepted — and they are not a random third. They are disproportionately homes the model over-valued, because an over-valued home generates a high offer that the seller happily accepts, while an under-valued home generates a low offer that is rejected. On the homes actually bought, the model's median error is +5.8%, and the average resale margin is −2.7% instead of the planned +3%.

This is the winner's curse, well known in auction theory (the winning bidder is typically the one who most over-estimated the value) and a form of adverse selection (the other side knows more and chooses accordingly). The defences are to shade offers by the expected selection bias (larger when the model's uncertainty is larger relative to the counterparty's knowledge), to require margins that cover forecast error at the holding horizon rather than average error, to evaluate the model on the cases acted upon rather than on all cases, and to monitor realised margins on recent purchases as the primary health metric — with a kill switch.

11. Sizing the nightly job, worked

Back-of-envelope sizing belongs in every design (sc1). Suppose 5 million store-product series, two years of daily history (730 days), 28-day horizon, 9 quantiles. Raw history is 5M × 730 ≈ 3.65 billion rows; at about 20 bytes per row in a columnar format that is roughly 73 GB — fine for a distributed job, painful for one machine. Recomputing 30 features over full history nightly means tens of billions of feature values; incremental updates (add yesterday, drop the oldest day from each rolling window) cut that to a few hundred million. Output is 5M × 28 × 9 ≈ 1.26 billion numbers, about 5 GB at 4 bytes each, written nightly — partition it by date and region so downstream readers fetch only what they need. Scoring with a gradient-boosted model at, say, 200,000 rows per second per core needs 5M × 28 = 140 million predictions per quantile model; with 9 quantile models that is 1.26 billion predictions, about 6,300 core-seconds — under 2 hours on one core, minutes on a modest cluster. The point of the exercise is to find the bottleneck (here, feature computation over history, not the model) and to leave headroom for re-runs when data is late.

12. New series, discontinued series and identity changes

Production series are born, die and change names. A new product needs a forecast before it has history: use a global model with static features and "days since launch" (ts6), borrow a launch curve from similar past products, or let a foundation model forecast from the first few days (ts9) — and widen intervals. A discontinued product should stop being forecast, or its zero sales will be read as a demand collapse and pull down its category. And when a product's identifier changes (a new barcode for a new pack size), the new identifier appears to have no history while the old one appears to die; a mapping table that links them, maintained by the business, prevents both errors. These lifecycle events are a large share of the forecast errors that monitoring flags, and handling them is mostly data engineering, not modelling.

13. Testing the pipeline, not just the model

Forecasting pipelines need tests at three levels, as any software does (swe5). Unit tests check feature functions on tiny hand-made series where the right answer is known: a 7-day rolling mean on seven known values, a lag that must never read a date after the origin (a test that fails if any feature row's source timestamp exceeds its origin catches most leaks automatically). Integration tests run the whole nightly job on a small fixed snapshot and compare outputs with stored expected results, so a refactor that changes forecasts is noticed. Data tests run in production on every batch: schemas, ranges, row counts, freshness, uniqueness of keys. A further check specific to forecasting is a parity test: recompute yesterday's production features from the historical store and confirm they match what was served, which proves training and serving still compute the same thing. These tests are cheap compared with the cost of a week of silently wrong orders.

14. Where LLMs fit

Language models do not belong inside the numeric forecast (ts9) but are useful around it. They can explain forecasts in plain language using tool outputs (forecast, drivers, events), answer planners' questions, draft override proposals from unstructured information ("the store manager says a road closure starts Monday") — always as suggestions with reasons, logged and evaluated like any override — summarise monitoring alerts and link them to recent pipeline or model changes, and help engineers write and review pipeline code. The guardrails are the same as for any LLM application (ae1): numbers come from tools and are checked, actions go through the same approval and logging as human ones, and outputs are evaluated.

Designing the evaluation around the decision

Offline metrics like MASE are proxies; the system's real objective is the decision outcome. The strongest evaluation replays decisions: feed each candidate's backtest forecasts into the decision rule (orders at the critical ratio, with lead times and pack sizes), simulate inventory against actual demand, and compare realised profit, waste and service level. This exposes trade-offs invisible to accuracy metrics: a model with slightly worse MASE but better-calibrated upper quantiles can produce fewer stock-outs at the same inventory. Decision-based evaluation has its own trap — simulated demand is censored where historical stock ran out — so use periods and items with good availability or model the censoring.

Forecast stability as a requirement

Planners and downstream systems dislike forecasts that jump between runs: an order placed on Monday's forecast looks wrong by Wednesday's, and trust erodes. Stability can be measured (the revision between consecutive forecasts for the same target date) and traded against accuracy: smoothing forecasts across runs, retraining less often, ensembling over recent model versions, or freezing near-term forecasts inside the decision lead time. A system that is 1% less accurate but half as jumpy is often the better product.

Scale engineering

Forecasting millions of series nightly is a distributed-computing job. Global models score fast (one model, vectorised inference), but feature computation over long histories dominates: rolling windows over years of daily data for 5 million series is billions of rows. Common techniques: incremental feature computation (update rolling statistics with the newest day instead of recomputing), partitioning by store or category for parallelism, storing features in columnar formats, and keeping heavy local models (per-series ETS) for the subset of series where they win. Estimate the nightly job's runtime and cost explicitly, with headroom for re-runs when data arrives late.

Hierarchies of decisions and forecasts

Large organisations make decisions at many levels from the same forecasts: store orders daily, distribution-centre replenishment weekly, supplier contracts quarterly, budgets annually. Each needs forecasts at its own level and horizon, and they must be consistent (ts11) — otherwise the supplier contract and the store orders disagree. The architecture answer is one forecasting system producing reconciled forecasts at all needed levels and horizons, with each consumer reading the level it needs, rather than separate teams running separate models.

Uncertainty about uncertainty

Zillow's chief executive described the problem as the unpredictability of forecasting exceeding expectations. That is a failure of second-order uncertainty: the system's estimate of its own error was too optimistic, so margins were too thin. Model-based intervals understate error (ts8), and error itself varies over time — calm markets give small errors, turning points large ones. Practical defences are to measure error on recent decisions continuously, to size margins and safety stock from error distributions at the actual holding horizon (not one-step error), to stress-test against historical turning points, and to set exposure limits so that a period of large errors cannot sink the business before monitoring catches it.

Data contracts with upstream teams

A forecasting system depends on data owned by other teams — point-of-sale, inventory, pricing, promotions — whose schemas and meanings change without notice. A data contract is an explicit agreement about each feed: fields, types, units, time zone, delivery deadline, how corrections are signalled, and who is notified of changes. Paired with automated checks at ingestion (schema, ranges, row counts, freshness), contracts turn silent breakages into loud, early failures. In post-incident reviews of forecasting systems, upstream data changes are among the most common root causes, which is why mature teams treat contracts as part of the system rather than paperwork.

Batch versus online learning

Some systems update model parameters continuously as each observation arrives (online learning), rather than retraining in batches. Exponential smoothing is naturally online; gradient-boosted trees are not; neural networks can be fine-tuned incrementally. Online updates adapt fastest to change but are hardest to validate — there is no backtest gate for each update — and a burst of bad data can corrupt the model in minutes. A common compromise is online updates for simple level and scale estimates (which feature refresh already provides) and batch retraining, with gates, for everything else.

Governance and auditability

Forecasts that drive money need an audit trail: which model version, trained on which data snapshot, with which features, produced this number; which overrides were applied by whom and why; which decision rule converted it into an order. Regulated settings (financial planning, energy markets) require it, and it is invaluable in incident reviews. Store forecasts as immutable records keyed by origin, target date, series and version, and never overwrite history. The same records then double as the training data for evaluating overrides, recalibrating intervals and replaying decisions, so auditability pays for itself.

The case: Zillow Offers

Zillow Offers, launched in 2018, was Zillow's entry into "iBuying": making algorithmic cash offers on homes, buying them, doing light renovation and reselling them, typically within months. Its offers relied on Zillow's home-valuation models and on forecasts of resale prices over the holding period, plus estimates of renovation and selling costs. The economics depended on small spreads on a high volume of transactions.

On 2 November 2021, Zillow Group's third-quarter results announced the business would be wound down. The earnings release records a write-down of inventory of approximately $304 million within the Homes segment "as a result of purchasing homes in Q3 at higher prices than the company's current estimates of future selling prices", an additional expected $240 million to $265 million of losses to be recognised in the fourth quarter, Homes segment revenue of $1.2 billion against an outlook of $1.45 billion at the midpoint, and a wind-down expected to take several quarters with a reduction of the workforce by approximately 25%. Chief executive Rich Barton stated: "We've determined the unpredictability in forecasting home prices far exceeds what we anticipated and continuing to scale Zillow Offers would result in too much earnings and balance-sheet volatility."

Public sources do not describe Zillow's models in detail, so any diagnosis is necessarily general, but the forecasting lessons are clear and widely discussed. First, margin versus error: a business with a thin spread cannot tolerate forecast errors of the same size, and home prices over a holding period of months carry errors of several percent even for good models — more when the market turns. Second, horizon: the relevant error is at the resale date, not today's valuation error; inventory held for months is exposed to market moves the valuation model did not forecast. Third, selection: homeowners choose whether to accept, and they know their homes; accepted offers are enriched for over-valuations, as the lab's simulation shows. Fourth, scale and speed: growing purchase volume quickly while market dynamics were changing increased exposure before realised resale margins could reveal the problem. Each corresponds to a design principle of this lesson: size decisions from uncertainty at the decision horizon, evaluate on the cases acted on, monitor realised outcomes as the primary signal, and cap exposure.

Common production failures, briefly

Late data treated as zeros: yesterday's sales for some stores arrive after the run, the pipeline fills zeros, and forecasts drop. Validate completeness; treat missing as missing; re-run when late data lands.

Backtest–production gap: the backtest used revised data or a different feature path. Point-in-time stores and one code path.

Silent fallback: a schema change makes the main model fail for a category, the fallback takes over, and nobody notices that a third of forecasts are seasonal-naive. Monitor fallback rate per segment, and alert when it moves, because a rising fallback rate is often the first visible sign of an upstream change.

Runaway feedback: stock-outs depress training data, forecasts fall, more stock-outs follow. Track in-stock rates alongside forecasts and treat censored periods explicitly.

Override drift: planners' adjustments grow in size and frequency after a bad month, adding bias. Report forecast value added per planner and reason.

Communicating forecasts to the people who use them

A forecasting system that planners do not trust gets overridden into irrelevance. Trust comes from three things that are part of the system design. Transparency: show each forecast with its interval, the main drivers (promotion, holiday, trend) and the model's recent accuracy for that item. Stability: avoid large unexplained revisions between runs. And accountability: publish forecast value added for both model and overrides, so arguments are settled by evidence. An explanation layer — increasingly LLM-written from tool outputs — helps, but only if the numbers it explains are the ones the system actually produced.

A checklist for the design interview

Decision, granularity, horizon and cost asymmetry first. Data with event and arrival time, point-in-time features. Baselines and a global model with quantiles; rolling-origin backtest gates. Batch scoring with validation and fallbacks; reconciliation; logged overrides. Decision rule at the critical ratio; evaluation on decision outcomes. Monitoring of accuracy, bias (tracking signal), coverage, inputs, revisions and outcomes. Retraining policy, shadow mode and rollback. Feedback loops: censoring and selection. LLMs around the system with tool-grounded numbers.

Goal: compute a newsvendor order and its profit gain, build point-in-time training data with an as-of join, simulate the winner's curse, and compute a tracking signal. NumPy and pandas from gpt_env (Python 3.12); set PYTHONIOENCODING=utf-8 on Windows. Save as ts12.py.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(12)

# 1. Newsvendor: order the demand quantile at the critical ratio
price, cost, salvage = 3.50, 1.00, 0.0
cu, co = price - cost, cost - salvage
ratio = cu / (cu + co)
demand = rng.poisson(rng.gamma(20, 4, 100_000))          # uncertain daily demand, mean ~80
def profit(q):
    sold = np.minimum(q, demand)
    return np.mean(price * sold + salvage * (q - sold) - cost * q)
q_star = int(np.quantile(demand, ratio))
print(f"critical ratio {ratio:.3f} -> order {q_star};  expected profit {profit(q_star):.1f}  "
      f"vs ordering the mean {demand.mean():.0f}: {profit(round(demand.mean())):.1f}")

# 2. Point-in-time joins: a revised feature leaks the future
sales = pd.DataFrame({"date": pd.to_datetime(["2026-03-02", "2026-03-09", "2026-03-16"]), "units": [120, 135, 90]})
# web visits are first published, then revised upward a week later
visits = pd.DataFrame({
    "week": pd.to_datetime(["2026-03-02", "2026-03-02", "2026-03-09", "2026-03-09"]),
    "known_at": pd.to_datetime(["2026-03-03", "2026-03-10", "2026-03-10", "2026-03-17"]),
    "visits": [1000, 1300, 1100, 1450]})
latest = visits.sort_values("known_at").groupby("week").last().reset_index()
print("\nwhat the table says today (final revisions):\n", latest[["week", "visits"]].to_string(index=False))
asof = pd.merge_asof(sales.sort_values("date"), visits.sort_values("known_at"),
                     left_on="date", right_on="known_at", direction="backward")
print("what was actually known on each forecast date:\n", asof[["date", "week", "visits"]].to_string(index=False))

# 3. Winner's curse: an unbiased price estimate still loses money when you only buy where you bid high
n = 50_000
true_value = rng.lognormal(np.log(300_000), 0.2, n)
estimate = true_value * rng.lognormal(0, 0.06, n)          # unbiased-ish model, ~6% error
offer = estimate * 0.97                                    # offer 3% below our estimate
seller_reserve = true_value * rng.lognormal(0, 0.03, n)    # sellers know their homes roughly
bought = offer > seller_reserve                            # sellers accept only when the offer looks good
margin = (true_value[bought] - offer[bought]) / offer[bought]
print(f"\nmodel error on all homes: median {np.median(estimate / true_value - 1):+.1%}")
print(f"homes bought: {bought.mean():.0%} of offers; error on bought homes: median {np.median(estimate[bought] / true_value[bought] - 1):+.1%}")
print(f"average resale margin on bought homes: {margin.mean():+.1%} (planned +3%)")

# 4. Monitoring bias with a tracking signal: cumulative error / mean absolute error
errors = np.array([4, -3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 6])               # actual - forecast, per week
cum, mad = np.cumsum(errors), np.cumsum(np.abs(errors)) / np.arange(1, len(errors) + 1)
print("\ntracking signal by week:", np.round(cum / mad, 1).tolist(), "(alarm beyond +-4)")

Work it out

You run GPU capacity planning for an inference platform. Each month you reserve GPUs for the next month's daily peak. A reserved GPU costs 1,000 a month; if peak demand exceeds reservations, you buy on-demand capacity at an effective extra cost of 4,000 per GPU for the month (and some requests are slowed). Your forecast of next month's peak (in GPUs) has quantiles: 50% → 800, 70% → 860, 80% → 900, 90% → 960, 95% → 1,010.

(a) How many GPUs should you reserve? (b) Over the last six months, actual peak minus forecast median was +40, +55, +30, +70, +60, +85. Compute the tracking signal and interpret it. (c) Name two system changes you would make.

Answer

(a) Under-cost c_u = 4,000 (each GPU short), over-cost c_o = 1,000 (each idle reserved GPU). Critical ratio = 4,000/5,000 = 0.8, so reserve the 80th percentile: 900 GPUs.

(b) All six errors are positive: cumulative sum 340, mean absolute error 340/6 ≈ 56.7, tracking signal = 340/56.7 = 6.0, far beyond ±4. The forecast is persistently biased low — demand is growing faster than the model assumes (or past throttling censored measured peaks, teaching the model lower demand).

(c) Fix the data: record throttled and queued requests so measured demand is not capped by past capacity (censoring). Fix the model: add a growth feature or retrain with recent data weighted more, and recalibrate quantiles conformally on recent errors so the 80th percentile is really exceeded 20% of the time. Add monitoring: a tracking-signal alarm per month and a check of realised over-capacity months versus the 20% target.
