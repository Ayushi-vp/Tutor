In the 1920s the statistician George Udny Yule noticed something unsettling: across a run of years, the proportion of Church of England marriages and the mortality rate in England and Wales moved together so closely that their correlation was about 0.95. Nobody believed marriages in church were killing people. Both series had simply drifted downward over the same decades, and any two series that drift together look correlated, whatever they measure. Yule called these "nonsense correlations". Fifty years later Clive Granger and Paul Newbold showed by simulation that the problem is not a curiosity but the default outcome: regress one random wandering series on another, completely independent one, and the standard statistics declare a significant relationship most of the time.

The same trap catches engineers today. A dashboard shows that GPU spend and the number of support tickets have risen together all year with a correlation of 0.9; someone proposes that inference latency is driving complaints. A model trained to predict sales from web traffic fits beautifully in-sample, because both grew with the company, and falls apart when the two stop growing in step. A gradient-boosted model trained on a series that has doubled predicts a flat line at the training maximum, because trees cannot extrapolate.

All three failures come from one property, or rather its absence: stationarity. A stationary series behaves the same way statistically wherever you look at it: same average level, same spread, same relationship between today and yesterday. Most forecasting models, and all the standard statistics about correlation and significance, quietly assume it. When the assumption fails, averages computed from the past describe nothing stable, correlations measure shared drift, and significance tests lie.

This lesson makes the idea precise and gives you the tools to diagnose and fix it. You will meet white noise and the random walk, the two reference series of the whole field; compute autocorrelation by hand on six numbers; learn to read the autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation plots that guide model choice in ts4; remove trends and seasons by differencing; test for unit roots with ADF and KPSS; and see the spurious-regression result reproduced in the lab, along with its cure.

A river and a drunkard

Two pictures carry most of the intuition. The first is the water level of a river at a fixed spot, measured daily over many years. It rises after rain and falls in dry spells, but it always comes back toward a typical level, and a wet week in 1990 looked statistically much like a wet week in 2020. Its average, its spread and the way today's level relates to yesterday's are the same in every decade. That is a stationary series: shocks happen, but they fade, and the series reverts to its mean.

The second picture is the classic "drunkard's walk": a person who takes one random step left or right each second. There is no typical position to come back to. After a hundred steps they are typically about ten steps from the start, after ten thousand steps about a hundred, and every step's effect is permanent. That is a random walk, and it is non-stationary: its spread grows with time and its "average level" is meaningless. Stock prices, exchange rates, many economic aggregates and many business metrics behave more like the drunkard than the river over the time scales we care about.

The analogy breaks in one place worth noticing. Plenty of business series are neither pure picture: a growing company's revenue is a drunkard walking up a slope (a random walk with drift), and a store's daily sales are a river whose level jumps when a competitor opens. Much of this lesson is about telling these cases apart and turning each into something stationary.

Why models want stationarity

A model learns from the past and applies what it learned to the future. That only works if the past and the future share the same rules. For a stationary series, an average or a correlation estimated over the last three years is an estimate of something fixed, and it will still hold next month. For a non-stationary series, the "average level of the last three years" is a number that will never be seen again. So the standard workflow is: transform the series until it is (close to) stationary, model the stationary version, then undo the transformation to forecast the original.

The two main tools

The first tool is the autocorrelation function (ACF): for each lag k, the correlation between the series and itself shifted k steps. It is the fingerprint of a series' memory. A stationary series' ACF drops toward zero within a few lags; a random walk's ACF stays near 1 for dozens of lags; a seasonal series shows spikes at the seasonal lag.

The second tool is differencing: replace each value with its change from the previous one. Differencing the drunkard's positions gives back the individual steps, which are independent random noise — perfectly stationary. Differencing a company's revenue gives its growth per period, which is far more stable than its level. When there is a seasonal pattern, a seasonal difference (this December minus last December) removes it.

A reader who stops here should hold this rule: before modelling or correlating, check whether the series is stationary; if not, transform it (log for growing swings, difference for trends and wandering, seasonal difference for stable seasons) until it is.

1. Stationarity, defined

A series is strictly stationary if the joint distribution of any stretch of values is unchanged when you shift the stretch in time: (y_1, …, y_10) has the same distribution as (y_101, …, y_110). That is a strong requirement and impossible to check from data, so in practice we use weak stationarity (also called covariance stationarity), which asks only three things:

  • the mean E[y_t] is the same constant μ for every t;
  • the variance Var(y_t) is the same constant σ² for every t;
  • the covariance between y_t and y_{t−k} depends only on the lag k, not on t.

The third condition is the interesting one. It says the relationship between "today and yesterday" is the same in January and July, this year and ten years ago. That covariance, as a function of k, is the autocovariance γ(k); dividing by the variance γ(0) gives the autocorrelation ρ(k) = γ(k)/γ(0), which lies between −1 and 1 (the same normalisation that turns covariance into correlation in math3).

What breaks stationarity? A trend breaks the constant mean. A seasonal pattern also breaks it (the mean in December differs from the mean in June). A swing that grows with the level breaks constant variance. A structural break breaks everything. A random walk keeps a constant mean of zero increments but its variance grows with t, so it breaks the second condition.

One more property lurks in the background: ergodicity, the guarantee that averaging over time along one long series converges to the true average of the process. We usually have exactly one history — one record of this store's sales — so we can only learn the process's properties by averaging over time. Stationarity plus ergodicity is what makes that legitimate.

2. The two reference series: white noise and the random walk

White noise is a sequence ε_1, ε_2, … of values with mean zero, constant variance σ² and zero correlation between any two different time points. It is the formal version of "pure unpredictable noise". Its ACF is 1 at lag 0 and 0 at every other lag. Every model in this track can be described as a recipe that turns white noise into a structured series, and every model is judged by whether its residuals look like white noise: if they do, the model has extracted all the predictable structure.

A random walk accumulates white noise: y_t = y_{t−1} + ε_t, starting from some y_0. Unrolling the recursion, y_t = y_0 + ε_1 + ε_2 + … + ε_t, a sum of t independent shocks, so Var(y_t) = tσ²: the spread grows linearly with time and the standard deviation as √t. Every shock stays in the series forever. A random walk with drift adds a constant: y_t = c + y_{t−1} + ε_t, so the series moves on average c per step, plus the wandering. The best forecast of a random walk is the last value (the naive forecast of ts1), and with drift it is the last value plus c·h.

3. The AR(1) process: the bridge between them

Between white noise and the random walk sits the autoregressive process of order 1, AR(1): y_t = φ·y_{t−1} + ε_t (add a constant to give it a non-zero mean). The coefficient φ controls memory. With φ = 0 it is white noise. With φ = 1 it is a random walk. For |φ| < 1 it is stationary: a shock's effect after k steps is φ^k, which fades. With φ = 0.8, a shock keeps 80% of its effect after one step, 64% after two, 33% after five and 11% after ten.

The same arithmetic gives the ACF. Substituting the equation into itself, y_t = φ^k·y_{t−k} + (shocks between t−k+1 and t). The shocks in between are independent of y_{t−k}, so the correlation between y_t and y_{t−k} is ρ(k) = φ^k: geometric decay. The lab simulates 400 points with φ = 0.8 and measures sample autocorrelations of 0.79, 0.64, 0.36 and 0.12 at lags 1, 2, 5 and 10, against theoretical values of 0.80, 0.64, 0.33 and 0.11. The value φ = 1 is the boundary where the decay stops; this is why a random walk is said to have a unit root — the coefficient on the lag equals one.

4. The sample ACF, computed by hand

Given data y_1 … y_n with mean ȳ, the sample autocorrelation at lag k is

r_k = Σ_{t=k+1..n} (y_t − ȳ)(y_{t−k} − ȳ)  /  Σ_{t=1..n} (y_t − ȳ)²

The numerator adds up products of deviations for every pair of points k apart; the denominator is the total sum of squared deviations. Take six values: 3, 5, 4, 6, 8, 7.

The mean is 33 / 6 = 5.5 and the sum of squared deviations is 17.5. For lag 1, multiply each deviation by the one before it: (−0.5)(−2.5) = 1.25, (−1.5)(−0.5) = 0.75, (0.5)(−1.5) = −0.75, (2.5)(0.5) = 1.25, (1.5)(2.5) = 3.75. These sum to 6.25, so r_1 = 6.25 / 17.5 ≈ 0.357. For lag 2, pair values two apart: (−1.5)(−2.5) = 3.75, (0.5)(−0.5) = −0.25, (2.5)(−1.5) = −3.75, (1.5)(0.5) = 0.75, summing to 0.5, so r_2 = 0.5 / 17.5 ≈ 0.029.

Two design choices in the formula deserve a word. It uses the overall mean, not separate means for the early and late values in each pair, and it divides by the full sum of squares rather than the sum over the n − k overlapping pairs. Both choices slightly shrink r_k toward zero at large lags (where few pairs exist, so estimates are noisy) and guarantee the set of autocorrelations is internally consistent, which matters when models are fitted from them.

How big must r_k be to matter? If the series were white noise, each sample autocorrelation would be roughly normal with mean 0 and standard deviation 1/√n, so about 95% would fall inside ±2/√n (more precisely ±1.96/√n). For n = 6, that band is ±0.82, so r_1 = 0.36 is entirely consistent with noise: six points can barely establish anything. For n = 400 the band is ±0.10, and a lag-1 autocorrelation of 0.36 would be overwhelming evidence. Because you look at 20 or 40 lags at once, expect about one bar in twenty outside the band even for pure noise; a single marginal bar at an odd lag is rarely meaningful. To test a group of lags jointly, the Ljung-Box test combines the squared autocorrelations of lags 1 to h into one statistic, compared with a chi-squared distribution; it is the standard check that a model's residuals are white noise.

5. Reading an ACF

The ACF's shape tells you what kind of series you have. A slow, almost linear decay from near 1 is the signature of a trend or a unit root: values far apart are still similar because the series has wandered or trended. A geometric decay suggests a stationary autoregressive process. Spikes at the seasonal lag and its multiples (7, 14, 21 for daily data; 12, 24 for monthly; 24, 48 for hourly) show seasonality. A sharp cut-off after a few lags — significant at lags 1 and 2, then nothing — suggests a moving-average process, introduced next. An ACF that is all inside the band means there is little linear structure left, as you want for residuals.

A practical warning: do not read model orders from the ACF of a series that is not stationary. Its ACF is dominated by the slow decay and hides everything else. Difference first, then read.

6. The PACF and the MA process

The ACF at lag 2 mixes two things: the direct relationship between y_t and y_{t−2}, and the indirect one passing through y_{t−1}. In an AR(1), y_{t−2} affects y_t only through y_{t−1}, yet r_2 = φ² is non-zero. The partial autocorrelation function (PACF) removes the indirect path: the partial autocorrelation at lag k is the coefficient on y_{t−k} in a regression of y_t on y_{t−1}, …, y_{t−k}. For an AR(1), the PACF is φ at lag 1 and zero afterwards, because once y_{t−1} is in the regression, y_{t−2} adds nothing. For an AR(p), the PACF cuts off after lag p.

The mirror image is the moving-average process, MA(q), where the value is a weighted sum of the current and q previous shocks: an MA(1) is y_t = ε_t + θ·ε_{t−1}. Values more than q steps apart share no shocks, so the ACF cuts off after lag q (an MA(1) has ρ(1) = θ/(1 + θ²) and zero beyond), while the PACF decays gradually. This pair of rules — AR: ACF decays, PACF cuts off; MA: ACF cuts off, PACF decays — is the classic Box-Jenkins identification method that ts4 uses to choose ARIMA orders.

7. Differencing

The first difference is Δy_t = y_t − y_{t−1}, the change from one step to the next. Differencing the six values above gives 5 − 3 = 2, 4 − 5 = −1, 6 − 4 = 2, 8 − 6 = 2 and 7 − 8 = −1. The original values rose from 3 to 7; the differences hover around their mean of 0.8 with no trend. That mean is the drift: the average change per step. A forecast of the differences (say 0.8 per step) is turned back into a forecast of the level by adding it up from the last value: 7.8, 8.6, 9.4 for the next three steps. This is exactly the drift method of ts4.

Why does differencing work? For a random walk, Δy_t = ε_t, white noise: the difference undoes the accumulation. For a linear trend y_t = a + bt + noise, Δy_t = b + (noise_t − noise_{t−1}): the trend becomes a constant. For a quadratic trend, one difference leaves a linear trend and a second difference (the difference of the differences) is needed; in practice a second difference is rarely required and two is the maximum anyone uses.

Seasonal differencing subtracts the value one season earlier: y_t − y_{t−m}. If December is always 200 units above the yearly level, December minus last December cancels those 200 units, along with the seasonal effect of every other month. It also removes a trend partially (a December-to-December difference contains a year of growth, now a constant), so sometimes a seasonal difference alone makes a series stationary. When both are needed, you apply both: (y_t − y_{t−m}) − (y_{t−1} − y_{t−m−1}). The order does not matter.

Differencing has a cost. Each difference loses one observation at the start (a seasonal difference loses m), and differencing amplifies short-term noise. Most importantly, over-differencing — differencing a series that was already stationary — creates structure that was not there. Differencing white noise gives ε_t − ε_{t−1}, which has twice the variance and a lag-1 autocorrelation of exactly −0.5. So a strongly negative lag-1 ACF after differencing, or a variance that went up rather than down, says you differenced once too often.

8. Deterministic versus stochastic trends

Two different processes can both look like "a rising line with noise". A trend-stationary series is a straight line plus stationary noise: y_t = a + bt + u_t with u_t stationary. If a shock pushes it above the line, it drifts back. A difference-stationary series is a random walk with drift: shocks are permanent, and after a shock the series continues from the new level. The two need different treatments: for a trend-stationary series you subtract the fitted line (detrend); for a difference-stationary series you difference. They also give very different forecasts far ahead. Trend-stationary forecast intervals stay a constant width around the line; random-walk intervals widen like √h forever. Deciding which you have is what unit-root tests are for.

9. Unit-root tests: ADF and KPSS

The Dickey-Fuller test starts from the AR(1) equation and subtracts y_{t−1} from both sides: Δy_t = c + γ·y_{t−1} + ε_t, where γ = φ − 1. A unit root (φ = 1) means γ = 0; a stationary series has γ < 0 (the further above its mean the series sits, the more it tends to fall back). So the test regresses the differences on the lagged level and looks at the t-statistic of γ. The twist is that under the null hypothesis of a unit root, this t-statistic does not follow the usual distribution, so the usual ±1.96 cut-off is wrong. With a constant in the regression, the 5% critical value is about −2.87 for a few hundred observations: you need a t-statistic below −2.87, not −1.96, to reject a unit root. The augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test adds a few lagged differences Δy_{t−1}, Δy_{t−2}, … to the regression so that short-term correlation in the errors does not distort the result. In the lab, the AR(1) series with φ = 0.8 gives a t-statistic of −6.82 (unit root rejected) and the random walk −1.21 (not rejected).

ADF has a known weakness: low power. A stationary series with φ = 0.95 looks so much like a random walk over a few hundred points that the test often fails to reject. Failing to reject is not evidence of a unit root; it may just be too little data. The KPSS test flips the hypotheses: its null is that the series is stationary (around a level, or around a linear trend), and it rejects when the cumulative sums of deviations from the mean wander too far. Using the two together gives four outcomes. ADF rejects and KPSS does not: stationary. ADF does not reject and KPSS rejects: unit root, so difference. Both reject: often a structural break or a series with very long memory. Neither rejects: the data cannot decide, so lean on the plot, the domain and backtests. Automated tools such as auto.arima in R's forecast package use repeated KPSS tests to choose the number of differences.

10. Spurious regression

Regression of one series on another assumes the residuals are stationary. Granger and Newbold's 1974 simulation made the failure vivid. Generate two completely independent random walks of 50 observations, regress one on the other, and test whether the slope is zero at the 5% level. If the standard theory applied, you would wrongly find a "significant" relationship 5% of the time. They found one 76% of the time. Later simulations by Granger and colleagues show the rate climbing with the sample size — 77% at 100 observations and 97.6% at 10,000 — because more data does not help when the assumption is broken; it only makes the false precision worse. Even two independent but highly persistent stationary series (AR(1) with φ = 0.9) gave false rejections about 52% of the time at every sample size, so the problem is not confined to unit roots: strong autocorrelation inflates t-statistics whenever the standard errors ignore it.

The lab repeats the experiment: across 1,000 pairs of independent 50-step random walks, |t| exceeded 1.96 in 66% of regressions (the original paper's 76% came from 100 replications, so some variation is expected); regressing the differences of the walks instead gave 5.7%, close to the correct 5%. The practical rule follows directly: never regress levels on levels for wandering series unless you have a reason to believe they are cointegrated (next section, and deep); regress changes on changes, or include lagged values of the target so the residuals are stationary, and check the residual ACF.

11. Variance stabilisation comes first

Stationarity also requires constant variance. If the seasonal swing or the noise grows with the level, difference the log, not the raw series: the first difference of the log is approximately the percentage change, Δlog y_t ≈ (y_t − y_{t−1}) / y_{t−1}, which is usually far more stable than the change in units. Revenue growing from 1 million to 10 million a month with 5% monthly noise has unit changes that grow tenfold but log-differences with a steady spread. The order is always: transform for variance (log or Box-Cox, ts2), then difference for mean, then read the ACF.

Cointegration: when levels regressions are legitimate

Some non-stationary series genuinely move together because something ties them. The price of a stock and the price of its futures contract both wander like random walks, but arbitrage keeps the gap between them small and mean-reverting. Two such series are cointegrated: each has a unit root, but a linear combination (price minus futures price, or y − βx in general) is stationary. In that case regressing the levels is meaningful — it estimates the long-run relationship — and differencing both would throw that relationship away. The Engle-Granger method tests for cointegration by regressing y on x in levels and running a unit-root test on the residuals (with adjusted critical values); if the residuals are stationary, the series are cointegrated. An error-correction model then combines short-run changes with a term that pulls the series back toward the long-run relationship. For engineers, the takeaway is that "difference everything" is a safe default for correlation questions but loses information when there is a real equilibrium, such as supply and demand at different points of the same pipeline.

Why the Dickey-Fuller distribution is non-standard

Under the unit-root null, y_{t−1} is itself a random walk, so its variance grows over time and the usual central-limit arguments for the slope estimate do not apply. The estimate of γ converges to zero faster than usual (at rate 1/n instead of 1/√n) and its t-statistic follows a skewed distribution, now called the Dickey-Fuller distribution, tabulated by simulation. Its critical values depend on whether the regression includes a constant, or a constant and a linear trend: about −1.95 with neither, −2.86 with a constant, and −3.41 with a constant and trend, at the 5% level for large samples (slightly more negative for small ones). Choosing the wrong version is a common error: testing a trending series without a trend term can make a trend-stationary series look like a unit root.

Structural breaks fool unit-root tests

A stationary series with a single permanent level shift looks, to a unit-root test, like a series whose shocks are permanent — because one shock was. Perron showed in 1989 that ignoring a break biases the ADF test toward not rejecting a unit root. The practical consequences: before testing, plot the series and look for breaks (a product launch, a pricing change, a pandemic); include a break dummy in the test regression if you know the date; and consider whether a model with a level-shift regressor describes the series better than differencing. Change-point detection (ts10) finds breaks you do not know about.

Long memory and fractional differencing

Some series sit between stationary and unit-root: their ACF decays, but far more slowly than geometrically, following a power law. River flows (the Nile, studied by Hurst), network traffic and volatility in financial returns behave this way. The model for this is the fractionally integrated process, which differences by a non-integer amount d between 0 and 0.5: the operator (1 − L)^d, where L shifts the series back one step, is expanded into an infinite weighted sum of past values with slowly decaying weights. Fractional differencing removes just enough memory to make the series stationary while keeping more of the long-run information than a full difference would. It is used in quantitative finance feature engineering to make price series stationary without destroying their predictive memory. Both ADF and KPSS rejecting is a hint that long memory may be present.

Effective sample size and comparing forecasters

Autocorrelation shrinks the information content of a sample. For an AR(1) with coefficient φ, the variance of the sample mean is approximately σ²/n × (1 + φ)/(1 − φ), so the effective sample size is n(1 − φ)/(1 + φ). With φ = 0.8 that is n/9: 900 hourly observations behave like about 100 independent ones. The same logic applies to forecast errors: errors from consecutive origins in a backtest are correlated, so a difference in average error between two models can look significant on a naive t-test and not be. The Diebold-Mariano test (ts5) compares two forecasters' errors with a standard error that accounts for this autocorrelation.

Stationarity for ML forecasters

Modern forecasters handle non-stationarity in their own ways, and recognising them as stationarity tricks helps in interviews. Gradient-boosted trees predict a piecewise-constant function of their inputs, so they cannot produce a value larger than any target seen in training; on a growing series they flatten out. The fixes are to predict the difference from the last value, the ratio to a recent mean, or the log ratio — all targets that stay in a stable range. DeepAR scales each series by its average, PatchTST and many others apply per-window normalisation (subtract the window mean, divide by its standard deviation, add them back after prediction, sometimes called RevIN), and foundation models like Chronos scale each context by its mean absolute value (ts7, ts9). Each converts a non-stationary level problem into a closer-to-stationary shape problem.

Seasonal unit roots and the choice between differencing and Fourier terms

A seasonal pattern can itself wander: the December peak may drift up and down from year to year without returning to a fixed size. That is a seasonal unit root, removed by seasonal differencing. If instead the seasonal pattern is stable, seasonal differencing over-differences at the seasonal lag (the ACF shows a strong negative spike at lag m), and a deterministic seasonal model — dummies or Fourier terms (ts2) — is better. Tests exist for seasonal unit roots (the OCSB and Canova-Hansen tests are used by automatic ARIMA tools), but the practical check is the same as for ordinary differencing: if the ACF at lag m goes strongly negative after seasonal differencing, you probably did not need it.

Where these ideas bite in real systems

Correlation dashboards. Observability tools make it easy to overlay two metrics and compute their correlation. For trending or wandering metrics this almost always produces high correlations that mean nothing; incident reviews that blame a metric because it "correlated 0.9 with the outage metric over the past month" are reasoning about shared drift. Correlate changes (first differences, or residuals after removing seasonality), not levels, and require a mechanism.

Trees on growing targets. A capacity model built with gradient-boosted trees on raw request counts flattens out at the training-set maximum; on a fast-growing product the forecast falls further and further below reality each week. Symptoms: residuals that grow in one direction over time and forecasts that never exceed a ceiling. The fix is to predict a ratio to a recent level or a log difference.

Drift that changes the rules. Production monitoring of model inputs often uses statistical distance between the training distribution and the live distribution. For non-stationary features (anything that grows, such as counts of users), that distance grows every day by construction and the alert becomes noise. Monitor stationary transformations — growth rates, ratios, per-user rates — instead.

A/B tests on time-series metrics. Daily active users or revenue per day are autocorrelated, so standard errors that treat each day as independent are too small, and a two-week test looks more precise than it is. Analysts use per-user metrics where possible, or methods that account for autocorrelation, and pre-period adjustments that remove persistent differences.

The case: Granger and Newbold, and what came after

Granger and Newbold's 1974 paper in the Journal of Econometrics was aimed at applied economists who reported regressions between trending macroeconomic series with high R² values and significant t-statistics, but with residuals that were strongly autocorrelated (a low Durbin-Watson statistic). Their simulation of independent random walks showed that such results should be expected even when there is no relationship at all: about three-quarters of regressions at their sample size of 50 produced a "significant" slope. Phillips later supplied the theory (1986): with unit roots, the t-statistic does not converge to a normal distribution but grows with the sample size, so rejection rates approach 100%. A 1998 follow-up by Granger, Hyung and Jeon quantified it: 77% false rejections at 100 observations and 97.6% at 10,000 for random walks, and around 52% for independent stationary series with φ = 0.9 regardless of sample size. The work changed econometric practice — unit-root testing, differencing and cointegration became standard — and Granger shared the 2003 Nobel prize in economics for the cointegration work that grew out of it. For an AI engineer, its lesson is general: standard errors assume independence, and time-series data violates it, so impressive statistics on raw levels are a warning sign, not evidence.

Practical checklist and tooling

In Python, statsmodels provides the ACF and PACF (with plotting helpers), the ADF test (adfuller), KPSS (kpss), the Ljung-Box test (acorr_ljungbox) and an ndiffs-style workflow through its ARIMA tools; the pmdarima and statsforecast libraries automate "how many differences?" with repeated KPSS tests. The checklist an experienced forecaster runs on a new series: plot it; log if the swing grows with the level; look at the ACF for slow decay and seasonal spikes; run ADF and KPSS with the right deterministic terms; difference the minimum needed; re-plot the ACF; check the differenced series' variance went down; and after modelling, check that residuals pass a Ljung-Box test.

Knobs

The knobs are the transform, the number of ordinary and seasonal differences, the deterministic terms in the unit-root test (none, constant, constant and trend), the number of augmenting lags in ADF (chosen by an information criterion), the number of lags in the Ljung-Box test (often 10 for non-seasonal data and 2m for seasonal), and the treatment of known breaks. Every one of them should be written down with the model, because changing any of them changes forecasts.

import numpy as np

def acf(x, nlags):
    """Sample autocorrelation, the textbook way: one mean, one denominator."""
    d = np.asarray(x, float) - np.mean(x)
    den = np.sum(d * d)
    return np.array([np.sum(d[k:] * d[:len(d) - k]) / den for k in range(nlags + 1)])

y6 = [3, 5, 4, 6, 8, 7]
print("ACF of the 6 values:", np.round(acf(y6, 2), 3), " band +-", round(2 / np.sqrt(6), 2))
print("first differences:", np.diff(y6))

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
n = 400
e = rng.normal(size=n)
ar = np.zeros(n)
for t in range(1, n):
    ar[t] = 0.8 * ar[t - 1] + e[t]          # stationary AR(1), phi = 0.8
rw = np.cumsum(e)                           # random walk: phi = 1

def ols_t(yv, xv):
    """t-statistic of the slope in y = a + b x + u, with the usual i.i.d. standard error."""
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(xv)), xv])
    w, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, yv, rcond=None)
    resid = yv - X @ w
    s2 = resid @ resid / (len(yv) - 2)
    se = np.sqrt(s2 * np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)[1, 1])
    return w[1] / se

#    5% critical value for the t-statistic (with constant, n about 250-500) is about -2.87, not -1.96.
for name, s in (("AR(1)", ar), ("random walk", rw)):
    print(f"DF t-stat {name:12s}: {ols_t(np.diff(s), s[:-1]):6.2f}  (reject unit root if below -2.87)")

Work it out

(a) An AR(1) process has φ = 0.8. What are its theoretical ACF values at lags 1, 2 and 5, and what does its PACF look like?

(b) You have n = 400 daily points. The ACF reads 0.98, 0.97, 0.95, 0.94 … still 0.80 at lag 30. ADF p = 0.61, KPSS p = 0.01. What is the significance band, what is going on, and what do you do?

Answer

(a) For AR(1), ρ(k) = φᵏ: lag 1 = 0.80, lag 2 = 0.64, lag 5 = 0.8⁵ ≈ 0.33 — geometric decay. The PACF has a single spike of 0.8 at lag 1 and is ~0 afterwards, because once lag 1 is accounted for, older lags add nothing.

(b) Band = ±1.96/√400 = ±0.098. An ACF that barely decays is the signature of a unit root or strong trend, and the tests agree: ADF cannot reject a unit root (its null), KPSS rejects stationarity (its null). First-difference, re-plot the ACF, re-run both tests, and check the differenced variance went down. If weekly spikes at 7 and 14 then appear, add a seasonal difference or weekly seasonal terms. Do not read AR/MA orders off the undifferenced ACF — they are meaningless there.
