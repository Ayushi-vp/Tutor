A data-science team spends three months building a deep-learning model to forecast weekly demand for 8,000 products. It uses product embeddings, an attention mechanism and a carefully tuned learning-rate schedule. On the day of the launch review, someone asks how it compares with "last year's number for the same week, scaled by this year's growth". Nobody has computed that. When they do, the simple rule is within 2% of the deep model's accuracy, runs in milliseconds, needs no GPU, and the planners already understand it. The project is not wasted — but the team has learned the most important lesson in forecasting the expensive way: you do not know whether a model is good until you know how good the simple alternatives are.

This happens so often that the forecasting field has institutionalised it. Since 1982 the M competitions, organised by Spyros Makridakis and colleagues, have invited anyone to forecast thousands of real series and have published the scores of every entry next to simple benchmarks. Their recurring finding is uncomfortable for anyone who likes complex models: sophisticated methods do not automatically beat simple ones, and combinations of simple methods are remarkably hard to beat. In the 2018 M4 competition, with 100,000 series, both pure machine-learning benchmarks finished below the naive benchmark.

The classical methods are not just baselines, though. Exponential smoothing and ARIMA are the workhorses of production forecasting in retail, supply chains, energy and finance. They are fast enough to fit to millions of series, they produce honest prediction intervals, they work with short histories, and their parameters mean something a planner can understand. They are also the building blocks inside many modern methods: the M4 winner used exponential smoothing inside a neural network, and the M5 winners used lags and smoothing as features.

This lesson builds the classical toolkit from the ground up. It starts with the four free baselines, then exponential smoothing worked one update at a time, adding trend, damping and seasonality until it reaches the ETS family. It then builds the ARIMA family from autoregressive and moving-average pieces, forecasts an AR(1) by hand, adds seasonal terms, and shows how automatic selection works. It ends with the M4 results read directly from the organisers' spreadsheet.

A thermostat with a memory

Think of a forecaster as a thermostat that has to guess tomorrow's temperature in a room. The crudest thermostat says "tomorrow will be like today" — the naive forecast. A slightly cleverer one says "tomorrow will be like the same day last week", because the heating schedule repeats weekly — seasonal-naive. A third keeps a running estimate of the "true" room temperature and, every time it reads the thermometer, nudges that estimate part of the way toward the new reading, ignoring single noisy readings but following sustained changes. That is exponential smoothing, and the size of the nudge is its one parameter. A fourth thermostat learns a rule like "if it was 2 degrees above normal yesterday, it will be about 1.2 degrees above normal today" — the room drifts back toward normal at a known rate. That is an autoregressive model, the core of ARIMA.

The analogy shows the two philosophies. Exponential smoothing describes the series directly in terms of its components — a level, a trend, a seasonal pattern — and updates each one as data arrives. ARIMA describes how each value relates to the values and surprises just before it — its autocorrelation. They turn out to overlap a great deal, and both are used every day.

How smoothing forgets

The nudge rule has a neat consequence. If each update moves the estimate a fraction α of the way toward the new reading, then the estimate is a weighted average of all past readings, with the newest weighted α, the one before α(1−α), the one before that α(1−α)², and so on. Weights shrink geometrically — exponentially — with age, which is where the name comes from. A large α trusts recent data and reacts fast; a small α averages over a long history and reacts slowly.

The family tree

From one level you can grow the whole exponential-smoothing family: add a trend (Holt), make the trend fade (damped), add a season (Holt-Winters), and choose for each piece whether it adds or multiplies (the ETS framework). From the autoregressive rule you grow ARIMA: use more lags (AR(p)), add terms for recent surprises (MA(q)), difference first to remove trends (the I in ARIMA), and repeat the whole thing at the seasonal lag (seasonal ARIMA).

Each member of the family makes a different promise about the future, and you can see the promises in the shape of the forecast. Naive and SES promise a flat line: "the level will stay where it is". Drift and Holt promise a straight line: "the recent direction continues". Damped trend promises a line that bends flat: "the direction continues for a while and then fades". Seasonal-naive and Holt-Winters promise the seasonal shape repeated. A stationary AR model promises a return toward the long-run mean. Choosing a model is largely choosing which of these promises is most believable for your series and horizon, and the figure below puts them side by side from one forecast origin.

A reader who stops here should keep two rules. Always compute naive, seasonal-naive and drift first. And remember that a good classical model's forecast is smooth — it predicts the average future — while the unpredictable wiggles belong in the prediction interval.

1. The four free baselines

Let y_1 … y_T be the history and ŷ(T+h|T) the forecast h steps ahead. Four methods need no fitting at all.

  • Mean: ŷ = (y_1 + … + y_T) / T for every h. Right for a series that fluctuates around a stable level with no memory.
  • Naive: ŷ = y_T for every h. Optimal for a random walk (ts3); excellent for stock prices and many economic series.
  • Seasonal-naive: ŷ(T+h) = the value one season earlier, y_{T+h−m·k} with k the smallest whole number that lands inside the history. Hard to beat for strongly seasonal, stable series.
  • Drift: ŷ = y_T + h × (y_T − y_1)/(T − 1). Draw a line from the first to the last observation and extend it: the naive forecast plus the average historical change per step.

Take the series 52, 55, 51, 58, 60, 57, 63 (T = 7). Mean: 396/7 ≈ 56.6. Naive: 63. Drift: average change = (63 − 52)/6 ≈ 1.83, so forecasts 64.8, 66.7, 68.5 for h = 1, 2, 3. If the series were weekly with a two-week pattern (m = 2), seasonal-naive would repeat the last two values: 57, 63, 57, 63, …

The M4 competition added a fifth, Naive2: deseasonalise the series with classical multiplicative decomposition (ts2), apply the naive forecast, and multiply the seasonal indices back. It is the reference point of M4's score.

2. Simple exponential smoothing

Simple exponential smoothing (SES) keeps one number, the level ℓ_t, its current estimate of the series' underlying value. When a new observation arrives, the level moves a fraction α toward it:

ℓ_t = ℓ_{t−1} + α (y_t − ℓ_{t−1})        equivalently   ℓ_t = α y_t + (1 − α) ℓ_{t−1}
ŷ(T+h | T) = ℓ_T   for every h

The term y_t − ℓ_{t−1} is the one-step forecast error e_t: how surprised the model was. The first form is called the error-correction form: the new level is the old level plus a fraction of the surprise. Worked update: with α = 0.3, a current level of 100 and a new observation of 110, the error is 10, so the new level is 100 + 0.3 × 10 = 103. If the next observation is 104, the error is 1 and the level becomes 103.3. If instead it is 90, the error is −13 and the level falls to 99.1. The forecast for every future period is the current level.

Substituting the recursion into itself shows what SES really computes: ℓ_T = α y_T + α(1−α) y_{T−1} + α(1−α)² y_{T−2} + … + (1−α)^T ℓ_0. With α = 0.3 the weights are 0.30, 0.21, 0.147, 0.103, 0.072: the last five observations carry 83% of the weight. With α = 0.7 the newest alone carries 70%. The two limits connect to the baselines: α = 1 puts all weight on y_T, which is the naive method; α near 0 averages over the whole history, approaching the mean method. SES sits between them and lets the data choose.

Two values must be chosen: α and the initial level ℓ_0. Both are fitted by minimising the sum of squared one-step errors over the history (or, equivalently for Gaussian errors, maximising the likelihood). The flat forecast is not a weakness; it is the optimal forecast if the series is a level that moves randomly with no predictable direction, which describes many demand series surprisingly well.

3. Holt's trend, and damping it

If the series trends, a flat forecast lags behind. Holt's linear method adds a second state, the trend b_t, the estimated change per step, with its own smoothing parameter β:

ℓ_t = α y_t + (1 − α)(ℓ_{t−1} + b_{t−1})
b_t = β (ℓ_t − ℓ_{t−1}) + (1 − β) b_{t−1}
ŷ(T+h | T) = ℓ_T + h · b_T

The level update now moves toward y_t from where the level was expected to be (last level plus last trend). The trend update moves toward the latest observed change in level. Worked step: level 100, trend 2, α = 0.5, β = 0.2, new observation 106. Expected position 102; new level 0.5 × 106 + 0.5 × 102 = 104. Observed level change 104 − 100 = 4; new trend 0.2 × 4 + 0.8 × 2 = 2.4. Forecasts: 106.4, 108.8, 111.2 for h = 1, 2, 3.

Holt extrapolates the trend forever, which is usually too optimistic: growth slows, markets saturate, and a slope estimated from noisy recent data is often too steep. The damped trend method multiplies the trend by a factor φ (typically 0.8 to 0.98) each step ahead, so the forecast is ℓ_T + (φ + φ² + … + φ^h) b_T. Because the sum converges to φ/(1−φ), the forecast levels off at ℓ_T + b_T·φ/(1−φ). With b = 2.4 and φ = 0.9 the increments are 2.16, 1.94, 1.75, … and the forecast rises at most 21.6 above the level. Damped trend is one of the most consistently accurate methods across large collections of series, and it is one third of M4's Comb benchmark.

4. Holt-Winters: adding the season

The Holt-Winters method adds a seasonal state s_t for each position in the season (twelve values for monthly data), updated by a third parameter γ. In the additive version the level is updated from the deseasonalised observation y_t − s_{t−m}, and the season from the detrended observation y_t − ℓ_t; the forecast is ℓ_T + h·b_T + s for the matching season. In the multiplicative version, seasonal values are factors (December = 1.2) and the subtraction becomes division. Because each seasonal value is updated only once per cycle — the January factor only when a January arrives — the season adapts slowly, and γ is usually small. An alternative that is often just as good is to deseasonalise first with a decomposition, forecast with SES or damped trend, and reseasonalise, which is exactly what the M4 benchmarks do.

5. The ETS framework

Hyndman and colleagues organised exponential smoothing into a family of statistical models named ETS, for Error, Trend, Seasonal. Each letter takes a value: Error is A (additive) or M (multiplicative); Trend is N (none), A (additive) or Ad (additive damped); Seasonal is N, A or M. SES is ETS(A,N,N); Holt is ETS(A,A,N); damped Holt is ETS(A,Ad,N); multiplicative Holt-Winters with multiplicative errors is ETS(M,A,M).

The point of writing them as models is that each becomes a state-space model: hidden states (level, trend, season) evolve by the update equations, and observations are the states plus a random error. That gives a likelihood, which brings three benefits. Parameters (α, β, γ, φ and the initial states) are fitted by maximum likelihood. Models can be compared with an information criterion such as AICc, which rewards fit and penalises the number of parameters, so an automatic procedure can fit every sensible ETS combination and pick the best. And prediction intervals come from the model's error distribution rather than guesswork. Additive and multiplicative errors give identical point forecasts but different intervals: multiplicative errors make interval width proportional to the level, which suits most business data.

6. Autoregressive models

The ARIMA family starts from a different question: how does each value depend on the values before it? An autoregressive model of order p, AR(p), says y_t = c + φ_1 y_{t−1} + … + φ_p y_{t−p} + ε_t, where ε_t is white noise. It is a linear regression of the series on its own lags, and can be fitted by least squares, exactly as in the lab.

Worked AR(1) forecast. Suppose a fitted AR(1) is y_t = 20 + 0.6 y_{t−1} + ε_t, and the last observation is 60. The forecast replaces the future shock by its expected value, zero, and uses each forecast as the next input:

ŷ(T+1) = 20 + 0.6 × 60   = 56
ŷ(T+2) = 20 + 0.6 × 56   = 53.6
ŷ(T+3) = 20 + 0.6 × 53.6 = 52.16
long-run mean μ = c / (1 − φ) = 20 / 0.4 = 50

The series is currently 10 above its mean; the gap shrinks to 6, then 3.6, then 2.16 — multiplied by φ = 0.6 each step. That is mean reversion, the defining behaviour of a stationary AR process. The prediction interval widens with h but levels off at the series' own spread, σ/√(1 − φ²), because the far future is just "a typical value around 50". Contrast the random walk (φ = 1), whose forecast stays at 60 and whose interval widens without limit.

7. Moving-average terms and ARMA

A moving-average model of order q, MA(q), says y_t = c + ε_t + θ_1 ε_{t−1} + … + θ_q ε_{t−q}: the value depends on recent surprises rather than recent values. (The name is historical and unrelated to the moving-average smoother of ts2.) An MA(1) with θ = 0.5 means half of yesterday's surprise carries into today and then vanishes. MA terms capture short-lived effects of shocks; their forecasts revert to the mean after q steps.

An ARMA(p,q) model combines both: y_t = c + Σ φ_i y_{t−i} + ε_t + Σ θ_j ε_{t−j}. Mixed models can describe complicated autocorrelation patterns with few parameters. Because the shocks ε are not observed directly, MA and ARMA models are fitted by maximum likelihood rather than plain least squares, using the Kalman filter or a similar recursion to compute the shocks implied by the parameters.

8. ARIMA: differencing plus ARMA

ARMA models describe stationary series. To handle trends and wandering, ARIMA(p,d,q) differences the series d times (ts3) and fits an ARMA(p,q) to the result; forecasts of the differences are then summed back up ("integrated", the I in ARIMA) to give forecasts of the original series. Several familiar methods are special cases:

  • ARIMA(0,0,0) with a constant is the mean method; ARIMA(0,1,0) is the random walk, so its forecast is naive; ARIMA(0,1,0) with a constant is the drift method.
  • ARIMA(0,1,1) gives exactly the SES forecasts, with θ = α − 1. Rearranging the SES error-correction form shows the change y_t − y_{t−1} equals e_t − (1 − α) e_{t−1}, an MA(1) in the differences.
  • ARIMA(0,2,2) corresponds to Holt's linear method.

So the two families overlap: all linear ETS models have ARIMA equivalents, while multiplicative ETS models have no ARIMA counterpart and many ARIMA models have no ETS counterpart.

9. Seasonal ARIMA

For seasonal data, seasonal ARIMA, written ARIMA(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)_m, adds seasonal differencing D (usually 0 or 1) and seasonal AR and MA terms at multiples of the period m. A seasonal AR(1) term at m = 12 says this January depends on last January. The most famous example is the airline model, ARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)_12, fitted by Box and Jenkins to monthly airline passengers in their 1970 book: take logs, difference once and seasonally once, and model the result with one ordinary and one seasonal MA term. It remains a strong default for monthly seasonal series. Seasonal ARIMA needs the period to be a whole number and not too large; for daily data with a yearly cycle (m = 365.25), use Fourier terms as regressors (ts2) with ARIMA errors instead.

10. Choosing orders: Box-Jenkins and automatic search

The classical Box-Jenkins workflow has four steps. Make the series stationary (transform, then difference d times as tests and the ACF suggest). Identify candidate orders from the ACF and PACF of the stationary series: an AR(p) shows a PACF that cuts off after lag p, an MA(q) an ACF that cuts off after lag q. Estimate the candidates by maximum likelihood. Check the residuals; if structure remains, revise.

At scale nobody inspects plots. The widely used Hyndman-Khandakar algorithm (auto.arima in R's forecast package, AutoARIMA in Python's StatsForecast) automates it: choose d by repeated KPSS tests, choose D by a seasonal test, then search p and q (and P, Q) stepwise from a few starting models, moving to neighbouring orders while AICc improves. An important subtlety: AICc can only compare models fitted to the same data, so it cannot choose d — differencing changes the data. That is why d comes from tests and p, q from AICc.

11. Checking residuals

A fitted model's one-step residuals should look like white noise. Plot them, plot their ACF, and run a Ljung-Box test on the first ten or so lags (2m for seasonal data). A significant result means the model left predictable structure on the table. Also check the residuals' mean (should be near zero — otherwise forecasts are biased) and spread over time (growing spread means you should have used logs or multiplicative errors). Residual checks do not tell you the model forecasts well — only out-of-sample testing does (ts5) — but they catch obvious mis-specification cheaply.

12. Two special-purpose methods: Theta and Croston

The Theta method won the M3 competition in 2000. It decomposes a deseasonalised series into "theta lines" that exaggerate or remove its curvature, forecasts each, and combines them. Hyndman and Billah later showed that its standard form is equivalent to SES with a drift equal to half the slope of a straight line fitted to the data: a simple, robust trend dampener. It is one of the M4 benchmarks.

Croston's method handles intermittent demand, such as spare parts that sell on a few days a year. Applying SES directly to a series of mostly zeros gives a forecast that dips after every zero and jumps after every sale. Croston smooths two things separately — the size of non-zero demands and the interval between them — and forecasts their ratio, an average demand per period. A bias-corrected variant (SBA, Syntetos-Boylan approximation) multiplies by (1 − α/2). These forecasts feed inventory rules rather than being used as literal daily predictions.

13. Putting it together: choosing a model for one series

Here is how the pieces combine on a real decision. Suppose you must forecast monthly sign-ups for a subscription product 12 months ahead, with five years of history. You plot the series: it grows, with a December dip and a January spike, and the swings grow as the product grows. So you take logs (ts2). You compute the baselines on a backtest with several origins (ts5): seasonal-naive and Naive2 give a mean absolute percentage error of, say, 9% and 8%. You then fit candidate models on the log scale: auto-ETS picks ETS(A,Ad,A) — additive errors, damped trend, additive season on the log scale, which is multiplicative on the original scale — and auto-ARIMA picks ARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)_12, the airline model. Their residuals pass Ljung-Box tests. On the backtest, ETS scores 6.1%, ARIMA 6.4% and their average 5.8%.

What do you ship? The average, most likely: it is the most accurate, it hedges the model choice, and it is still cheap and explainable. You then check two things the backtest score does not show. First, the long-horizon shape: does the forecast for month 12 make business sense, or does an undamped trend imply growth the marketing team does not believe? Second, the intervals: on the backtest, did the 80% intervals contain about 80% of outcomes? If they covered only 65%, widen them using backtest residuals before anyone plans against them. This whole procedure — transform, baselines, a few principled candidates, backtest, combine, sanity-check shape and intervals — is what interviewers want to hear when they ask "how would you forecast this series?"

Information criteria, and what they can and cannot do

AIC = −2 log L + 2k, where L is the maximised likelihood and k the number of estimated parameters; AICc adds a correction 2k(k+1)/(n − k − 1) that matters for short series. Lower is better. AIC approximates how well a model would predict new data from the same process, so it is a principled replacement for a hold-out set when data is short. Its limits are worth stating in an interview: it compares models on the same data only (not across different d, transforms, or ETS versus ARIMA with differencing), it assumes the model family contains something close to the truth, and it measures one-step-ahead fit, not the multi-step accuracy that many decisions need. When in doubt, backtest.

State space and the Kalman filter

Both ETS and ARIMA can be written in state-space form: a state vector (level, trend, seasonal values, or lagged values and shocks) evolves linearly with noise, and observations are a linear function of the state plus noise. The Kalman filter processes observations one at a time, keeping a mean and a covariance for the state: predict the next state, compare the prediction with the observation, and correct the state by a gain that depends on how uncertain the prediction was relative to the observation noise. It computes the exact Gaussian likelihood, handles missing observations naturally (skip the correction step), and gives forecast distributions directly. Exponential smoothing's update ℓ_t = ℓ_{t−1} + α e_t is a Kalman correction with a fixed gain α — the "single source of error" formulation used by ETS.

Prediction intervals and their honesty

For ETS(A,N,N), the h-step forecast variance is σ²[1 + α²(h − 1)]: it grows linearly in h, at a rate set by α. For a stationary AR(1), it is σ²(1 + φ² + … + φ^{2(h−1)}), which levels off. Model-based intervals are usually too narrow in practice because they ignore uncertainty in the parameters, in the model choice and in future structural change. Empirical studies routinely find nominal 95% intervals covering 70-90% of outcomes. Remedies include simulating with parameter uncertainty, calibrating intervals on backtest residuals (ts8 covers conformal methods), and widening for horizons where the model has no track record.

Why combinations win

If two forecasts have errors with the same variance σ² and correlation ρ, their average has error variance σ²(1 + ρ)/2, smaller than σ² unless ρ = 1. Methods built on different assumptions — SES assumes no trend, Holt a persistent trend, damped a fading one — make different mistakes, so their errors are imperfectly correlated and averaging cancels part of them. Combining also removes the risk of a bad model choice, which matters because selection on short noisy histories is unreliable. Equal weights usually beat "optimal" weights estimated from data, because the weight estimates are noisy — the forecast combination puzzle. This is why M4's Comb benchmark (equal average of SES, Holt and damped) was so hard to beat, and why the second-placed M4 entry used a learned combination of nine methods.

Regression with ARIMA errors

Real forecasts need covariates: price, promotions, holidays, temperature. The classical way is regression with ARIMA errors (often called ARIMAX, loosely): y_t = β·x_t + η_t, where η_t follows an ARIMA model. The regression captures the effect of the covariates; the ARIMA errors capture the autocorrelation the covariates leave. Fitting by ordinary least squares and ignoring the autocorrelated errors gives the spurious-regression trouble of ts3 (overconfident coefficients); fitting the whole thing by maximum likelihood fixes it. Future covariate values must be known or forecast — known-future covariates (ts1) fit naturally, past-only ones need their own forecasts.

Bias after transformation

Box-Cox and log transforms (ts2) are routine before ETS and ARIMA. Forecasts back-transformed with exp() are medians, not means. For a log model with forecast variance σ_h² on the log scale, the mean is approximately exp(μ_h)(1 + σ_h²/2); for long horizons and noisy series the gap is several percent, and it compounds when you add up thousands of product forecasts into a total. Libraries offer a bias-adjust option; know which one your planners need.

Local models at global scale

Classical models are local — one model per series — but they scale well because each fit is tiny. Implementations such as StatsForecast compile the fitting loops and parallelise across series, fitting AutoETS or AutoARIMA to hundreds of thousands of series in minutes on a laptop-class machine. The limitations are not speed but information: a local model cannot learn from other series, cannot use cross-series effects such as a promotion on a substitute product, and has nothing to fit for a new product. That is the opening for the global ML models of ts6 — and the M4 winner was precisely a bridge: local exponential smoothing for each series' level and season, with a global recurrent network learning the rest across series.

ETS or ARIMA? A practical answer

For series with clear level, trend and seasonal structure that grows with the level, ETS is usually the more natural choice: multiplicative components have no ARIMA equivalent, and its parameters map onto things a planner understands. ARIMA is more natural when the series' memory is the main structure — mean-reverting operational metrics, series with short-lived shock effects, or residuals of a regression on covariates. Large comparisons find neither family dominates, and in automated systems the pragmatic answer is often to fit both and average, or to select per series by backtest rather than by AICc (which cannot compare them when differencing differs). In an interview, saying "both, compared on a rolling-origin backtest against seasonal-naive" beats a confident one-family answer.

Robustness and outliers

Squared-error fitting lets one extreme value drag parameters: a single promotion spike can push α up, making the model jumpy everywhere. Remedies include cleaning outliers before fitting (replace with an interpolated value and record the event as a covariate), using robust losses, or modelling known events as regressors. The same applies to structural breaks: an ETS model will eventually adapt to a new level through α, but slowly if α is small; an intervention regressor at the break date lets it adapt at once.

The case: M4, read from the official scores

The M4 competition ran in 2018, organised by the University of Nicosia's forecasting centre under Spyros Makridakis, with results published in the International Journal of Forecasting in 2020. Participants forecast 100,000 real series: 23,000 yearly (horizon 6), 24,000 quarterly (8), 48,000 monthly (18), 359 weekly (13), 4,227 daily (14) and 414 hourly (48), from domains including finance, industry, demographics and macro- and micro-economics. Accuracy was scored by sMAPE (a symmetric percentage error) and MASE (error scaled by the in-sample seasonal-naive error), both defined in ts5, and combined into the OWA: the average of a method's sMAPE and MASE, each divided by Naive2's value. Naive2 therefore has OWA 1.000, and lower is better.

The organisers' Evaluation and Ranks spreadsheet, published on GitHub with every submission's forecasts, gives the numbers. The winner, Slawek Smyl of Uber, had sMAPE 11.374 and OWA 0.821, an 8.6% improvement on the Comb benchmark. His method was a hybrid: per-series exponential-smoothing equations estimated each series' level and seasonality, which normalised the inputs to a recurrent neural network trained across all series that forecast the rest — local classical structure plus global learning. Second place (Montero-Manso, Talagala, Hyndman and Athanasopoulos, OWA 0.838) used a gradient-boosted model to learn, from features of each series, how to weight nine classical forecasting methods. Most of the remaining top ten were combinations of statistical methods.

The benchmarks tell the classical story. Comb, the equal average of SES, Holt and damped trend on deseasonalised data, scored OWA 0.898 (sMAPE 12.555), and Theta 0.897; only 17 submissions beat Comb. Damped trend alone reached 0.907, SES 0.975 and Holt 0.971. Seasonal-naive was worse than Naive2 (1.078), because Naive2 handles seasonality by decomposition rather than by repeating one noisy year. The two machine-learning benchmarks, an MLP and an RNN fitted per series, scored OWAs of about 1.64 and 1.48 — worse than Naive2 — which the organisers took as evidence that ML methods needed more data and care than per-series fitting allowed. M5, two years later, with related retail series and covariates, reversed this for global tree models (ts6), which is the real lesson: method rankings depend on the data, and benchmarks are how you find out for yours.

How classical models fail in production

Unstable automatic selection. Auto-ARIMA or auto-ETS re-run every week can pick a different model each time because several candidates have nearly equal AICc; forecasts jump and planners lose trust. Fix the structure and re-estimate only parameters, re-select monthly, or average the top few models. Track forecast revision (how much this week's forecast for a fixed future date changed from last week's) as a first-class metric.

Explosive or silly trends. An undamped trend fitted on a short burst of growth extrapolates to absurd values at long horizons. Use damped trends by default for horizons beyond a few periods, and cap forecasts with business constraints (capacity, market size).

Short and new series. With fewer than two seasons of data, seasonal models cannot be estimated; with a few points, nothing can. Fall back to a simpler model, borrow seasonal indices from the product category, or use a global model.

Multiplicative models and zeros. Multiplicative errors or seasonality fail when values are zero or near zero. Intermittent series need Croston-type methods or a count-distribution model.

Monitoring and tooling

Each fitted model's parameters are signals: an α that jumps from 0.1 to 0.9 between re-fits says the series' behaviour changed (or bad data arrived). Monitor parameter drift, residual autocorrelation (Ljung-Box p-values), forecast error by horizon against the seasonal-naive baseline, and forecast revisions. In Python, StatsForecast provides AutoETS, AutoARIMA, AutoTheta, Croston variants and the baselines with fast compiled fitting; statsmodels provides ETSModel, ARIMA/SARIMAX and diagnostics; R's fable and forecast packages are the reference implementations by the method's authors.

    level = 200 * np.exp(rng.normal(0.004, 0.003) * t)          # gentle growth, varies by series
    season = 1 + rng.uniform(0.05, 0.3) * np.sin(2 * np.pi * (t + rng.integers(12)) / m)
    noise = np.exp(np.cumsum(rng.normal(0, 0.02, n)) * rng.uniform(0, 1))  # some series wander
    return level * season * noise * rng.lognormal(0, 0.04, n)

def seasonal_index(y):
    """Classical multiplicative indices from a 2x12 moving average (as in the M4 benchmarks)."""
    ma = np.convolve(y, np.r_[0.5, np.ones(11), 0.5] / 12, mode="same")
    ratio = (y / ma)[6:-6]
    pos = np.arange(6, len(y) - 6) % m
    idx = np.array([ratio[pos == k].mean() for k in range(m)])
    return idx / idx.mean()

def ses(y, alpha):
    level = y[0]
    for v in y[1:]:
        level = level + alpha * (v - level)      # move a fraction alpha toward the new value
    return level

def holt(y, alpha, beta, phi, h):
    level, trend = y[0], y[1] - y[0]
    for v in y[1:]:
        prev = level
        level = alpha * v + (1 - alpha) * (level + phi * trend)
        trend = beta * (level - prev) + (1 - beta) * phi * trend
    damp = np.cumsum(phi ** np.arange(1, h + 1))   # phi + phi^2 + ... + phi^h
    return level + damp * trend

def sse_fit(f, y, grid):
    """Pick parameters by one-step-ahead squared error on the training data."""
    best = min(grid, key=lambda g: sum((y[i] - f(y[:i], *g)) ** 2 for i in range(10, len(y))))
    return best

def ar_diff(y, p, h):
    """ARIMA(p,1,0) by least squares: regress the change on its own p lags, forecast, add back up."""
    d = np.diff(y)
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(d) - p)] + [d[p - k - 1:len(d) - k - 1] for k in range(p)])
    w, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, d[p:], rcond=None)
    hist = list(d)
    for _ in range(h):
        hist.append(w[0] + sum(w[k + 1] * hist[-k - 1] for k in range(p)))
    return y[-1] + np.cumsum(hist[-h:])

def smape(a, f): return np.mean(200 * np.abs(a - f) / (np.abs(a) + np.abs(f)))
def mase(train, a, f): return np.mean(np.abs(a - f)) / np.mean(np.abs(train[m:] - train[:-m]))

scores = {k: [] for k in ["Naive", "sNaive", "Drift", "Naive2", "SES", "Holt", "Damped", "Comb", "ARIMA(2,1,0)"]}
for _ in range(100):
    y = make_series()
    train, test = y[:-H], y[-H:]
    idx = seasonal_index(train)
    pos_tr = np.arange(len(train)) % m
    pos_te = np.arange(len(train), len(y)) % m
    des = train / idx[pos_tr]                       # deseasonalised training data
    a = sse_fit(lambda s, al: ses(s, al), des, [(x,) for x in np.linspace(0.05, 1, 20)])[0]
    hb = sse_fit(lambda s, al, be: holt(s, al, be, 1.0, 1)[0], des, [(x, z) for x in (0.1, 0.3, 0.6, 0.9) for z in (0.01, 0.05, 0.2)])
    db = sse_fit(lambda s, al, be, ph: holt(s, al, be, ph, 1)[0], des, [(x, z, p) for x in (0.1, 0.3, 0.6, 0.9) for z in (0.01, 0.05, 0.2) for p in (0.8, 0.9, 0.98)])
    reseason = idx[pos_te]
    fc = {
        "Naive": np.repeat(train[-1], H),
        "sNaive": np.resize(train[-m:], H),
        "Drift": train[-1] + (train[-1] - train[0]) / (len(train) - 1) * np.arange(1, H + 1),
        "Naive2": np.repeat(des[-1], H) * reseason,
        "SES": np.repeat(ses(des, a), H) * reseason,
        "Holt": holt(des, *hb, 1.0, H) * reseason,
        "Damped": holt(des, *db, H) * reseason,
        "ARIMA(2,1,0)": ar_diff(des, 2, H) * reseason,
    }
    fc["Comb"] = (fc["SES"] + fc["Holt"] + fc["Damped"]) / 3
    for k, f in fc.items():
        scores[k].append((smape(test, f), mase(train, test, f)))

base = np.mean(scores["Naive2"], axis=0)
print(f"{'method':14s} sMAPE   MASE   OWA")
for k, v in scores.items():
    s, q = np.mean(v, axis=0)
    print(f"{k:14s} {s:6.2f} {q:6.3f} {0.5 * (s / base[0] + q / base[1]):6.3f}")

What to look for. Methods that ignore seasonality (naive, drift) are far worse than those that handle it, and Naive2 (deseasonalise, then naive) beats seasonal-naive, as in M4. Damped trend beats Holt, and Comb is close to the best single smoothing method without anyone choosing it. On this synthetic data, whose series drift with persistent growth, ARIMA(2,1,0) on the deseasonalised series wins; on M4 itself it was combinations that led. The point is the procedure: every method is scored on the same holdout against the same baseline.

Work it out

Monthly demand for a product: the fitted SES model has α = 0.2 and current level 500. A Holt model on the same data has level 500, trend +8 per month, α = 0.3, β = 0.1. This month's actual demand is 540.

(a) Update both models. (b) Give each model's forecasts for 1, 6 and 12 months ahead. (c) A damped version uses φ = 0.9. What is its 12-month-ahead forecast after the update, and why might a planner prefer it?

Answer

(a) SES: error 540 − 500 = 40, new level 500 + 0.2 × 40 = 508. Holt: expected position 500 + 8 = 508; new level 0.3 × 540 + 0.7 × 508 = 162 + 355.6 = 517.6; new trend 0.1 × (517.6 − 500) + 0.9 × 8 = 1.76 + 7.2 = 8.96.

(b) SES: 508 for every horizon. Holt: 517.6 + 8.96h → 526.6, 571.4, 625.1 for h = 1, 6, 12.

(c) Damped sum for h = 12: φ(1 − φ¹²)/(1 − φ) = 0.9 × (1 − 0.2824)/0.1 ≈ 6.46, so the forecast is 517.6 + 6.46 × 8.96 ≈ 575.5, about 50 units below Holt's 625. A planner might prefer it because a year of uninterrupted 9-a-month growth is a strong assumption from one month's surprise; damped trends are empirically more accurate at long horizons, and over-forecasting a year out ties up inventory.
