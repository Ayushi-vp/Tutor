Two teams present demand forecasts for the same 5,000 products. Team A reports a mean absolute percentage error of 18%; team B reports a root mean squared error of 42 units. Team A's model was evaluated on the last four weeks of data; team B's on a random 20% of days. Team A's MAPE excluded the 600 products that had zero sales in some week, because the metric divided by zero. Which model is better? Nobody in the room can say, and the decision — which model will drive ordering for every store — gets made on presentation style.

Evaluation is where forecasting projects most often go wrong, and the errors are rarely in the models. They are in how the test was set up and which number was reported. A single test period happens to contain a holiday or happens not to. A metric that divides by the actual value goes wild on slow-moving products. A feature quietly computed with future data makes the backtest look brilliant. A model wins on one-day-ahead accuracy and loses on the four-week horizon the buyers actually use. Each of these makes a worse model look better, and you only find out in production.

The fix is a discipline with two halves. The first is the backtest: replay history as if you had been running the forecaster all along — stand at many past dates, train with only what was known then, forecast, and score against what happened. The second is choosing metrics that are defined for your data, comparable across series, and aligned with the cost of errors in the decision the forecast serves. Done well, a backtest predicts production accuracy; done badly, it is the most convincing way to fool yourself.

This lesson builds both halves. It sets up expanding- and sliding-window backtests on a concrete 24-month example, computes five common metrics on the same five forecasts by hand, explains what each one rewards and where each one breaks, shows how to report accuracy by horizon and segment, and how to tell whether one model is really better than another. The case is Hyndman and Koehler's 2006 paper, which showed that several metrics used in the official M competitions were undefined or misleading on ordinary data and proposed MASE as the standard.

A flight simulator for forecasts

Pilots are not certified by flying one route on one calm day. They fly a simulator through hundreds of scenarios — storms, engine failures, crosswinds — and are judged on the whole record. A backtest is a flight simulator for a forecaster. You choose many past dates, called origins. At each origin you hide everything after it, train the model on what was known up to that date, ask it to forecast the next H periods, and then reveal what actually happened. The collection of errors across all origins is the forecaster's flight record: an average, a spread, and a breakdown by how far ahead it was looking.

The simulator analogy also explains the most important rule. A simulator is only useful if it reproduces real conditions faithfully. If the simulated cockpit showed the pilot the weather an hour ahead, every pilot would look brilliant. In a backtest, "showing the future" happens whenever anything used to build the forecast — a feature, a scaling factor, a hyperparameter choice, a revised data value — was computed with information from after the origin. The model then looks better in the backtest than it can ever be in production.

Metrics: what kind of wrong hurts?

Once you have the errors, you need a way to summarise them, and the choice is not neutral. Averaging the absolute errors (MAE) says every unit of error costs the same. Averaging squared errors (RMSE) says big misses are disproportionately bad. Dividing by the actual value (MAPE) says errors are judged relative to the size of the thing — and breaks when the thing is zero. Dividing by how well a naive forecast would have done (MASE) says "how much better than doing nothing clever?" Each is a different definition of a good forecast, and different definitions can crown different winners.

What a backtest produces

The output of a backtest is not a single score but a table with one row per forecast: which series, which origin, which horizon, what was forecast, what happened. Everything else is a query on that table. "Average error" is one query; "error at horizon 3 for slow-moving products in December" is another. Teams that keep only the headline number cannot answer the questions that come up when the model misbehaves, and the questions always come. So the habit to build is: run the backtest, keep the table, and compute metrics from it in as many cuts as the decision needs.

The decision also tells you which cut matters most. If buyers place orders that arrive in ten days and cover a week, the forecasts that matter are horizons 10 to 17, and accuracy at horizon 1 is a curiosity. If a capacity team cares about the busiest hour of each day, the error on the daily peak matters more than the average hourly error. Before choosing a metric, write down what the forecast is used for, at what horizon and level, and which kind of error is more expensive.

A reader who stops here should keep three rules: evaluate with many origins, never one split; recompute everything inside each fold from its training data only; and choose a metric that is defined for your data and matches the cost of errors in the decision.

1. The anatomy of a backtest

A backtest is defined by a handful of choices. The origins: the dates at which you pretend to forecast. The horizon H: how many steps ahead each forecast reaches, matching what the decision needs. The training window: which data before each origin the model may use. The step between origins: often the production re-run cadence (weekly re-forecasting means weekly origins). And the refit policy: whether the model is re-fitted at every origin or only periodically, again matching production.

For each origin T the procedure is the same. Cut the data at T. Build every input the model needs — features, scalers, holiday tables, hyperparameters — from data up to T only. Fit (or update) the model. Forecast T+1 to T+H. Store each forecast with its origin, horizon and the actual value when it arrives. At the end you have a table of (origin, horizon, series, actual, forecast) rows, from which every metric and breakdown is computed. Keeping that table, rather than only summary numbers, is what lets you later ask "how did it do on holiday weeks?" or "at horizon 14 for slow movers?"

This is called rolling-origin evaluation, time-series cross-validation or walk-forward validation — the same idea under three names. It is cross-validation (ml9) with one constraint: every test block lies after its training block.

2. Worked example: expanding versus sliding windows

Take 24 months of data and a decision that needs forecasts three months ahead. Choose an initial training length of 12 months (one full seasonal cycle at minimum, so seasonal models can be estimated) and a step of 3 months. An expanding window gives four folds:

FoldOrigin (last training month)TrainTest
1121–1213, 14, 15
2151–1516, 17, 18
3181–1819, 20, 21
4211–2122, 23, 24

That is 12 scored forecasts, 4 at each horizon h = 1, 2, 3. A sliding window of fixed length 12 would train on months 1–12, 4–15, 7–18 and 10–21: the same test blocks, but old data drops out. With a step of 1 instead of 3 you would get 10 origins (12 to 21) and 30 forecasts, at the cost of more fits and more strongly overlapping test blocks.

Which window? Expanding windows use all the data, which reduces the variance of estimates and suits stable processes. Sliding windows adapt to change by forgetting the past, which suits series with drifting behaviour, and they keep training cost constant. If production will use one, the backtest should use the same. If you are unsure, backtest both: a sliding window that wins says the past is going stale.

Two refinements matter in practice. A gap (or embargo) leaves out some time between training and test to mimic data latency — if last week's sales arrive on Wednesday but forecasts run on Monday, the most recent two days are not available at the origin, and the backtest should hide them too. And the number of origins should be large enough to cover the patterns you care about — at least a full seasonal cycle of origins, so holiday periods are included — while each fold's training data stays long enough to fit the model.

Suppose at one origin a model forecast five periods and these happened (error e = actual − forecast, so a positive error means the forecast was too low):

The training history before the origin was 90, 105, 95, 110, 100.

Mean error (bias). (−10 − 5 + 20 − 10 + 10)/5 = +1: on average the forecast was 1 unit too low. Bias is not accuracy — errors of +100 and −100 average to zero — but a persistent non-zero bias is the cheapest problem to fix and the most damaging if ignored, because it accumulates in inventory.

MAE. Mean absolute error: (10 + 5 + 20 + 10 + 10)/5 = 11 units. Easy to explain ("off by 11 units on average"), in the data's units, and robust to a single big miss.

RMSE. Root mean squared error: squares 100, 25, 400, 100, 100 sum to 725; mean 145; square root 12.04. RMSE is always at least MAE, and the gap grows with the spread of the errors: the single error of 20 contributes 400 of the 725. Use RMSE when big misses are disproportionately costly.

MAPE. Mean absolute percentage error: (10.0 + 6.25 + 16.7 + 20.0 + 6.67)/5 = 11.9%. Notice the 10-unit miss on an actual of 50 counts as 20% and the same 10-unit miss on 150 counts as 6.7%: MAPE weights low-volume periods heavily.

sMAPE. Symmetric MAPE divides by the average of actual and forecast: 200 × |e| / (|y| + |f|) gives 9.52, 6.06, 18.18, 18.18 and 6.90, averaging 11.8%.

MASE. Mean absolute scaled error divides MAE by the in-sample MAE of the one-step naive forecast. The training history's one-step changes are |105 − 90| = 15, |95 − 105| = 10, |110 − 95| = 15, |100 − 110| = 10, averaging 12.5. So MASE = 11 / 12.5 = 0.88: the model's errors are 88% the size of the errors naive made, one step at a time, on the training data. For seasonal data, use the seasonal-naive forecast in the denominator (lag m instead of lag 1).

4. What each metric rewards

Each metric is minimised by a different summary of the uncertain future. If you could choose a single number to forecast for a period whose outcome is uncertain, the number that minimises expected absolute error is the median of the distribution, and the number that minimises expected squared error is the mean (ml5). For symmetric uncertainty they coincide; for skewed data such as sales, where a few days are very large, the median is below the mean. A model tuned for MAE will therefore forecast lower than a model tuned for RMSE, and if those forecasts are summed (store forecasts into a regional total) the MAE-tuned totals will be biased low. The M5 organisers chose a squared-error metric (RMSSE) precisely because their data was intermittent: with mostly zeros, the median forecast is often zero, so absolute-error metrics reward predicting nothing.

MAPE is minimised by a value below the median: because over-forecasts of small actuals are punished hard and under-forecasts are capped at 100%, optimising MAPE pushes forecasts down. That is the asymmetry Hyndman and Koehler warned about. Know what your metric rewards before you tune to it.

5. Where percentage errors break

MAPE has three problems. It is undefined at zero: one zero actual makes it infinite, so people silently drop those periods, which drops exactly the slow-moving products that are hardest to forecast. It explodes near zero: in the lab, a series with actuals 0.5, 4, 6, 5 and 0.2 and quite reasonable forecasts gets a MAPE of 202%, driven by the two tiny actuals, while its MASE is 0.48 (better than naive). And it assumes a meaningful zero: a percentage error on temperatures in Celsius differs from one in Fahrenheit, because the zero point is arbitrary.

sMAPE was introduced to fix the asymmetry by dividing by the average of actual and forecast. It is bounded (at most 200%) and handles a zero actual if the forecast is non-zero, but it still divides by small numbers when both are small, and it is not actually symmetric: for a fixed actual, a forecast too low by 10 units gets a larger penalty than one too high by 10 units, because the too-high forecast enlarges the denominator. In the worked example, the forecast of 60 for an actual of 50 scores 18.2%, while a forecast of 40 would score 22.2%.

A business-friendly alternative is the weighted APE (WAPE, also called MAD/mean ratio): total absolute error divided by total actual volume, Σ|e| / Σ|y|. In the worked example, 55 / 500 = 11%. Because it divides by the total rather than by each period's value, small periods cannot dominate it, and it reads as "our errors are 11% of the volume we sell".

6. Scaled errors: MASE and RMSSE

To compare or average accuracy across many series, a metric must be scale-free: an error of 10 units means something different for a product selling 20 a day and one selling 20,000. Percentages are one way to remove scale, with the problems above. Scaled errors are the other: divide each error by a measure of how hard the series is to forecast in-sample. MASE uses the in-sample MAE of the naive (or seasonal-naive) one-step forecast. This denominator is zero only if the series never changes, so MASE is defined for intermittent data; it is in a familiar unit ("times the naive error"); and it is symmetric in the sense that over- and under-forecasts of the same size count the same. Its squared-error analogue, the RMSSE, divides mean squared error by the in-sample mean squared naive error and takes the square root; M5 used a revenue-weighted RMSSE (WRMSSE) over all 42,840 series.

One subtlety: MASE compares an h-step-ahead out-of-sample forecast with a one-step in-sample naive forecast, so a MASE above 1 at long horizons does not necessarily mean the model is worse than naive at those horizons — it may just be that 14-step-ahead forecasting is harder than one-step. To answer "is it better than naive?", compute the naive forecast in the same backtest and compare directly, as M4's OWA did with Naive2.

7. Error by horizon, segment and origin

A single average hides what you need to know. Report error by horizon: it usually grows with h, and rankings can flip. In the lab, the naive forecast's MAE at h = 1 is 6.83 but at h = 7 only 3.72, because seven days ahead lands on the same weekday as the origin — naive accidentally becomes seasonal-naive at the seasonal lag. A model that looks mediocre on average may be the best at the horizon the decision uses.

Report error by segment: high and low volume, intermittent and smooth, new and mature products, regions. Global averages are dominated by whichever segment has the most series or the largest errors. And look at error by origin: a model that is great on average but terrible at every holiday origin is a model that will fail every holiday.

8. Comparing two forecasters honestly

Suppose model B has a lower average error than model A across 14 origins. Is B really better, or did it get lucky? The per-origin loss differences d_k = loss_A − loss_B form a time series. If B is no better, their mean is zero. The Diebold-Mariano test computes the mean of d divided by its standard error and compares it with a normal distribution, with one important twist: the standard error must account for autocorrelation in d. When test windows overlap (origins one step apart, horizon 14), consecutive differences share most of their test periods and are strongly correlated, so a naive standard error is far too small. The usual fix includes autocovariances up to lag H − 1 in the variance. The lab avoids the issue by spacing origins 14 days apart with H = 14, so test windows do not overlap, and finds a statistic of 4.84 in favour of the seasonal smoothing model over seasonal-naive — a clear difference.

Statistical significance is not the whole story. A difference can be real and too small to matter, or large and too noisy to trust. Plot the per-origin differences: a model that wins on 12 of 14 origins is more trustworthy than one that wins on average because of two lucky origins.

9. Leakage checklist for backtests

The most common ways future information slips into a backtest are: features computed over windows that extend past the origin (centred rolling means, "average sales this month" including future days); preprocessing fitted on the whole dataset (scalers, target encoders, seasonal indices, outlier thresholds); hyperparameters tuned on the full history and then "backtested" with the same data; revised data (final figures instead of what was known on the date — ts12's point-in-time store); covariates whose actual values were unknown at the origin (ts1); and selecting the model family by peeking at the backtest results, then reporting the same backtest. The rule that covers all of them: everything the forecast depends on must be reproducible using only data stamped before the origin.

10. Metrics for the decision, not the leaderboard

Ultimately, the right metric is the cost of the decision the forecast drives. For inventory, the cost of an under-forecast is a lost sale and the cost of an over-forecast is holding or waste, usually different amounts; that leads to quantile forecasts and the pinball loss (ts8) and the newsvendor rule (ts12). For capacity planning, under-forecasting the peak is what hurts, so evaluate the error on the daily maximum, not the daily mean. A good evaluation reports a standard metric for comparability (MASE or WAPE) and a decision metric (simulated cost, service level, stock-outs) for the business.

11. A complete evaluation report, worked

Putting the pieces together, here is what a good evaluation of a new demand model looks like in practice. The setup: 3,000 store-product series, daily data, weekly re-forecasting, decisions use horizons 3 to 9 (orders arrive in three days and cover a week). The backtest: expanding window, 26 weekly origins covering six months including a holiday season, a two-day gap for data latency, everything refitted inside each fold. Candidates: seasonal-naive, the current production model, and the new model.

The report then has five parts. One: a headline table of WAPE and mean MASE (seasonal-naive scaling) for each model, at the decision horizons 3–9, with the per-origin spread shown as the 10th to 90th percentile across origins. Two: the same metrics by segment — top-revenue items, slow movers, new items — because the headline can hide a segment that got worse. Three: bias by horizon and segment, because a model that is accurate but consistently 5% low will empty shelves. Four: a significance check on the per-origin loss differences between the new and production models, with the number of origins on which the new model won. Five: the decision metric — simulated lost sales and inventory cost from feeding each model's forecasts into the ordering rule.

A typical honest conclusion reads like: "The new model reduces WAPE at horizons 3–9 from 31.2% to 29.4%, winning on 21 of 26 origins (DM p = 0.01); the gain is concentrated in high-volume items; slow movers are unchanged; bias is within ±1% everywhere; simulated lost sales fall 4% at equal inventory. Recommend a four-week shadow run before switching." Every number in it comes from the backtest table, and every claim can be checked.

Why ordinary k-fold can sometimes be used — and when it cannot

A result by Bergmeir, Hyndman and Koo (2018) is worth knowing: for purely autoregressive models whose residuals are uncorrelated — models that use only lagged values as inputs, fitted well — standard k-fold cross-validation on the rows of the lag-feature matrix gives valid error estimates, and uses data more efficiently than rolling origins. The intuition is that each row already contains its own past as features, and if the model captures the dependence, the rows' errors are nearly independent. The condition is strong: it fails when residuals are autocorrelated (an under-fitted model), when features include anything beyond lags of the target (calendar trends, covariates that drift), and for non-stationary series. In interviews, the safe answer is rolling origins; the senior addition is knowing when k-fold is defensible and why.

Overlapping windows and the variance of the backtest

Origins one step apart with horizon H create test windows that share H − 1 periods; their errors are highly correlated, so 100 origins may carry the information of a dozen. This does not bias the average error but makes it look more precise than it is. Two remedies: space origins at least H apart (fewer, nearly independent folds), or keep dense origins for a smoother average and compute uncertainty with methods that respect the dependence, such as a block bootstrap over origins or a heteroskedasticity-and-autocorrelation-consistent (Newey-West) variance. The same issue affects the Diebold-Mariano test, whose small-sample version (Harvey, Leybourne and Newbold) adds a correction and uses a t distribution.

Aggregating across series

How to combine accuracy over thousands of series is itself a modelling decision. An unweighted mean of MASE treats a product selling 2 units a week like one selling 20,000. A revenue-weighted mean (M5's WRMSSE weights each series by its dollar sales in the last 28 days of training) focuses on what matters financially. Geometric means of relative errors (the ratio of a model's error to a benchmark's error, per series) are robust to a few extreme series and have a clean interpretation ("on a typical series, B's error is 0.9 times A's"). Medians resist outliers but ignore the tail where the expensive failures live. Report more than one, and always look at the distribution across series.

Hierarchical evaluation

When forecasts exist at several levels (SKU, category, store, total), evaluate at every level decisions are made, because accuracy does not transfer: aggregation cancels errors, so a model can look excellent at the total and poor at SKU level, or vice versa. M5 scored all 12 aggregation levels and weighted them equally in WRMSSE, which forced methods to be good at the top and the bottom simultaneously. ts11 discusses how to make forecasts at different levels add up.

Probabilistic evaluation in one paragraph

Point metrics judge one number. For interval or distribution forecasts you need proper scoring rules, which reward both calibration (90% intervals should contain 90% of outcomes) and sharpness (narrower is better, if still calibrated). The pinball loss scores a single quantile; averaged over many quantiles it approximates the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS); the Winkler or interval score scores an interval by its width plus a penalty for misses. ts8 covers these in full; the backtest machinery here is identical — only the per-forecast score changes.

Backtesting the whole pipeline, not just the model

A model-only backtest assumes the data the model will see in production looks like the cleaned history. A pipeline backtest replays the production data path: the same queries against snapshot tables, the same late-arriving-data rules, the same feature code, the same fallback logic when a series has too little history. It is more work and it catches different bugs — the ones that cause most production surprises. Many teams run a pipeline backtest before any major change and keep a "shadow" deployment running the candidate model alongside production for a few weeks, scoring both live.

How many origins are enough?

There is no universal number, but there is a principle: the origins should sample the conditions the model will face. For weekly re-forecasting with yearly seasonality, a year of weekly origins (52) covers every season and holiday once; fewer origins risk an evaluation that never saw the hardest weeks. The statistical precision of the average error grows only with the number of effectively independent origins, so 300 daily origins with a 28-day horizon may be worth fewer than 20 independent ones. When compute is the constraint, it is usually better to evaluate fewer series over many origins than many series over few, because errors vary more across time than across similar series.

Evaluating a retraining policy, not a model

In production a model is retrained on a schedule, and its accuracy depends on the schedule as much as on the architecture. A backtest can evaluate the policy: "retrain weekly with a sliding 2-year window" versus "retrain monthly with all history". This matters most for global ML models (ts6), which are expensive to retrain, and for series with drift, where a stale model decays. Simulate the policy faithfully — at each origin use the model that would have been in production then, not one freshly trained — and you will often find that a cheaper model retrained more often beats an expensive one retrained rarely.

Metrics that encourage gaming

Optimising a metric changes forecasts in its favour, not necessarily the business's. MAPE-optimised forecasts drift low; MAE-optimised forecasts of intermittent items drift to zero; a bias-only target can be met by alternating large over- and under-forecasts. Forecast value added (FVA) analysis, common in industry, compares each step of the forecasting process (statistical model, planner overrides, consensus meeting) with the naive forecast on the same metric, to find steps that make accuracy worse — planners' manual adjustments, studies have found, often add bias in the optimistic direction.

The case: Hyndman and Koehler on forecast accuracy measures

By the early 2000s the forecasting literature used a zoo of accuracy measures, and the influential M-competitions had reported many of them, including MAPE, sMAPE, median versions and relative errors against the naive method. Rob Hyndman and Anne Koehler's paper "Another look at measures of forecast accuracy", published in the International Journal of Forecasting in 2006, surveyed them critically. Their abstract states the result bluntly: the measures used in the M-competition and the M3-competition, and many recommended by earlier authors, "are found to be degenerate in commonly occurring situations", and they proposed that the mean absolute scaled error become the standard for comparing accuracy across multiple series.

Their first example is M3 series N0472, a perfectly ordinary monthly series. Several measures came out undefined or infinite on it, not because of zeros in the data but because some consecutive observations took the same value — so the naive forecast's error for those periods was exactly zero, and relative errors that divide by the naive error blew up. "Something that occurs very often with real data," they note. They catalogued the other failure modes covered above: percentage errors undefined at zero, explosive near zero, assuming a meaningful zero, and asymmetric; sMAPE able to take negative values in its usual definition and not truly symmetric; relative errors with infinite variance. Scaling by the in-sample naive MAE avoids all of these, because that denominator averages over the whole history.

The paper changed practice. MASE was one of the two measures in the M4 competition (with sMAPE, combined into OWA), its squared-error cousin RMSSE was the basis of M5's WRMSSE, and it is the default comparison measure in Hyndman and Athanasopoulos's textbook and in most forecasting libraries. For an engineer the lesson generalises: before trusting a metric, ask how it behaves at zero, near zero, under asymmetric errors, and when its denominator can vanish.

How evaluation fails in production

The backtest–production gap. Production accuracy comes in worse than the backtest. Usual causes: the backtest used data cleaned or revised after the fact; production features arrive late, so the latest values are missing or partial at forecast time; the backtest period was calmer than the live one; or the production code differs from the backtest code. Remedies: point-in-time data, pipeline backtests, shadow runs, and alerting when live error leaves the backtest's range.

Silent metric definitions. Two dashboards show "MAPE" computed differently — one excludes zeros, one caps errors at 100%, one weights by volume. Teams argue about which model is better when they are reading different numbers. Define metrics once, in code, with tests, and name them precisely (WAPE, not "accuracy").

Averages that hide failures. A new model improves overall WAPE by 3% while getting much worse on the top 100 products, which carry 40% of revenue. Always break results down by revenue tier and by the segments planners care about.

What to monitor live

Once a model is deployed, the backtest becomes a live scorecard: store every forecast with its origin, horizon and model version, join actuals as they arrive, and compute the same metrics continuously by horizon and segment. Track bias separately from accuracy (bias is the earliest sign of a regime change), track forecast revisions (how much each week's forecast for a fixed date moves), and keep the seasonal-naive forecast running alongside so the live MASE-against-naive is always available. When live error drifts outside the range seen across backtest origins, investigate data first, then the model.

Knobs

The backtest knobs are the number and spacing of origins, the horizon, expanding versus sliding window and its length, the gap for data latency, and the refit cadence; the metric knobs are the error measure, the scaling (naive versus seasonal-naive), the aggregation across series (weights, mean versus median) and the levels of the hierarchy evaluated. Write them down in the evaluation config and keep them fixed while comparing models; change them deliberately and re-run every candidate when you do.

Goal: compute the five metrics on the lesson's example, run an expanding-window backtest of three forecasters with errors by horizon, test whether the winner is really better, and watch MAPE explode on near-zero values. NumPy only, gpt_env (Python 3.12). Save as ts5.py.

import numpy as np

def metrics(y, f, train, m=1):
    e = y - f
    scale = np.mean(np.abs(train[m:] - train[:-m]))         # in-sample (seasonal) naive MAE
    return {"ME": e.mean(), "MAE": np.abs(e).mean(), "RMSE": np.sqrt((e ** 2).mean()),
            "MAPE%": 100 * np.mean(np.abs(e / y)),
            "sMAPE%": 100 * np.mean(2 * np.abs(e) / (np.abs(y) + np.abs(f))),
            "MASE": np.abs(e).mean() / scale}

# 1. The worked example: five forecasts scored five ways
train = np.array([90, 105, 95, 110, 100.])
y = np.array([100, 80, 120, 50, 150.])
f = np.array([110, 85, 100, 60, 140.])
print({k: round(float(v), 3) for k, v in metrics(y, f, train).items()})

# 2. Expanding-window (rolling-origin) backtest on a synthetic daily series
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
n, m, H = 400, 7, 14
t = np.arange(n)
series = 50 + 0.02 * t + 8 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * t / m) + np.cumsum(rng.normal(0, 0.6, n)) + rng.normal(0, 2, n)

def ses_forecast(x, alpha=0.3):
    level = x[0]
    for v in x[1:]:
        level += alpha * (v - level)
    return np.repeat(level, H)

def seasonal_ses(x, alpha=0.3):
    """Remove day-of-week means (from the training window only), smooth, add them back."""
    pos = np.arange(len(x)) % m
    dow = np.array([x[pos == k].mean() for k in range(m)]) - x.mean()
    level = ses_forecast(x - dow[pos], alpha)
    future = np.arange(len(x), len(x) + H) % m
    return level + dow[future]

methods = {"naive": lambda x: np.repeat(x[-1], H),
           "snaive": lambda x: np.resize(x[-m:], H),
           "ses+season": seasonal_ses}
origins = range(200, n - H + 1, 14)                          # expanding window, step 14 days
errs = {k: [] for k in methods}
for T in origins:
    hist, actual = series[:T], series[T:T + H]               # train on everything before T only
    for k, fn in methods.items():
        errs[k].append(actual - fn(hist))
print(f"\n{len(list(origins))} origins x {H} horizons")
for k, E in errs.items():
    E = np.array(E)
    by_h = np.abs(E).mean(axis=0)
    print(f"{k:11s} MAE {np.abs(E).mean():5.2f}  h=1 {by_h[0]:5.2f}  h=7 {by_h[6]:5.2f}  h=14 {by_h[13]:5.2f}")

# 3. Is ses+season really better than snaive? Diebold-Mariano on squared-error differences,
#    using a variance that allows for autocorrelation in the loss differences (h - 1 lags)
d = (np.array(errs["snaive"]) ** 2 - np.array(errs["ses+season"]) ** 2).mean(axis=1)  # one value per origin
lr_var = d.var()  # origins are 14 days apart and H = 14, so consecutive windows do not overlap
dm = d.mean() / np.sqrt(lr_var / len(d))
print(f"DM statistic (positive favours ses+season): {dm:.2f}  over {len(d)} non-overlapping origins")

# 4. MAPE explodes on near-zero actuals; MASE does not
small = np.array([0.5, 4, 6, 5, 0.2])
fc = np.array([2, 5, 5, 6, 1.5])
print("\nnear-zero series:", {k: round(float(v), 2) for k, v in metrics(small, fc, np.array([3, 5, 4, 6, 2, 5.])).items()})

What to look for. The first line reproduces the hand calculations. In the backtest, errors grow with the horizon for the two sensible models, and naive shows the seasonal quirk: worst at h = 1, much better at h = 7 and 14, where it lines up with the same weekday. The smoothing model's day-of-week profile is computed from each fold's training data only — move that computation outside the loop and the backtest leaks. The Diebold-Mariano statistic of 4.84 is far beyond ±1.96. On the near-zero series, MAPE reports 202% for forecasts that MASE rates as half the naive error.

Work it out

A retailer forecasts weekly sales for two products over four test weeks.

Product A (big seller): actuals 1,000, 1,100, 900, 1,050; forecasts 950, 1,000, 1,000, 1,000. In-sample one-step naive MAE: 80.

Product B (slow mover): actuals 2, 0, 5, 1; forecasts 1, 1, 2, 2. In-sample one-step naive MAE: 2.

(a) Compute MAE and MASE for each. (b) What does MAPE do on product B? (c) The team wants one number for both products. What do you report?

Answer

(a) A: errors 50, 100, −100, 50 → MAE = 300/4 = 75; MASE = 75/80 = 0.94. B: errors 1, −1, 3, −1 → MAE = 6/4 = 1.5; MASE = 1.5/2 = 0.75.

(b) Week 2 has an actual of 0, so its percentage error is infinite and MAPE is undefined. Dropping that week gives 50%, 60% and 100% → MAPE ≈ 70%, which looks catastrophic next to A's ≈ 7.5%, even though B's forecast is relatively better than A's against naive.

(c) Average the scaled errors: mean MASE = (0.94 + 0.75)/2 ≈ 0.85 — both beat their in-sample naive, B by more. If the business cares about money, report a revenue-weighted version or WAPE as well: WAPE = (300 + 6)/(4,050 + 8) ≈ 7.5%, dominated by A as it should be when A is the money. Do not average MAPEs or raw MAEs across products of different scales.
