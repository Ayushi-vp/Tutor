A grocery chain has 3,000 stores and 40,000 products. It wants a forecast for every product in every store, every day, 28 days ahead, to drive ordering. The classical toolkit of ts4 — one exponential-smoothing or ARIMA model per series — runs into three walls. Most store-product series are sparse: a jar of a speciality sauce sells on a dozen days a year at one store, too few for any model to estimate a level, a trend and a weekly pattern. New products arrive every week with no history at all. And the most important drivers — promotions, price changes, holidays, a product's relationship with similar products — are shared across thousands of series, but a per-series model has to learn each of them from scratch in each series, from a handful of examples.

Machine learning answers all three walls with one move: instead of one model per series, train one model on rows drawn from all the series together. Each row says "for this product at this store, standing at this date, here is what we knew — the last few weeks of sales, the day of the week of the target date, whether there is a promotion, the price, the category — and here is what happened h days later." A gradient-boosted tree model trained on hundreds of millions of such rows learns that promotions lift snacks by 60%, that Saturdays are busy in suburban stores, and that a new crisp flavour behaves like its siblings — and applies all of it to every series, including the sparse and new ones.

That is not a hypothetical. In the 2020 M5 competition on Walmart's sales data, the winning team averaged 220 LightGBM models trained across many series, and almost every top entry did something similar; the winner beat the best classical benchmark by 22.4%. Two years earlier, in M4, where series were unrelated and had no covariates, pure ML entries had done badly. The difference was the data: related series plus covariates is exactly where a global ML model shines.

The price of this power is that the time structure is no longer built into the model. A tree does not know that rows are ordered; you encode the past as features, and every feature is a new opportunity to leak the future. This lesson teaches how to build the table — lags, rolling windows, calendar and covariate features — with a worked example; how to forecast many steps ahead recursively or directly, with numbers; how to scale series so one model can serve them all; which losses suit sales data; the classic leaks; and how the M5 winners put it together.

From a diary to a spreadsheet

Ts1 compared a time series to a diary whose pages refer to each other. A machine-learning model cannot read a diary; it reads spreadsheets, one row at a time, with no idea which row came before which. So the first job is to turn the diary into a spreadsheet in which each row carries its own context. Pick a page (the origin) and a day ahead you want to predict (the target). Copy into the row everything that was written before the origin that might help — yesterday's entry, last week's entry on the same weekday, the average of the last month — plus anything you knew in advance about the target day: it is a Saturday, it is a holiday, there is a planned sale. The target column is what was eventually written on the target day. Do this for many origins and you have a training table. This is called windowing.

One model for many series

Once every series is a stack of rows with the same columns, there is no reason to keep the stacks apart. Pile the rows from all 40,000 products into one table, add columns saying which product, store and category each row belongs to, and train a single model. That is a global model. A sparse series contributes its few rows and borrows the patterns learned from everyone else's. The analogy is a doctor who has seen thousands of patients: faced with a new one, they do not start from nothing, they recognise a type.

Two things must be true for a global model to work. The series must be related enough to share patterns — products in the same chain, sensors of the same kind, servers in the same fleet. And they must be on comparable scales: a model trained on raw units would spend all its effort on the bestsellers, so each series is usually divided by its own recent average and multiplied back afterwards.

Many steps ahead

Most decisions need several days ahead, not one. There are two basic ways. Recursive: train a model to predict tomorrow, predict tomorrow, pretend that prediction is real, predict the day after, and so on. Direct: train a separate model for each horizon — one that predicts two days ahead from today's information, another for three days, and so on. Recursive reuses one model but lets its mistakes snowball; direct never feeds mistakes back but needs many models. The best systems often do both and average.

A reader who stops here should hold on to three ideas: ML forecasting is feature engineering on a table built by windowing; every feature must be computable at the origin; and one global model across many related, scaled series usually beats thousands of separate ones.

1. The supervised table, precisely

Fix a horizon h. For a series i and an origin T, a training example is the pair (x_{i,T,h}, y_{i,T+h}), where x contains features computed from information available at T, and y is the value h steps later. Stacking examples over origins T (and series i, for a global model) gives a design matrix. Three rules make it honest. Every feature uses data stamped at or before T (or known-future plans for the target period as they stood at T). Origins in training all lie before the origins in validation and test (ts5). And the same code computes features for training, backtesting and live serving.

How many origins? Every day of history can be an origin, so a two-year daily series gives about 700 rows per horizon. Neighbouring rows are highly correlated (their lag features overlap almost entirely), so subsampling origins — every second or third day — often loses little and saves memory, as the lab does. For 120 million store-product series even one row per day is billions of rows, which is why M5 entrants trained per store or per department.

2. Feature families, with a worked row

Take one product at one store with daily sales for days 1–10: 12, 15, 11, 14, 20, 25, 13, 14, 16, 12. The origin is the end of day 10 and the target is day 12 (h = 2). Day 12 is a Friday with a planned promotion.

Lag features copy specific earlier values. The most recent value we know is day 10 = 12; relative to the target it is lag 2, the shortest lag allowed at h = 2. The same weekday a week before the target is day 5 = 20 (lag 7). For daily retail, the standard lags are the most recent available values, the same weekday in recent weeks (7, 14, 21, 28 days before the target), and the same date last year (364 days, which keeps the weekday aligned).

Rolling features summarise a trailing window ending at the origin. The 7-day mean over days 4–10 is (14 + 20 + 25 + 13 + 14 + 16 + 12)/7 = 114/7 ≈ 16.3. Others: a 28-day mean (the level), 7-day standard deviation (volatility), the share of zero days in the last 28 (intermittency), the maximum, and ratios such as 7-day mean ÷ 28-day mean (momentum). Rolling means of the same weekday ("average of the last four Fridays") are especially useful for weekly patterns.

Calendar features describe the target date: day of week (Friday), day of month (paydays), month, week of year, holiday flags, days until the next holiday and days since the last one (shopping builds up before Christmas), and special events. They are known-future by construction, so they are always safe.

Covariates: known-future ones for the target period (the promotion flag = 1, the planned price, whether a competitor sale is scheduled), lagged values of past-only ones (yesterday's footfall, last week's weather), and static ones (store, product, category, brand, price tier) encoded as categories. Gradient-boosted tree libraries such as LightGBM accept categorical columns directly.

Derived and target-based features: price relative to the product's usual price (a 20% discount is more informative than a raw price), the product's share of its category's sales, and target encodings (the average sales of this product on promotions). Target-based features must be computed only from data before the origin, inside each fold, or they leak.

The finished row for this example might read: lag2 = 12, lag7 = 20, mean7 = 16.3, mean28, std7, zero_share28, dow = Friday, promo = 1, price_ratio = 0.8, store = S3, category = sauces; target = whatever day 12's sales turned out to be.

3. Scaling series so one model can serve them all

A global model sees rows from products selling 2 a day and 2,000 a day. Without scaling, a squared-error loss is dominated by the big sellers, and the model's splits are about their levels. The common fix is to divide every value-based feature and the target by a per-series scale computed at the origin — the mean of the last 28 days plus a small constant to avoid dividing by zero — and to multiply predictions back. The model then learns shapes ("tomorrow is 1.3 times the recent level on Fridays with a promotion") that transfer across series. Including the scale itself as a feature lets the model still treat big and small series differently where it matters.

Scaling also fixes the extrapolation problem of trees. A regression tree predicts the average target in each leaf, so it can never forecast above the largest target in its training data. On a growing series this produces a ceiling; in the lab, a depth-3 tree fitted to a straight trend that reached 498 predicted 396 for the last day. Predicting the ratio to the recent level, or the difference from the last value, keeps targets in a stable range, and the trend is carried by the scale that is multiplied back.

4. Recursive forecasting, worked

Suppose a fitted one-step model is ŷ_{t+1} = 2 + 0.8 y_t (a deliberately simple model so the arithmetic is clear), and the last observed value is y_T = 50. The recursive forecast plugs each prediction in as the next input:

ŷ(T+1) = 2 + 0.8 × 50   = 42
ŷ(T+2) = 2 + 0.8 × 42   = 35.6
ŷ(T+3) = 2 + 0.8 × 35.6 = 30.48

If the actual value at T+1 turns out to be 46 rather than 42, the error of 4 at step 1 propagates: the two-step forecast used 42 where 46 was true, so it is off by an extra 0.8 × 4 = 3.2 from that source alone, on top of its own new error. With nonlinear models and many lags the propagation is harder to see but works the same way. There is also a subtler issue: the model was trained on real lags but at forecast time it is fed predicted lags, which are smoother than real data; the inputs at step 5 look unlike anything in training. This is called exposure bias, and it is one reason recursive forecasts can drift.

Recursive forecasting has real strengths: one model to train, maintain and explain; every step uses the most recent information (predicted or not); and the forecast path is internally consistent.

5. Direct forecasting, worked

A direct strategy trains a separate model for each horizon. The model for h = 2 learns the relationship between y_{T+2} and information at T. Fitted on the same data, it might come out as ŷ(T+2) = 8 + 0.6 y_T, giving 8 + 0.6 × 50 = 38 directly — no intermediate prediction involved. Its lags start at h: a direct model for day 7 cannot use the six days between origin and target. Each model is trained for exactly the quantity it predicts, so there is no compounding and no exposure bias.

The costs are H models to train (28 for M5's horizon, though one model with "horizon" as a feature is a common shortcut), less information per model (the model for h = 28 cannot use anything from the last 27 days), and possible inconsistency between neighbouring horizons, since separately trained models can give jagged paths. A third option, MIMO (multi-input multi-output), trains one model that outputs the whole vector of H values at once; it is natural for neural networks (ts7) and shares learning across horizons.

6. Global versus local, measured

The lab simulates 150 related series of daily counts that share a weekday shape and a promotion effect (+60%) but differ a hundredfold in size and in growth rate. It trains a ridge regression on scaled lag, rolling, calendar and promotion features in two ways: one global model on all series' rows, and one local model per series. With short histories the local models collapse toward noise, while the global model barely notices, because it learns the shared patterns from all 150 series' rows. That is the whole argument for global models in two lines of numbers. (Montero-Manso and Hyndman made it theoretically: a global model with enough capacity can represent any set of local models, and in practice it generalises better because it has far more data per parameter.)

7. Losses for sales data

Squared error assumes symmetric noise with constant variance and lets predictions go negative. Retail sales at product-store-day level are none of these: they are non-negative counts, often zero, right-skewed, and noisier when larger. Two losses fit better. Poisson loss is the negative log-likelihood of a Poisson distribution, ŷ − y·log ŷ (up to a constant), with ŷ kept positive by predicting log ŷ; it assumes variance equal to the mean. Tweedie loss uses a distribution with a power parameter p between 1 and 2 that combines a Poisson number of purchases with gamma-distributed sizes, giving an exact probability of zero plus a skewed continuous part — a good match for intermittent sales. LightGBM and XGBoost implement both as objectives (with tweedie_variance_power to set p), and most M5 winners used one of them.

Loss choice also decides what the point forecast means. Squared, Poisson and Tweedie losses all aim at the mean; absolute error aims at the median, which for an item that sells on 30% of days is zero. If you need quantiles for ordering, train with the pinball loss (ts8) instead.

8. Training with gradient-boosted trees

Gradient-boosted trees (ml10) became the default for tabular forecasting because they handle mixed feature types (numeric lags, categorical store IDs), missing values, nonlinear interactions ("promotion × weekend × category") and monotone effects without manual transformation, train quickly on very large tables, and need relatively little tuning. LightGBM, the most popular in competitions, speeds things up by bucketing feature values into histograms and growing trees leaf by leaf. Typical settings for forecasting: learning rate 0.03–0.1, a few hundred to a few thousand trees, 63–255 leaves, feature and row subsampling around 0.8, and early stopping on a time-ordered validation fold — though the M5 winner trained without early stopping and chose the number of trees by cross-validation over four 28-day windows.

9. Leaks specific to ML forecasting

ML pipelines leak in more places than classical models, because there are more features. The classic ones: rolling windows that are centred or include the target day (in the lab, a 3-day mean centred on the target scored an MAE of 8.4 against 13.4 for the honest trailing mean — a "discovery" that disappears in production); target encodings or category averages computed over the full dataset; scalers fitted on all data; lags computed with a shift of 1 in a direct model for h > 1 (so the model sees days between origin and target); features from tables that were later revised or back-filled; and past-only covariates used as if known (actual weather). The structural defence is to compute every feature with an explicit as-of time and to generate backtest rows and live rows with the same code path.

10. Forecasting at hierarchy levels and for new series

A global model naturally forecasts the bottom of a hierarchy (product-store), and aggregates are obtained by summing. That keeps forecasts coherent (they add up) but can lose accuracy at the top, which is why the M5 runner-up adjusted LightGBM's product-store forecasts with multipliers derived from N-BEATS forecasts of the top aggregation levels (ts11 covers reconciliation). For new series, a global model needs features that exist before history does: static attributes (category, brand, price tier, pack size), similarity to existing products, and "days since launch". Rows from past launches teach it what the first weeks of a product typically look like.

11. Validating a global model without fooling yourself

Hyperparameters — number of trees, leaves, learning rate, which lags to include — are chosen on validation data, and for forecasting that data must lie after the training data in time. The M5 winners all used the same pattern: hold out the last few 28-day windows before the test period as validation folds, train on everything before each fold, score each configuration across the folds, and look at both the average error and its spread. The winner picked the configuration that was accurate and stable across the four folds rather than the one that won a single fold. After choosing, retrain on all data up to the real origin and forecast.

Three details trip people up. First, early stopping on a validation fold and then reporting that same fold as the backtest score is double-dipping: the number of trees was tuned on the data being scored. Use separate folds for tuning and for the final estimate, or choose the tree count by cross-validation as the M5 winner did and train without early stopping. Second, features that depend on the origin (rolling means, scales, target encodings) must be recomputed for each validation fold's origin; computing them once at the final origin and reusing them for earlier folds leaks. Third, random row-level validation splits — common habits from tabular ML — reintroduce the shuffled-split problem of ts1, because rows from the same series and neighbouring dates are nearly duplicates.

12. How much history, and how many features?

Global models raise two sizing questions. How far back should lag features reach? Long enough to see the longest cycle that matters: for daily retail, lags out to 28 days capture monthly paydays and four weeks of weekday pattern, and a 364-day lag captures last year's same week. Longer lags cost memory and add noise, and for new series they are missing, which tree models handle (they route missing values) but linear and neural models need imputation for. How many training origins? Enough to cover the seasons that matter at least once or twice — a year or two for daily data — and more if the model must learn rare events such as holidays, each of which appears once a year.

On features, more is not automatically better. Each extra feature adds a chance to leak, a column to keep consistent between training and serving, and a source of spurious splits in small segments. Competitive M5 solutions used dozens to a hundred features, not thousands, and many strong entries used only calendar, price and a handful of lag and rolling features. A disciplined approach starts with a small set (recent lags, same-weekday lags, 7- and 28-day means, calendar, known-future covariates, static IDs), measures backtest improvement as groups are added, and keeps a group only if it helps across folds and segments.

13. Walking one prediction through the model

It helps to picture what a trained ensemble does with the worked row from section 2. The scaled target is sales ÷ recent level; suppose the 28-day mean is 15, so the lag-2 value of 12 becomes 0.8 and the same-weekday lag of 20 becomes 1.33. The first tree might split on the promotion flag (yes), then on category (sauces), and land in a leaf that says +0.45; the second tree splits on day of week (Friday) and says +0.12; later trees make smaller corrections from the lag ratios (a recent dip below the same weekday last week pulls slightly down). Adding the base value (1.0, the scaled mean) and a few hundred small corrections gives, say, 1.62 — and multiplying back by the scale of 15 gives a forecast of about 24 units. Each tree is a small, readable rule; the ensemble's power comes from summing hundreds of them, each fitted to what the previous ones got wrong.

Why global models generalise: a closer look

Montero-Manso and Hyndman (2021) showed that for any set of series, a global model with enough capacity can produce exactly the forecasts of any set of local models — so globality does not restrict what can be represented. The difference is statistical. A local model with k parameters fitted to each of N series estimates Nk parameters from N series' worth of data, each from its own short history. A global model of comparable expressiveness pools all the data, so each pattern is estimated from far more examples, and its complexity can grow with the total data rather than with each series' length. Their experiments showed that even surprisingly simple global models (a linear autoregression shared across series, with long lags) can beat per-series ARIMA and ETS on standard collections, and that higher-capacity global models keep improving as the number of series grows. The practical lesson: the more series you have, the more you should lean on global models, and the bigger they can be.

Feature importance is not causal

Gradient-boosted models report feature importances, and teams love to present them ("price is the third most important driver"). Importance measures how much the model used a feature to reduce loss, not what would happen if you changed it. Correlated features share importance arbitrarily; a promotion flag and the price ratio carry overlapping information, and which one the trees pick is partly chance. For "what if we cut the price 10%?" questions you need causal methods (ts11) or experiments, not importances. For debugging — "is the model leaning on a suspicious feature?" — importances and SHAP values (per-row contributions) are very useful, and a leaky feature often shows up as suspiciously dominant.

Ensembles across groupings, strategies and seeds

The M5 winner's 220 models were not 220 different algorithms. They were the same LightGBM recipe trained on different slices (per store, store-category, store-department) and with two strategies (recursive and non-recursive), then averaged per series. Each slice sees the data from a different angle — a store model learns store-specific weekday patterns, a department model learns product-type promotion effects — so their errors are partly independent and the average is better than any member. This is the combination principle of ts4 applied inside ML. Cheap versions in practice: average models trained with different random seeds and feature subsets, different lookbacks, or recursive and direct variants.

Exposure bias and ways around it

Recursive models are trained with true lags and served with predicted lags. Remedies include training on a mix of true and predicted lags (feeding the model its own one-step predictions during training, a form of what neural-network practitioners call scheduled sampling), adding noise to lag features during training so the model learns not to over-trust them, or using direct features for long horizons and recursive ones for short horizons. Another practical trick is a "horizon-aware" recursive model whose feature set includes the step number, so it can learn to discount its own fed-back predictions as they get older.

Intermittency and the two-part view

For very sparse series (sales on 5% of days), it can help to model separately whether a sale happens and how big it is when it does — a classifier for P(y > 0) and a regressor for E[y | y > 0] — and multiply them, the ML analogue of Croston's method. Tweedie loss achieves something similar inside one model. A third approach is to forecast at a coarser grain (weekly) where the series is smoother and allocate to days with a learned weekday profile. All three should be compared on a backtest using a metric that respects zeros (MASE, RMSSE, or a cost metric), never MAPE.

Probabilistic forecasts from tree models

Gradient-boosted trees produce point forecasts by default, but ordering and capacity decisions need quantiles (ts8). Three common routes: train one model per quantile with the pinball (quantile) loss, which LightGBM and XGBoost support directly; take the distribution implied by the loss — a Poisson or Tweedie model's predicted mean defines a whole distribution, from which quantiles follow; or calibrate intervals empirically from backtest residuals, grouped by segment and horizon. Separate quantile models can cross (the 90th percentile below the 80th) and need sorting; distribution-based quantiles are coherent but only as good as the distributional assumption. The M5 uncertainty track, run in parallel with the accuracy track on the same data, asked for nine quantiles per series, and LightGBM-based entries did well there too.

Covariates that themselves need forecasting

Some useful covariates are not known in advance: weather, competitor prices, macro indicators. Three options: use only their lags; plug in forecasts of them (and train on the forecasts that were available historically, not the actuals, or the model will over-trust them); or build scenarios ("if it is hot next week…") for planning rather than point forecasts. The hidden cost of the second option is a dependency on another forecasting system's history, which many organisations do not keep.

Censored demand in ML models

When stock runs out, sales stop at the stock level. Global models are especially exposed: if popular items regularly sell out, the model learns their sales, not their demand, and under-forecasts the items that matter most. Remedies: drop or down-weight stock-out days (requires inventory data), add an "in stock" feature and set it to 1 at prediction time, or use a censored likelihood. The M5 data had no inventory information, which the organisers acknowledged as a limitation.

The case: how M5 was won

The M5 accuracy competition (Kaggle, March–June 2020) asked for 28-day forecasts of 42,840 hierarchical Walmart series — 3,049 products across 10 stores in 3 states, aggregated through categories, departments, stores and states. Unlike M4, the data came with covariates: calendar and special events, prices, and SNAP days (days on which US food-stamp benefits could be used). 5,507 teams took part. Scoring was WRMSSE, a revenue-weighted RMSSE across all 12 aggregation levels (ts5).

According to the organisers' results paper, the winner — YeonJun Im, a senior undergraduate in South Korea, entered as YJ_STU — built an equal-weight combination of LightGBM models. Models were trained per store (10 models), per store and category (30) and per store and department (70), each in a recursive and a non-recursive version: 220 models in all, with each product-store series forecast as the average of six. They were trained with a Tweedie loss, without early stopping, on features covering identifiers, calendar, special events, promotions, prices and lagged sales in both recursive and non-recursive form. The configuration was chosen on the last four 28-day windows of the training data, looking at both the mean and the standard deviation of the error so that the solution would be accurate and robust. The result, WRMSSE 0.520, was 22.4% better than the best benchmark (exponential smoothing applied bottom-up), and all of the top 50 entries beat that benchmark by more than 14%.

The other top entries confirm the pattern. Second place combined store-level LightGBM models (using calendar and price features only, no past sales) with multipliers derived from N-BEATS forecasts of the top aggregation levels. Third place averaged 43 LSTM-based neural networks in the DeepAR style, also with a Tweedie loss. Fourth place trained non-recursive LightGBM models per store and per week of the horizon. Fifth place used recursive LightGBM per department with a Poisson loss and rescaled forecasts so store-department means matched the previous 28 days. The organisers' summary: M5 was the first M competition in which all top methods were "pure" ML and significantly better than all statistical benchmarks, with LightGBM the method of choice, cross-learning across series and combination of models the key ingredients.

What goes wrong in production

Training/serving skew. The backtest builds features with pandas over a historical table; production builds them with SQL over live tables. One computes "last 28 days" including today's partial data, the other not; one treats missing days as zero, the other as null. The model sees subtly different inputs and degrades. Defence: one feature definition, executed by the same code for both, plus parity tests that recompute yesterday's live features from history and compare.

Data latency. Sales for yesterday arrive at 10 am; the forecast runs at 6 am. If the backtest assumed yesterday was known, the shortest lag in production is really 2, and lag-1 features are stale or null. Build the latency into the backtest (a gap, ts5).

Retraining cost and drift. A global model over billions of rows is expensive to retrain, so it is retrained weekly or monthly, while the world moves daily. Monitor error by segment between retrains, and keep the feature pipeline fresh even when the model is not: lag and rolling features update daily, so the model adapts partially without retraining.

Promotion and price data quality. Known-future covariates are only as good as the plans they come from. If the promotion calendar used at forecast time differs from the one recorded after the fact (promotions get cancelled, added, extended), the backtest overstates accuracy. Store the plan as it stood at each origin.

Monitoring and tooling

Monitor feature distributions (fraction of nulls, zeros, ranges) per day, prediction distributions per segment, and live error against a seasonal-naive baseline by horizon and by revenue tier. Libraries such as Nixtla's MLForecast generate lag, rolling and date features with correct shifting for global LightGBM or XGBoost models and support recursive and direct strategies; Darts and sktime provide similar wrappers. Whatever the tool, insist on an explicit as-of time for every feature and a backtest that calls the production feature code.

Goal: build lag, rolling, calendar and promotion features for 150 related series, compare one global model with 150 local models at two history lengths, catch a leaky rolling feature, and watch a tree fail to extrapolate. NumPy only, gpt_env (Python 3.12); ridge regression stands in for gradient boosting because no tree library is installed. Save as ts6.py.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(6)
S, n, H = 150, 730, 7                     # 150 related series, two years daily, 7-day horizon
t = np.arange(n)
weekly = np.array([0.8, 0.9, 0.95, 1.0, 1.1, 1.35, 0.9])        # shared day-of-week shape
scale = rng.lognormal(3, 1, S)                                  # sizes differ ~100x across series
growth = rng.normal(0.0005, 0.0005, S)
promo = rng.random((S, n)) < 0.05                               # planned promotions: known future
Y = np.empty((S, n))
for i in range(S):
    lam = scale[i] * np.exp(growth[i] * t) * weekly[t % 7] * np.where(promo[i], 1.6, 1.0)
    Y[i] = rng.poisson(lam)

def rows(i, origins, h):
    """Features known at origin T for target day T+h, normalised by the series' recent mean."""
    X, y, s = [], [], []
    for T in origins:
        hist = Y[i, :T]
        sc = hist[-28:].mean() + 1.0
        d = (T + h - 1) % 7
        X.append(np.r_[1.0, hist[-7:] / sc, hist[-28:-14].mean() / sc,
                       np.eye(7)[d], float(promo[i, T + h - 1])])
        y.append(Y[i, T + h - 1] / sc); s.append(sc)
    return np.array(X), np.array(y), np.array(s)

def fit(X, y, lam=1e-3):
    return np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X + lam * np.eye(X.shape[1]), X.T @ y)

test_origins = range(n - 70, n - H + 1, 7)        # 9 weekly origins in the last 10 weeks
def evaluate(train_start):
    """Train on origins from train_start to just before the test period; score MASE-like ratio."""
    train_origins = range(train_start, n - 70 - H + 1, 3)   # every 3rd day
    res = {"global": [], "local": [], "snaive": []}
    for h in range(1, H + 1):
        Xs, ys = zip(*[rows(i, train_origins, h)[:2] for i in range(S)])
        wg = fit(np.vstack(Xs), np.concatenate(ys))               # ONE model for all series
        for i in range(S):
            Xt, yt, st = rows(i, test_origins, h)
            wl = fit(Xs[i], ys[i], lam=1.0)                      # one model per series
            res["global"] += list(np.abs(Xt @ wg - yt) * st)
            res["local"] += list(np.abs(Xt @ wl - yt) * st)
            res["snaive"] += list(np.abs(np.array([Y[i, T + h - 1 - 7 * ((h - 1) // 7 + 1)] for T in test_origins]) - yt * st))
    tot = sum(res["snaive"])
    return {k: round(float(sum(v) / tot), 3) for k, v in res.items()}

print("error relative to seasonal-naive (lower is better)")
print("long history  (~197 training origins per series):", evaluate(28))

# Trees cannot extrapolate: a depth-3 regression tree on the day index predicts a flat ceiling
def tree_predict(x_tr, y_tr, x_te, depth=3):
    if depth == 0 or len(x_tr) < 4:
        return np.full(len(x_te), y_tr.mean())
    best = min(np.unique(x_tr)[1:], key=lambda c: ((y_tr[x_tr < c] - y_tr[x_tr < c].mean()) ** 2).sum()
               + ((y_tr[x_tr >= c] - y_tr[x_tr >= c].mean()) ** 2).sum())
    out = np.empty(len(x_te)); L, R = x_te < best, x_te >= best
    out[L] = tree_predict(x_tr[x_tr < best], y_tr[x_tr < best], x_te[L], depth - 1)
    out[R] = tree_predict(x_tr[x_tr >= best], y_tr[x_tr >= best], x_te[R], depth - 1)
    return out
x = np.arange(200.0); yy = 100 + 2 * x                      # a clean straight trend
pred = tree_predict(x[::4][:40], yy[::4][:40], x[160:])      # train on days 0-156, predict 160-199
print(f"trend at day 199 = {yy[-1]:.0f}; tree predicts {pred[-1]:.0f} (the mean of its last leaf)")

Work it out

You are building a direct LightGBM model for day-14-ahead forecasts of store-product sales, with daily data and the forecast running each Monday at 06:00. Sales for a day are complete by 10:00 the next day. A colleague proposes these features: (1) sales yesterday; (2) mean sales over the 7 days centred on the target day last year; (3) mean sales over the last 28 complete days; (4) the promotion flag for the target day from the current promotion calendar; (5) average sales of this product on all promotion days in the dataset; (6) actual temperature on the target day.

(a) Which features are legal, which leak, and how do you fix the leaky ones? (b) What is the shortest sales lag you can use?

Answer

(a) (1) Leaks twice: at 06:00 Monday, Sunday's sales are not complete until 10:00, and in a direct 14-day model "yesterday" relative to the target is 13 days in the future. Use the most recent complete day (Saturday). (2) Legal: last year's window is entirely in the past, even though it is centred — centring is only a problem when the window crosses the origin. (3) Legal if "complete" is respected: the 28 days ending Saturday. (4) Legal only if the training rows use the promotion calendar as it stood at each origin; otherwise train/serve differ when promotions are added or cancelled. (5) Leaks if computed over the whole dataset (it includes the target period); compute it per fold from data before each origin. (6) Leaks: the actual temperature is unknown at the origin. Use a weather forecast archived at each origin, or drop it.

(b) The latest complete day at Monday 06:00 is Saturday, two days before the origin day. For a target 14 days after Monday, Saturday is 16 days before the target, so the shortest usable lag is 16. Build that latency into the backtest with a gap.
