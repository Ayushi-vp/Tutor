By 2021, deep learning had transformed vision, speech and language, and forecasting seemed next. A series of papers introduced transformers designed for long-horizon forecasting — Informer, Autoformer, FEDformer, Pyraformer — each reporting large accuracy gains on a shared set of benchmarks: electricity demand, road traffic, weather, exchange rates, transformer-oil temperatures, influenza-like illness. Engineering teams started asking whether they should replace their exponential smoothing and gradient-boosted trees with attention.

Then, in 2022, a team from the Chinese University of Hong Kong and the International Digital Economy Academy asked a deflating question: had anyone checked whether these transformers beat a model with no attention at all? Their answer was a linear layer — a single matrix that maps the last 336 values directly to the next 96 to 720 — with two small tricks. On all nine benchmarks it beat every transformer, often by 20-50%. The field had been comparing sophisticated models with each other and with weak baselines, not with the simplest reasonable thing.

The story did not end there. A few months later, researchers from Princeton and IBM showed that transformers could beat the linear baselines after all, if you changed what a token is: instead of feeding one time step per token, cut the series into short patches, as Vision Transformers cut images, and process each variable separately. Their PatchTST reduced error by about a fifth relative to the earlier transformers and edged out the linear models. Meanwhile, two older deep forecasters had been quietly successful in industry: Amazon's DeepAR, an LSTM that forecasts probability distributions across millions of products, and N-BEATS, a stack of fully connected blocks that beat the M4 competition winner.

For an AI engineer the story has a second edge. The same transformer architecture that powers language models is now used for time-series foundation models (ts9), and interviewers probe whether you understand why it works for one and needed rethinking for the other. Being able to explain what a token is for a time series, why normalisation decides results, and why a linear baseline can embarrass a large model, is exactly the kind of understanding that separates engineers who can evaluate a model from those who can only run one.

This history is the best way to understand deep learning for time series, because each step teaches a principle: neural forecasters are global models that live or die by normalisation; the right input representation matters more than the attention mechanism; simple linear baselines are mandatory; and probabilistic outputs are often the real reason to use a network. This lesson builds DeepAR, N-BEATS, DLinear and PatchTST from the ground up, works their key arithmetic by hand, and ends with when a deep model is worth it in production.

Four ways to read the past

Every forecaster reads a window of the past and writes a forecast. The neural families differ in how they read. Picture four analysts given the last two weeks of hourly electricity demand.

The first reads one hour at a time, left to right, keeping a running mental summary, and at the end extends the story one hour at a time, each new guess becoming part of the story. That is a recurrent network, and DeepAR is its forecasting form. Its strength is natural handling of any horizon and of uncertainty — it can tell many possible stories by guessing differently each time.

The second looks at the whole window at once, writes down the most obvious pattern it sees and the forecast that pattern implies, crosses that pattern out of the window, and hands what remains to a colleague who does the same, and so on down a chain; the final forecast is the sum of everyone's contributions. That is N-BEATS.

The third is a spreadsheet formula: each forecast hour is a fixed weighted sum of the past 336 hours, the weights learned once from history. That is the linear model (DLinear, NLinear). It cannot learn anything nonlinear, and that turned out to matter less than anyone expected.

The fourth cuts the window into overlapping 16-hour pieces, treats each piece like a word, and lets every piece look at every other piece to decide what matters — "this morning's ramp looks like last Tuesday's ramp". That is a transformer over patches, PatchTST.

What all of them share

All four are global models in the sense of ts6: one network trained on windows cut from many series (or many variables), not one model per series. All four must normalise, because one network cannot cope with series that differ a thousandfold in size, and because networks extrapolate poorly to levels they have not seen. And all four must be judged the same way as any forecaster: against seasonal-naive, exponential smoothing and gradient-boosted trees in a rolling-origin backtest. The lesson of 2022 was that skipping that last step can make a whole research area look better than it is.

One more shared idea is the output. Some neural forecasters output one number per future step (a point forecast). Others output a probability distribution per step — a mean and a spread, or a set of quantiles — so the forecast says not just "about 120" but "probably between 95 and 150". DeepAR goes further and generates whole sample paths, each one a plausible future, so you can ask questions about the path as a whole, such as "what is the chance that stock runs out at some point in the next two weeks?" For many business decisions this probabilistic output, not a small gain in average accuracy, is the main reason to pick a neural model.

A reader who stops here should remember three things: the input representation (time steps or patches, mixed or separate channels, normalised or not) matters more than the attention mechanism; a linear model over the lookback is a mandatory baseline; and the strongest practical reason to use a neural forecaster is often that it learns across many series and outputs full probability distributions.

1. Training data for neural forecasters: windows

A neural forecaster is trained on windows. Choose a lookback length L (the context) and a horizon H. From each series, cut many (L + H)-long slices at random positions in the training period: the first L values are the input, the last H the target. A batch mixes windows from different series and different times. This is the windowing of ts6 without hand-made features: the network receives the raw (normalised) lookback, plus covariates such as calendar features and static series identifiers, and learns its own features.

The lookback is a key hyperparameter. Too short and the model cannot see the weekly cycle in hourly data (which needs at least 168 steps) or the slow trend; too long and it has more parameters, more noise and fewer training windows per series. The long-horizon benchmarks typically use L = 336 or 512 for hourly data and H from 96 to 720. Validation, as always, uses windows from a later period than training.

2. DeepAR: an RNN that forecasts distributions

DeepAR, developed at Amazon (Salinas, Flunkert and Gasthaus, first posted in 2017), is an autoregressive recurrent network. At each time step t, an LSTM (dl7) receives the previous value z_{t−1}, the covariates x_t (calendar, price, item category embedding) and its own hidden state h_{t−1}, and produces a new hidden state h_t. A small output layer maps h_t to the parameters of a probability distribution for z_t: for real-valued data the mean μ and standard deviation σ of a Gaussian; for sales counts the mean μ and a shape parameter α of a negative binomial, whose variance μ + αμ² can exceed the mean, as real sales do.

Training maximises the likelihood of the observed values: at each step in the window, how probable was the actual next value under the predicted distribution? Inputs are the true previous values (teacher forcing). Forecasting uses ancestral sampling: at the forecast origin, run the LSTM over the context to get its state; draw one sample from the predicted distribution for T+1; feed that sample in as if it were data to get the distribution for T+2; sample again; continue to T+H. That gives one possible future path. Repeating 100 or 1,000 times gives a cloud of paths, from which any quantile, the mean, or path-level quantities (the total over the next four weeks, the probability of a stock-out on any day) can be read. This is DeepAR's big practical advantage: coherent probabilistic forecasts across the horizon.

Scale handling, worked. DeepAR divides each series' inputs by a scale factor ν = 1 + (mean of the series over the conditioning range), and multiplies the output distribution's mean by the same factor. For a product averaging 40 units a day over the context, ν = 41: an input of 52 becomes 52/41 ≈ 1.27, and if the network outputs a scaled mean of 1.10, the forecast mean is 1.10 × 41 = 45.1 units. A product averaging 0.3 units a day has ν = 1.3, so its values are barely changed — the "1 +" keeps small series from being blown up. The paper adds a second trick: training windows are sampled with probability proportional to ν, so the relatively few high-volume series, which behave differently and matter most commercially, are seen often enough. The paper reported accuracy improvements of around 15% over the state-of-the-art methods it compared with, on datasets including Amazon's own sales.

3. N-BEATS: deep learning without time-series tricks

N-BEATS (Oreshkin, Carpov, Chapados and Bengio, ICLR 2020) asked whether a pure deep-learning architecture, with no recurrence, convolution, attention or hand-designed time-series components, could beat the statistical methods that had dominated M4. Its building block is simple: a few fully connected layers that take the lookback window and output two vectors — a backcast (the block's reconstruction of its own input) and a forecast (its contribution to the horizon).

Blocks are chained with doubly residual stacking. The input to block 2 is the original window minus block 1's backcast — the part of the past block 1 did not explain. Block 3 receives what blocks 1 and 2 together did not explain, and so on. Meanwhile, the forecasts of all blocks are added up to give the final forecast. It is like boosting inside one network: each block focuses on the residual signal. In the interpretable configuration, blocks in a "trend" stack are forced to output low-degree polynomials and blocks in a "seasonality" stack Fourier series (ts2), so the model's forecast arrives already decomposed into trend and seasonal parts. The generic configuration has no such constraints and is slightly more accurate.

N-BEATS was trained per frequency on M4 data and ensembled over many models with different lookbacks and losses. The paper reports improvements of 11% over a statistical benchmark and 3% over the M4 winner — the first pure deep-learning model to beat the hybrid that won M4 — and it later served as the top-level component in M5's second-placed entry (ts6). Its successor N-HiTS adds multi-rate sampling and hierarchical interpolation for long horizons.

4. Transformers for long horizons, and what went wrong

A vanilla transformer (lm2, lm3) applied to forecasting treats each time step as a token: at each step, the values of all variables are projected into an embedding, positional or timestamp encodings are added, and self-attention lets every step attend to every other. Two problems follow. Cost: attention over L tokens costs O(L²), so a 720-step lookback means about half a million attention scores per head per layer. And meaning: a single time step's value is a weak token — unlike a word, one number says little on its own — and attention's weighted average is insensitive to the order of the tokens except through position encodings.

Papers in 2021-2022 attacked the cost: Informer used sparse "ProbSparse" attention, Autoformer replaced attention with auto-correlation and built in a moving-average decomposition, FEDformer worked in the frequency domain, Pyraformer used a pyramid. All reported large gains on the long-horizon benchmarks. Zeng, Chen, Zhang and Xu (AAAI 2023, "Are Transformers Effective for Time Series Forecasting?") tested them against what they called embarrassingly simple one-layer linear models, collectively LTSF-Linear, on nine benchmarks covering electricity, traffic, weather, exchange rates and illness. The linear models outperformed the transformers in all cases, often by 20% to 50%. They also found that the transformers' accuracy often did not improve, or got worse, as the lookback grew, which a model that truly extracted temporal patterns should not do, and that much of the transformers' advantage over older baselines came from their decomposition and normalisation components rather than attention.

5. Linear, NLinear and DLinear, worked

The plain Linear model is one matrix W of size H × L (plus a bias), applied to each variable separately: ŷ = W·x, where x is the lookback window. Each forecast step is a learned weighted sum of the past L values. Fitted on standard benchmarks, its weights show clear structure: large weights at multiples of the seasonal period (24, 48, … for hourly data), which is a learned seasonal-naive with smoothing.

NLinear handles distribution shift with one subtraction: take the last value of the window, x_L, subtract it from every input, apply the linear layer, add x_L back to every output. The model then predicts changes relative to the current level, so a series that has shifted upward since training is handled gracefully.

DLinear borrows the decomposition idea from Autoformer and FEDformer. It computes a trend with a moving average over the window (with the ends padded by repeating the edge values), takes the remainder as the "seasonal" part, applies one linear layer to each, and sums. A tiny example with L = 4, a moving-average kernel of 3 and one output step: input x = (10, 12, 11, 15). Pad to (10, 10, 12, 11, 15, 15); the moving averages are 32/3 ≈ 10.67, 33/3 = 11.0, 38/3 ≈ 12.67 and 41/3 ≈ 13.67, so the trend is (10.67, 11.0, 12.67, 13.67) and the remainder x − trend is (−0.67, 1.0, −1.67, 1.33). Suppose the learned trend weights are (0, 0, 0.2, 0.8) and the remainder weights (0, 0.5, 0, 0.3). The trend part gives 0.2 × 12.67 + 0.8 × 13.67 ≈ 13.47; the remainder part gives 0.5 × 1.0 + 0.3 × 1.33 ≈ 0.90; the forecast is 14.37.

A subtle point worth raising in an interview: the moving average is itself a fixed linear operation on x, so DLinear's output W_t·M·x + W_s·(I − M)·x equals a single matrix times x. Fitted exactly by least squares, Linear and DLinear have the same expressive power, and in the lab they give the same test error (25.75 and 25.76). The decomposition matters for training by gradient descent with limited data — the trend and seasonal weights are regularised separately and start from a more sensible place — not for what the model can represent.

6. PatchTST: patches and channel independence

Nie, Nguyen, Sinthong and Kalagnanam (ICLR 2023) titled their paper "A Time Series is Worth 64 Words", borrowing from the Vision Transformer's "An Image is Worth 16×16 Words" (mm1). Their model, PatchTST, makes two changes to the transformer recipe.

Patching. Each univariate input window of length L is cut into overlapping patches of length P with stride S, and each patch is linearly projected to an embedding: one token per patch. The number of patches is N = ⌊(L − P)/S⌋ + 2, where the +2 counts the first patch and one extra patch made by padding the end of the window with copies of its last value. With the paper's settings P = 16 and S = 8: L = 336 gives (336 − 16)/8 + 2 = 42 tokens, and L = 512 gives (512 − 16)/8 + 2 = 64 tokens — the "64 words" of the title. Attention cost goes from 336² = 112,896 entries to 42² = 1,764, about 64 times fewer, and the paper reports training up to 22 times faster on large datasets. Each token now carries a local shape (16 consecutive values: a ramp, a peak, a dip), which is a far more meaningful unit to attend over than one number, and for the same cost the model can read a longer history. In the paper's ablation on Traffic, increasing the lookback from 96 to 336 steps reduced MSE from 0.518 to 0.397, and patching at L = 336 reduced it further to 0.367.

Channel independence. Earlier transformers built each token from all variables at one time step (channel mixing). PatchTST instead treats a multivariate series as a set of univariate series and applies the same transformer, with the same weights, to each separately. This multiplies the number of training windows by the number of channels, forces the model to learn patterns that hold across variables, and avoids overfitting to spurious cross-variable correlations. Compared with the best transformer baselines, PatchTST/64 reduced MSE by 21.0% and MAE by 16.7% overall, and PatchTST/42 by 20.2% and 16.4%; it also beat DLinear on most settings. The paper also showed self-supervised pretraining — mask random patches and train the model to reconstruct them, as BERT does with words — followed by fine-tuning beat purely supervised training on large datasets, and representations transferred across datasets: a step toward the foundation models of ts9.

7. Normalisation, the unglamorous essential

Every model in this lesson depends on normalisation. Per-series scaling (DeepAR's ν) handles series of different sizes. Per-window normalisation handles drift within a series: RevIN (reversible instance normalisation, Kim et al., 2022) subtracts each input window's mean and divides by its standard deviation, runs the model, then multiplies the output by the standard deviation and adds the mean back. PatchTST uses it; NLinear's last-value subtraction is the simplest version of the same idea. In the lab, a series shifts up by 15 units in the test period, a level never seen in training. The plain linear model's test MSE is 25.8; NLinear's 9.3; per-window standardisation brings it to 7.5. Repeating the last value scores 79.4. Without normalisation, the cleverest architecture forecasts the old level.

8. Covariates and the Temporal Fusion Transformer

DLinear and PatchTST, as published, read only the target's own history. Real forecasting needs covariates: known-future ones (calendar, prices, promotions), past-only ones (observed weather, traffic) and static ones (store, product). Architectures built for this include the Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT, Lim et al., 2021), which encodes the past with an LSTM, the known future with another, uses variable-selection networks to weigh inputs, and attention over time for long-range patterns, and outputs quantiles; and TiDE (Das et al., 2023), an MLP encoder-decoder that projects covariates for every future step and is much cheaper than a transformer. DeepAR handles covariates naturally as extra inputs at each step. When covariates dominate the signal, as in retail with promotions, these models — or gradient-boosted trees — usually beat history-only models.

9. Training details that decide results

Neural forecasters have more knobs than trees, and results on benchmarks are sensitive to them: lookback length, patch length and stride, model width and depth, dropout, learning-rate schedule, number of training windows per epoch, the loss (MSE, MAE, a likelihood, or quantile loss), early stopping on a later validation period, and the normalisation scheme. Ensembling several models trained with different seeds and lookbacks, as N-BEATS did, often adds more accuracy than architecture changes. For probabilistic outputs, choose between a parametric head (DeepAR's distributions), quantile heads trained with pinball loss (TFT), or sampling-based approaches; ts8 compares them.

10. When deep models are worth it

Neural forecasters earn their cost in identifiable situations: many related series (thousands or more) with enough history to learn shared shapes; high-frequency data with long, multiple seasonalities, where a long lookback matters; a need for coherent sample paths or full distributions (DeepAR); multi-step horizons that suit multi-output models; and pretraining or transfer across datasets. They earn it less when there are few series, short histories, heavy dependence on tabular covariates, intermittent sparse demand, or a team that needs fast iteration and clear explanations — where gradient-boosted trees (ts6) or classical models (ts4) usually match them at lower cost. The production answer is frequently an ensemble: M5's runner-up combined LightGBM with N-BEATS, and the M4 winner fused exponential smoothing with an RNN.

11. A DeepAR forecast step by step

To make ancestral sampling concrete, follow one product with a scale ν = 41 (its context mean is 40). At the origin, the LSTM has read the context and its output layer gives, for T+1, a scaled negative-binomial mean of 1.10 and shape α = 0.05. The real-scale mean is μ = 1.10 × 41 = 45.1 and the variance is μ + αμ² = 45.1 + 0.05 × 2,034 ≈ 146.8, a standard deviation of about 12.1 — wider than a Poisson's √45.1 ≈ 6.7, reflecting the over-dispersion of real sales. One sample path draws, say, 52 for T+1. That 52, scaled to 52/41 ≈ 1.27, is fed back as the "previous value" with T+2's covariates (a Saturday, no promotion); the network updates its state and outputs a new mean, perhaps 1.25 (μ = 51.3), from which the path draws 47; and so on to T+28. A second path might draw 38 at T+1, feed that back, and wander lower. After 1,000 paths, the 50th and 950th largest values at each step give a 90% interval, the average of the path totals gives the expected four-week demand, and the fraction of paths whose cumulative demand exceeds the stock on hand gives a stock-out probability. Because each path carries its own history forward, a high draw early makes later draws higher too, so path-level quantities such as totals have realistic spreads — something independent per-step quantiles cannot provide.

Why point-wise attention struggles with time series

Self-attention computes, for each token, a weighted average of all tokens' values, with weights from query-key similarity. Without positional information this operation is permutation-invariant: shuffle the tokens and the outputs shuffle the same way. Positional or timestamp embeddings add order back, but only as one more input feature, which the network may use weakly. For language this is fine — word identity carries most of the information and order is learned well. For a time series, the information is mostly in the order and in local shape; a single value at one time step means little. Zeng and colleagues argued this is why point-wise transformers failed to exploit longer lookbacks. Patches change the unit of meaning: a 16-step patch encodes local shape inside its embedding, and attention then compares shapes, which is a more natural task for it.

Channel mixing versus independence, a closer look

Consider a dataset with 321 electricity customers. A channel-mixing model builds each token from all 321 values at a time step and has to learn a joint map in a 321-dimensional input space from a few thousand time windows — easy to overfit. A channel-independent model sees 321 times more windows, each univariate, and learns one shared map. If the channels are genuinely coupled (a supply chain where upstream inventory predicts downstream sales), mixing can help, but it needs regularisation or structure: later models add channel-mixing modules on top of independent backbones, or learn which channels to attend to. The general lesson echoes ts6's local-versus-global argument: more parameters for each interaction need more data per parameter.

Likelihood choices and their consequences

A Gaussian likelihood suits continuous, roughly symmetric data; it is wrong for counts with zeros. A negative binomial suits over-dispersed counts; a Poisson assumes variance equal to the mean, usually too small for sales. Student's t handles heavy tails. Zero-inflated or hurdle distributions separate "did anything happen?" from "how much?". The choice matters for the quantiles: a Gaussian on intermittent data puts probability on negative sales and underestimates the chance of exactly zero. A non-parametric alternative is to predict many quantiles directly with pinball loss, which avoids choosing a family but can produce quantiles that cross. DeepAR's original paper used negative binomial for sales and Gaussian for real-valued series.

Exposure bias and multi-step training

Autoregressive networks trained with teacher forcing see true previous values during training but their own samples at forecast time, the exposure-bias problem of ts6. For probabilistic models sampling paths, this is partly benign — sampling from the predicted distribution produces inputs with realistic noise — but errors in the predicted distribution compound over long horizons. Multi-output models sidestep it by predicting all horizons from the context at once, which is one reason long-horizon work moved toward them. Hybrids train an autoregressive model with some steps fed its own predictions, or decode several steps per call.

Benchmark hygiene in long-horizon forecasting

The long-horizon literature has known pitfalls. The datasets are few and small; ETTh1 and ETTh2 have about 17,000 hourly points each. Results are often reported on normalised values with different lookbacks per model, and some codebases dropped the last incomplete test batch, which changed scores. The Exchange-rate data behaves like a random walk, so repeating the last value is very strong there — the DLinear paper notes that repeating the last value was worse than the transformers on strongly seasonal data such as Electricity and Traffic but beat all of them on Exchange-Rate, by around 45%. Treat leaderboard differences of a few percent as noise, check whether a strong statistical baseline was included, and test on your own data.

Self-supervised pretraining and transfer

PatchTST's masked-patch pretraining is a bridge to foundation models. Mask 40% of the patches, train the transformer to reconstruct them from the rest, then fine-tune a forecasting head. Pretraining forces the model to learn general structure — smoothness, seasonality, typical shapes — that helps when labelled forecasting windows are scarce or when transferring to a new dataset. The step from "pretrain on one dataset, fine-tune on another" to "pretrain on billions of points from thousands of datasets and forecast zero-shot" is the subject of ts9.

Cost and latency in serving

Inference costs differ more than training costs suggest. A DLinear forecast is one matrix-vector product per series. N-BEATS and PatchTST are a single forward pass. DeepAR with 500 sample paths over 28 steps is 14,000 sequential LSTM steps per series — cheap per step but sequential, so batching across series is essential. For millions of series refreshed nightly, sampling cost and memory can dominate; for interactive "what if" planning tools, a single-pass quantile model may be the only practical option. Estimate inference cost per series per refresh before choosing an architecture.

The case: "Are Transformers Effective for Time Series Forecasting?" and PatchTST

Zeng, Chen, Zhang and Xu posted their paper in May 2022 and presented it at AAAI 2023. They compared five transformer forecasters published between 2019 and 2022 — LogTrans, Informer, Autoformer, Pyraformer and FEDformer — with three one-layer linear models on nine public benchmarks (ETTh1, ETTh2, ETTm1, ETTm2, Traffic, Electricity, Weather, Exchange-Rate and ILI) at horizons from 96 to 720 steps (24 to 60 for the weekly ILI data). Their abstract states the finding plainly: LTSF-Linear "surprisingly outperforms existing sophisticated Transformer-based LTSF models in all cases, and often by a large margin". They also ran ablations showing that the transformers' errors did not fall as the lookback grew, that shuffling the input order barely hurt some of them (suggesting they were not using temporal order much), and that their efficiency innovations mattered little at realistic lengths. The code and datasets were released, which is why the result was quickly accepted.

PatchTST, from Princeton and IBM Research, appeared at ICLR 2023 as a direct response: its introduction cites the linear result as the challenge to answer. With patches (P = 16, S = 8), channel independence and per-window normalisation, it achieved overall reductions of 21.0% in MSE and 16.7% in MAE against the best transformer baselines with a 512-step lookback (PatchTST/64), and 20.2% and 16.4% with 336 (PatchTST/42), and generally beat DLinear. The pair of papers is now the standard interview story about evaluation discipline: an entire line of work had looked successful because it was compared with weak baselines, and progress resumed when the input representation, not the attention mechanism, was rethought.

DeepAR in industry

DeepAR's influence is mostly practical. Amazon offered it as a built-in algorithm in SageMaker and in the Amazon Forecast service, and the open-source GluonTS library from Amazon's researchers made it, and many later models, easy to train. Its design choices — global training across related series, per-series scaling with window sampling proportional to scale, a likelihood suited to counts, and sample paths for downstream decisions — are the template that later probabilistic neural forecasters followed. AWS closed the managed Amazon Forecast service to new customers in July 2024, pointing them to SageMaker Canvas; the open-source implementations are where the method mostly lives on.

Failure modes in production

Scale and shift failures. A network trained without per-window normalisation forecasts the old level after a shift; one with RevIN forecasts the new level but may lose information when the absolute level itself matters. Monitor bias by segment after any known shift.

Mushy forecasts on intermittent data. MSE-trained networks on sparse sales predict small positive values every day, never zero and never a spike. If decisions need the probability of zero or the size of a spike, use a count likelihood or quantile loss.

Silent preprocessing mismatch. Training normalises with statistics computed one way (over the full window, or using future values in an offline pipeline) and serving another. The model is sensitive to both. Ship the normalisation inside the model graph where possible.

Retraining cost and staleness. Deep models are retrained less often than trees, so they drift. Fine-tuning from the last checkpoint on recent data, rather than training from scratch, is cheaper and often sufficient.

Tooling and what to monitor

NeuralForecast (Nixtla), GluonTS (Amazon), Darts and PyTorch Forecasting provide tested implementations of DeepAR, N-BEATS, N-HiTS, PatchTST, TFT, TiDE and the linear baselines, with backtesting. Monitor the same things as for any forecaster — error by horizon and segment against seasonal-naive, bias, interval coverage — plus training-specific signals: validation loss across retrains, the distribution of normalisation statistics at serving time compared with training, and inference latency per series.

Goal: compute PatchTST's token counts, then fit Linear, DLinear, NLinear and a per-window-normalised linear model on a series whose test period sits at a level never seen in training. NumPy only (closed-form ridge fits stand in for gradient descent), gpt_env (Python 3.12). Save as ts7.py.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
L, H, P, S = 336, 96, 16, 8                 # lookback, horizon, patch length, stride

# 1. Patching arithmetic (PatchTST): tokens and attention-matrix size
for lookback in (336, 512):
    n_tok = (lookback - P) // S + 2          # +1 for the last patch, +1 for end padding
    print(f"L={lookback}: {lookback} point tokens -> {n_tok} patch tokens; "
          f"attention entries {lookback**2:,} -> {n_tok**2:,} ({lookback**2 / n_tok**2:.0f}x fewer)")

# 2. An hourly-like series: daily cycle, weekly cycle, slow drift, noise; a level shift late on
n = 6000
t = np.arange(n)
y = (10 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * t / 24) + 4 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * t / 168)
     + np.cumsum(rng.normal(0, 0.05, n)) + rng.normal(0, 1.5, n))
y[5000:] += 15                                # the test period sits at a new level

def windows(lo, hi, step=4):
    X = np.array([y[s - L:s] for s in range(lo, hi - H, step)])
    Y = np.array([y[s:s + H] for s in range(lo, hi - H, step)])
    return X, Y

Xtr, Ytr = windows(L, 4500)                   # train
Xte, Yte = windows(5100, n, step=24)          # test, after the shift

def moving_avg(X, k=25):
    """DLinear's trend: moving average with edge padding, same length as the input."""
    pad = np.concatenate([np.repeat(X[:, :1], k // 2, 1), X, np.repeat(X[:, -1:], k // 2, 1)], 1)
    c = np.cumsum(np.pad(pad, ((0, 0), (1, 0))), 1)
    return (c[:, k:] - c[:, :-k]) / k

def fit_linear(X, Y, lam=10.0):
    A = np.c_[X, np.ones(len(X))]
    return np.linalg.solve(A.T @ A + lam * np.eye(A.shape[1]), A.T @ Y)
def predict(W, X): return np.c_[X, np.ones(len(X))] @ W

mse = lambda a, b: float(np.mean((a - b) ** 2))
results = {}
results["Repeat last value"] = mse(np.repeat(Xte[:, -1:], H, 1), Yte)
W = fit_linear(Xtr, Ytr); results["Linear"] = mse(predict(W, Xte), Yte)
# DLinear: separate linear maps for trend and remainder, summed
tr_tr, tr_te = moving_avg(Xtr), moving_avg(Xte)
W = fit_linear(np.c_[tr_tr, Xtr - tr_tr], Ytr); results["DLinear"] = mse(predict(W, np.c_[tr_te, Xte - tr_te]), Yte)
# NLinear: subtract the window's last value, predict, add it back (a simple normaliser)
last_tr, last_te = Xtr[:, -1:], Xte[:, -1:]
W = fit_linear(Xtr - last_tr, Ytr - last_tr); results["NLinear"] = mse(predict(W, Xte - last_te) + last_te, Yte)
# RevIN-style: standardise each window by its own mean and std, then undo
mu_tr, sd_tr = Xtr.mean(1, keepdims=True), Xtr.std(1, keepdims=True)
mu_te, sd_te = Xte.mean(1, keepdims=True), Xte.std(1, keepdims=True)
W = fit_linear((Xtr - mu_tr) / sd_tr, (Ytr - mu_tr) / sd_tr)
results["Linear + RevIN"] = mse(predict(W, (Xte - mu_te) / sd_te) * sd_te + mu_te, Yte)
print("\ntest MSE after a level shift the model never saw in training:")
for k, v in results.items():
    print(f"  {k:18s} {v:7.2f}")

What to look for. The token counts match the paper's PatchTST/42 and PatchTST/64. Linear and DLinear give the same error, because the moving-average decomposition is itself linear and the fit is exact — the decomposition is about optimisation and regularisation, not capacity. Both carry part of the old level into the forecast after the shift; subtracting the last value (NLinear) or standardising each window (RevIN-style) removes most of that error. Repeating the last value is poor because it ignores the strong daily cycle.

Work it out

You forecast hourly demand for 2,000 data-centre racks, 48 hours ahead. You have 18 months of history per rack, plus known-future maintenance windows. Candidates: DeepAR, PatchTST (L = 512, P = 16, S = 8), DLinear (L = 512), and a LightGBM global model with lag and calendar features.

(a) How many tokens does PatchTST see per rack per forecast, and what is the attention cost relative to point tokens? (b) Which candidates can use the maintenance windows as input without modification? (c) Capacity planning needs the 95th percentile of the total load across all racks for each hour. Which candidate gives that most naturally, and how?

Answer

(a) N = (512 − 16)/8 + 2 = 64 tokens. Attention entries 64² = 4,096 versus 512² = 262,144: about 64 times fewer.

(b) DeepAR (covariates enter at every step, including future ones) and LightGBM (known-future features for the target hour). Published PatchTST and DLinear read only the target's history; you would need to add a covariate pathway or switch to a model like TFT or TiDE.

(c) DeepAR: sample, say, 1,000 joint paths per rack. If racks are modelled independently, summing paths across racks gives sample paths of the total, whose 95th percentile per hour you read directly — but independent sampling ignores correlations between racks (a shared heat wave), so the total's tail will be too thin. Quantile models per rack cannot simply be added (the sum of 95th percentiles is not the 95th percentile of the sum). Better: also forecast the total directly with a quantile model, or model the dependence (ts11), and compare in a backtest of interval coverage (ts8).
