A forecasting team tells the warehouse that next week's demand for a product will be 1,000 units. The warehouse stocks 1,000. Demand comes in at 1,180, the shelf is empty by Thursday, and the store loses three days of sales. The forecast was not even bad — 1,180 was well within what the model would have called plausible — but nobody asked how plausible. A single number hid the one thing the warehouse needed: how much extra to hold so it runs out only rarely.

The same gap appears everywhere forecasts drive decisions. A cloud team reserves GPUs for next month's inference load; what matters is not the expected peak but the level they will exceed only one hour in a hundred. A finance team commits to a quarterly revenue guidance; they need the range in which the number will land with high confidence. An on-call engineer looks at a latency forecast to set an alert; the threshold should sit where normal traffic almost never reaches. In every case the decision lives in a tail of the distribution, and a point forecast is silent about tails.

A probabilistic forecast answers the real question: not "what will happen?" but "what could happen, and how likely is each outcome?" It comes as quantiles ("90% chance demand stays below 1,250"), intervals ("80% chance between 880 and 1,140"), full distributions, or sample paths — many simulated futures that together show both the spread and how the days relate. The hard part is honesty. Intervals that look reassuring and miss twice as often as they claim are worse than no intervals, because people trust them.

There is also an AI-engineering angle. Teams that serve models forecast their own load — requests per second, tokens per hour, GPU memory — and the reservations they buy are priced per hour whether used or not. Reserve at the mean and you throttle users in every busy hour; reserve at the maximum ever seen and you pay for idle hardware most of the day. The right reservation is a chosen quantile of the peak, and choosing it well needs a forecast that is honest about its tails. The same logic applies to rate limits, autoscaling thresholds and budget alerts.

This lesson builds the toolkit: quantiles and intervals, why they widen with the horizon, the pinball loss worked by hand and why it is the right way to train and score quantiles, calibration and sharpness and how to check them, the main ways to produce intervals, conformal prediction for any model and its adaptive version for drifting series, and the M5 Uncertainty competition, which asked nearly 900 teams for nine quantiles of every Walmart series.

A weather forecast, not a prophecy

A good weather forecaster does not say "it will rain tomorrow". They say "70% chance of rain". That number is useful because it is calibrated: across all the days they said 70%, it rained on about 70% of them. And it is useful because it is sharp — 70% or 10% tells you much more than 50%. A forecaster who always said 40% (the long-run average) would be calibrated but useless; one who said 95% or 5% with confidence and was often wrong would be sharp but untrustworthy. The goal of probabilistic forecasting is to be as sharp as possible while staying calibrated.

For a quantity like demand, the equivalent of "70% chance of rain" is a set of quantiles. The τ-quantile is the value below which a fraction τ of the possible outcomes lie: the 0.5-quantile (median) splits outcomes in half; the 0.9-quantile is exceeded only 10% of the time. Two quantiles make an interval: the 0.1- and 0.9-quantiles bound an 80% interval. Several nested intervals plotted over the horizon make a fan chart.

The weather analogy has one limit worth noticing. Weather forecasters get feedback every single day on thousands of locations, so their calibration is checked constantly. A demand forecaster for a product launched last month has little feedback, and a capacity planner forecasting next year's peak gets one check per year. The less feedback a forecast gets, the more its intervals depend on assumptions — and the more important it is to calibrate on whatever related history exists (similar products, other regions, past years) and to say clearly how uncertain the uncertainty is.

Scoring a quantile: the pinball loss

How do you judge a 90th-percentile forecast? Not by whether the actual was close to it — most of the time it should be below it. The right score is the pinball loss (named for its tilted V shape). For a τ-quantile forecast, if the actual lands above the forecast you pay τ for every unit of shortfall; if it lands below, you pay 1 − τ per unit. For τ = 0.9, landing below costs only 0.1 per unit and landing above costs 0.9 per unit, so the best strategy is to set the forecast high enough that the actual exceeds it only about 10% of the time — which is exactly the 90th percentile. Train with this loss and the model learns quantiles; score with it and you reward calibrated, sharp quantiles.

Where the numbers come from

There are four broad sources of forecast uncertainty: a statistical model's own error distribution (ETS and ARIMA give intervals for free, ts4), simulation of many future paths (DeepAR, ts7, or bootstrapping past errors), models trained directly to output quantiles (quantile regression with gradient-boosted trees or neural networks), and calibration of any point forecaster from its past errors (conformal prediction). Whatever the source, the intervals must be checked on a backtest: do 80% intervals really contain 80% of outcomes?

A reader who stops here should remember: decisions need distributions; quantiles are trained and scored with the pinball loss; and calibration must be checked, never assumed.

1. What a probabilistic forecast can be

A point forecast is one number per future step. A quantile forecast gives several quantiles per step, for example τ = 0.1, 0.5, 0.9. An interval forecast gives a lower and upper bound with a stated probability. A distributional forecast gives the full predictive distribution per step, often as the parameters of a family (mean and standard deviation of a normal, mean and dispersion of a negative binomial). And a set of sample paths gives many simulated futures over the whole horizon, which describe not only each step's distribution but also how steps relate — whether a high day tends to be followed by another high day.

The difference between per-step distributions and paths matters for any question about more than one step at once. "What is the 90th percentile of total demand over the next 28 days?" cannot be answered from 28 separate per-day 90th percentiles: the days are correlated (a strong week tends to stay strong), and percentiles do not add. Sum each sample path over the 28 days and take the 90th percentile of the sums, and you have the answer.

2. Why uncertainty grows with the horizon, worked

For a random walk with step standard deviation σ, the naive forecast's error at horizon h is the sum of h independent shocks, so its variance is hσ² and its standard deviation σ√h. If errors are normal, a 95% interval has half-width 1.96σ√h. With σ = 10:

With φ = 0.6, σ = 10, the long-run standard deviation is 10/√(1 − 0.36) = 12.5, so the 95% half-width approaches 24.5 and stays there. Whether your intervals grow without limit or level off is a modelling decision with large consequences for long-range planning, which is why ts3's unit-root question matters.

3. The pinball loss, worked

For a τ-quantile forecast q and an actual y, the pinball (quantile) loss is

L_τ(y, q) = τ · (y − q)        if y ≥ q   (actual above the forecast)
           (1 − τ) · (q − y)   if y < q   (actual below the forecast)

Suppose the actual is y = 120 and a model gave three quantile forecasts:

  • τ = 0.1, q = 90: the actual is above, so the loss is 0.1 × (120 − 90) = 3.
  • τ = 0.5, q = 110: above, so 0.5 × (120 − 110) = 5.
  • τ = 0.9, q = 140: below, so (1 − 0.9) × (140 − 120) = 0.1 × 20 = 2.

Notice the asymmetry. Had the 0.9-quantile forecast been 100 (20 below the actual instead of 20 above), its loss would have been 0.9 × 20 = 18, nine times worse. A high quantile is supposed to be above the actual most of the time, so falling short is heavily penalised. For τ = 0.5 the two slopes are equal and the pinball loss is half the absolute error, so the median forecast is the MAE-optimal point forecast (ts5).

4. Why the pinball minimiser is the quantile

Imagine nudging the forecast q up by one unit. Every possible outcome above q is now one unit closer, saving τ each; every outcome below q is one unit farther, costing 1 − τ each. If a fraction p of outcomes lie below q, the expected change in loss is (1 − τ)·p − τ·(1 − p) = p − τ. As long as p < τ, raising q lowers the expected loss; once p > τ, raising it increases the loss. The minimum is where p = τ: the forecast below which a fraction τ of outcomes fall — the τ-quantile. The lab confirms it numerically: on 10,000 draws of skewed demand, the grid point that minimises the 0.9 pinball loss is 97.8, while NumPy's 0.9-quantile is 97.7.

This is why quantile regression works: train any model — linear, gradient-boosted trees, a neural network — with the pinball loss for a given τ, and it learns to predict that quantile as a function of its features. LightGBM, XGBoost and neural forecasting libraries all offer a quantile objective.

5. Calibration and sharpness

A probabilistic forecast is calibrated if its probabilities match frequencies: 80% intervals contain the outcome about 80% of the time; actuals fall below 0.9-quantile forecasts about 90% of the time. It is sharp if its distributions are concentrated — narrow intervals. Calibration is a joint property of forecasts and outcomes; sharpness is a property of the forecasts alone. The guiding principle, from Gneiting and colleagues, is to maximise sharpness subject to calibration.

Checking calibration is counting. On a backtest of 100 forecasts with 80% intervals, count how many actuals fell inside. Sampling noise alone gives a standard error of √(0.8 × 0.2 / 100) = 0.04, so anything from about 72% to 88% is consistent with good calibration; 68% (three standard errors low) is a real problem. Check each tail separately — an interval can have 80% coverage overall with all the misses above it, which means the whole distribution is too low. Check by horizon (intervals that are fine at h = 1 often under-cover at h = 28) and by segment (fine on normal days, terrible on holidays). A PIT histogram — for each outcome, the forecast distribution's cumulative probability at the actual value — should be flat for a calibrated forecaster; a U shape means intervals are too narrow, a hump means too wide.

6. Proper scores: pinball, interval score and CRPS

To compare probabilistic forecasters, use a proper scoring rule: a score whose expected value is best when the forecaster reports their true belief, so it cannot be improved by hedging or exaggerating. The pinball loss is proper for a single quantile; averaging it over many quantile levels scores a set of quantiles. The interval score (Winkler score) for a (1 − α) interval [l, u] is (u − l) + (2/α)(l − y) if y < l, + (2/α)(y − u) if y > u: the width plus a penalty for misses. For an 80% interval [90, 140] and y = 150, it is 50 + (2/0.2) × 10 = 150. The continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) scores a whole distribution: it equals twice the pinball loss averaged over all τ from 0 to 1, is measured in the units of the data, and collapses to the absolute error when the forecast is a single point — so point and probabilistic forecasts can be compared on one scale.

7. Sources of intervals

Model-based. Statistical models such as ETS and ARIMA (ts4) give a forecast variance at each horizon; with normal errors, the interval is the point forecast ± z × standard deviation. They are cheap and principled but usually too narrow in practice, because they ignore parameter uncertainty, model-selection uncertainty and future change, and because real errors are rarely normal.

Simulation and residual bootstrap. Fit a model, collect its historical one-step errors, and simulate many futures by applying the model recursively with errors drawn at random from that collection. This drops the normality assumption and naturally produces sample paths. It still assumes future errors look like past ones.

Quantile regression. Train one model per quantile with the pinball loss, or one model with several quantile outputs. It makes no distributional assumption and lets uncertainty depend on features (wider on holidays, narrower on quiet days). Separately trained quantiles can cross (the 0.9 forecast below the 0.8); sort them, or train jointly with a monotonic structure.

Parametric heads. Predict the parameters of a distribution family and train by maximum likelihood, as DeepAR does with a negative binomial (ts7). Any quantile follows from the parameters, quantiles never cross, and the probability of zero is explicit. Accuracy depends on choosing a sensible family.

Ensembles. The spread of forecasts from several models or seeds indicates model uncertainty, but on its own it usually understates total uncertainty, because all members share the same data noise. It is best combined with one of the methods above.

8. Conformal prediction, worked

Split conformal prediction turns any point forecaster into an interval forecaster with a coverage guarantee. Hold out a calibration set of n past forecasts, compute their absolute errors (the nonconformity scores), sort them, and use the ⌈(n + 1)(1 − α)⌉-th smallest as the interval half-width q̂. The new interval is ŷ ± q̂. If calibration and test errors are exchangeable (any ordering is equally likely), the probability that a new error is no larger than q̂ is at least 1 − α, exactly, in finite samples.

Worked: with n = 19 calibration errors and a 90% target, the rank is ⌈20 × 0.9⌉ = 18: take the 18th smallest of the 19 absolute errors. If the sorted errors end …, 11, 13, 17, 26, the 18th smallest is 17, so the interval is ŷ ± 17. With n = 9, the rank would be ⌈10 × 0.9⌉ = 9, the largest error — small calibration sets give wide, conservative intervals.

The lab shows why this beats a normal-theory interval. Errors are drawn from a heavy-tailed distribution (Student's t with 3 degrees of freedom). The normal 80% interval, ±1.28 standard deviations, covers 88.8% — too wide, because the heavy tails inflate the standard deviation — while the normal 99% interval covers only 97.7%, too narrow in the tail. The conformal intervals cover 80.4% and 99.4%. Normal theory is wrong in both directions at once; conformal calibration gets both right because it uses the empirical error distribution.

9. Conformal under drift: adaptive conformal inference

Time series break exchangeability: errors are autocorrelated and their size changes. A conformal width calibrated in a calm period is too narrow after volatility rises. In the lab, volatility triples halfway through; an 80% interval calibrated once on the first 200 errors covers only 33% afterwards.

Adaptive conformal inference (ACI, Gibbs and Candès, 2021) keeps adjusting the level it targets. Keep a working miss-rate α_t, starting at the target α. After each outcome, update α_{t+1} = α_t + γ(α − miss_t), where miss_t is 1 if the outcome fell outside the interval and 0 if inside, and γ is a small step size. Each miss lowers α_t by γ(1 − α), which raises the quantile used next time and widens the interval; each hit raises α_t by γα, narrowing it slightly. With α = 0.2 and γ = 0.01, a miss lowers α_t by 0.008 and a hit raises it by 0.002, so four hits balance one miss — exactly the 80/20 split targeted. Gibbs and Candès prove that the long-run fraction of misses converges to α whatever the data do. In the lab, ACI (on a rolling window of recent errors) recovers to 80.3% coverage after the shift. The guarantee is about the long-run average, not each interval: right after a shift, misses cluster until the level catches up.

10. From distributions to decisions

The payoff of a probabilistic forecast is that each decision can pick its own point. The classic example is the newsvendor problem: order a quantity Q before seeing demand D; each unit short costs c_u (lost margin), each unit left over costs c_o (waste or holding). The expected-cost-minimising order is the demand quantile at the critical ratio c_u / (c_u + c_o). If running out costs 4 and overstocking costs 1, the ratio is 0.8: order the 80th percentile. The pinball loss at τ = 0.8 is exactly this cost structure scaled, which is why training with pinball loss at the critical ratio is training for the decision. Ts12 builds this into a production ordering system.

Other decisions use other summaries: capacity planning uses a high quantile of the peak; alerting uses a quantile of normal behaviour (ts10); financial planning uses intervals on totals, which need sample paths. One probabilistic forecast serves all of them.

11. Bootstrapped sample paths, worked

Here is how simulation turns a point model into sample paths. Take a fitted AR(1) for a demeaned series, y_t = 0.6 y_{t−1} + e_t, with last value y_T = 10, and suppose the model's historical one-step errors were −7, −3, −1, 0, 2, 4, 9. To build one path, draw an error at random from that list for each step and run the model forward. Path A draws 4, −1, 9: y(T+1) = 0.6 × 10 + 4 = 10; y(T+2) = 0.6 × 10 − 1 = 5; y(T+3) = 0.6 × 5 + 9 = 12. Path B draws −7, 0, −3: y(T+1) = 6 − 7 = −1; y(T+2) = −0.6 + 0 = −0.6; y(T+3) = −0.36 − 3 = −3.36. Repeat 1,000 times. The 10th and 90th percentiles of the 1,000 values at each step give an 80% interval; the 1,000 sums over the three steps give the distribution of the three-day total. No normality assumption was made — the paths inherit the skew of the historical errors — and the dependence between steps (path A stayed high, path B stayed low) is preserved because each path carries its own history forward. The method still assumes future errors resemble past ones, so it inherits any change in volatility as a blind spot, which is where adaptive calibration comes in.

12. An interval pipeline end to end

Putting the pieces together, a production team forecasting daily demand for 10,000 products might build intervals like this. The point model is a global gradient-boosted model (ts6). On a 26-origin backtest, they store every forecast and its error by product, horizon and segment. For each horizon h and segment (say, volume tier × weekday versus weekend), they take the empirical distribution of scaled errors (error divided by the product's recent mean) and read off the 5th, 10th, 90th and 95th percentiles; multiplying back by each product's scale gives per-product intervals that are wider for volatile segments and long horizons. This is a grouped, scaled version of conformal calibration.

They then check coverage on a later holdout: the 80% intervals cover 79% overall, 81% for high-volume products, but only 70% for new products and 72% at horizons beyond three weeks. New products get their own calibration group with wider multipliers, and long horizons get an additional adjustment. In production, an adaptive layer (ACI-style) nudges each group's level after each week's outcomes, and a dashboard tracks coverage per group, alerting when any group's 8-week coverage leaves the band [72%, 88%]. The ordering system consumes the quantile at each product's critical ratio. None of this required changing the point model — which is the practical appeal of conformal-style calibration.

Why model-based intervals are too narrow

A model's interval accounts only for the noise term of the model as fitted. Three sources of uncertainty are left out. Parameter uncertainty: α in exponential smoothing or φ in an AR model are estimates, and with short series their errors are large. Model uncertainty: the chosen model is one of several plausible ones, and the forecast would differ under another. And structural uncertainty: the process may change in the forecast period. Each adds variance. Simulation with parameters drawn from their estimated distribution handles the first; combining models handles part of the second; nothing handles the third except empirical calibration on a long backtest that includes changes. This is why serious forecasting systems calibrate intervals on historical forecast errors rather than trusting model formulas.

Conditional versus marginal coverage

Split conformal guarantees marginal coverage: averaged over all test points, the miss rate is at most α. It says nothing about any subgroup. A forecaster can achieve 80% overall by being 95% on easy days and 40% on hard days — exactly the days that matter. Remedies include conformalising within groups (Mondrian conformal: separate calibration per segment), using normalised scores (divide each residual by a predicted scale, so the interval width tracks predicted difficulty), and conformalised quantile regression (CQR), which starts from quantile-regression intervals that already vary with the features and adjusts them by a conformal correction. CQR combines the adaptivity of quantile regression with conformal's coverage guarantee and is a strong default.

Multi-step conformal and joint coverage

For a 28-step forecast, calibrating each horizon separately gives 80% coverage per step, but the probability that the whole path stays inside all 28 intervals is much lower. If you need joint coverage — "the entire next four weeks stay within the band with 80% probability" — you must widen the per-step intervals, for example by calibrating on the maximum normalised error across the horizon, or by a Bonferroni-style adjustment (each step at 1 − α/28), which is conservative. Decide which guarantee the decision needs before choosing.

Quantiles across a hierarchy

Quantiles are not additive. If two products each have independent demand with 90th percentile 130 (mean 100, standard deviation about 23.4 if normal), the total has mean 200 and standard deviation 23.4 × √2 ≈ 33.1, so its 90th percentile is about 242, not 260. With perfectly correlated products it would be 260; with negative correlation, less than 242. The M5 Uncertainty analysis by Chen and colleagues found that simply summing lower-level quantiles was sometimes better than the teams' direct forecasts for extreme left-tail quantiles at granular levels, a sign of how hard tail forecasting at low levels is. The robust approach is joint sample paths that respect dependence, followed by probabilistic reconciliation (ts11).

Choosing the distribution family

For parametric heads, the family matters most in the tails. Normal distributions understate extreme events in heavy-tailed data and put mass on impossible negative values for counts. Student's t or log-normal handle heavy tails. Negative binomial suits over-dispersed counts. Zero-inflated or hurdle models suit data with more zeros than the count model expects. When unsure, compare families by CRPS and tail coverage on a backtest, or avoid the choice with quantile regression plus conformal calibration.

Probabilistic forecasts from foundation models and LLMs

Time-series foundation models (ts9) produce probabilistic outputs in different ways: Chronos samples from a categorical distribution over binned values, others output quantile heads or mixture distributions. Their raw intervals are not automatically calibrated for your data, especially under zero-shot use on a new domain; conformal calibration on your own recent errors is a cheap, effective wrapper. Language models asked for a "90% interval" in text are typically poorly calibrated, so treat such intervals as unverified until backtested.

Why proper scores matter: a gaming example

Suppose a team is judged on interval coverage alone. The easy win is to publish enormous intervals: 100% coverage, zero usefulness. Judged on width alone, they would publish tiny ones. Judged on an improper hybrid — say, coverage within 2 points of target plus the narrowest width — they could publish intervals that are right on average but wrong by segment. A proper score such as the interval score or CRPS removes these games: in expectation, the best possible score is achieved only by reporting the true predictive distribution. That is why competitions (M5's WSPL) and serious internal evaluations use proper scores, with coverage reported alongside as a diagnostic, not as the objective.

Bayesian forecasting in one paragraph

Bayesian methods treat parameters as uncertain and average forecasts over their posterior distribution, which automatically widens intervals when data is scarce — exactly the parameter uncertainty that model formulas omit. Bayesian structural time-series models (used by Google's CausalImpact, ts11) and hierarchical Bayesian models that share information across products are the common forms. They are slower and need care with priors, but they are a principled way to get honest intervals for short series and new products, and their posterior samples are sample paths ready for decision-making.

Evaluating tails with few events

A 99% interval should miss once in a hundred forecasts. To check it to within ±0.5 points needs thousands of independent forecasts, which a single series rarely provides. Pool across series (after scaling), use proper scores rather than coverage alone, and accept that extreme-tail calibration is uncertain. For decisions driven by rare events (capacity for a once-a-year peak), complement statistical intervals with scenario analysis: what if demand matched last year's record plus growth?

The case: the M5 Uncertainty competition

Alongside the accuracy track (ts1, ts6), the M5 competition ran an Uncertainty track on the same 42,840 hierarchical Walmart series. Instead of point forecasts, each team submitted nine quantiles for every series and each of the 28 days: 0.005, 0.025, 0.165, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.835, 0.975 and 0.995. These define the median and the 50%, 67%, 95% and 99% central intervals. Almost 900 teams took part. Each quantile forecast was scored with the pinball loss, scaled by the series' in-sample one-step naive error (so series of different sizes are comparable, as with MASE in ts5), averaged over quantiles and days, and then weighted across series by dollar sales and across the 12 aggregation levels: the Weighted Scaled Pinball Loss (WSPL).

Two findings matter for practitioners. First, as in the accuracy track, the top-performing teams all used machine-learning methods rather than classical statistical models. Second, an independent evaluation by Chen, Gaba, Tsetlin and Winkler, published in the International Journal of Forecasting, found the submitted quantiles "remarkably well calibrated" overall, with poorer calibration only for the middle quantiles at the most aggregated levels. Performance, rescaled to be comparable, worsened from the top level (total sales) to the most granular level (product-store), except for the left-tail quantiles at the granular levels, where many quantile forecasts are simply zero. They also showed, theoretically and in the data, that pinball scores follow an inverted-U across quantile levels — highest in the middle of the distribution, lower in the tails — so raw scores should not be compared across τ without care.

The competition is a model for evaluating probabilistic systems: a proper score, scaled across series, weighted by business value, computed at every level of the hierarchy, plus explicit calibration checks.

How probabilistic forecasts fail in production

Overconfident intervals. The most common failure. Model formulas ignore parameter, model and structural uncertainty, so intervals miss far more often than stated, and planners stop trusting them. Calibrate on backtest errors (conformal or empirical quantiles of past errors by horizon) and monitor live coverage.

Coverage that collapses after a shift. After a regime change, errors grow and fixed-width intervals under-cover for weeks. Adaptive calibration (ACI, rolling-window conformal) and change-point alerts (ts10) shorten the bad period.

Summing quantiles. A planning tool adds store-level 95th percentiles to get a regional safety stock and over-stocks the region by a wide margin. Aggregate through sample paths or forecast the aggregate directly.

Crossing quantiles. Separately trained quantile models return a 0.9 below the 0.8 on some rows, breaking downstream code. Sort per row, or use a joint model.

Communicating uncertainty. Planners shown a fan chart often anchor on the median anyway. Show the decision-relevant number (the recommended order at the critical ratio, the probability of exceeding capacity) rather than raw quantiles, and explain intervals in frequency terms ("in 10 similar weeks, about 2 came in above this").

A worked capacity decision

An inference team must reserve GPU capacity for next month's daily peak. Their probabilistic forecast of the peak (sample paths summed into daily maxima) gives a median of 820 GPUs, a 90th percentile of 910 and a 99th of 1,010. Reserved GPUs cost far less per hour than on-demand ones, but unused reservations are wasted, and on-demand capacity may be unavailable at peak. If being short means degraded service that the business values at roughly nine times the cost of an idle reserved GPU, the critical ratio is 9/(9 + 1) = 0.9: reserve about 910 and cover the rare excess with on-demand or graceful degradation. The forecast's job was not to say "820"; it was to make the 910 defensible — and that only works if the 90th percentile has been shown, on past months, to be exceeded about one month in ten.

What to monitor

Live coverage per interval level, per horizon and per segment, with both tails separately; pinball loss or CRPS against a baseline such as seasonal-naive with empirical intervals; interval width over time (sudden narrowing is suspicious); quantile crossing rate; and, for decisions, the realised service level (how often stock covered demand) against the target. Libraries: LightGBM and XGBoost quantile objectives, NeuralForecast and GluonTS distribution heads, MAPIE and statsforecast's conformal intervals for conformal calibration.

import numpy as np

def pinball(y, q, tau):
    """Quantile (pinball) loss: tau per unit when y is above q, (1 - tau) per unit when below."""
    d = y - q
    return np.mean(np.maximum(tau * d, (tau - 1) * d))

# 1. The worked example: one actual, three quantile forecasts
y = 120.0
for tau, q in ((0.1, 90.0), (0.5, 110.0), (0.9, 140.0)):
    print(f"tau={tau}: forecast {q:5.0f} -> pinball loss {pinball(np.array([y]), q, tau):.1f}")

# 2. The pinball loss is minimised at the empirical quantile
rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
demand = rng.gamma(2.0, 25.0, 10_000)                 # skewed demand, mean 50
grid = np.linspace(0, 200, 2001)
for tau in (0.5, 0.9):
    best = grid[np.argmin([pinball(demand, g, tau) for g in grid])]
    print(f"tau={tau}: pinball minimiser {best:.1f}  vs  np.quantile {np.quantile(demand, tau):.1f}")

# 3. Random-walk forecast intervals fan out like sqrt(h)
sigma = 10
print("\n95% half-width by horizon:", {h: round(float(1.96 * sigma * np.sqrt(h)), 1) for h in (1, 4, 9, 16)})

# 4. Coverage of a 'normal' 80% interval when errors are really heavy-tailed, then split conformal
n_cal, n_test = 500, 5000
cal_err = rng.standard_t(3, n_cal) * 8               # residuals from a calibration period
test_err = rng.standard_t(3, n_test) * 8
sd = cal_err.std()
z80 = 1.2816
normal_cover = np.mean(np.abs(test_err) <= z80 * sd)
k = int(np.ceil((n_cal + 1) * 0.8))                   # split-conformal rank
qhat = np.sort(np.abs(cal_err))[k - 1]
conf_cover = np.mean(np.abs(test_err) <= qhat)
print(f"\nnominal 80%: normal interval +-{z80 * sd:.1f} covers {normal_cover:.1%}; "
      f"conformal +-{qhat:.1f} covers {conf_cover:.1%}")
k99 = int(np.ceil((n_cal + 1) * 0.99))
print(f"nominal 99%: normal +-{2.5758 * sd:.1f} covers {np.mean(np.abs(test_err) <= 2.5758 * sd):.1%}; "
      f"conformal +-{np.sort(np.abs(cal_err))[k99 - 1]:.1f} covers {np.mean(np.abs(test_err) <= np.sort(np.abs(cal_err))[k99 - 1]):.1%}")

# 5. Under a shift, fixed conformal under-covers; adaptive conformal inference (ACI) recovers
T = 3000
err = rng.normal(0, 5, T)
err[1500:] *= 3                                       # volatility triples halfway through
alpha, gamma = 0.2, 0.01                              # target 80% coverage, ACI step size
window = list(np.abs(err[:200]))
a_t, hits_fixed, hits_aci = alpha, [], []
q_fixed = np.quantile(window, 1 - alpha)
for t in range(200, T):
    q_aci = np.quantile(window[-500:], min(1.0, max(0.0, 1 - a_t)))
    hits_fixed.append(abs(err[t]) <= q_fixed)
    hit = abs(err[t]) <= q_aci
    hits_aci.append(hit)
    a_t += gamma * (alpha - (0.0 if hit else 1.0))    # miss -> lower a_t -> wider interval
    window.append(abs(err[t]))
hf, ha = np.array(hits_fixed), np.array(hits_aci)
print(f"after the shift: fixed interval covers {hf[1300:].mean():.1%}, ACI covers {ha[1300:].mean():.1%}")

What to look for. The first three lines reproduce the worked example. The grid search lands on the empirical quantile, which is the whole justification for quantile regression. The half-widths double from h = 1 to h = 4 (√4 = 2). With heavy-tailed errors, the normal interval is too wide at 80% and too narrow at 99% at the same time, while conformal is right at both levels. After volatility triples, the interval calibrated once at the start covers a third of outcomes; ACI's level adjustment brings coverage back near 80%.

Work it out

A bakery sells a pastry that costs 1.00 to make and sells for 3.50; unsold pastries are thrown away. A model gives these daily demand quantiles for tomorrow: 0.5 → 80, 0.7 → 92, 0.8 → 100, 0.9 → 112.

(a) How many should the bakery bake? (b) Tomorrow's demand turns out to be 95. Compute the pinball loss of the 0.7- and 0.9-quantile forecasts. (c) Over the last 50 days, demand exceeded the 0.9-quantile forecast on 12 days. What does that tell you, and what would you do?

Answer

(a) Cost of a unit short (lost margin) c_u = 3.50 − 1.00 = 2.50; cost of a unit over (waste) c_o = 1.00. Critical ratio 2.5/3.5 ≈ 0.71, so bake about the 0.71-quantile: interpolating between 92 (0.7) and 100 (0.8) gives about 93.

(b) τ = 0.7, q = 92, y = 95 (above): 0.7 × 3 = 2.1. τ = 0.9, q = 112, y = 95 (below): 0.1 × 17 = 1.7.

(c) A calibrated 0.9-quantile is exceeded about 10% of the time — 5 days in 50 — with a standard deviation of √(50 × 0.1 × 0.9) ≈ 2.1 days. Twelve exceedances is more than three standard deviations above 5: the upper quantiles are too low (the distribution is too narrow or biased low). Check bias of the median too, then recalibrate: conformal adjustment using recent errors, or retrain the quantile model with recent data and features for what changed (a new office nearby, a season).
