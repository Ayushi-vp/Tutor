A business-intelligence company wants to add a "forecast" button to its dashboards. Customers upload any metric — daily orders, weekly sign-ups, hourly server load, monthly churn — and expect a sensible forecast with an uncertainty band in a few seconds. There is no time to train a model per metric, no labelled history across customers, and no data scientist to tune anything. Classical automatic methods (AutoETS, AutoARIMA) work, but they fit each series from scratch, struggle with short histories and know nothing about what series of this kind usually do.

Language models changed natural-language processing by pretraining once on enormous text corpora and then handling new tasks with little or no extra training. Starting around 2023, researchers asked whether the same recipe works for time series: pretrain one large model on billions of observations from thousands of datasets — electricity, traffic, retail, weather, web traffic, finance, plus synthetic series — and then forecast any new series zero-shot, straight from its history. Amazon's Chronos, Google's TimesFM, Salesforce's Moirai and others answered yes: on broad benchmarks these models matched or beat models trained specifically on each dataset, without seeing those datasets at all.

At the same time, a second wave of papers tried a different shortcut: take an existing text LLM such as GPT-2 or LLaMA, feed it time series through a small adapter, and claim the language knowledge helps forecasting. In 2024 a careful ablation study removed the LLM from three such methods and found the forecasts got no worse — usually better — at a tiny fraction of the cost. Both stories matter in interviews: one shows what pretraining on the right data can do, the other how easily impressive-sounding architectures fail a simple ablation.

For an AI engineer this topic sits at a crossroads. Interviewers ask about it because it tests whether you can transfer what you know about language models — tokenisation, pretraining, zero-shot use, fine-tuning, scaling — to a different kind of data, and whether you can tell genuine transfer from hype. The right answers combine both halves: respect for what pretraining on the right data achieves, and the reflex to ask "compared with what, measured how?"

This lesson explains how time-series foundation models turn numbers into something a transformer can learn from (Chronos's tokenisation is worked by hand), how TimesFM's patching design differs, what the newest models add (covariates and multivariate inputs), what general LLMs are and are not good for around forecasting, how to benchmark and adapt these models honestly, and how to build a product feature on top of them. The cases are the Chronos paper and the "Are language models actually useful?" study.

A forecaster who has seen everything

Imagine an experienced analyst who has spent years looking at every kind of chart: retail sales with Christmas spikes, electricity load with daily cycles, web traffic with weekend dips, sensor readings with slow drifts, stock prices that wander. Show them a new chart they have never seen, with no labels and no units, and they can still make a decent guess about where it goes next, because they recognise the shape: "that is a weekly cycle on a gentle uptrend", "that looks like a series that just had a level shift". That is what a time-series foundation model is: a model that has learned the common shapes of time series from a huge, diverse collection, and applies that knowledge to a new series zero-shot.

The analogy breaks in instructive ways. The analyst knows that the chart is "ice-cream sales" and that a heat wave is forecast; a history-only foundation model sees only numbers scaled to a standard size, so it cannot know any of that unless you provide it as a covariate. And the analyst's experience is limited by what they have seen: a foundation model pretrained mostly on smooth, regular data may stumble on intermittent spare-parts demand.

Three ingredients

Every time-series foundation model needs answers to three questions. What is a token? Language models read tokens from a vocabulary; time series are real numbers of every scale. Chronos answers by scaling and binning values into a vocabulary of 4,096 tokens; TimesFM and others answer with patches, as in PatchTST (ts7). What data? Diversity matters as much as size: real datasets from many domains plus synthetic series to fill gaps in the patterns. What output? A probabilistic one — sampled paths or quantiles — because a zero-shot forecast with no sense of its own uncertainty is dangerous.

A ladder of adaptation

There are four rungs for using a pretrained forecaster, from least to most effort. Zero-shot: hand the model a context and take its forecast — seconds of work, no training. In-context: give the model more to read in that context, such as related series or covariates (newer models accept them), so it can infer relationships on the fly without any weight changes. Fine-tuned: continue training the model's weights (all of them, or small adapter layers) on your own history. Ensembled: combine the foundation model's forecast with a local model or a tree model, letting each cover the other's blind spots. Climb only as far as the backtest says you need to: many teams find zero-shot plus conformal calibration is already competitive, while domain-heavy problems such as promotion-driven retail usually need covariates or fine-tuning.

It also helps to see what the model is not doing. A foundation model does not look up similar series in a database and copy them, and it does not "understand" that a series is revenue. It applies a learned function from context shapes to future distributions. That function encodes a great deal of experience, which is why it works zero-shot, but it can only use the information in the context it is given — which is why the quality and length of that context, and the covariates you pass, matter so much.

A reader who stops here should remember: time-series foundation models are pretrained on time series, not text; they forecast new series zero-shot from shape alone unless given covariates; and they are excellent baselines that still have to win your backtest.

1. What "foundation model" means for time series

A foundation model is pretrained once on broad data and then used for many downstream tasks with little or no task-specific training (dl11). For forecasting, "broad data" means many datasets across domains (energy, transport, retail, web, nature, finance, health) and frequencies (seconds to years), and "downstream task" means forecasting a series the model has never seen. Zero-shot forecasting uses the pretrained model directly: give it the recent history (the context), get a forecast. Fine-tuning continues training on your own data to specialise it.

This only works if the model has learned patterns that recur across domains: trends, seasonal cycles at various periods, mean reversion, level shifts, noise of different shapes, and how these combine. The bet of the field is that such shared structure exists and that a large enough model trained on diverse enough data can recognise it in a new series from the context alone — the same bet that made global models work across a retailer's products (ts6), scaled to "all time series".

2. Chronos: tokenising numbers

Chronos (Ansari et al., Amazon, published in Transactions on Machine Learning Research in 2024) asks: what is the least we must change to train a standard language model on time series? Its answer is to change only the tokeniser.

Mean scaling. Series differ by orders of magnitude, so each context is divided by the mean of its absolute values: s = (1/C) Σ |x_i|. This keeps zeros as zeros (useful for "no sales" or "no solar at night") and maps typical values to around 1.

Quantisation. The scaled values are mapped to B bins with uniformly spaced centres between −15 and +15. Chronos uses a vocabulary of 4,096 tokens, two of which are special — PAD (for padding and missing values) and EOS (end of sequence) — leaving 4,094 value bins. The spacing between bin centres is 30/4,093 ≈ 0.00733, and each value is assigned to the nearest centre.

Worked example. Context 20, 30, 40, 50. Scale s = (20 + 30 + 40 + 50)/4 = 35. Scaled values 0.571, 0.857, 1.143, 1.429. The bin index (counting from 0 at −15) is round((x + 15)/0.00733): for 0.571, (15.571)/0.00733 ≈ 2,124.4 → bin 2124; the others land in bins 2163, 2202 and 2241. Decoding takes each bin's centre (−15 + 2124 × 0.00733 ≈ 0.5681 for the first) and multiplies by 35: 19.88, 29.89, 39.89, 49.90. At this scale each bin is 0.257 units wide, so the round-trip error is at most about 0.13 units — negligible. But a future value of 700, twenty times the context's scale, would map beyond +15 and be clipped to the top bin, decoding to 15 × 35 = 525: Chronos cannot forecast a value more than 15 times the context's average magnitude, a limit the authors note matters for strongly trending series.

Training. The token sequences are fed to an unmodified T5 encoder-decoder (sizes Mini 20M, Small 46M, Base 200M and Large 710M parameters) trained with ordinary cross-entropy to predict the next token, as a language model would. Context length was 512 and prediction length 64. One consequence is that the model treats bins as unrelated categories: predicting bin 2125 when the truth is 2124 is as wrong, to the loss, as predicting bin 10. The model still learns that neighbouring bins are similar, from the data.

Forecasting. At inference, the model gives a probability for each of the 4,094 bins for the next step; sample one, append it, repeat for the horizon. Each run gives one path; Chronos's evaluation used 20 sample paths per series to estimate quantiles. The categorical output can represent any distribution shape, including several peaks, which a Gaussian head cannot.

3. Chronos's data: real, mixed and synthetic

Chronos was trained on 28 public datasets — about 890,000 univariate series with about 84 billion observations — augmented two ways. TSMixup creates new series as random convex combinations (weights from a Dirichlet distribution) of up to three series drawn from different datasets, increasing pattern diversity: 10 million such mixtures were generated. KernelSynth generates synthetic series from Gaussian processes whose kernels are built by randomly combining basic kernels (linear for trends, periodic for seasonality, radial basis for smooth variation, white noise), adding 1 million series with shapes the real data might lack. The evaluation used 42 datasets: 15 that were also in training (Benchmark I, "in-domain") and 27 never seen in training (Benchmark II, "zero-shot"), scored with MASE and a weighted quantile loss (the pinball loss averaged over nine quantiles, ts8) relative to seasonal-naive.

4. TimesFM: patches and long outputs

TimesFM (Das, Kong, Sen and Zhou, Google Research, ICML 2024) takes the patching route of PatchTST (ts7) and combines it with the decoder-only training of GPT-style language models. The input series is cut into non-overlapping patches of 32 points; each patch is embedded by a small residual network; a stack of causal transformer layers (each position sees only earlier patches) processes them; and the output at each position is decoded into the next output patch of 128 points. Because output patches are longer than input patches, a 256-step forecast takes two generation steps instead of eight. Decoder-only training means every prefix of the context is a training example, so the model learns to forecast from any context length up to its maximum. The original model had about 200 million parameters and was pretrained on around 100 billion time points, including Google Trends search-interest series, Wikipedia page-view series and synthetic data.

5. The model landscape and how it moved

Several families appeared in 2024. Moirai (Salesforce) used patches of several sizes for different frequencies and a mixture-distribution output, trained on a large open archive (LOTSA). Lag-Llama fed lagged values into a LLaMA-style decoder with a Student's t output. Moment, Timer, Time-MoE and others varied architecture and scale. Commercial offerings such as Nixtla's TimeGPT exposed a forecasting foundation model through an API.

Two shifts followed. First, speed and direct outputs: Chronos-Bolt (November 2024) replaced token-by-token sampling with patches and direct multi-step quantile outputs; its maintainers report 5% lower error and up to 250 times faster inference than original Chronos models of the same size. Second, covariates and multiple series: early foundation models were univariate and history-only, which is a severe limit in business forecasting where promotions, prices and calendars drive demand. Chronos-2 (released 20 October 2025, 120M parameters, encoder-only) supports univariate, multivariate and covariate-informed forecasting — past-only and known-future covariates, including categorical ones — in one model, and its authors report the best results among pretrained models on fev-bench and GIFT-Eval, with the largest gains on tasks with covariates. TimesFM 3.0 (August 2026) likewise added native multivariate forecasting and past-only and known-future covariates and reports top ranks on fev-bench, GIFT-Eval and the TIME benchmark. Leaderboards change month to month; the durable lesson is the direction of travel — from univariate shape-matching toward models that take the same inputs a good tree model takes.

6. General LLMs as forecasters

A separate line of work tried to use text LLMs for forecasting. LLMTime (Gruver et al., 2023) wrote series as digit strings — 123.4, 125.1 becomes "1234 , 1251" with fixed precision — and asked GPT-3 or LLaMA to continue the text, sampling several continuations for uncertainty. It worked surprisingly well on some benchmarks, which showed that LLMs have learned some generic sequence-continuation abilities. Its costs are large: each number takes several tokens (the lab's four numbers take 25 characters), context windows fill quickly, tokenisers split digits inconsistently (lm1), and API inference is far more expensive than a dedicated model.

Other methods — OneFitsAll (also called GPT4TS), Time-LLM and CALF — embedded time-series patches into a pretrained GPT-2 or LLaMA backbone, fine-tuning a few layers or adapters, and reported state-of-the-art results on the long-horizon benchmarks. The question of whether the language model contributed anything was answered by the ablation study in the next section, and the answer was mostly no.

7. The ablation that removed the LLM

Tan, Merrill, Gupta, Althoff and Hartvigsen (NeurIPS 2024) reproduced the three methods on the eight standard long-horizon datasets the original papers used plus five more, then ran simple ablations: remove the LLM entirely; replace it with a single multi-head attention layer; or replace it with a basic transformer block. Across 13 datasets and two metrics, the ablations matched or beat the LLM-based versions in 26 of 26 cases for Time-LLM, 22 of 26 for CALF and 19 of 26 for OneFitsAll, while reducing training and inference time by up to three orders of magnitude. Pretrained LLM weights did no better than training the same architecture from scratch, shuffling the input sequence hurt the LLM-based models no more than the ablations (suggesting they were not using sequential structure), and the LLMs did not help in few-shot settings. The authors concluded that patching and simple attention, not language knowledge, explained the performance.

For interviews, the general point is as important as the specific result: when a new component is credited with gains, the first experiment is to remove it. The DLinear result of ts7 and this study are the field's two standard examples.

8. Using a foundation model on your data

The practical workflow has five steps. Prepare: regular timestamps, explicit missing values (not zeros), the right frequency, and a context long enough to include at least a few seasonal cycles. Run zero-shot inside your rolling-origin backtest (ts5), producing quantiles. Compare against seasonal-naive, AutoETS and your best tree model on scaled point and probabilistic metrics, by segment and horizon. Adapt if needed: supply covariates (if the model accepts them), fine-tune on your data (fully or with LoRA, using later origins for validation), or ensemble the foundation model with a local model — ensembles of a foundation model and a statistical model are often more robust than either. Calibrate intervals conformally on recent errors (ts8), because zero-shot intervals are not automatically calibrated for your domain.

The lab builds a miniature version of the idea: it "pretrains" a single linear forecaster on 17,100 normalised windows from 300 synthetic series with random trends and cycles of period 7, 12 or 24, then forecasts a new series with a weekday/weekend pattern it never saw. Zero-shot, it beats the naive forecast easily (MAE 3.73 against 11.77) but loses to seasonal-naive (2.92), because its pretraining data had smooth sine-wave cycles and the new series has a square weekend jump. That is the foundation-model trade-off in two numbers: broad knowledge beats knowing nothing, but a simple model matched to the domain can still win.

9. Where language models help around forecasts

If LLMs are poor forecasters, where do they fit in a forecasting product? Around the numbers. An LLM can explain a forecast and its drivers in plain language for planners, given the model's outputs and feature contributions. It can turn unstructured information — "store 42 is closed for renovation from 3 to 10 March", a news article about a competitor's launch, a planner's note — into structured covariates or adjustment flags. It can write and review forecasting code and SQL. And as an agent it can call a forecasting tool (a foundation model or an internal model), run a backtest, check the results against baselines and draft a report. In all of these the numeric forecast comes from a model that was evaluated on numbers, and the LLM is never asked to invent figures or uncertainty bands; when it states numbers, they are passed in from tools and checked.

10. Licences, cost and deployment

Model choice is also a legal and operational decision. Chronos models are released under Apache-2.0 and can be self-hosted, including through SageMaker JumpStart. TimesFM weights up to version 2.5 are Apache-2.0, but TimesFM 3.0's downloadable weights carry a non-commercial licence, with commercial use offered through Google Cloud services such as BigQuery ML. Inference cost varies by orders of magnitude: a small patch-based model forecasts thousands of series per second on a GPU and is usable on CPU, while token-by-token sampling with a large model, or prompting a general LLM, can cost thousands of times more. Estimate cost per series per refresh, latency per request and licence terms before committing.

11. A worked zero-shot evaluation

Here is what an honest first evaluation looks like for a team considering a foundation model for 5,000 daily product series. They choose 12 weekly origins over the last quarter, horizon 28 days, and four candidates: seasonal-naive, AutoETS per series, their existing global LightGBM model with covariates, and a foundation model zero-shot with a 512-day context. For each origin, series and candidate they store the nine deciles and the median. The scorecard reports mean MASE and weighted quantile loss relative to seasonal-naive, per segment.

A typical outcome looks like this (illustrative numbers): overall, seasonal-naive 1.00, AutoETS 0.86, foundation model 0.82, LightGBM 0.78. By segment, the foundation model is best on new products with under 90 days of history (0.80 versus LightGBM's 0.91) and on smooth high-volume items, but worst on promotion-heavy categories (0.95 versus 0.72), because it cannot see the promotion calendar. Its 80% intervals cover 74% of outcomes before calibration and 80% after a per-segment conformal adjustment. The decision that follows is not "replace LightGBM" but "use the foundation model for cold-start and as an ensemble member, and test a covariate-aware version for promotions". Every claim in that decision comes from the scorecard, and the segment split is what made it possible.

12. Choosing among foundation models

When picking a model to try, five questions narrow the field. Does it accept the inputs you have — covariates, multiple related series, missing values? Does it output what you need — quantiles, sample paths, a fixed horizon or any horizon? What context length and frequency was it trained for, and do your series fit? What does it cost to run at your volume and latency — patch-based direct-output models are far cheaper than token-by-token samplers? And can you use it legally — licence for self-hosting, data-residency limits on API services? Benchmarks such as GIFT-Eval and fev-bench help rank candidates that pass these filters, but the final choice belongs to your own backtest.

13. Explaining a forecast with an LLM, worked

A planner asks the assistant: "Why is next week's forecast for store 42 down 18%?" A safe design never lets the language model compute the answer. The assistant calls tools: one returns the forecast and last year's actuals (week 23: forecast 4,100 units, same week last year 5,000); one returns the model's feature contributions for that series (store closure flag on Tuesday and Wednesday: −14%; school holidays ending: −6%; price increase on the top product: −2%; recent trend: +4%); one returns the store-events table ("Closed 3-4 June for refurbishment"). The LLM receives these numbers as structured context and writes: "The forecast is 4,100 units, 18% below last year's 5,000. Most of the drop comes from the planned two-day closure for refurbishment (about −14%), with school holidays ending (−6%) and the price change (−2%) partly offset by recent growth (+4%)." Every number in the sentence came from a tool; the model's job was selection, ordering and plain language. A validation step checks that each number in the output appears in the tool results, and the response links to the underlying data. This is the pattern — LLM for language, models for numbers, tools in between — that makes language models useful in forecasting products without letting them invent forecasts.

Why mean scaling and binning work — and what they lose

Mean scaling makes the model scale-invariant: multiplying a series by 1,000 multiplies the forecast by 1,000. That is what lets one vocabulary serve retail units and server bytes. The cost is that absolute level carries no information, so the model cannot learn that "series around 1,000 behave differently from series around 1". Binning to a categorical output lets the model represent any distribution shape and reuse language-model training code unchanged; the costs are the clipping range, the fixed relative precision and a loss that ignores numeric distance. Chronos's authors note that cross-entropy is not distance-aware; later designs moved to direct regression of quantiles from patches (Chronos-Bolt, Chronos-2) or to mixture-distribution heads (Moirai), which encode distance and are much faster.

Data diversity, synthetic data and contamination

Time-series data is less abundant and less diverse than text: a few domains (energy, traffic, web) dominate public collections. Synthetic data fills gaps: KernelSynth's Gaussian-process series cover trends, seasonalities and their combinations at many scales, and the Chronos ablations found the clearest improvement at about 10% synthetic data, with larger proportions tending to worsen performance. The flip side is contamination: when a benchmark's datasets (or overlapping series) are in a model's pretraining corpus, its "zero-shot" score is partly in-domain. Benchmarks such as GIFT-Eval publish training-test separation rules, and Chronos explicitly separated in-domain (Benchmark I) from zero-shot (Benchmark II) evaluation. When reading a leaderboard, check which models trained on which datasets.

Context length and patching trade-offs

Longer contexts let a model see more cycles, but cost grows with the number of tokens, and many series are short. Patching reduces tokens by the patch length, which is why most second-generation models use it; TimesFM's masking of partial patches during training keeps it from depending on context lengths that are multiples of 32. The Chronos ablations found accuracy improved with context length up to about 1,024 and then saturated or worsened, likely because the training data had few long series. In practice, give the model several seasonal cycles of context (for hourly data, at least one or two weeks), and backtest one or two context lengths.

Fine-tuning and parameter-efficient adaptation

Fine-tuning helps when your domain is unlike the pretraining mix, when you have covariates or patterns the model has not seen, or when a few percent of accuracy matters. Chronos reported that an inexpensive fine-tuning of its Small model moved it to the top of its zero-shot benchmark. Fine-tuning risks overfitting on short histories and forgetting general skills; use low learning rates, few steps, held-out later origins and comparisons against zero-shot. Parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA (low-rank adapters on attention weights) cut memory and make it practical to keep one adapter per domain on a shared base model, an approach TimesFM's repository demonstrates.

Multivariate and covariate modelling in foundation models

Supporting covariates in a zero-shot model is harder than in a tree model, because the model must learn, from context alone, how an unlabelled input relates to the target: whether "covariate 3" is a price, a temperature or a holiday flag. Covariate-aware foundation models handle this by letting the target and covariates attend to each other within the context, learning the relationship in-context from the history window — a form of in-context learning. This works when the context contains enough examples of the covariate's effect (several promotions, several holidays). It does not replace domain knowledge when effects are rare; one Christmas in the context window is not enough to learn Christmas.

Scaling laws for time series

Language models improve predictably with more parameters and data. Time-series foundation models show weaker and noisier scaling: larger Chronos models were somewhat better than smaller ones, but gains flatten, and a small, well-designed model (Chronos-Bolt Small, Chronos-2 at 120M) can beat a much larger earlier one. The likely reasons are data — public time-series corpora are far smaller and less diverse than text corpora, so models run out of new patterns to learn — and the inherent unpredictability of many series, which caps achievable accuracy no matter the model size. For an engineer this means model size is a weak proxy for quality; evaluate small models first, since they are much cheaper to serve.

Agents and tool use

The most practical role for language models in forecasting today is as orchestrators. An agent can receive a question ("will we run out of GPU capacity in Europe next month?"), query the right metrics, call a forecasting tool for quantiles, call a capacity calculator with the 95th percentile, and report with caveats. The engineering challenges are those of any agent: validating tool outputs, preventing the model from inventing numbers, keeping humans in the loop for consequential decisions, and logging every tool call so a forecast can be traced. The forecasting model behind the tool is evaluated with the methods of ts5 and ts8, independently of the agent.

The case: Chronos, and the LLM ablation study

The Chronos paper is the clearest public account of how a time-series foundation model is built and evaluated. Its design is deliberately minimal — an unmodified T5 trained on tokenised values — and its evaluation is careful: 42 datasets split into 15 used in training (in-domain) and 27 never seen (zero-shot), probabilistic and point metrics both reported relative to seasonal-naive, and comparisons against local statistical models (Naive, Seasonal Naive, AutoETS, AutoARIMA, AutoTheta), task-specific deep models trained on each dataset (DeepAR, PatchTST, N-BEATS, N-HiTS, TFT, DLinear and others) and other pretrained models. Its headline: on in-domain data Chronos models beat all of these; on the 27 zero-shot datasets they comfortably outperformed statistical baselines and other pretrained models and performed on par with the best deep models that had been trained on those very datasets; and a cheap fine-tune of the 46M-parameter Small model took the top spot. The repository, models and benchmark datasets were released openly, and the line continued with Chronos-Bolt and Chronos-2, and with the fev benchmarking package.

The Tan et al. study is its counterpoint. It did not test time-series foundation models; it tested text LLMs repurposed for forecasting, and found their language pretraining contributed nothing measurable while costing up to a thousand times more compute. Together the two papers give the interview answer: pretraining helps when the pretraining data is time series and the evaluation is honest; borrowing a text LLM's weights for numeric forecasting is not justified by the evidence.

How foundation-model forecasting fails in production

Silent domain mismatch. The model is excellent on smooth, regular series and poor on intermittent or covariate-driven ones, but its forecasts always look plausible. Without a per-segment backtest against seasonal-naive, nobody notices. Keep baselines running and route segments to whichever model wins.

Bad inputs. Missing timestamps filled with zeros, wrong frequency detection, or a context too short to contain a seasonal cycle produce confident nonsense. Validate inputs (regularity, length, share of missing values) before calling the model and fall back to simple methods when they fail.

Uncalibrated intervals. Zero-shot quantiles are calibrated on the pretraining mix, not your data. Measure coverage and apply conformal calibration per segment.

Licence and dependency risk. A model whose licence changes between versions, or an API whose pricing changes, can force a migration. Keep the forecasting interface model-agnostic and keep a self-hosted fallback.

LLM-generated numbers. A chat assistant asked "what will sales be next month?" without a forecasting tool will produce a number anyway. Route numeric questions to tools, show where numbers came from, and refuse to answer when no validated forecast exists.

A rough cost estimate

Suppose 2 million series are re-forecast nightly with a 28-day horizon. A patch-based model that forecasts a batch of 1,000 series in about a second on one GPU needs roughly 2,000 GPU-seconds — about half a GPU-hour per night, a few dollars. A token-by-token sampler drawing 20 paths of 28 steps per series does hundreds of times more sequential work, turning the same job into hundreds of GPU-hours. Prompting a general LLM API with 500 numbers per series (several thousand tokens each) would cost thousands of dollars per night and still need validation. These orders of magnitude, rather than small accuracy differences, often decide the architecture.

What to monitor

Accuracy by segment and horizon against seasonal-naive and the incumbent model; interval coverage; the share of series where inputs failed validation and a fallback was used; latency and cost per series; and drift in input characteristics (frequency mix, series length, share of zeros) that would move the workload away from what the model handles well.

Goal: implement Chronos's tokeniser and see its precision and clipping, count what LLMTime-style text serialisation costs, and "pretrain" a tiny global forecaster on synthetic series to forecast an unseen series zero-shot. NumPy only, gpt_env (Python 3.12); no model downloads. Save as ts9.py.

import numpy as np

# 1. Chronos-style tokenisation: mean scaling + uniform bins in [-15, 15]
B = 4094                                   # 4096 vocabulary minus PAD and EOS
centers = np.linspace(-15, 15, B)
def tokenize(context):
    s = np.mean(np.abs(context))            # mean absolute value of the context
    ids = np.clip(np.round((context / s + 15) / (30 / (B - 1))).astype(int), 0, B - 1)
    return ids, s
def detokenize(ids, s):
    return centers[ids] * s

ctx = np.array([20.0, 30.0, 40.0, 50.0])
ids, s = tokenize(ctx)
print("scale", s, "token ids", ids.tolist(), "-> back to", np.round(detokenize(ids, s), 3).tolist())
print("bin width in original units:", round(30 / (B - 1) * s, 4))
# A future value 20x the context's mean cannot be represented: it clips to the top bin
future_id = np.clip(np.round((700.0 / s + 15) / (30 / (B - 1))).astype(int), 0, B - 1)
print("future value 700 (20x the context mean) decodes to", round(float(centers[future_id] * s), 1))

# 2. LLMTime-style serialisation: how many characters does a series cost as text?
series = [123.4, 125.1, 130.9, 128.7]
text = " , ".join(str(int(round(v * 10))) for v in series)    # fixed precision, no decimal point
print("\nas text:", repr(text), f"({len(text)} characters for {len(series)} numbers)")

# 3. A tiny 'foundation model': pretrain one global linear forecaster on many synthetic series,
#    then forecast a new series it has never seen (zero-shot)
rng = np.random.default_rng(9)
L, H = 48, 12
def synthetic(n=400):
    t = np.arange(n)
    period = rng.choice([7, 12, 24])
    y = rng.normal(0, 1) * t / n + sum(rng.normal(0, 1) * np.sin(2 * np.pi * k * t / period + rng.uniform(0, 6))
                                       for k in (1, 2))
    return y + rng.normal(0, 0.3, n) + 5
def windows(y, step=6):
    X, Y = [], []
    for e in range(L, len(y) - H, step):
        ctx, fut = y[e - L:e], y[e:e + H]
        mu, sd = ctx.mean(), ctx.std() + 1e-8                 # per-window normalisation
        X.append((ctx - mu) / sd); Y.append((fut - mu) / sd)
    return np.array(X), np.array(Y)
Xs, Ys = zip(*[windows(synthetic()) for _ in range(300)])
X, Y = np.vstack(Xs), np.vstack(Ys)
W = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X + 1.0 * np.eye(L), X.T @ Y)        # 'pretraining'
print(f"\npretrained on {len(X):,} windows from 300 synthetic series")

# A new 'real' series: weekly cycle with a different shape, level 200, noise
t = np.arange(300)
new = 200 + 30 * (t % 7 >= 5) + 10 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * t / 7) + rng.normal(0, 4, 300) + 0.1 * t
ctx, fut = new[-L - H:-H], new[-H:]
mu, sd = ctx.mean(), ctx.std()
zero_shot = (ctx - mu) / sd @ W * sd + mu
snaive = np.resize(ctx[-7:], H)
mae = lambda f: np.mean(np.abs(f - fut))
print(f"zero-shot MAE {mae(zero_shot):.2f}   seasonal-naive MAE {mae(snaive):.2f}   naive MAE {mae(np.repeat(ctx[-1], H)):.2f}")

What to look for. The first line reproduces the worked tokenisation. The bin width shows the precision at this scale, and the clipped 700 → 525 shows the ±15× range limit. The text serialisation shows why prompting a general LLM with long numeric histories is token-hungry. The miniature foundation model, never trained on the new series, beats naive by a wide margin — it has learned generic seasonal continuation from synthetic data — but loses to seasonal-naive, because the square weekend pattern of the new series is unlike the smooth sine cycles it was pretrained on.

Try next. Add square-wave (weekday/weekend) series to the synthetic generator and re-run: the zero-shot error should drop toward or below seasonal-naive, the effect of more diverse pretraining data. Shorten the context L to 14 and see the zero-shot model lose its grip on the weekly cycle.

Work it out

Your company runs 40,000 internal metrics (service latencies, queue depths, sign-ups, spend) and wants a weekly 8-week forecast for each, with 80% intervals, to flag metrics likely to breach thresholds. The data team proposes (A) a time-series foundation model zero-shot, (B) AutoETS per metric, (C) prompting a general LLM with each metric's last 200 values.

(a) Rank the options for a first version and justify. (b) How would you evaluate before launch? (c) A metric's context averages 12 and contains a spike of 250 two weeks ago. What does a Chronos-style tokeniser do with it, and why might the forecast misbehave?

Answer

(a) A or B first, C last. A gives strong zero-shot quantiles across heterogeneous metrics with no per-series fitting; B is cheap, robust, interpretable and a natural baseline; running both and choosing per metric by backtest (or averaging) is a strong v1. C is expensive (200 values ≈ 1,000+ characters per metric, 40,000 metrics weekly), slow, and evidence shows general LLMs add no forecasting accuracy; its intervals would be unvalidated.

(b) Rolling-origin backtest over the last 6-12 months with weekly origins and an 8-week horizon; MASE and pinball/coverage of the 80% intervals relative to seasonal-naive, by metric family (latency, counts, spend); and the decision metric — precision and recall of threshold-breach alerts. Calibrate intervals conformally per family before launch.

(c) The scale is the mean absolute value of the context — the spike pulls it up, to roughly 12 plus 250/C (with C = 512, about 12.5). The spike scales to about 250/12.5 = 20, beyond +15, so it clips to the top bin. Meanwhile the spike sits in the context the model conditions on, so forecasts may echo it (a repeating spike) or widen intervals. Flag or clean known outliers before forecasting, or pass them as an event covariate, and check the forecast against a baseline.
