A RoPE model is trained with a 4,096-token context. You want 32,768 without retraining from scratch. What does position interpolation do, and why does it work where naive extrapolation fails?

Answer

Naive extrapolation feeds position indices 4,096–32,767 that the model has literally never seen; the rotation angles fall outside the trained range and attention scores become nonsense.

Position interpolation instead compresses the new range into the old one — divide every position index by 8 so index 32,767 maps to 4,096. Every angle is now inside the trained distribution. The cost is reduced angular resolution between adjacent tokens, which a short fine-tune (a few hundred steps on long documents) recovers.

YaRN refines this: it interpolates low-frequency dimensions (which encode long-range position) while leaving high-frequency ones (local ordering) alone, so fine local resolution is preserved. That is why YaRN needs ~10× less fine-tuning than uniform interpolation.

You have 50,000 preference pairs and a 7B SFT model. Sketch what you would train with DPO rather than RLHF, and name the one hyper-parameter that most controls how far the policy drifts.

Answer

DPO needs no reward model and no rollouts: you load two copies of the SFT model — one trainable policy, one frozen reference — and minimise a loss over (prompt, chosen, rejected) triples that raises the policy's log-probability of chosen relative to rejected, each measured against the reference.

The controlling hyper-parameter is β, the implicit KL penalty. Low β (0.01) lets the policy move far and chase the preference signal — faster gains, higher risk of degeneration and over-refusal. High β (0.5) keeps it close to the SFT model and barely moves. Typical starting point: β = 0.1, learning rate 5e-7 to 5e-6, one to three epochs. Watch the KL from the reference as a training metric, not just the loss.

An agent completes a task in 12 tool calls. Each call succeeds 96% of the time. What is the end-to-end success rate, and what would you change to reach 90%?

Answer

0.96¹² = 0.613 — 61%. Reaching 90% by improving per-step reliability alone would need 0.90^(1/12) = 0.991, i.e. 99.1% per step. Going from 96% to 99.1% is usually not achievable by prompting.

So change the structure instead:

  • Fewer steps. Collapse 12 calls into 5 composite tools: 0.96⁵ = 0.82, and each composite tool is deterministic code you can test.
  • Checkpoint and retry per step. A step that can be retried twice moves from 96% to 1 − 0.04³ = 99.99%, provided failures are independent and the step is idempotent.
  • Verify before proceeding. Catching a bad step early prevents it compounding through the remaining calls.

Mixtral 8×7B has 8 experts per layer and routes each token to the top 2. Why is it 47B parameters rather than 56B, and why does it not save you any VRAM?

Answer

Why not 56B: only the FFN blocks are replicated across experts. Attention projections, embeddings, norms and the LM head are shared. 8 × 7B would double-count all of that shared weight; the actual total is ~47B.

Why no VRAM saving: the router picks experts per token, and a batch contains many tokens, so in practice nearly every expert is needed on every forward pass. All 47B parameters must be resident. What you save is compute — roughly 13B parameters are activated per token, so you get 13B-model FLOPs at close to 47B-model quality.

The one-line summary for an interview: MoE decouples parameter count from per-token compute. It trades memory for quality, which is the right trade only when you are compute-bound rather than memory-bound.
