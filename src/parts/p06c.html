In scikit-learn you write LogisticRegression(C=0.01). A colleague says that is barely any regularisation. Who is right, and what happens to the coefficients as you sweep C from 100 down to 0.001?

Answer

Your colleague has it backwards. scikit-learn parameterises by C = 1/λ, the inverse of regularisation strength. C=0.01 is λ=100 — very strong. C=100 is nearly unregularised.

Sweeping down from C=100: coefficients shrink smoothly toward zero under the default L2 penalty; with penalty='l1' they hit exactly zero one by one, and at small enough C every coefficient is zero and the model predicts the base rate.

Two follow-ons worth knowing. Scale first — an unscaled feature measured in pounds and one in thousands get wildly unequal penalties, so the penalty is silently deciding which features survive based on units. And the intercept is not penalised, which is why the model can still fit the base rate at any C.

The same inversion trips people up in SVMs, where C is also 1/λ.

You are minimising f(w) = (c/2)w² with plain gradient descent, and the curvature is c = 50. Find the largest stable learning rate, the fastest one, and say what happens just above and just below the limit.

Answer

The update is w ← w − ηcw = w(1 − ηc). Each step multiplies w by (1 − ηc), so it converges exactly when |1 − ηc| < 1, i.e. 0 < η < 2/c = 0.04.

  • η = 0.02 = 1/c — the factor is 0, so it lands on the optimum in one step. Fastest possible.
  • η = 0.039 — factor −0.95: converges, but oscillating across the minimum, taking ~60 steps to shrink by 20×.
  • η = 0.04 — factor −1: oscillates forever between ±w₀, never converging, never diverging.
  • η = 0.05 — factor −1.5: each step is 50% further out. NaN in about 40 steps.

Real losses have many curvatures at once, and stability is set by the largest one while progress along the flattest direction is set by the smallest — the ratio between them is the condition number, and it is why momentum and per-parameter step sizes exist at all.

What doubles is the odds, not the probability: e^0.693 = 2.0.

Fraud runs at 1%. You undersample negatives to train on a balanced 1:1 set. The model now outputs 0.5 for a transaction. What is the actual probability that it is fraud?

Answer

About 1%, not 50%.

Undersampling changed the prior the model learned. Correct it on the odds scale:

true_odds = model_odds × (sampled_neg/sampled_pos)⁻¹ × (orig_neg/orig_pos)⁻¹, which here is model_odds × (1/1) × (1/100) .

At 0.5 the model odds are 1.0, so true odds = 0.01, giving p = 0.01/1.01 = 0.99%.

Why it matters: the ranking is unaffected, so if you only need a top-N review queue, undersampling costs you nothing. But the moment anything computes expected value — p × loss for a decline decision, or an alert budget — a 50× inflated probability produces 50× wrong decisions. Either apply the offset above, or recalibrate on a held-out set at the true prevalence.

Same warning applies to class weights and to SMOTE, and SMOTE must be applied inside each CV fold or synthetic neighbours of validation points leak into training.

Answer

At least two, and both are structural rather than statistical.

1. Group leakage. 40,000 rows over 12,000 patients means ~3.3 visits each. A random 5-fold split puts the same patient in train and validation, so the model learns the patient, not the condition. Fix: GroupKFold on patient id. Expect CV to drop sharply — that drop is the measurement becoming honest, not a regression.

A random forest has 500 trees, each 80% accurate. If their errors were independent, how accurate is the majority vote? Then explain why real forests are nowhere near that.

Answer

The vote is wrong only if 251+ trees are wrong. With X ~ Binomial(500, 0.8), mean 400 and standard deviation √(500×0.8×0.2) = 8.94, the threshold of 250 sits 16.8 standard deviations below the mean. The failure probability is vanishingly small — effectively 100% accuracy.

Real forests land around 85–90%, because the independence assumption is false. Trees trained on bootstrap samples of the same data, with the same dominant features available, make highly correlated mistakes. Averaging B models with pairwise correlation ρ leaves variance ρσ² + (1−ρ)σ²/B — as B → ∞ the second term vanishes but ρσ² never does.

That residual term is the whole reason a random forest samples a random subset of features at every split: it is not a speed optimisation, it is a deliberate attack on ρ. And it is why adding trees past a few hundred stops helping, while decorrelating them still does.

(a) Inertia is within-cluster sum of squares, and it cannot increase when you add a centroid — at k = n every point is its own cluster and inertia is exactly 0. That is why you look for the elbow (where marginal improvement drops off) or use silhouette score, which penalises clusters that are close together, or gap statistic, or BIC on a GMM.   • Halving the detectable effect quadruples the sample. A 5% relative lift needs ~4× as long — over three months. That is usually the moment to ask whether a 5% lift is worth detecting at all.
  • Do not peek. Checking daily and stopping at significance inflates the false-positive rate well past 5%. Fix the horizon in advance, or use a sequential test designed for continuous monitoring.
