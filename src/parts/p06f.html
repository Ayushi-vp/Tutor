The one assumption you lose

Almost everything in the ML track assumes rows are exchangeable: shuffle them and nothing changes. A time series is the opposite — the order is the information. Today's value is correlated with yesterday's, so rows are not independent, random train/test splits leak the future, and a model can look brilliant by memorising the neighbourhood of each test point. Every technique in this track is some answer to that one fact.

The vocabulary interviewers expect

  • Frequency — the sampling interval (minutely, hourly, daily, weekly). It fixes which seasonalities exist: hourly data has daily (24) and weekly (168) cycles; monthly data has yearly (12).
  • Lookback / context — how much history the model sees. Horizon — how far ahead you predict (h). The forecast origin is the last observed timestamp.
  • Univariate vs multivariate — one series, or several whose joint behaviour matters (CPU, memory and latency of one host).
  • Covariates, three kinds. Static (store size, product category). Known-future (calendar, holidays, planned price, scheduled promotions). Past-only (weather actuals, competitor price you observed). Mixing up the last two is the most common leak in forecasting: you cannot use tomorrow's actual weather to forecast tomorrow.
  • Local vs global models — one model per series (classical ARIMA/ETS) versus one model trained across thousands of series (LightGBM, DeepAR, foundation models). Global models share strength, handle cold-start and are how the M5 competition was won.

The task families

Forecasting (predict future values — point or distribution), anomaly detection (flag points or windows that do not fit), classification (label a whole window — an ECG beat, a machine state), imputation (fill gaps), and causal / counterfactual questions (what would sales have been without the campaign?). Most AI-engineer interviews touch forecasting and anomaly detection; the others appear as follow-ups.

Where an AI engineer meets this

Capacity planning for GPU fleets and LLM traffic, token-cost forecasting, demand forecasting, fraud and latency anomaly alerts, sensor/IoT monitoring, and — increasingly — agents that call a forecasting model as a tool. You will be asked to design one of these, not to derive the Kalman filter.

The four components

Almost every business series reads as trend (the slow level change), seasonality (a fixed-period pattern — daily, weekly, yearly), cycle (rises and falls with no fixed period, like business cycles) and remainder (what is left). Decomposition separates them so you can model, plot or remove each one.

Additive or multiplicative

Additive: y = T + S + R — the seasonal swing is the same size whatever the level. Multiplicative: y = T × S × R — the swing grows with the level (December is +20%, not +200 units). Look at the plot: if the seasonal amplitude fans out as the series grows, it is multiplicative. The standard move is to take logs, which turns multiplicative into additive; a Box-Cox transform generalises this with a tunable exponent. Remember to back-transform — and that the back-transformed mean of a log forecast is the median, not the mean, unless you bias-adjust.

STL — the decomposition to know

STL (Seasonal-Trend decomposition using Loess) iterates: smooth the detrended series within each seasonal position to get the seasonal component, smooth what remains to get the trend, repeat. It handles any period, lets seasonality drift slowly over time, and has a robust mode that down-weights outliers so one spike does not distort the seasonal shape. MSTL extends it to several periods at once (daily + weekly on hourly data). Classical moving-average decomposition is what textbooks start with; STL is what you use.

Calendar effects are not seasonality

  • Moving holidays (Easter, Diwali, Lunar New Year) shift dates each year — a fixed period-365 seasonality smears them. Model them as explicit indicator features, including the days before and after.
  • Trading-day effects: a month with five Saturdays sells more. Monthly data needs day-of-week counts as regressors.
  • Long or fractional periods (weekly data has 52.18 weeks a year) break seasonal-ARIMA; use Fourier terms — sin(2πkt/P), cos(2πkt/P) for k = 1…K — which handle any real-valued period with 2K features.

Stationarity, precisely

A series is (weakly) stationary if its mean and variance do not change over time and the covariance between y_t and y_{t−k} depends only on the lag k, not on t. Trend breaks the first condition, growing volatility breaks the second, and seasonality makes the covariance depend on where in the cycle you are. Classical models (ARMA) assume stationarity; ML models do not require it, but they still extrapolate badly outside the range they were trained on — which is exactly what a trending series asks them to do.

Autocorrelation — the core diagnostic

The ACF at lag k is the correlation between the series and itself shifted by k. The PACF at lag k is that correlation after removing what lags 1…k−1 already explain. Read them together:

  • Slow, near-linear ACF decay from ~1 → trend or a unit root. Difference first.
  • Spikes at 12, 24, 36 (monthly) or 7, 14 (daily) → seasonality at that period.
  • ACF decays geometrically, PACF cuts off after lag p → AR(p). ACF cuts off after q, PACF decays → MA(q).
  • Everything inside ±1.96/√n → white noise: nothing linear left to model. This is what you want your residuals to look like (the Ljung-Box test formalises it).

Unit roots and differencing

A random walk, y_t = y_{t−1} + ε_t, has a unit root: shocks never decay, variance grows with t, and the best forecast is simply the last value. Stock prices are the canonical example. First differencing (y_t − y_{t−1}) removes a unit root or a linear trend; seasonal differencing (y_t − y_{t−12}) removes a stable seasonal pattern. Over-differencing is a real mistake — it injects a negative lag-1 autocorrelation near −0.5 — so difference only as much as the tests say.

The two tests, and why you run both

ADF: null hypothesis = unit root (non-stationary); a small p-value says stationary. KPSS: null = stationary; a small p-value says non-stationary. Because the nulls are opposite, running both tells you more than either: ADF rejects and KPSS does not → stationary; ADF fails to reject and KPSS rejects → difference it; both reject → probably trend-stationary or a structural break, look at the plot.

Spurious regression — the classic trap

Regress one random walk on another independent random walk and you routinely get R² above 0.5 and "significant" coefficients. Two trending series correlate because both trend. Before claiming X drives Y, difference both (or test for cointegration) and check whether the relationship survives.

Baselines first, always

Before anything else, compute four baselines. Naive: forecast = last value (optimal for a random walk). Seasonal naive: forecast = value one season ago. Drift: extend the line from first to last observation. Mean. Seasonal naive is embarrassingly hard to beat on retail and traffic data; in the M-competitions, simple methods and their combinations beat many sophisticated ones for decades. A model you cannot show beats seasonal naive in a proper backtest is not ready — and saying this unprompted is a strong interview signal.

Exponential smoothing (ETS)

Simple exponential smoothing keeps a level updated as ℓ_t = α·y_t + (1−α)·ℓ_{t−1} — a weighted average with geometrically decaying weights. Its forecast is flat because it has no notion of slope. Holt adds a trend term (optionally damped, which is usually safer for long horizons). Holt-Winters adds a seasonal term, additive or multiplicative. The ETS framework (Error, Trend, Seasonal) writes all 30 variants as state-space models, which gives you likelihoods, AIC-based automatic selection and proper prediction intervals. Fast, robust, and a very strong default for a single series.

ARIMA

AR(p): regress on your own p previous values. MA(q): regress on the previous q forecast errors. I(d): difference d times first. SARIMA(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)ₘ adds the same structure at the seasonal lag m, and ARIMAX adds external regressors. The Box-Jenkins workflow is: make it stationary → read orders off ACF/PACF → fit → check residuals are white noise → forecast. In practice auto_arima-style search over orders by AICc replaces the manual reading, but you should be able to explain what it is choosing.

Where they stop working

  • One model per series: no sharing, so no help for new or short series, and millions of fits.
  • Single seasonality: hourly data with daily + weekly + yearly cycles needs TBATS, MSTL-plus-ARIMA, or Fourier regressors.
  • Linear and Gaussian: poor on counts, intermittent demand and non-linear covariate effects like promotions interacting with price.

Prophet (a trend + Fourier seasonality + holiday regression, fitted as a curve) is worth naming: easy for analysts, handles holidays and missing data, but in independent benchmarks it frequently loses to ETS and even seasonal naive. Know it; do not lead with it. The Theta method — a simple decomposition that won the M3 competition — is the other name worth recognising.

Never shuffle

A random K-fold on time series trains on points after the ones it is tested on, and every test point has near-identical neighbours in training. Errors look tiny and do not survive contact with production. The honest scheme is walk-forward (rolling-origin) backtesting: pick a forecast origin, train on everything before it, forecast h steps, score, move the origin forward, repeat.

  • Expanding window — training always starts at the beginning. More data per fold; assumes old data is still relevant.
  • Sliding window — fixed-length training window. Adapts to regime change; less data.
  • Gap (embargo) — leave a buffer between train and test equal to the data latency or the feature lookback. If your sales data lands two days late in production, your backtest must also be two days blind.
  • Mirror production exactly: same horizon, same retraining cadence, same feature availability at the origin. A backtest that evaluates one-step-ahead when production forecasts 14 days ahead is measuring a different product.

Metrics and their failure modes

  • MAE — optimised by the median; robust, in the units of the series. RMSE — optimised by the mean; punishes large misses. Neither is comparable across series of different scales.
  • MAPE — scale-free and loved by business, but undefined at zero, explodes near zero, and asymmetric: it punishes over-forecasts more than under-forecasts, so optimising it biases forecasts low. sMAPE tries to fix this and introduces its own asymmetry.
  • WAPE (Σ|e| / Σ|y|) — the retail workhorse: scale-free, fine with zeros in individual rows, and weights high-volume items appropriately.
  • MASE — MAE divided by the in-sample MAE of the (seasonal) naive forecast. Below 1 means you beat naive. Scale-free, defined with zeros, and the recommended default for comparing across series.
  • Pinball loss / CRPS — for probabilistic forecasts (next topics). Report coverage of your intervals too.

Report it the way it will be used

Break error down by horizon (day 1 error is always lower than day 28), by segment (new vs mature items, high vs low volume), and around events (holidays, promos). An aggregate WAPE can hide a model that is great on staples and useless on the promoted items that actually drive ordering decisions. And ask what the forecast is for: an inventory decision cares about an asymmetric cost, which a symmetric metric will never capture.

Turn forecasting into supervised learning

Reframe each (series, timestamp) as a row: target = value at t + h, features = what is known at t. Then any tabular model works — and gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM, XGBoost, CatBoost) are the workhorse. In the M5 competition on Walmart data, almost every top solution was a LightGBM variant trained globally across all series.

The feature set

  • Lags: y_{t−1}, y_{t−7}, y_{t−28}, y_{t−364} — chosen from the seasonal periods and the ACF.
  • Rolling statistics on shifted data: mean, std, min, max over 7/28/91 days. They must end at or before the forecast origin — a centred or unshifted window leaks the target.
  • Calendar: day of week, week of year, month, holiday flags and days-to/from holiday, payday, Fourier terms.
  • Known-future covariates: planned price, promotion flags, price relative to its own recent average.
  • Static / ID features: store, category, item — as categoricals, or target-encoded within the training window only.
  • Per-series scaling: divide by each series' recent mean so one global model can learn shapes across series of very different volumes.

Multi-step strategies — a favourite follow-up

  • Recursive: one one-step model; feed each prediction back in as a lag for the next step. One model, but errors compound over the horizon, and training/serving mismatch appears if you trained on true lags.
  • Direct: a separate model per horizon h, each using only lags ≥ h. No compounding, but H models and less coherent paths.
  • Multi-output (MIMO): one model emits the whole horizon vector — how most neural forecasters work.

Where trees struggle

Trees cannot extrapolate: a tree never predicts outside the range of targets it saw, so a strongly trending series flattens. The fix is to model a detrended or differenced target (or the ratio to a recent mean) and add the trend back. Trees also need you to hand them the temporal structure — which is precisely the part deep models try to learn for themselves.

The lineage, in one breath

RNN/LSTM/GRU read the series step by step with a hidden state; gates fix the vanishing gradient enough for a few hundred steps. Seq2seq encoders/decoders emit a whole horizon. TCNs use dilated causal convolutions (the WaveNet trick) — parallel to train, with a receptive field that grows exponentially with depth. Then the global-model families:

  • DeepAR (Amazon, 2017): one autoregressive LSTM trained across all series, outputting the parameters of a distribution (Gaussian, negative binomial for counts) and sampling paths. Established that a global deep model plus probabilistic output beats per-series methods at scale.
  • N-BEATS / N-HiTS: stacks of fully-connected blocks with backward and forward residual links; no recurrence, no attention. Won-M4-level accuracy with pure MLPs; N-BEATS has an interpretable variant with trend and seasonal bases, N-HiTS adds multi-rate sampling for long horizons.
  • Temporal Fusion Transformer (Google, 2019): the "production" architecture — explicit handling of static, known-future and past-only covariates, variable-selection networks you can inspect, attention over time, and quantile outputs. The one to cite for "forecast with rich covariates".

The transformer detour

Informer, Autoformer, FEDformer and others adapted attention to long horizons. Then in 2022 DLinear — a single linear layer on a trend/remainder decomposition — beat them on the standard long-horizon benchmarks. The lesson: point-wise attention over individual timestamps is a poor inductive bias, because one timestamp carries almost no semantic information (unlike a word).

What fixed it

  • Patching (PatchTST): split the lookback into patches of, say, 16 steps; each patch becomes a token. Local shape is captured inside the token, sequence length drops 8–16×, and attention now compares meaningful segments.
  • Channel independence: model each variable of a multivariate series with shared weights instead of mixing channels early — surprisingly robust. iTransformer goes the other way, treating each variable as a token so attention learns cross-series correlation.
  • Instance normalisation (RevIN): normalise each input window by its own mean and std, predict in that space, de-normalise. It is one of the cheapest and most effective fixes for distribution shift between training and test windows.
  • TiDE and similar MLP encoders showed, again, that a well-designed MLP matches transformers at a fraction of the cost.

When to reach for deep learning at all

Many related series (thousands+), long histories, rich covariates, or a need for learned representations shared across tasks. For a handful of short series, ETS or a tree model will usually win and be far cheaper to run and explain. Say this before you say "transformer".

Why a point forecast is not enough

Decisions are asymmetric. Running out of stock loses the margin and the customer; overstocking costs holding and waste. The newsvendor result says the optimal order is the quantile c_u / (c_u + c_o) of the demand distribution, where c_u is the cost of under-stocking and c_o of over-stocking. Capacity planning for a GPU cluster is the same problem: you provision to the p95 of forecast load, not the mean. So the product needs a distribution, not a number.

Three ways to get one

  • Quantile regression: train with the pinball loss for each quantile τ — τ·(y − ŷ) if you under-predicted, (1−τ)·(ŷ − y) if you over-predicted. LightGBM and TFT support it directly. Cheap, distribution-free; watch for quantile crossing (p90 below p80) and sort or constrain.
  • Parametric heads: predict the parameters of a distribution and train by negative log-likelihood — Gaussian for continuous data, negative binomial or Tweedie for non-negative counts with zeros (DeepAR). Sampling gives coherent paths, which you need when a decision depends on the sum over the horizon.
  • Conformal prediction: wrap any point model; use the empirical quantile of calibration residuals as the interval half-width. Classic conformal assumes exchangeability, which time series breaks, so use the time-series variants: EnbPI (bootstrap ensembles with a rolling residual set) or adaptive conformal inference, which nudges the quantile level up after each miss and down after each hit so long-run coverage tracks the target even under drift.

Evaluate the distribution, not the mean

Coverage: does the 80% interval contain the truth ~80% of the time, per horizon and segment? Sharpness: given correct coverage, narrower is better. CRPS scores the whole predictive distribution (it reduces to MAE for a point forecast), and averaged pinball loss across quantiles approximates it. A model with lower MAE and badly calibrated intervals can be worse for the business than one with slightly higher MAE and honest intervals.

Intermittent demand

Spare parts, long-tail SKUs, rare-event counts: mostly zeros with occasional spikes. MAPE is meaningless and Gaussian models forecast fractional nonsense. Croston's method forecasts demand size and inter-demand interval separately; modern practice uses global models with Tweedie or negative-binomial losses and evaluates with scaled errors or pinball loss.

Three kinds of anomaly

Point (one absurd value), contextual (a normal value at the wrong time — heavy traffic at 4am), and collective (a window whose shape is wrong even though every point is in range). Your method has to match the kind; a z-score catches the first and misses the other two.

The methods, simplest first

  • Forecast-residual detection: forecast each point from its past, flag residuals outside a (conformal or quantile) interval. Handles seasonality and trend for free and is explainable ("we expected 400, saw 1,900"). The production default.
  • Robust decomposition: STL, then a robust z-score (median and MAD rather than mean and std) on the remainder.
  • Isolation forest on windowed features — good for multivariate points, blind to order unless you engineer it.
  • Matrix profile: for every subsequence, the distance to its nearest neighbour elsewhere in the series. Large values are discords — shapes that occur nowhere else — which catches collective anomalies with one parameter (window length).
  • Reconstruction models: an LSTM or convolutional autoencoder trained on normal data; high reconstruction error = anomaly. Also foundation models (MOMENT) used the same way.

Change points are not anomalies

A change point is a persistent shift in level, variance or trend — a deploy that permanently raises latency, a price change. CUSUM accumulates small deviations until they cross a threshold (fast detection of mean shifts online); PELT finds the optimal set of change points offline under a penalty; Bayesian online change-point detection maintains a distribution over "time since last change". After a change point your forecaster must re-level, or it will fire an alert on every point that follows.

The evaluation trap

Many anomaly-detection papers use point adjustment: if any point inside a labelled anomaly segment is flagged, the whole segment counts as detected. With long segments this inflates F1 so much that random scoring can look state of the art. Use event-level precision/recall with a detection-delay budget, or range-based metrics. And in production, measure alert precision — a pager that cries wolf gets muted.

Label whole windows (activity recognition, ECG, machine state). Hierarchical forecasting

Sales roll up SKU → category → store → region → total. Forecast each level independently and they will not add up: the total forecast will not equal the sum of the store forecasts, and finance, supply and store managers end up arguing from different numbers. Reconciliation makes them coherent:

  • Bottom-up: forecast the leaves, sum upward. Coherent and keeps detail, but leaf series are noisy.
  • Top-down: forecast the total, split by historical proportions. Stable, but loses leaf-level dynamics.
  • Middle-out: forecast one middle level, do both.
  • Optimal reconciliation (MinT): forecast every level, then find the coherent forecasts closest to all of them, weighting by the forecast-error covariance. It uses information from every level and usually improves accuracy everywhere. OLS reconciliation is the simple special case with equal weights.

Multivariate series

VAR (vector autoregression) regresses each variable on lags of all variables — the classical multivariate model, practical for a handful of variables since parameters grow as k²·p. Granger causality asks whether lags of X improve forecasts of Y beyond Y's own lags. It is predictive precedence, not causation: a common driver or a faster-reporting proxy produces it too. Say so when you use the word. Deep models handle many channels either independently (PatchTST) or with explicit cross-channel mixing (iTransformer, graph neural networks for sensor networks and traffic).

Counterfactual forecasting for causal impact

"Did the campaign lift sales?" when you could not randomise: train a model on the pre-period using control series unaffected by the intervention (other regions, other products), forecast what the treated series would have done, and compare to what it did. The gap, with its interval, is the estimated effect. Google's CausalImpact does this with Bayesian structural time series; synthetic control builds a weighted combination of untreated units that tracks the treated one before the change. The assumptions to state: controls were not affected by the intervention, and their relationship to the treated series is stable across the pre and post periods.

The data layer is where forecasting systems fail

  • Point-in-time correctness: every feature must be the value as it was known at the forecast origin. Data gets revised (late sales, corrected sensor readings, restated finance); if training uses today's revised history, the backtest sees data production never had. Store data bitemporally (event time and knowledge time) and use as-of joins.
  • Late and missing data: decide explicitly whether a missing day is a zero, a gap to impute, or an outage to exclude. A stock-out day with zero sales is censored demand, not low demand — train on it naively and you learn to under-forecast the items that sell out.
  • Time zones and DST: store in UTC, compute calendar features in local time; hourly local data has 23- and 25-hour days twice a year.

The pipeline

Ingest → validate (schema, volume, freshness) → build features as of the origin → forecast (batch, typically) → post-process (non-negativity, reconciliation, business rules, human overrides) → publish with the model version and the origin → store forecasts and later actuals so accuracy can be computed continuously. Keep every published forecast: you cannot audit or improve a system whose past predictions were overwritten.

Retraining and monitoring

Retrain on a schedule matched to how fast the world changes (weekly is common), and refit lightweight components (level, recent scale) more often than heavyweight ones. Monitor accuracy by horizon and segment against the seasonal-naive baseline, interval coverage, input drift (volume, share of zeros, new items), and the rate of manual overrides — rising overrides are the earliest sign planners have stopped trusting the model. Have a fallback: if the model or its data fails validation, publish seasonal naive rather than nothing.

Scale

Millions of series: per-series classical models parallelise trivially (Spark, Ray, or vectorised libraries), global models train once on sampled series and infer in batch. Cold-start items get a global model's prediction from static features and similar items, or a foundation model zero-shot, until they have history.

Where the LLM fits

Not as the forecaster. As the interface and orchestrator: an agent that turns "how many A100s do we need in Q3 if the new feature launches in July?" into a query, calls the forecasting service as a tool with a scenario (known-future covariate = launch date), retrieves the relevant history, and explains the forecast and its interval in plain language — with the numbers coming from the tool, never generated.
