Start here — what is actually being tested

Candidates hear "elevator" and start optimising a scheduling algorithm. That is not the round. The interviewer wants to see you model a system with independent state machines (each lift), a pluggable policy (which lift answers a call), and a clean boundary between them.

The requirement that trips everyone

There are two different kinds of request and confusing them is the classic error:

  • Hall call — someone on floor 7 presses up. They have not chosen a destination. Any lift may serve it, and the system decides which. This is a dispatch problem.
  • Car call — someone inside lift B presses floor 12. Only lift B can serve it. This is a queue problem inside one lift.

A design with one list of "requests" cannot express this, and it shows immediately when you try to write step().

The vocabulary

  • SCAN / elevator algorithm — keep moving in one direction, serving every stop along the way, then reverse. The same algorithm disk schedulers use, and the reason it is named after lifts.
  • State machine — a lift is in exactly one of IDLE, MOVING_UP, MOVING_DOWN, DOORS_OPEN, MAINTENANCE, with defined transitions. Guarding those transitions is where correctness lives.
  • Dispatcher — the strategy object that assigns a hall call to a lift. Nearest-car, least-load, or zoned. Swappable is the point.

Dispatch strategies

  • Nearest car — smallest absolute floor distance. Simple and naive: it will send a lift travelling away from you.
  • Directional cost — the real one. Cost is small if the lift is moving toward you in your direction, larger if idle, largest if it must finish its run and reverse. Encoding that as a cost function keeps the policy in one testable method.
  • Zoned — each lift owns a floor band, used in tall buildings to cut travel time.

Extensions they will ask for

  • Capacity: a full lift should not answer hall calls, only car calls. One boolean in cost_to_serve.
  • Maintenance: a state that removes the lift from dispatch but lets it finish its current passengers.
  • Peak modes: morning up-peak parks idle lifts at the lobby; evening down-peak spreads them. This is a policy on idle behaviour, which most designs forget to model at all.

The senior layer

  • Make dispatch a cost function, not an if-tree. cost_to_serve(call) returning an integer lets you swap policies, unit-test each one against fixed scenarios, and later replace the whole thing with a learned model without touching the lift. An if-tree spread across the dispatcher is the same logic in a form nobody can change safely — and "I would express the policy as a scoring function" is a sentence that repays itself in every follow-up.
  • Reassignment matters more than assignment. Real systems do not commit a hall call to a lift permanently. If a better-placed lift becomes free, the call moves. That means the dispatcher must own the pending hall calls rather than pushing them into a lift's queue, which is a genuine modelling decision: hall calls belong to the system, car calls belong to the lift. Getting that ownership right is the difference between a design that can grow and one that cannot.
  • Simulate in ticks and inject the clock. Driving the model from a tick() the test controls makes every scenario reproducible — "three calls arrive on tick 4, assert lift 2 is at floor 7 on tick 11". A design that reads wall-clock time or sleeps is untestable, and this round is partly marked on whether your code can be tested at all.
  • The invariants are worth writing down before the code: a lift is never in both stop sets for the same floor; a moving lift always has at least one stop; doors never open while moving; a floor in up_stops is always above the current floor. Assert them in a debug build and most bugs in this problem surface on the first test.

If asked about a building with 40 lifts across 80 floors, the answer stops being OOP and becomes an optimisation problem — destination-dispatch systems where passengers enter their floor in the lobby and are grouped, which changes the interface, not just the algorithm. Start here — the modelling decision that determines everything

Four friends share expenses. The obvious model is a table of debts: "Anna owes Ben £20". It falls apart immediately, because after ten expenses you have Anna owing Ben, Ben owing Chloe, Chloe owing Anna, and nobody knows what anyone actually owes.

The right model: store immutable expenses, derive balances. An expense records who paid, how much, and how it was split. A balance is computed by summing. This is the same principle as the payments ledger — never mutate a running total, always derive it — and it gives you history, editability and auditability for free.

The vocabulary

  • Split type — equal, exact amounts, percentages, or shares (Anna gets 2 shares, Ben 1). The API must support all four, and they all reduce to "a map of user → amount owed that must sum to the total".
  • Net balance — one number per person: positive means they are owed, negative means they owe. The sum across a group is always zero, and that is your invariant.
  • Settlement — an actual payment that reduces a debt. Also an expense, in the opposite direction.
  • Debt simplification — rearranging who pays whom to minimise the number of transactions, without changing anyone's net position.

The trap

Rounding. £10 split three ways is £3.33 each, which is £9.99. The missing penny must go somewhere explicit, or your balances drift and the "sums to zero" invariant breaks. Every interviewer who has used the real product asks about this.

The senior layer

  • Debt simplification is a flow problem and the optimum is NP-hard, which is worth knowing precisely. Minimising the number of transfers to settle a set of net balances is equivalent to a partition problem. The practical algorithm is greedy: repeatedly match the largest creditor with the largest debtor, settle the smaller amount, and repeat. It produces at most n−1 transfers for n people with non-zero balances, which is usually optimal and always close. Saying "greedy gives n−1 and the exact optimum is NP-hard, so I would ship greedy" is a much stronger answer than presenting greedy as if it were optimal.
  • Simplification changes who pays whom, and that is a product decision. If Anna owes Ben and Ben owes Chloe, simplification may tell Anna to pay Chloe directly — someone she never transacted with. That is mathematically correct and socially surprising, and some users hate it. Real products make it opt-in per group. Raising the social consequence of an algorithm is exactly the kind of judgement this round rewards.
  • Concurrent edits need an explicit answer. Two people add an expense to the same group simultaneously — fine, expenses are independent appends. But two people edit the same expense is a conflict, and two people settle the same debt simultaneously double-settles it. Use optimistic concurrency with a version on the expense, and make settlement idempotent with a client-supplied key. Same pattern as the booking hold.
  • Balances are derived, but you will need a cached version, and that is where correctness quietly leaks. Recomputing over every expense is fine for a group of six and unacceptable for a shared house with 4,000 expenses over five years. Cache the net balance with the id of the last expense included, so a stale cache is detectable and repairable rather than silently wrong — and keep the full recompute available as a reconciliation job.
  • Integers in minor units, always. Floating point in a money app produces balances that are off by fractions of a penny, the sum-to-zero assertion fails, and the bug reports are unreproducible. This is the same rule as the payment system and it is the fastest thing to get wrong.

Start here — why this is the state-pattern question

A vending machine is a textbook finite state machine, and the interviewer wants to see whether you model states as objects with behaviour or as a switch statement inside one enormous class.

The states: IDLE → COLLECTING_MONEY → DISPENSING → back to IDLE, with REFUNDING and OUT_OF_SERVICE as branches. The insight that makes the state pattern worth it: the same action means different things in different states. Pressing "select" while idle starts a purchase; pressing it while dispensing should be ignored; pressing it while out of service should show an error.

Why the switch statement fails

With five states and six actions you have thirty combinations. In a switch, they are thirty branches across one method that every change touches. With the state pattern, each state class implements only the actions it cares about and rejects the rest by inheriting a default — and adding a state is a new file rather than thirty edits. That difference is the whole point of the exercise.

The vocabulary

  • State pattern — each state is a class; the context delegates every action to its current state object, which may return a new state.
  • Change-making — dispensing the correct coins. A greedy algorithm works for standard currencies and is provably wrong for some coin systems, which is a nice thing to know.
  • Inventory — slots holding a product, a price and a count. Dispensing must decrement atomically with taking payment.

Start here — expiry is the whole problem

A hash map gives you get, set and delete in O(1). Add "entries expire after N seconds" and you have a genuine design question, because expiry is an event that has to happen without anyone asking for it.

Three strategies, and the right answer uses two of them together:

  • Lazy expiry — check the expiry on read and treat an expired entry as absent. Zero background cost, correct results. But an expired entry nobody reads occupies memory forever, which for a write-heavy workload with short TTLs is a slow leak.
  • Active expiry — a background sweeper deletes expired entries. Reclaims memory, but scanning every key periodically is O(n) per pass and unaffordable at scale.
  • Timer per key — schedule a deletion at the expiry instant. Precise, and with a million keys it means a million timers, which is enormous overhead.

The production answer is lazy plus sampled-active: check on read, and have the sweeper repeatedly sample a small random batch of keys with TTLs, deleting expired ones and continuing while the expired fraction stays high. This is what Redis does, and it bounds the work per tick while keeping the expired fraction low probabilistically.

    def __init__(self, max_bytes, clock=time.monotonic):

The volatile set is the important detail. The sweeper samples from keys that have a TTL, not from all keys. In a store where 5% of keys expire, sampling the full keyspace wastes 95% of its work — and this one set makes active expiry viable.

The sweeper

def sweep(self, sample=20, threshold=0.25, max_rounds=10):
    for _ in range(max_rounds):
        keys = random.sample(self.volatile, min(sample, len(self.volatile)))
        expired = [k for k in keys if self._is_expired(k)]
        for k in expired: self._remove(k)
        if len(expired) / max(1, len(keys)) < threshold:
            break                       # few expired; stop for this tick

Bounded work per tick, adaptive to how much is actually expired. The guarantee is probabilistic — expired-but-not-yet-collected keys stay a small fraction of the whole — and that is the honest thing to state rather than claiming exactness.

Eviction when full

Memory is bounded, so a set into a full store must remove something. Policies: LRU (the default), LFU (better under scan-heavy access), volatile-LRU (evict only keys with a TTL, so keys the user marked permanent survive), or no-eviction (reject the write). Making the policy pluggable and naming volatile-LRU shows you have thought about the semantics rather than just the mechanism.

(a) How many keys can the sweeper examine per second? (b) Does it keep up? (c) What happens to memory, and what do you change?

Answer

(a) Sweeper throughput. Maximum work is 10 ticks/s × 10 rounds × 20 keys = 2,000 key examinations per second — but only if every round finds ≥25% expired and continues. In steady state with a low expired fraction it stops after the first round, giving 10 × 20 = 200 examinations/s.

(b) Does it keep up? With 2M volatile keys and a 60-second average TTL, keys expire at roughly 2,000,000 ÷ 60 ≈ 33,000 per second. The sweeper examines at most 2,000/s and realistically far fewer. It is short by more than an order of magnitude.

Sampling helps less than the raw numbers suggest, though — it is not that the sweeper must examine every key, only that it must delete as many as expire. Each examination deletes a key only if that key happens to be expired. In equilibrium the expired fraction f satisfies: deletions per second = 2,000 × f (when rounds continue) must equal 33,000. That would need f ≈ 16, which is impossible since f ≤ 1. There is no equilibrium below f = 1: the expired fraction rises until essentially every sampled key is expired, the sweeper runs all 10 rounds every tick, deletes 2,000/s, and still falls behind by 31,000/s.

(c) Memory. Expired-but-resident keys accumulate at ~31,000/s. After an hour that is 112 million dead keys — more than ten times the live keyspace — consuming memory for data that is logically gone. Reads of those keys return correctly (lazy expiry catches them), so nothing is functionally wrong: no errors, no bad results, just memory climbing until eviction starts discarding live data or the process is killed.

This is the characteristic failure of sampled expiry and it is invisible in correctness tests.

What to change, in order:

  • Scale the sweeper's budget to the volatile keyspace, not a fixed constant. Sample size and rounds should be a function of len(volatile) and the observed expired fraction — if the fraction stays near 1, you are behind and should do far more work per tick, up to a CPU cap.
  • Add a time-bucketed expiry index. Keep a map from expiry-second to the set of keys expiring in it. The sweeper then deletes exactly the buckets that have passed — O(expired) rather than O(sampled), with no probability involved. Cost is a little memory and an extra structure to maintain on every TTL'd write. At 33,000 expirations/s this is the right answer; sampling is appropriate when expiry rates are low relative to keyspace size, and this workload is the opposite.
  • Expose the diagnostic. Track and export an estimate of expired-but-resident keys from the sweeper's own sample — if 90% of sampled keys are expired, you are badly behind, and that single number turns an invisible leak into an alert.

The design lesson: sampled expiry is a good default because it bounds worst-case work, but it is a heuristic with a workload assumption baked in — that expirations are slow relative to the sampling budget. When that assumption breaks, it fails silently in the direction of unbounded memory. Knowing which regime you are in, and switching to an indexed approach when expiry rate is high, is the judgement being tested.

Start here — three orthogonal concerns

A logging framework is the clearest example of separating concerns that vary independently:

  • What to log — the level (DEBUG, INFO, WARN, ERROR) and whether this logger is enabled for it.
  • Where it goes — console, rotating file, network, an aggregation service. Several destinations at once, each possibly with a different threshold.
  • How it looks — plain text, JSON, with or without timestamps and context.

Those three vary independently, so they are three interfaces: Logger, Appender (or Handler), Formatter. A design that puts formatting inside the file writer cannot produce JSON to the network and text to the console, which is the first thing anyone asks for.

The performance requirement people miss

A disabled log call must cost almost nothing. Applications contain thousands of log.debug(...) calls on hot paths that are disabled in production. If building the message happens before the level check, you pay string formatting for a message that is discarded. That is why frameworks offer lazy evaluation — pass the format string and arguments, or a closure, and only render if the level passes.

Start here — two ways to undo, and one of them scales

  • Memento (snapshots): save a full copy of the document before each change. Undo restores the previous copy. Simple and correct, and it costs O(document size) per edit — a 10 MB file with 1,000 edits is 10 GB of history.
  • Command (operations): store what changed, with enough information to reverse it. Undo applies the inverse. Memory is O(size of the change), which for typing one character is a few bytes.

The command pattern is the answer, and the interview is largely about whether you can define invertible operations correctly.

The subtlety that catches people

A delete command must store what it deleted, not just the range — you cannot undo "delete characters 10 to 20" without knowing what those characters were. Similarly a replace stores both the old and the new text. Getting the inverse right means each command carries the state needed to reverse itself, and forgetting that is the classic bug.

The vocabulary

  • Command — an object with execute() and undo(), holding its own parameters and the data needed to reverse.
  • Undo stack / redo stack — two stacks. Undo pops from one and pushes to the other. Any new edit clears the redo stack, because the future you could have redone no longer follows from the present.
  • Coalescing — merging consecutive small commands (typing individual characters) into one undo unit, so Ctrl+Z does not undo one letter at a time.
  • Gap buffer / piece table — the document representations that make insertion at a cursor cheap, rather than an O(n) array shift.

class Command(ABC):
    def execute(doc) -> None
    def undo(doc) -> None
    def merge_with(other) -> Command | None   # for coalescing

class InsertCommand(Command):
    def __init__(self, pos, text): self.pos, self.text = pos, text
    def execute(self, doc): doc.insert(self.pos, self.text)
    def undo(self, doc):    doc.delete(self.pos, len(self.text))

class DeleteCommand(Command):
    def __init__(self, pos, length):
        self.pos, self.length, self.removed = pos, length, None
    def execute(self, doc):
        self.removed = doc.text(self.pos, self.length)   # capture first!
        doc.delete(self.pos, self.length)
    def undo(self, doc): doc.insert(self.pos, self.removed)

class Editor:
    def __init__(self, doc, max_history=1000):
        self.doc, self.undo_stack, self.redo_stack = doc, [], []
    def run(self, cmd):
        cmd.execute(self.doc)
        if self.undo_stack:
            merged = self.undo_stack[-1].merge_with(cmd)
            if merged: self.undo_stack[-1] = merged
            else:      self.undo_stack.append(cmd)
        else: self.undo_stack.append(cmd)
        self.redo_stack.clear()           # the redo future is gone
        if len(self.undo_stack) > self.max_history: self.undo_stack.pop(0)
    def undo(self):
        if not self.undo_stack: return
        c = self.undo_stack.pop(); c.undo(self.doc); self.redo_stack.append(c)
    def redo(self):
        if not self.redo_stack: return
        c = self.redo_stack.pop(); c.execute(self.doc); self.undo_stack.append(c)

DeleteCommand.execute captures the text before removing it. This is the line that makes undo possible, and it means a command is not fully constructed until it has run once — which is worth a comment, because it surprises readers.

Coalescing

Typing "hello" produces five insert commands. Undo should remove the word, not one letter. Merge two inserts when the second begins exactly where the first ends, the gap since the last keystroke is under a threshold, and neither crosses a word boundary or a cursor jump. Getting this almost right is fine; getting it absent is what users notice.

The document representation

A plain string means every insert is O(n) — copying the whole document per keystroke. Options: a gap buffer (keep spare space at the cursor, so typing is O(1) and moving the cursor is O(distance)), or a piece table (the document is a list of spans into an original buffer and an append-only add buffer, so every edit is O(1) amortised and the original is never modified). Piece tables are what production editors use, and they give you cheap undo almost for free because nothing is ever overwritten.

Extensions

  • Grouped commands: a MacroCommand holding a list, executed and undone as one unit. Needed for find-and-replace-all.
  • Persistence: serialise the command log and you get a file format that is also a complete history.
  • Selection and multiple cursors: a command becomes a list of per-cursor edits applied from the end of the document backwards, so earlier edits do not shift later positions.

The senior layer

  • Apply multi-position edits back to front. With multiple cursors or a replace-all, editing position 10 shifts everything after it, so positions captured earlier become wrong. Sorting the edits descending by position and applying in that order means each edit only affects text after itself, which has already been handled. It is a two-line fix and an off-by-many bug if missed.
  • Cursor position is part of the undo state, not a side effect. Undoing a delete should restore the cursor to where it was, not leave it where the undo happened. So each command records the cursor before and after, and undo restores the "before". Users perceive an editor whose undo leaves the cursor in the wrong place as broken even when the text is correct — a good example of state that is invisible in the data model and highly visible in the product.
  • Bounding history has to be deliberate. Unlimited undo means unbounded memory for a long editing session. Cap by count, by total bytes of stored data, or by both, and drop from the oldest end. Note the asymmetry: an insert command stores the inserted text and a delete stores the deleted text, so deleting a 5 MB block puts 5 MB into the undo stack. Size-based bounding is the correct policy for the same reason it is correct for caches.
  • Command versus memento is a real choice, not a foregone conclusion. Commands are compact but require every operation to be invertible, which is hard for operations like "reformat the whole document" or anything involving external state. A hybrid works well: snapshot periodically, store commands between snapshots, and undo by restoring the nearest snapshot and replaying forward — the same checkpoint-plus-log pattern as a database or a CRDT document. Offering the hybrid shows you have thought past the textbook answer.
  • Collaborative editing invalidates this design and you should say so. With two users, undo cannot simply pop the global stack — undoing your last edit when someone else has edited since is a selective undo that must be transformed against the intervening operations. That is the operational-transformation or CRDT problem, and recognising that single-user undo does not generalise is a much better answer than attempting to bolt it on.

What actually makes calendars hard

Recurring events. "Every Tuesday at 10:00 for a year" is one stored rule that expands into 52 occurrences. You cannot store the occurrences (an event recurring forever has infinite occurrences) and you cannot avoid materialising them (you need to check overlap against each). So a calendar stores rules and expands them lazily over the queried window — and then someone moves a single occurrence, which becomes an exception attached to the rule.

Timezones compound it: "every Tuesday at 10:00 London time" shifts in UTC when the clocks change, so an event stored in UTC drifts by an hour twice a year. Recurring events must store a local time plus a timezone, never a UTC instant.

Finding common free slots

def find_slots(self, user_ids, duration, window):
    busy = []
    for u in user_ids:
        busy += [(o.start_utc, o.end_utc) for o in self.occurrences(u, window)]
        busy += self.outside_working_hours(u, window)   # treat as busy
    busy.sort()
    # merge overlapping busy intervals
    merged = []
    for s, e in busy:
        if merged and s <= merged[-1][1]:
            merged[-1] = (merged[-1][0], max(merged[-1][1], e))
        else:
            merged.append((s, e))
    # invert: gaps between merged busy blocks are free
    out, cursor = [], window.start
    for s, e in merged:
        if s - cursor >= duration: out.append((cursor, s))
        cursor = max(cursor, e)
    if window.end - cursor >= duration: out.append((cursor, window.end))
    return out

Sort, merge, invert. O(n log n) dominated by the sort. Treating non-working hours as busy intervals is the trick that avoids a second pass — working hours, holidays and declined-but-blocking events all become ordinary busy blocks, and the algorithm never learns about them.

Room booking

Rooms differ from people: a person can be double-booked, a room cannot. So room booking needs the same atomic claim as a seat reservation — a conditional insert guarded by "no overlapping booking exists", or a database exclusion constraint on the range. A read-then-write races, and two meetings end up in the same room.

Store a timezone identifier and convert using a real timezone database at the moment of expansion, because the offset is a function of the instant, not a property of the location.
